### Bridge: Vectors recap: components, cross product and the right-hand rule

This is a short refresher; the full introduction to vectors, dot products and cross products is in bridge PH-001-B1 of the Newton-laws lesson (PH-001), and you should read that first if the words 'component' or 'unit vector' are not yet comfortable.

A **vector** has a size and a direction. With three perpendicular axes and unit vectors x̂, ŷ, ẑ, a vector is A = Aₓ x̂ + A_y ŷ + A_z ẑ. The **cross product** of two vectors is a third vector that is perpendicular to both. Its size is |A||B| sin θ, where θ is the angle between them, and its direction follows the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers of your right hand from A towards B through the smaller angle, and the thumb points along A × B.

In components:

$$ A × B = ( A_y B_z − A_z B_y ,  A_z Bₓ − Aₓ B_z ,  Aₓ B_y − A_y Bₓ ) $$

The three basic products to memorise follow a cycle: x̂ × ŷ = ẑ, ŷ × ẑ = x̂, ẑ × x̂ = ŷ. Going backwards in the cycle gives a minus sign, for example ŷ × x̂ = −ẑ. The order matters: B × A = −(A × B). Parallel vectors give zero.

One identity is used repeatedly in this lesson, the 'back-cab' rule:

$$ A × (B × C) = B (A · C) − C (A · B) $$

Applied with two equal vectors it gives ω × (ω × r) = ω (ω · r) − r ω². That is exactly what we need later to see that the centrifugal force points away from the axis.

Worked check. Let ω = 2 ẑ (rad s⁻¹) and v = 3 x̂ (m s⁻¹). Then ω × v = 2·3 (ẑ × x̂) = 6 ŷ. Using components: (0·0 − 2·0, 2·3 − 0·0, 0) = (0, 6, 0). Both routes agree.

### Bridge: The derivative of a rotating unit vector

Everything in the rotating-frame theory rests on one small fact: how fast does a unit vector change when it turns? Start in a flat plane. A unit vector pointing at angle θ from the x-axis is e = (cos θ, sin θ). Its length is 1 for every θ. If θ grows with time at the rate θ̇ = dθ/dt = ω (radians per second), the chain rule gives

$$ de/dt = ω ( −sin θ , cos θ ) $$

The vector (−sin θ, cos θ) has length 1 and is perpendicular to e: check by the dot product, (cos θ)(−sin θ) + (sin θ)(cos θ) = 0. So the tip of e moves at speed ω, at right angles to e itself. That is the familiar picture of a point moving on a circle of radius 1.

Now introduce the angular velocity vector: its direction is the axis of rotation chosen by the right-hand rule (fingers curl the way the rotation goes, thumb gives the axis) and its size is ω. For rotation in the x–y plane the axis is ẑ, so the angular velocity is ω = ω ẑ. Compute ẑ × e = ẑ × (cos θ x̂ + sin θ ŷ) = cos θ ŷ − sin θ x̂ = (−sin θ, cos θ). Hence

$$ de/dt = ω × e $$

This is a vector statement, so it is true for any axis, not only ẑ: if a rigid set of arrows rotates about an axis with angular velocity ω, every arrow e fixed in that rigid set changes at the rate ω × e. We will apply it to the three axes of a rotating frame.

Dimensional check: ω has units s⁻¹ and e is dimensionless, so ω × e has units s⁻¹, as a rate of change of a pure number should. Limiting case: ω = 0 gives de/dt = 0, a frame that is not turning keeps its axes fixed.

### Bridge: Calculus of vector-valued functions: derivative, product rules and constant length

A vector that depends on time, A(t) = (Aₓ(t), A_y(t), A_z(t)), is differentiated one component at a time: dA/dt = (dAₓ/dt, dA_y/dt, dA_z/dt). Everything you know about derivatives of ordinary functions carries over, with one warning for cross products.

**Product rules.** For a scalar function f(t) and a vector A: d(fA)/dt = (df/dt)A + f dA/dt. For the dot product: d(A·B)/dt = Ȧ·B + A·Ḃ. For the cross product: d(A × B)/dt = Ȧ × B + A × Ḃ. In the last rule the order of the factors must be preserved in every term, because B × A = −A × B.

Worked check. Take A = (t, t², 0) and B = (0, 0, t). Then A × B = (t²·t − 0, 0 − t·t, 0) = (t³, −t², 0), whose derivative is (3t², −2t, 0). By the product rule: Ȧ × B = (1, 2t, 0) × (0, 0, t) = (2t², −t, 0) and A × Ḃ = (t, t², 0) × (0, 0, 1) = (t², −t, 0). Sum = (3t², −2t, 0). The two routes agree.

**A vector of constant length changes only in direction.** If |A| is constant then A·A is constant, so 0 = d(A·A)/dt = 2 A·Ȧ. A zero dot product means Ȧ is perpendicular to A. This is why the rate of change of a unit vector (previous bridge) is perpendicular to it, and why the rate of change of a vector that merely rotates is ω × A with no component along A.

**Why this matters here.** A vector such as the velocity can be written in two ways, using fixed axes or using axes that turn. When the axes turn, differentiating the product (component × axis vector) needs the product rule: one term from the changing component and one from the changing axis. That second term is the whole content of the rotating-frame theory, and it is derived in module 3.

Notation: a dot over a letter means d/dt; the cross-product order is always kept as written.

### 1. Orientation: what 'rotating frames, centripetal and Coriolis accelerations' asks of you

*Objective:* Say in your own words what the syllabus phrase asks, and recognise the three ideas (centripetal, centrifugal, Coriolis) before any algebra.

## Where this sits in the syllabus

The Physics Paper I syllabus, section 1(a) 'Mechanics of particles', contains the phrase 'applications to rotating frames, centripetal and Coriolis accelerations'. This lesson is topic PH-002. It follows PH-001 (Newton's laws and conservation laws), where you learnt that Newton's second law F = m a is valid only in an **inertial frame**, that is, a frame that is not accelerating or turning relative to the distant universe. This lesson asks: what happens if you insist on working in a frame that is turning?

## Why anyone would want that

We live on a spinning planet. A laboratory, a river, a weather map and an artillery shell all sit in a frame that turns once per day. To describe a ball on a merry-go-round it is far more natural to ride along with the ride than to stand outside. The price is that Newton's second law, written in the turning frame, picks up extra terms. These extra terms are mathematically accelerations; multiplied by mass and moved to the force side they behave like forces.

## The three words you must separate now

- **Centripetal** means 'centre-seeking'. It describes the real, inward net force (or acceleration, m ω² r or ω² r) that any body in circular motion needs, seen from an **inertial** frame. Tension in a string, friction on a tyre, gravity on a planet can each play this role. It is a job description, not a new kind of force.
- **Centrifugal** means 'centre-fleeing'. It is the outward **fictitious force** m ω² r that appears only if you describe the motion from the **rotating** frame.
- **Coriolis** is a second fictitious force, −2m ω × v, that appears only for bodies that are **moving** relative to the rotating frame, and always acts at right angles to that motion.

A **fictitious (pseudo, inertial) force** is a term that must be added to the real forces so that F = m a may still be used in a non-inertial frame. It has no source body and no reaction partner.

### 2. Foundation: frames, angular velocity and why fictitious forces are needed

*Objective:* Define a rotating frame precisely, describe angular velocity as a vector, and explain why a turning observer must add terms to Newton's second law.

## Two observers

Let **S** be an inertial frame (say, an observer standing on a platform at rest relative to the stars) and **S′** a frame whose origin coincides with the origin of S but whose axes turn about it with angular velocity ω. A merry-go-round with its centre at the origin is the model: S is the ground, S′ rides on the ride. Both observers describe the same particle, so there is one position vector r from the common origin to the particle; only the components differ, because they are measured along different axes.

## Angular velocity as a vector

The angular velocity of the frame is a vector ω. Its **direction** is along the axis of rotation, given by the right-hand rule (curl the fingers the way the frame turns; the thumb is ω). Its **magnitude** is the rate of turning in radians per second. For the Earth, taking one sidereal day of 86 164 s, ω = 2π / 86 164 ≈ 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹ (the solar day of 86 400 s would give 7.272 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹; the small difference does not matter unless a question prescribes a value). Its direction is along the axis from the south pole to the north pole.

Units: [ω] = s⁻¹ (radian is dimensionless). The angular acceleration dω/dt has units s⁻².

## The conventions used in this lesson

- A subscript 'in' on a time derivative means the rate of change as seen by the inertial observer S; 'rot' means as seen by the rotating observer S′.
- The velocity of the particle seen from S′ is v_rot = (dr/dt)_rot, its acceleration a_rot = (d²r/dt²)_rot.
- ω is the angular velocity of S′ relative to S. Since ω × ω = 0, the rate of change of ω is the same whether measured in S or S′ (module 3), so one symbol dω/dt is enough.
- Coordinate system for Earth problems (module 6 onwards): x east, y north, z up, with latitude λ positive in the northern hemisphere and negative in the southern.

## Why fictitious forces are needed

Take a smooth ball released at rest in S′ on a frictionless merry-go-round. In S nothing pushes the ball (ignore the surface), so it moves in a straight line at constant velocity: the first law. The observer in S′ sees the ball drift and curve across the ride. If this observer still insists that F = m a, she must invent forces, outward and sideways, to account for the curve. These invented forces are the fictitious forces. They are not interactions between bodies (no body exerts a centrifugal force), they have no third-law partner, and they vanish when the frame stops turning.

## The programme of the next two modules

We want a machine that converts the true law m a_in = F into an equation for a_rot. For that we need to compare how a vector changes in S with how it changes in S′. Module 3 derives the comparison rule; module 4 applies it twice to get a_in in terms of a_rot and reads off the fictitious forces.

### 3. Core: the time-derivative rule (d/dt)_in = (d/dt)_rot + ω ×

*Objective:* Derive, from the derivative of rotating unit vectors, the operator relation between rates of change seen from an inertial and from a rotating frame.

## What we want to prove

For any vector A (position, velocity, momentum, angular velocity, anything):

$$ (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A $$

Read it as an operator rule: to find how fast A changes for the inertial observer, take its rate of change as the rotating observer sees it and add ω × A.

## Setting up

Let e₁, e₂, e₃ be three mutually perpendicular unit vectors that are **fixed in the rotating frame S′** (the axes of S′). The rotating observer sees them as constant. The inertial observer sees them turn. Write the vector in terms of them:

$$ A = A₁ e₁ + A₂ e₂ + A₃ e₃ $$

The numbers A₁, A₂, A₃ are the components measured by the rotating observer. Assumptions: the two frames share an origin; ω may vary in time (the derivation does not use constancy); the axes of S′ stay perpendicular and of unit length (they form a rigid set).

## Derivation

Step 1. The rotating observer measures the change of A by watching only the components, because the axes look still to her:

$$ (dA/dt)_rot = (dA₁/dt) e₁ + (dA₂/dt) e₂ + (dA₃/dt) e₃ $$

Step 2. The inertial observer differentiates the same product A = Σ Aᵢ eᵢ with the product rule (bridge B3). Each term gives two pieces, one from the component and one from the axis vector:

$$ (dA/dt)_in = Σ (dAᵢ/dt) eᵢ + Σ Aᵢ (deᵢ/dt)_in $$

Step 3. The first sum is exactly (dA/dt)_rot from Step 1.

Step 4. The axis vectors are fixed in a rigid frame turning with angular velocity ω, so by bridge B2 each satisfies (deᵢ/dt)_in = ω × eᵢ. Hence

$$ Σ Aᵢ (deᵢ/dt)_in = Σ Aᵢ (ω × eᵢ) = ω × ( Σ Aᵢ eᵢ ) = ω × A $$

where we used that the cross product is linear, ω × (a + b) = ω × a + ω × b, and that ω is the same for all three terms.

Step 5. Combine Steps 2 to 4: (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A. ∎

## Physical interpretation

The term ω × A is the rate of change A would have in S if A were frozen in S′ and simply carried round by the turning axes. The term (dA/dt)_rot is the extra change relative to the turning axes. Together they make the total change seen from S.

## Checks

- Dimensions: dA/dt has dimensions [A]/T; ω × A also has [A]/T because [ω] = 1/T.
- Limiting case ω = 0: the frames do not turn relative to each other and the two rates agree.
- A fixed vector: if A is fixed in S′ then (dA/dt)_rot = 0 and (dA/dt)_in = ω × A, the velocity of a point carried round by the rotation, as for a point on a wheel.
- Apply it to A = ω: (dω/dt)_in = (dω/dt)_rot + ω × ω = (dω/dt)_rot, which justifies using a single symbol dω/dt.

## Common derivation traps

Writing A × ω instead of ω × A (the order gives the sign), or forgetting that the components Aᵢ must be those measured along the **rotating** axes.

### 4. Core: velocity, acceleration and the effective force law

*Objective:* Apply the derivative rule twice to obtain a_in = a_rot + 2ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r, and rearrange it into the effective force in the rotating frame, naming each term.

## Velocity

Put A = r, the common position vector, in the rule of module 3:

$$ v_in = v_rot + ω × r $$

So the inertial velocity of a particle is its velocity relative to the turning frame plus the velocity ω × r of the point of the frame that is momentarily at the particle.

## Acceleration, step by step

By definition a_in = (d v_in/dt)_in. Apply the rule to the vector A = v_in:

$$ a_in = (d v_in/dt)_rot + ω × v_in $$

Substitute v_in = v_rot + ω × r in both places:

$$ a_in = (d/dt)_rot ( v_rot + ω × r ) + ω × ( v_rot + ω × r ) $$

Expand the rotating-frame derivative with the product rule, using (dr/dt)_rot = v_rot and (dv_rot/dt)_rot = a_rot:

$$ (d/dt)_rot ( ω × r ) = (dω/dt) × r + ω × v_rot $$

Collect all terms:

$$ a_in = a_rot + (dω/dt) × r + ω × v_rot + ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) $$

Two terms ω × v_rot are identical, so:

$$ a_in = a_rot + 2 ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r $$

**Mid-module stopping point (about 30 minutes in):** if you must pause, stop here, once you can reproduce a_in = a_rot + 2 ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r. The force law and the reading of each term can wait for the next sitting.

## The force law

In S the true law is m a_in = F, where F is the sum of real forces. Solve for m a_rot:

$$ m a_rot = F − 2m ω × v_rot − m ω × (ω × r) − m (dω/dt) × r $$

The rotating observer therefore uses the ordinary law with an **effective force** F_eff = F + F_cor + F_cf + F_Euler, where

- **Coriolis force** F_cor = −2m ω × v_rot,
- **centrifugal force** F_cf = −m ω × (ω × r),
- **Euler force** F_Euler = −m (dω/dt) × r.

## Reading the terms

*Centrifugal.* By the back-cab rule (bridge B1), ω × (ω × r) = ω (ω·r) − ω² r = −ω² r_⊥, where r_⊥ = r − (r·ω̂) ω̂ is the perpendicular vector from the axis to the particle. Hence F_cf = m ω² r_⊥: magnitude m ω² ρ (ρ the distance from the axis), direction straight away from the axis, independent of velocity.

*Coriolis.* Its magnitude is 2m ω v_rot sin θ (θ the angle between ω and v_rot). It is perpendicular to both ω and v_rot, so it never changes the speed in the rotating frame: its power F_cor·v_rot is zero. It vanishes for a particle at rest in S′ or moving parallel to the axis.

*Euler.* It exists only if the rotation rate or axis changes with time; it is perpendicular to r and to dω/dt, with sense opposite to the angular acceleration (for a fixed axis this is the tangential direction, as when a turntable starts up).

## Checks

Dimensions: [ω v] = T⁻¹ · L T⁻¹ = L T⁻², an acceleration, as is [ω² r] and [(dω/dt) r]. Limiting cases: ω = 0 gives a_in = a_rot; uniform ω gives no Euler term; a particle at rest in S′ with uniform ω gives a_in = ω × (ω × r) = −ω² r_⊥, the inward centripetal acceleration of circular motion seen from S.

### 5. Core: worked cases - bead on a rotating rod, turntable and Euler force

*Objective:* Use the effective force law on three standard systems and cross-check one of them from the inertial frame.

## Case 1: bead on a smooth rod rotating at constant ω

A rod in a horizontal plane rotates about a vertical axis through one end with constant angular speed ω. A bead of mass m slides on it without friction. Rotating frame S′: the rod. Axes: r̂ along the rod, θ̂ perpendicular to it in the direction of rotation, ẑ up. ω = ω ẑ constant (so no Euler force). Bead at distance r, velocity relative to the rod v_rot = ṙ r̂.

Centrifugal force: m ω² r r̂ (outward). Coriolis force: −2m ω × v_rot = −2m ω ṙ (ẑ × r̂) = −2m ω ṙ θ̂, perpendicular to the rod, opposing the rotation sense. The rod can push only perpendicular to itself (it is smooth), so it supplies a normal force N θ̂; gravity is cancelled by the vertical support. Along the rod:

$$ m r̈ = m ω² r $$

Perpendicular to the rod the bead stays on the rod, so a_rot has no θ̂ component: N − 2m ω ṙ = 0, giving N = 2m ω ṙ.

Solve with r(0) = r₀ and ṙ(0) = 0. The equation r̈ = ω² r has solutions A e^{ωt} + B e^{−ωt}; the initial conditions give A = B = r₀/2, so

$$ r(t) = r₀ cosh ωt ,  ṙ = r₀ ω sinh ωt ,  N = 2m r₀ ω² sinh ωt $$

*Cross-check in the inertial frame.* In polar coordinates the acceleration is (r̈ − r θ̇²) r̂ + (r θ̈ + 2ṙ θ̇) θ̂. With θ̇ = ω and θ̈ = 0: radial, no real radial force, so r̈ = ω² r (same); transverse, N = m·2ṙ ω (same). Dimensions: ω² r is m s⁻²; N is kg m s⁻². Limiting case ω → 0: r̈ = 0, the bead keeps its constant speed. The bead's energy alone is not conserved, because the rod does work through N; the combination ½ṙ² − ½ω²r² is conserved, since d/dt of it is ṙ(r̈ − ω² r) = 0 (the Coriolis force does no work).

## Case 2: particle at rest on a turntable

A coin sits at distance ρ from the axis of a turntable turning at constant ω. In the rotating frame it is at rest (v_rot = 0), so the Coriolis force is zero; the centrifugal force is m ω² ρ outward, and static friction f must balance it: f = m ω² ρ inward. The coin stays if f ≤ μ_s m g, i.e. ω² ρ ≤ μ_s g. In the inertial frame friction is the centripetal force m ω² ρ. The same equation, two descriptions.

## Case 3: Euler force when the turntable speeds up

Let ω = α t ẑ with constant angular acceleration α. A particle at rest at radius ρ in S′ feels an Euler force −m α ẑ × (ρ r̂) = −m α ρ θ̂, opposite to the direction of the speed-up, like being pushed back in a bus that accelerates.

### 6. Core: the Earth as a rotating frame - effective gravity and the Coriolis components

*Objective:* Set up local east-north-up axes, derive the effective gravity including its latitude variation, and obtain the three components of the Coriolis acceleration at latitude λ.

## Setup and assumptions

Treat the Earth as a sphere of radius R turning at constant ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹, so there is no Euler force. Ignore the Earth's motion round the Sun. At a point of latitude λ (positive north, negative south) use local axes: x̂ east, ŷ north, ẑ up. The angular velocity points along the polar axis. The north direction is tilted by the angle λ from the polar axis, and the vertical makes the angle (90° − λ) with it, so

$$ ω = ( 0 , ω cos λ , ω sin λ ) $$

Check: at the equator (λ = 0) ω points north along the horizontal; at the north pole (λ = 90°) it points straight up.

## Effective gravity

The pull of the Earth's mass on a body of mass m is −m g₀ ẑ (towards the centre; g₀ the pure gravitational value, about 9.8 m s⁻²). The centrifugal acceleration is ω² r_⊥ where r_⊥ is the perpendicular vector from the polar axis; its size is ω² R cos λ, and in local axes its direction is outward from the axis: (0, −sin λ, cos λ). Check: at the equator it is straight up; at latitude λ in the north it also points slightly south. Hence

$$ g_eff = ( 0 , −ω² R sin λ cos λ , −g₀ + ω² R cos² λ ) $$

The vertical component is reduced by ω² R cos² λ. To first order in the small ratio ω²R/g₀,

$$ |g_eff| ≈ g₀ − ω² R cos² λ $$

Numbers: ω² R = (7.29 × 10⁻⁵)² × 6.378 × 10⁶ ≈ 0.0339 m s⁻², about 1/289 of g₀. So the centrifugal effect alone lowers g by 0.034 m s⁻² at the equator and by nothing at the poles. The plumb line (the direction of g_eff) is turned away from the true centre-pointing direction towards the equator by the angle ≈ (ω²R/g₀) sin λ cos λ, maximum at λ = 45°, where it is about 1.7 × 10⁻³ rad ≈ 0.1° (six arc-minutes). Observed g also varies because the Earth is slightly flattened (flag: this oblateness effect is standard geophysics not derived in the extracts; the total equator-to-pole difference is about 0.05 m s⁻², larger than the centrifugal part alone).

## Coriolis acceleration in local axes

The Coriolis acceleration is a_cor = −2 ω × v. With v = (v_E, v_N, v_U) and the cross-product formula (bridge B1),

$$ ω × v = ω ( cos λ v_U − sin λ v_N , sin λ v_E , −cos λ v_E ) $$

$$ a_cor = 2ω ( sin λ v_N − cos λ v_U , −sin λ v_E , cos λ v_E ) $$

## Reading it

- **Horizontal motion** (v_U = 0): a_cor = 2ω sin λ (v_N, −v_E, 0) plus a vertical piece. The horizontal part has size 2ω v sin λ and is perpendicular to the velocity, **to the right in the northern hemisphere** (sin λ > 0) and **to the left in the southern**. It is zero at the equator and largest at the poles.
- **Vertical motion** (v_E = v_N = 0): a_cor = 2ω (−cos λ v_U, 0, 0). Falling (v_U < 0) gives an acceleration towards the **east** (module 7); rising gives westward.
- **Eastward motion** adds an upward acceleration 2ω cos λ v_E, effectively reducing g (the Eötvös effect).

Dimension check: [ω v] = L T⁻². Example: air at 10 m s⁻¹ at 20° N has horizontal Coriolis acceleration 2 × 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × sin 20° ≈ 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻², tiny, but acting over hours it shapes weather systems.

### 7. Core: free fall deflection and the projectile - Coriolis effects to first order

*Objective:* Derive the eastward deflection of a freely falling body, d = (1/3) ω g cos λ t³, and the lateral deflection of a projectile fired due north, to first order in ω.

## Free fall

Question: a stone is released from rest (relative to the Earth) from height h at latitude λ. Where does it land relative to the foot of the plumb line?

Assumptions. First order in ω only: terms of order ω² (centrifugal change of g, the second-order Coriolis corrections) are dropped. Constant g downward, no air, h ≪ R so λ does not change. Frame: local x east, y north, z up, from module 6.

Step 1 (zeroth-order motion). Without rotation the velocity is v = (0, 0, −g t), so v_U = −g t, v_E = v_N = 0.

Step 2 (Coriolis acceleration). From module 6, a_cor = 2ω (sin λ v_N − cos λ v_U , −sin λ v_E , cos λ v_E). Inserting the zeroth-order velocity:

$$ a_cor = ( 2ω g t cos λ , 0 , 0 ) $$

Only an eastward acceleration appears; the other components vanish at this order.

Step 3 (integrate). ẍ = 2ω g cos λ t with ẋ(0) = 0, x(0) = 0 (released at rest relative to the Earth, from the plumb line):

$$ ẋ = ω g cos λ t² ,  x = (1/3) ω g cos λ t³ $$

Step 4 (fall time). The unperturbed fall time satisfies h = ½ g t², so t = √(2h/g). Hence

$$ d = (1/3) ω g cos λ (2h/g)^{3/2} = √(8/(9g)) ω h^{3/2} cos λ $$

This is the form printed in the standard textbooks.

Checks. Dimensions: ω [T⁻¹] × h^{3/2} [L^{3/2}] / g^{1/2} [L^{1/2} T⁻¹] = L. Limits: ω = 0 gives zero; λ = 90° (pole) gives zero because the fall is parallel to the axis; the equator gives the maximum. Interpretation from the inertial frame: The stone starts with the Earth's angular velocity. As it falls its distance from the axis shrinks, and angular-momentum conservation makes it turn slightly faster than the Earth. Its eastward speed relative to the ground grows as 2 ω (h − z) cos λ = ω g t² cos λ, which integrates to (1/3) ω g cos λ t³. The naive picture, keeping a constant extra speed ω h cos λ, gives 1/2 and overestimates, because 'down' turns with the Earth and gravity pulls the stone back westward relative to its original direction. Number: h = 100 m, λ = 45°, ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵: t = 4.52 s and d = 1.55 cm; at the equator 2.20 cm. A numerical integration of the full Coriolis equation gave 1.553 cm at 45°, agreeing with the formula.

**Mid-module stopping point (about 30 minutes in):** if you must pause, stop here, once you can derive d = (1/3) ω g cos λ t³ and say why the naive 1/2 is wrong. The projectile can wait.

## Projectile fired due north

Question: lateral deflection by the Coriolis force of a shell fired due north at speed v₀, elevation α, in vacuum (a teaching idealisation).

Step 1. Zeroth-order velocity: v_N = u = v₀ cos α constant, v_E = 0, v_U = w₀ − g t with w₀ = v₀ sin α; flight time T = 2 w₀/g.

Step 2. From module 6 with v_E = 0 only the eastward component survives: a_E = 2ω (u sin λ − (w₀ − g t) cos λ).

Step 3. Integrate twice from 0 to T:

$$ x(T) = 2ω [ ½ u T² sin λ − cos λ ( ½ w₀ T² − g T³/6 ) ] $$

Step 4. Insert T = 2w₀/g: ½ w₀T² = 2w₀³/g² and gT³/6 = 4w₀³/(3g²). Therefore

$$ x(T) = ω sin λ (u T) T − (4/3) ω cos λ w₀³/g² $$

The first term (range u T times T times ω sin λ) is the horizontal Coriolis drift to the right; the second is the westward effect of the vertical motion. Number: v₀ = 500 m s⁻¹, α = 45°, λ = 30° N: T = 72.2 s, range 25.5 km, first term 67.1 m, second 38.7 m, net 28.4 m east (to the right of the line of fire); numerical integration gave 28.4 m. Air drag would change these figures greatly.

### 8. Master: Foucault pendulum, winds and cyclones

*Objective:* Derive the rotation rate ω sin λ of the plane of a Foucault pendulum and explain, qualitatively, trade winds and cyclones as Coriolis effects.

## The Foucault pendulum

A long pendulum with a heavy bob swings in a nearly horizontal plane. Foucault showed in 1851 that the plane of swing turns slowly relative to the ground, direct evidence that the Earth turns. We derive the rate.

Assumptions. Small swings, so the bob moves in the horizontal x–y plane; length L; Ω₀² = g/L. Only the vertical component of ω matters for horizontal motion, ω_z = ω sin λ (the other component multiplies the tiny vertical velocity). The terms of order ω² are neglected.

Equations of motion. From module 6 the horizontal Coriolis acceleration is 2ω_z (v_N, −v_E) = 2ω_z (ẏ, −ẋ), where x is east, y north. With the restoring acceleration −Ω₀² (x, y):

$$ ẍ + Ω₀² x = 2ω_z ẏ ,  ÿ + Ω₀² y = −2ω_z ẋ $$

Complex trick. Let q = x + i y. Multiply the second equation by i and add: q̈ + Ω₀² q = 2ω_z (ẏ − i ẋ) = −2i ω_z (ẋ + i ẏ) = −2i ω_z q̇.

Try q = u e^{−i ω_z t} where u(t) carries the fast pendulum oscillation (the substitution is exact, not an approximation). Then q̇ = (u̇ − i ω_z u) e^{−iω_z t} and q̈ = (ü − 2i ω_z u̇ − ω_z² u) e^{−iω_z t}. Substituting, the u̇ terms cancel:

$$ ü + ( Ω₀² + ω_z² ) u = 0 $$

Because ω_z ≪ Ω₀, u oscillates at essentially the pendulum frequency, along an approximately fixed direction in the (x, y) plane (for a release from rest the path is very slightly elliptical); the factor e^{−iω_z t} then **rotates that whole pattern clockwise (seen from above) at angular rate ω_z = ω sin λ** in the northern hemisphere. In the southern hemisphere sin λ < 0 and the rotation is anticlockwise.

Result: the plane turns through 360° in

$$ T_F = 2π/(ω sin λ) = (one sidereal day)/sin λ $$

Textbooks with a 24 h day write 15° sin λ per hour. Checks: at the pole T_F = one day (the plane stays fixed in space while the Earth turns underneath); at the equator it never turns; dimensions: ω is T⁻¹ so the angle rate is T⁻¹. Number: at 28.6° N (about the latitude of Delhi, an approximate value) the plane takes about 50 h to turn 360° and 12.5 h to turn 90°.

## Winds and cyclones (qualitative; flag: standard geophysics, not derived in the extracts)

Air starts moving down a pressure gradient from high to low pressure; the horizontal Coriolis acceleration then turns it to the right in the northern hemisphere. Around a low-pressure centre the air spirals inward but is deflected until it circulates **counter-clockwise** in the north and **clockwise** in the south. Tropical cyclones of the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea therefore rotate counter-clockwise. Because the effect is proportional to sin λ, it is negligible within a few degrees of the equator, which is why cyclones do not form there. The north-east trade winds are air flowing towards the equator (southward) and deflected to the right, i.e. westward, so that they blow from the north-east; the south-west monsoon is air from the southern hemisphere trades that cross the equator and bend into the south-west direction as the sense of deflection reverses. Small scale vortices such as water in a basin are governed by other factors, so claims about their sense of rotation are not Coriolis predictions.

### 9. Exam: the 2022 Paper I question and how to present a rotating-frame answer

*Objective:* Solve the verified 2022 Optional question in full, show how to defend the interpretation of the data, and learn the presentation template for a 10 or 15 mark answer.

## The question

Official text (verified record, word for word, UPSC 2022 Physics Optional Paper I, Q2(a), 15 marks):

> Consider two frames of reference S and S′ having a common origin O. The frame S′ is rotating with respect to the fixed frame S with a uniform ω⃗ = 3a_x rad s⁻¹. A projectile of unit mass at position vector r⃗ = 7a_x + 4a_y m is moving with v⃗ = 14a_y m s⁻¹. Calculate in the rotating frame S′ the following forces on the projectile :
> (i) Euler’s force
> (ii) Coriolis force
> (iii) Centrifugal force

The wording 'is moving with' does not say in which frame v is measured, which is why the analysis below discusses the reading. We give the working; no marking scheme is asserted.

## Step 0: decide what the data mean

The forces are asked 'in the rotating frame', and the formulae of module 4 use the position r and the velocity v_rot relative to the rotating frame. The natural reading is that r and v are the position and velocity measured in S′. A cautious candidate writes that assumption in the first line.

## Step 1: Euler force

F_Euler = −m (dω/dt) × r. The rotation is uniform, so dω/dt = 0 and

$$ F_Euler = 0 $$

## Step 2: Coriolis force

F_cor = −2m ω × v_rot. First, ω × v = 3 a_x × 14 a_y = 42 (a_x × a_y) = 42 a_z. With m = 1 kg:

$$ F_cor = −2 × 42 a_z = −84 a_z N $$

Check the size: 2 m ω v sin 90° = 2 × 3 × 14 = 84. The direction is perpendicular to the plane of ω and v.

## Step 3: centrifugal force

F_cf = −m ω × (ω × r). First ω × r = 3 a_x × (7 a_x + 4 a_y) = 12 a_z (a_x × a_x = 0). Then ω × (ω × r) = 3 a_x × 12 a_z = 36 (a_x × a_z) = −36 a_y, because a_x × a_z = −a_y. Therefore

$$ F_cf = +36 a_y N $$

Independent check by the geometric form: the component of r perpendicular to the axis is 4 a_y, so m ω² r_⊥ = 9 × 4 a_y = 36 a_y N, outward from the axis.

## Interpretation and the alternative reading

Results: Euler 0; Coriolis −84 a_z N; centrifugal 36 a_y N (units: unit mass is 1 kg, so N). If a student read 14 a_y m s⁻¹ as the velocity in the inertial frame, then v_rot = v − ω × r = 14 a_y − 12 a_z and the Coriolis force would become −72 a_y − 84 a_z N; the centrifugal and Euler results would not change. Do not present both as equally likely; state the reading you adopt and why (forces requested in S′, data given for the projectile in S′ components). This is analysis, not an official key.

## Presentation template for a ten- or fifteen-mark answer

1. State the frames and the law: m a_rot = F + F_cor + F_cf + F_Euler.
2. Write each force formula before substituting.
3. Show the cross-product component algebra, not only the result.
4. Give units and the direction in words as well as vectors.
5. Do one independent check (magnitude formula, or r_⊥).
6. If a derivation is asked, give the five steps of modules 3 and 4, then the interpretation.

### 10. Practice: modelling the thinking on two problems

*Objective:* Practise the decision sequence - frame, ω, velocity, cross product, sign, interpretation - on a merry-go-round walk and on an eastward throw on the Earth.

## The decision sequence

Every rotating-frame problem can be run through the same six questions. Which frame am I in (rotating or inertial)? What is ω as a vector? Is ω constant (Euler force)? What is the velocity relative to the rotating frame (Coriolis)? What is the distance from the axis (centrifugal)? What is real force and what is fictitious? Then interpret: direction, size, units, limiting case.

## Problem A: a person on a merry-go-round

A 50 kg person walks radially outward at 1.5 m s⁻¹ on a merry-go-round turning anticlockwise (seen from above) at constant ω = 0.8 rad s⁻¹. At r = 3 m, find the sideways force friction must supply and the radial force it must supply.

Thinking aloud. Frame: the merry-go-round (rotating). ω = 0.8 ẑ, constant, so no Euler force. In this frame the person has velocity v_rot = 1.5 r̂. Coriolis: −2m ω × v_rot = −2 × 50 × 0.8 × 1.5 (ẑ × r̂) = −120 θ̂ N, magnitude 120 N, pointing clockwise, which is to the right of the direction of motion. Centrifugal: m ω² r = 50 × 0.64 × 3 = 96 N outward. For the person to move at constant relative velocity, the net force in the rotating frame must vanish, so friction must supply +120 θ̂ N (sideways, anticlockwise) and −96 r̂ N (radially inward). In the inertial frame these frictional forces give the person's acceleration (r̈ − r θ̇²) r̂ + 2ṙ θ̇ θ̂: radial −rω² = −1.92 m s⁻², sideways 2 × 1.5 × 0.8 = 2.4 m s⁻²; multiplying by 50 kg gives the same 96 N and 120 N. Dimensions: ω v is L T⁻². Limiting case: standing still at the centre needs no force.

Common mistake: forgetting that the Coriolis term needs the velocity relative to the ride, and using the ground velocity.

## Problem B: a ball thrown due east at latitude 45° N

A ball is thrown horizontally due east at 20 m s⁻¹. Find the Coriolis acceleration.

Thinking aloud. Frame: Earth, local axes east, north, up. v = (20, 0, 0). From module 6: a_cor = 2ω (sin λ v_N − cos λ v_U, −sin λ v_E, cos λ v_E) = 2ω (0, −20 sin 45°, 20 cos 45°). Numbers: 2 × 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ × 14.14 = 2.06 × 10⁻³ m s⁻². So the Coriolis acceleration is 2.06 × 10⁻³ m s⁻² towards the south (right of the eastward motion) and the same size upward (the Eötvös effect, a tiny reduction of weight). Interpretation: a northern-hemisphere deflection to the right; the magnitude is about 2 × 10⁻⁴ of g, so it is invisible in a playground but not for a long-range shell.

### Notes: Definitions

- Inertial frame: a frame in which Newton's first and second laws hold without extra terms; any frame not accelerating or turning relative to the distant stars.
- Rotating frame S′: a frame sharing the origin of an inertial frame S whose axes turn with angular velocity ω (a vector along the axis, size in rad s⁻¹).
- Fictitious (pseudo, inertial) force: a term added to the real force so that F = m a can be used in a non-inertial frame; it has no source body and no reaction partner.
- Centripetal acceleration: the inward acceleration ω² ρ (or v²/ρ) of circular motion seen in an inertial frame; the real force causing it is the centripetal force.
- Centrifugal force: F_cf = −m ω × (ω × r) = m ω² r_⊥, outward from the axis, in the rotating frame.
- Coriolis force: F_cor = −2m ω × v_rot, perpendicular to v_rot and to ω.
- Euler force: F_Euler = −m (dω/dt) × r, present only when ω changes.

### Notes: Governing laws and equations

- (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A for any vector A.
- v_in = v_rot + ω × r.
- a_in = a_rot + 2 ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r.
- m a_rot = F − 2m ω × v_rot − m ω × (ω × r) − m (dω/dt) × r.
- Centrifugal: m ω² ρ outward (ρ = perpendicular distance from the axis); Coriolis size: 2 m ω v sin θ.
- Power of the Coriolis force in the rotating frame: F_cor · v_rot = 0.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: operator rule and acceleration

- Fix axes e₁, e₂, e₃ in the rotating frame; write A = Σ Aᵢ eᵢ.
- Differentiate in S by the product rule: Σ (dAᵢ/dt) eᵢ + Σ Aᵢ deᵢ/dt.
- First sum is (dA/dt)_rot; second uses deᵢ/dt = ω × eᵢ and linearity to give ω × A.
- Apply the rule to r to get v_in; apply it again to v_in = v_rot + ω × r.
- Expand (d/dt)_rot(ω × r) = (dω/dt) × r + ω × v_rot; combine the two ω × v_rot terms into 2ω × v_rot.
- Multiply by m and move the extra terms to the force side.

### Notes: Centripetal, centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler compared

- Only the first is a real force or acceleration; the other three are fictitious terms of the rotating frame.

### Notes: Earth as a rotating frame: formulae

- Local axes: x east, y north, z up; ω = (0, ω cos λ, ω sin λ); latitude λ positive north.
- ω_Earth = 2π/(sidereal day 86 164 s) ≈ 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹ (7.27 × 10⁻⁵ if the 24 h solar day is used).
- g_eff = (0, −ω² R sin λ cos λ, −g₀ + ω² R cos² λ); |g_eff| ≈ g₀ − ω² R cos² λ; ω² R ≈ 0.034 m s⁻².
- a_cor = 2ω (sin λ v_N − cos λ v_U, −sin λ v_E, cos λ v_E).
- Free fall from height h: eastward deflection d = (1/3) ω g cos λ t³ = √(8/(9g)) ω h^{3/2} cos λ.
- Foucault pendulum: plane turns at ω sin λ (clockwise seen from above in the north); period of one turn = sidereal day / sin λ.

### Notes: Worked numbers to remember

- 2022 data (ω = 3 a_x, r = 7 a_x + 4 a_y, v = 14 a_y, m = 1 kg): Euler 0; Coriolis −84 a_z N; centrifugal +36 a_y N.
- Free fall of 100 m at 45°: t = 4.52 s, deflection 1.55 cm east; at the equator 2.20 cm.
- Foucault at 28.6° N: 360° in about 50 h; at the pole one sidereal day; at the equator never.
- Coin on a turntable (μ = 0.30, r = 0.15 m): slips when ω > 4.43 rad s⁻¹, about 42 rpm.
- Bead on a rod from rest at r₀: r = r₀ cosh ωt, N = 2m r₀ ω² sinh ωt.

### Notes: Commonly confused ideas

- Centripetal and centrifugal forces are not an action-reaction pair and do not 'balance' in the inertial frame; centrifugal does not exist there.
- Coriolis force exists only for motion relative to the rotating frame; an object at rest in the frame feels none.
- The Coriolis force never changes speed in the rotating frame, only direction.
- Using the inertial velocity in the Coriolis formula instead of the velocity relative to the rotating frame.
- Deflection 'to the right' is a northern-hemisphere statement; reverse it for λ < 0.
- Water draining from a sink is not a reliable demonstration of the Coriolis effect.

### Notes: Limits and exceptions

- The first-order results (deflection, Foucault rate) neglect terms of order ω²; they hold when ωt ≪ 1 over the motion.
- Free-fall formula assumes small h, constant g, no air; the north-south component of deflection is second order in ω.
- At the equator the horizontal Coriolis acceleration of horizontal motion vanishes (sin λ = 0), but vertical motion gets the east-west term 2 ω v_U cos(λ), and east-west motion gets a vertical term. At the poles (cos λ = 0) vertical motion gets no Coriolis term.
- The Euler force vanishes for uniform rotation, so the Earth problems here have none.

    - **a:** No: it has no source body or third-law partner and it vanishes in an inertial frame.
  - - **q:** State the unit of each of ω, dω/dt, the Coriolis acceleration.
    - **a:** s⁻¹ (rad s⁻¹), s⁻², m s⁻².
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Explain why a particle at rest in a uniformly rotating frame feels a centrifugal force but no Coriolis force.
    - **a:** Centrifugal depends on position (m ω² ρ) and is present whenever ρ ≠ 0; Coriolis is proportional to v_rot, which is zero.
  - - **q:** Why is the Euler force absent for the Earth problems of this lesson?
    - **a:** The Earth's angular velocity is taken as constant, so dω/dt = 0.
  - - **q:** Explain the eastward deflection of a falling stone using the inertial frame.
    - **a:** The stone starts with the Earth's angular velocity. As it falls its distance from the axis shrinks, and angular-momentum conservation makes it turn slightly faster than the Earth. Its eastward speed relative to the ground grows as 2 ω (h − z) cos λ = ω g t² cos λ, which integrates to (1/3) ω g cos λ t³. The naive picture, keeping a constant extra speed ω h cos λ, gives 1/2 and overestimates, because 'down' turns with the Earth and gravity pulls the stone back westward relative to its original direction.
  - - **q:** Why does the horizontal Coriolis acceleration of horizontal motion vanish at the equator, and why does the Foucault rate vanish there too?
    - **a:** Both depend on the local vertical component of ω, which is ω sin λ and vanishes at λ = 0.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A river in the northern hemisphere flows due south. Which bank is eroded more and why?
    - **a:** The Coriolis force on southward-moving water is to the right of the motion, i.e. towards the west; this is a small effect compared with local geology, but it tends to press the west bank (qualitative, standard textbook remark).
  - - **q:** Why does a long-range shell fired due east at mid-latitudes show an upward and a rightward Coriolis acceleration?
    - **a:** From a_cor = 2ω(…, −sin λ v_E, cos λ v_E): the south-ward component (right of eastward motion) and an upward component both appear.
  - - **q:** Why is a laboratory on the Earth treated as inertial for most problems?
    - **a:** The centripetal acceleration due to rotation is at most ω² R ≈ 0.034 m s⁻², about 0.3 percent of g, and Coriolis terms are of order 10⁻⁴ g for ordinary speeds.

  - - **id:** PH-002-D1
    - **task:** Derive (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A for a frame rotating with angular velocity ω and sharing the origin of an inertial frame. Timed written solution target: 10 minutes for a 10-mark answer (practice target, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: the frames share an origin; the axes e₁, e₂, e₃ of the rotating frame are mutually perpendicular unit vectors fixed in it; ω may depend on time.
      - Write A = A₁e₁ + A₂e₂ + A₃e₃ with components measured in the rotating frame.
      - The rotating observer sees e₁, e₂, e₃ as constant, so (dA/dt)_rot = Σ (dAᵢ/dt) eᵢ.
      - The inertial observer applies the product rule: (dA/dt)_in = Σ (dAᵢ/dt) eᵢ + Σ Aᵢ (deᵢ/dt)_in.
      - Each axis vector is fixed in a rigid frame rotating with ω, so (deᵢ/dt)_in = ω × eᵢ (derived from the rotating unit vector in bridge B2).
      - Using linearity of the cross product: Σ Aᵢ (ω × eᵢ) = ω × Σ Aᵢ eᵢ = ω × A.
      - Combine: (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A. Interpretation: ω × A is the rate at which A would change if it were frozen in the rotating frame.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions: both sides have dimension [A]/T since [ω] = 1/T.
      - Limiting case ω = 0: the two rates agree.
      - If A is fixed in the rotating frame, (dA/dt)_in = ω × A, the velocity of a point on a rotating rigid body.
      - A = ω gives (dω/dt)_in = (dω/dt)_rot.
    - **traps:**
      - Reversing the cross product order.
      - Using components along the inertial axes in the first term instead of the rotating axes.

    - **task:** Derive a_in = a_rot + 2ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r and hence the effective force in the rotating frame, naming each fictitious force. Timed written solution target: 18 minutes for a 15-mark answer (practice target, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Apply D1 to r: v_in = v_rot + ω × r.
      - Apply D1 to v_in: a_in = (d v_in/dt)_rot + ω × v_in.
      - Substitute v_in = v_rot + ω × r in both terms: a_in = (d/dt)_rot(v_rot + ω × r) + ω × (v_rot + ω × r).
      - Use the product rule and (dr/dt)_rot = v_rot: (d/dt)_rot(ω × r) = (dω/dt) × r + ω × v_rot, and (d v_rot/dt)_rot = a_rot.
      - Collect terms: a_in = a_rot + (dω/dt) × r + ω × v_rot + ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r).
      - Combine the two equal terms: a_in = a_rot + 2ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r.
      - Apply Newton's second law in the inertial frame, m a_in = F, and solve: m a_rot = F − 2m ω × v_rot − m ω × (ω × r) − m (dω/dt) × r.

      - ω = 0 gives a_in = a_rot.

    - **traps:**

      - Forgetting that (dω/dt)_in = (dω/dt)_rot.

    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: small swings in the horizontal plane; Ω₀² = g/L; only ω_z = ω sin λ matters for horizontal motion; terms of order ω_z² are neglected relative to Ω₀².

      - Omitting the horizontal-plane approximation.
      - Using the full ω instead of ω sin λ.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-OSX-NEWTON1 (verified):** Newton's first law: a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. A frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial; a frame accelerating relative to an inertial frame is not inertial.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 5.2 'Newton's First Law'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-2-newtons-first-law)
- **F-OSX-FICTITIOUS (verified):** Earth is nearly an inertial frame; a car turning or a merry-go-round is a non-inertial frame, in which extra 'inertial' (fictitious) forces such as the centrifugal and Coriolis forces appear. OpenStax stresses that centrifugal force is a commonly used term but does not correspond to a force with an identifiable physical origin.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 6.3 'Centripetal Force'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/6-3-centripetal-force)
- **F-OSX-CORIOLIS (verified):** On a rotating merry-go-round a ball slid outward curves to the right (in the cited example) because of the Coriolis force. Any motion in Earth's Northern Hemisphere experiences a Coriolis force to the right, which makes hurricanes in the Northern Hemisphere rotate counterclockwise.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 6.3 'Centripetal Force'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/6-3-centripetal-force)

#### Coriolis deflection on a rotating turntable (top view) (draft)

In the rotating frame an outward-moving body is pushed sideways, to the right of its velocity when the frame turns counter-clockwise; in the inertial frame the path is straight.

A circle for the turntable with a counter-clockwise rotation arrow omega outside the rim; a radial dashed line from the centre; a ball moving outward along it with velocity v-prime to the right, the Coriolis force arrow pointing straight down (to the right of the motion), and a curved path bending downward.

2. Draw a dashed radius to the right and a ball on it, with velocity v' pointing outward along the radius.
3. Draw the Coriolis force on the ball perpendicular to v', pointing to the right of the direction of motion (downward on the page).

#### Northern Hemisphere cyclone: inflow turned to the right (draft)

Air flows toward low pressure; the Coriolis force turns each parcel to its right in the Northern Hemisphere, giving counter-clockwise rotation.

A letter L at the centre with four curved arrows entering from east, north, west and south; each starts heading toward the centre and bends to the right, so together they circulate counter-clockwise.

1. Mark the low-pressure centre with L.
2. Draw four arrows starting on the east, north, west and south that first head toward L.
3. Bend each arrow to the right of its direction of motion (Northern Hemisphere), so the arrow from the east turns north.
4. Note that the combined flow is counter-clockwise.

### Memory skeleton

- Operator rule: (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A
- Apply twice: a_in = a_rot + 2ω × v_rot + ω × (ω × r) + (dω/dt) × r
- Effective force law: centrifugal, Coriolis, Euler as fictitious terms
- Centripetal (real, inertial) versus centrifugal (fictitious, rotating)
- Earth frame: x east, y north, z up; ω = (0, ω cos λ, ω sin λ); g_eff
- Free fall: eastward deflection (1/3) ω g cos λ t³
- Foucault pendulum: plane turns at ω sin λ

### Last-week summary

- (dA/dt)_in = (dA/dt)_rot + ω × A, from deᵢ/dt = ω × eᵢ; apply to r, then to v_in.
- m a_rot = F − 2m ω × v_rot − m ω × (ω × r) − m (dω/dt) × r; Coriolis uses v_rot and does no work.
- Centrifugal m ω² ρ outward, position only; Euler zero for uniform ω; centripetal is the real inward force, not a reaction pair.
- Earth: ω ≈ 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹; free fall d = (1/3) ω g cos λ t³ = √(8/(9g)) ω h^{3/2} cos λ, east, zero at poles.
- Foucault: ω sin λ, clockwise from above in the north; one turn = sidereal day / sin λ.
