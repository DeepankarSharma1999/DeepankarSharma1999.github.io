## GS-103: Atmospheric structure and heat budget

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Bridge: heat, temperature, wavelength and the units used in climate

## Heat and temperature are not the same

NCERT: **heat** represents the molecular movement of the particles that make up a substance; **temperature** is the measurement, in degrees, of how hot or cold a thing or place is (kegy208, PDF p4). **Specific heat** is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius. Water has a higher specific heat than land, so it warms and cools more slowly.

## Radiation and wavelength

Radiation is energy that travels as waves and needs no material. A **wavelength** is the distance between two crests. NCERT quotes Planck's law in plain words: the hotter a body, the more energy it radiates and the shorter the wavelength of that radiation. The sun is very hot (Leong gives its surface as more than 5,982 degrees Celsius), so it sends **short waves**. The earth is cool, so it sends **long waves**. The whole heat budget is this: short waves in, long waves out.

## Units used in the extracts

- **Degrees Celsius to Fahrenheit**: F = 1.8 x C + 32 (Leong, PDF p168). So 20 degrees Celsius is 68 degrees Fahrenheit.
- **Heat flow**: NCERT gives the solar energy at the top of the atmosphere as 1.94 calories per square centimetre per minute, and the surface insolation as watts per square metre (W/m2). A **watt** is one joule of energy per second; one calorie is 4.184 joules.
- **Per cent and units**: the budget is written on a scale where the incoming sunlight is 100 units, so every figure is also a percentage.

### 1. Orientation: the atmosphere and its energy on one page

*Objective:* State in a minute what the atmosphere is, where its energy comes from and what the heat budget says.

## The picture first

The **atmosphere** is the envelope of air round the earth: a mixture of gases, water vapour and dust. NCERT says 99 per cent of its mass lies within 32 km of the surface (kegy207, PDF p2). Almost all its energy comes from the sun. The earth in turn sends energy back to space, and because the two amounts match over time, the earth neither warms up nor cools down (kegy208, PDF p1). The unequal heating of different places drives pressure differences, winds and weather.

## Words to fix first

- **Weather**: the condition of the atmosphere at a given time. **Climate**: the average condition over a long period; Leong says a minimum of 35 years is desirable (PDF p163).
- **Insolation**: incoming solar radiation, the part of the sun's output that reaches the earth.
- **Albedo**: the share of incoming radiation reflected back to space.
- **Terrestrial radiation**: the long-wave energy the earth gives out.
- **Heat budget**: the account of energy in and out.
- **Isotherm**: a line joining places of equal temperature. **Lapse rate**: the fall of temperature with height.
- **Elements of weather and climate**: temperature, pressure, winds, humidity, clouds and precipitation (NCERT). This lesson covers temperature and what controls it.

## The whole lesson in a diagram of words

Sun (short waves) -> [reflected: 35 of every 100 units] -> [absorbed in the atmosphere: 14] -> [absorbed by the surface: 51] -> the surface heats the air from below and radiates long waves -> the atmosphere sends 48 units and the surface 17 units to space. Total back to space: 35 + 17 + 48 = 100.

## What this lesson leaves for later

Pressure belts, winds, air masses, cyclones and precipitation are separate topics. Here only the part of them needed to explain temperature appears. Latitude, the tilt of the axis and day length were taught in Day 17 lesson GS-095 (module on noon sun, solstices, equinoxes and day length); link back to it rather than relearning.

### 2. Foundation: what the atmosphere is made of

*Objective:* Name the gases, water vapour and dust of the atmosphere with their roles, and state the key heights and variations the extracts give.

## The three ingredients

NCERT: the atmosphere is composed of **gases**, **water vapour** and **dust particles** (kegy207, PDF p2). Air is colourless and odourless; it is felt only when it moves as wind.

## Gases

Leong gives the proportions in the lower atmosphere by volume: about 78 per cent nitrogen, 21 per cent oxygen, 0.003 per cent carbon dioxide and traces of argon, helium and other rare gases (PDF p160). The NCERT extract does not reproduce a table of percentages. Leong's carbon dioxide value is out of date: the figure is nearer 0.04 per cent and rising (standard knowledge, **verify**). NCERT states the rise directly: carbon dioxide has been increasing for the last few decades mainly from the burning of fossil fuels, and this has raised air temperature.

- **Oxygen** becomes almost negligible at about 120 km; **carbon dioxide and water vapour** are found only up to about 90 km.
- **Carbon dioxide** is transparent to incoming solar radiation but opaque to outgoing terrestrial radiation. It absorbs part of that radiation and sends some back towards the surface, so it is largely responsible for the **greenhouse effect**.
- **Ozone** lies between 10 and 50 km above the surface and absorbs the sun's ultraviolet rays, protecting life.

## Water vapour

Water vapour is a variable gas. It **decreases with altitude** and also decreases from the equator towards the poles. In the warm, wet tropics it may be up to about 4 per cent of the air by volume; in cold, dry deserts and polar regions less than 1 per cent. It absorbs part of the insolation and holds back the earth's radiated heat, acting like a blanket, and contributes to stability and instability of the air. Leong agrees that the water is never more than about 4 per cent (PDF p160).

## Dust particles

Dust includes sea salt, fine soil, smoke-soot, ash, pollen and fragments of meteors. It is mostly in the lower layers, though convection currents may carry it high. NCERT says the concentration is higher in **subtropical and temperate regions because of dry winds**, than in equatorial and polar regions. Dust and salt particles act as **hygroscopic nuclei**: water vapour condenses around them to form clouds.

## A density check

Density is greatest near the surface and falls with height. NCERT's 99 per cent of mass within 32 km can be checked with Leong's pressure rule: pressure halves about every 5.6 km (PDF p166). 32 / 5.6 = 5.7 halvings, so about 0.5^5.7 = 1.9 per cent of the air lies above 32 km, which leaves about 98 per cent below. This is close to NCERT's 99 per cent; the small gap arises because the real halving height changes with temperature.

## Why water vapour matters for weather

Leong notes that without the variable water content of the atmosphere there would be no weather and not much climate (PDF p160): water vapour feeds clouds, precipitation and the latent heat that enters the heat budget (module 6).

### 3. Core: the layers of the atmosphere and the temperature in each

*Objective:* Describe the five layers with heights and temperature trends, and explain why the troposphere is thickest at the equator.

## The principle

The atmosphere is divided into five layers by **temperature behaviour**. Density is highest near the surface and falls with height (NCERT, kegy207, PDF p3).

| Layer | Height (NCERT) | What temperature does | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Troposphere | about 13 km on average; about 8 km at the poles and 18 km at the equator | falls by 1 degree Celsius per 165 m (normal lapse rate 6.5 degrees Celsius per 1,000 m, module 7) | dust, water vapour, all weather, most biological activity |
| Tropopause | the boundary above it | nearly constant; about minus 80 degrees Celsius over the equator and minus 45 over the poles | |
| Stratosphere | to about 50 km | NCERT does not state the trend; it rises with height because ozone absorbs ultraviolet (standard knowledge, **verify**) | ozone layer |
| Mesosphere | to about 80 km | falls again, reaching about minus 100 degrees Celsius at 80 km | upper limit: mesopause |
| Thermosphere | from the mesopause (about 80 km) to about 400 km above the surface | rises with height | contains ions, so NCERT's text calls it the ionosphere; radio waves are reflected back to earth |
| Exosphere | above the thermosphere | extremely rarefied | merges gradually with outer space |

NCERT adds that geographers are concerned with the first two layers.

## Why the troposphere is thicker at the equator

Thickness is greatest at the equator because heat is transported to great heights by strong **convectional currents** (rising warm air). At the poles there is little convection, so the layer is shallow. 18 km at the equator against 8 km at the poles is a ratio of 2.25.

## The tropopause puzzle

The tropopause is colder over the equator (minus 80 degrees Celsius) than over the poles (minus 45 degrees Celsius), though the equatorial surface is far warmer. The reason is height: a warm surface plus a taller layer to cool through. With a surface at 27 degrees Celsius and 18 km of cooling at 6.5 degrees per km the result is minus 90 degrees Celsius; at 1 degree per 165 m it is minus 82. NCERT's minus 80 lies between the two, because the lapse rate is not exactly constant.

## Leong's variant

Leong (PDF p160) gives other heights (10 km, 80 km, 402 km, 966 km). Use NCERT's 13, 50, 80 and 400 km and do not mix the sets.

## Why the layers matter for answers

- Weather is in the troposphere: it holds the dust, water vapour and convection.
- The ozone shield is in the stratosphere.
- The ionosphere enables radio communication and is where the upper-atmosphere effects of the sun's activity are felt (module 9).

### 4. Core: insolation and why it varies

*Objective:* Explain what insolation is and how angle, day length, atmosphere, surface and the earth-sun distance change it, using the NCERT figures.

## What reaches the earth

The earth receives energy mostly in **short wavelengths**, called **insolation** (incoming solar radiation). Because the earth is nearly a sphere, it intercepts only a small part of the sun's output. On average it receives **1.94 calories per square centimetre per minute** at the top of the atmosphere (NCERT, kegy208, PDF p1). Converting (1 calorie = 4.184 joules; 1 cm2 = 0.0001 m2; 1 minute = 60 s) gives about 1,353 W/m2. This matches the modern solar constant of about 1,360 W/m2 (standard knowledge, **verify**). Because a sphere's surface is four times the area of the disc of sunlight it intercepts, the average over the whole globe is about one quarter of that, near 338 W/m2 (derived here).

## Distance: aphelion and perihelion

The earth is farthest from the sun (152 million km) around 4 July (**aphelion**) and nearest (147 million km) around 3 January (**perihelion**). Energy falls with the square of distance, so (152 / 147)^2 = 1.069: about 7 per cent more at the top of the atmosphere in January (derived). NCERT says the effect is masked by the distribution of land and sea and atmospheric circulation, so this does not change daily weather much. It is also why the seasons are caused by the tilt and not by distance (Day 17 lesson GS-095).

## Factors of variation (NCERT)

The amount and intensity of insolation vary through the day, the season and the year because of: (i) rotation of the earth on its axis; (ii) the **angle of inclination** of the sun's rays; (iii) the **length of the day**; (iv) the **transparency** of the atmosphere; (v) the **aspect** of the land. The last two matter least. The tilt of the axis (66 1/2 degrees with the orbit plane) strongly affects insolation at different latitudes.

**Angle.** The higher the latitude, the smaller the angle the rays make with the surface, so the rays are slant. Slant rays do two things: the same energy spreads over a larger area, so less energy falls on each square metre; and the rays cross a thicker slice of atmosphere, so more is absorbed, scattered and diffused. Leong makes the same point with the two bands R1 (direct, equator) and R2 (oblique, temperate).

**Day length.** More hours of sun means more energy; summer days at high latitudes are long and so partly offset the slant rays (day length is taught in Day 17 lesson GS-095).

**Atmosphere and surface.** Clouds and dust reduce insolation. Leong notes that light-coloured soils reflect more heat than dark ones (PDF p180).

## Distribution at the surface (NCERT)

Surface insolation varies from about **320 W/m2 in the tropics to about 70 W/m2 at the poles**; the ratio is 320 / 70 = 4.6. The maximum is over the **subtropical deserts**, where cloudiness is least; the equator gets somewhat less than the tropics. At the same latitude the continents receive more than the oceans, and in winter the middle and high latitudes receive less than in summer.

## Hidden trap

A common wrong claim is that the equator receives about 10 times the poles. NCERT's figures give 4.6, not 10.

### 5. Core: how sunlight passes through, and how the air is heated and cooled

*Objective:* Explain scattering and absorption of sunlight, and distinguish terrestrial radiation, conduction, convection and advection as ways of heating the atmosphere.

## Passage of sunlight through the air

The atmosphere is largely **transparent to short-wave solar radiation**. Within the troposphere, water vapour, ozone and other gases absorb much of the **near infrared** part. Very small suspended particles **scatter** visible light both to space and towards the earth; this gives the blue of the sky and the red of the rising and setting sun (NCERT, kegy208, PDF p2). Leong describes the sun's radiation as three parts: visible light (most intense and most important for climate), ultraviolet and infrared (PDF p161). Infrared is a part of the sun's radiation outside the visible range.

## The surface heats first

The surface absorbs the short waves and warms. It then becomes a radiating body and sends out **long waves**: this is **terrestrial radiation**. The long waves are absorbed by atmospheric gases, particularly carbon dioxide and the other greenhouse gases, so the atmosphere is **indirectly heated from below**. The atmosphere in turn radiates to space, so the heat received from the sun is finally returned (NCERT p3).

## Three ways heat moves in the air

| Process | Meaning | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction | flow of energy from warmer to cooler body in contact; air touching the warm ground is heated | the lowest layers of the atmosphere |
| Convection | the air in contact with the earth rises as currents and spreads heat vertically | confined to the troposphere |
| Advection | transfer of heat by horizontal movement of air | more important than vertical movement; in middle latitudes most daily weather changes come from advection; the hot loo of northern India in summer is advection |

## Cooling at night and the role of clouds

At night the earth keeps radiating while no insolation replaces it, so the surface cools. Leong explains that thick clouds absorb some insolation by day and **blanket the earth's outgoing radiation** at night, sending heat back. Cloudy equatorial regions therefore have no very hot days and no cold nights; cloudless deserts have scorching days and very cold nights, so a desert's daily range of temperature is two or three times that of the cloudy equator (PDF p180). This is the reasoning behind the 2019 dew question (module 10).

## Temperature in the sun and in the shade

Leong notes that the temperature quoted in climate graphs is the **shade temperature** of the air, read in a white louvred Stevenson Screen 1.2 m above the ground. A reading in open daylight measures direct insolation (PDF p168).

## Putting short and long together

| Feature | Insolation | Terrestrial radiation |
|---|---|---|
| Source | sun, very hot | earth, much cooler |
| Wavelength | short | long |
| Passes through clear air | mostly yes | largely absorbed by greenhouse gases |
| Main effect | warms the surface | warms the atmosphere from below |

### 6. Core: the 100-unit heat budget and the greenhouse effect

*Objective:* Reproduce NCERT's heat budget, verify that each part balances, and say what it shows about albedo, the heating of the atmosphere and the greenhouse effect.

## The principle

Over time the earth does not warm or cool, which can happen only if heat received as insolation equals heat lost as terrestrial radiation. Take the insolation at the top of the atmosphere as **100 units** (NCERT, kegy208, PDF p3).

## The units

**Reflected to space: 35.** Of these 27 come from the tops of clouds and 2 from snow and ice; the extract does not list the rest, which by subtraction is 6 (NCERT's figure labels it as scattered by the atmosphere, **verify**). The reflected share is the **albedo** of the earth.

**Absorbed: 65.** The atmosphere absorbs **14** directly and the surface **51**.

**The surface gives back 51.** 17 go directly to space; **34** are absorbed by the atmosphere: 6 directly, 9 by convection and turbulence, 19 as latent heat of condensation.

**The atmosphere sends 48 to space** (14 from insolation plus 34 from the earth).

## Three balance tests

| Account | In | Out | Balanced? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top of atmosphere | 100 | 35 + 17 + 48 = 100 | yes |
| Earth's surface | 51 | 17 + 6 + 9 + 19 = 51 | yes |
| Atmosphere | 14 + 34 = 48 | 48 | yes |

NCERT's wording: the total returning from earth and atmosphere is 17 + 48 = 65, which balances the 65 received after reflection. Leong gives the same three round numbers: 35 per cent reflected, 14 per cent absorbed in the air and 51 per cent reaching the earth (PDF p161).

## What the budget says

1. **The air is heated mostly from below.** Of the 48 units the atmosphere takes in, 34 come from the earth (34 / 48 = 71 per cent) and only 14 (29 per cent) directly from the sun.
2. **Latent heat is large.** The 19 units of latent heat of condensation are the biggest single channel from surface to air and tie the budget to water vapour (module 2).
3. **Clouds matter twice.** They reflect 27 of the 35 reflected units, and they hold back outgoing heat at night.
4. **Albedo** is 35 per cent in NCERT. Modern satellite estimates are nearer 30 per cent (standard knowledge, **verify**); use 35 for NCERT-based questions.

## In watts (derived)

With 100 units equal to the global average of about 338 W/m2 (module 4), 35 units are about 118 W/m2, 14 about 47, 51 about 172, and 48 about 162. Modern studies give somewhat different splits, so treat NCERT's units as a teaching model.

## The greenhouse effect inside the budget

Carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases let the incoming short waves through but absorb outgoing long waves and send part of them back (NCERT kegy207 p2). In the simple 100-unit picture this is hidden, because it shows only net flows to space. In reality the atmosphere's downward radiation adds to the surface heating, which is why a planet with an atmosphere is warmer than one without; without it, the average surface would be well below freezing, about minus 18 degrees Celsius (standard knowledge, **verify**). More greenhouse gas strengthens the effect.

### 7. Core: latitudinal heat balance and the factors controlling temperature

*Objective:* Explain the surplus and deficit of heat by latitude and apply the factors that control temperature, including the lapse rate.

## Surplus and deficit

The heat budget balances for the whole earth, but not at each latitude. NCERT Figure 8.3 shows a **surplus** of net radiation between about 40 degrees north and 40 degrees south, and a **deficit** near the poles. The surplus heat of the tropics is carried poleward, so the tropics do not heat up without limit and the high latitudes do not freeze permanently (kegy208, PDF p4). This redistribution by winds, and by ocean currents, is the reason for the circulation topics that follow later.

## Heat and temperature

Temperature is measured in degrees, while heat is molecular movement (bridge GS-103-B1). The temperature of air at a place is influenced by five factors (NCERT).

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Latitude | insolation falls from equator to poles, so temperature does too |
| Altitude | the air is heated from below, so temperature falls with height |
| Distance from the sea | sea heats and cools slowly, land quickly; coasts have a smaller range |
| Air masses and ocean currents | warm air masses and warm currents raise temperature; cold ones lower it |
| Local aspects | slope, shelter, vegetation, soil, cloud (Leong) |

## Land, sea and specific heat

Water has a higher specific heat than land, so it needs more energy to warm. Leong says it takes only about one third as much energy to raise the same volume of land by a degree. Hence the interiors of continents have warmer summers, colder winters and a bigger range (PDF p177).

## Lapse rate

The rate at which temperature decreases with height is the **normal lapse rate: 6.5 degrees Celsius per 1,000 m** (NCERT p4). NCERT's earlier page gives 1 degree per 165 m; Leong gives 1 degree per 164 m. Both are about 6.1 degrees per km (1,000 / 165 = 6.06), a little below 6.5. Use 6.5 in calculations, and remember it is an average: Leong says the rate varies by place and season and is larger in summer and by day. A worked case: at 1,370 m with a sea-level temperature of 27 degrees Celsius, Leong gives 18 degrees; by 164 m per degree it is 18.6, and by 6.5 per km it is 18.1.

Do not confuse this **environmental lapse rate** with the cooling of a rising air parcel. Leong notes that rising dry air cools at the dry adiabatic rate and saturated air at a slower rate (PDF p182).

## Cloud and ground

Why are the hottest places the subtropical deserts, not the equator? Cloud cover is least there (NCERT exercise; Leong). Thick equatorial cloud cuts the day's insolation and blankets the night's heat.

## Hill, valley and slope

South-facing slopes in the northern hemisphere receive more sunlight than north-facing ones; Leong gives the Alps as the example (PDF p179).

## Currents and winds

Warm currents (Gulf Stream, North Atlantic Drift) raise coastal temperatures and cold ones (Labrador) lower them; the full account is in Day 17 lesson GS-102, 'Core: effects of ocean currents on coasts, climate and marine life', and Day 17 lesson GS-109, 'Core: temperature, salinity and density together'.

### 8. Core: isotherms, January and July patterns, and temperature range

*Objective:* Read an isotherm map in words, explain the January bends over the North Atlantic and Eurasia, and compute mean and range from data.

## Isotherms

An **isotherm** is a line joining places of equal temperature. On maps, temperatures are reduced to sea level (shown as if the station were at the sea) by adding 1 degree Celsius for each 165 m of altitude, so a highland station is shown warmer than recorded (Leong, PDF p171).

## The general rule

Isotherms run roughly parallel to the latitudes because latitude controls insolation. The departure from this is larger in **January than in July**, and especially in the northern hemisphere, because the northern hemisphere has much more land (NCERT, kegy208, PDF p5).

## January, northern hemisphere (map in words)

- Over the oceans the isotherms bend **north** (towards the pole); over the continents they bend **south** (towards the equator).
- In the North Atlantic the warm Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift make the ocean warmer than the land, so the isotherms bend north.
- Over Europe the temperature falls sharply on land and the isotherms bend south; the effect is greatest over the Siberian plain, where the mean January temperature along 60 degrees east is minus 20 degrees Celsius at both 80 and 50 degrees north.
- Mean January values: over 27 degrees Celsius over equatorial oceans, over 24 in the tropics, 2 to 0 in the middle latitudes, and minus 18 to minus 48 in the Eurasian interior.

## January, southern hemisphere

The oceans dominate, so the isotherms are nearly parallel to the latitudes and change gradually. The 20, 10 and 0 degree Celsius isotherms run along about 35, 45 and 60 degrees south.

## July

Isotherms again run mostly parallel to the latitudes. Equatorial oceans are above 27 degrees Celsius; over land more than 30 occurs in the subtropical continental region of Asia along 30 degrees north. The 10 degree isotherm runs along 40 degrees south. NCERT (PDF p7) also puts it along 40 degrees north, an NCERT slip: the July mean there is about 20 to 25 degrees Celsius, and the northern July 10 degree isotherm lies about 60 to 70 degrees north (standard knowledge, **verify**).

## Range

The annual range is the difference between the means of the warmest and coldest months. The greatest range, over 60 degrees Celsius, is over north-eastern Eurasia because of **continentality**; the least, about 3 degrees, lies between 20 degrees south and 15 degrees north. The **diurnal range** is the difference between the day's highest and lowest.

Worked calculations (daily mean, the Delhi example and the Mukden check) are in the Master Notes, 'Worked examples'.


### 9. Master: inversion of temperature, air drainage, solar storms and aurora

*Objective:* Explain inversion with its causes and effects, and state the effects of solar storms and the cause of auroras, with the verify flags the extra facts need.

## Inversion of temperature

Normally temperature falls with height. At times this is reversed: this is **inversion** (NCERT, kegy208, PDF p7). It is usually short but common.

**Conditions.** A long winter night, clear sky and still air. The day's heat is radiated away, and by early morning the ground is cooler than the air above. Over the polar regions inversion is normal throughout the year.

**Effects.** Surface inversion makes the lower air stable. Smoke and dust gather beneath the inversion layer and spread horizontally. Dense morning fogs are common in winter and last a few hours until the sun warms the ground. In smog-prone plains such as the Indo-Gangetic plain in winter, inversion is widely cited as a reason pollution stays near the ground (standard knowledge, **verify**).

**Hills and valleys: air drainage.** On hills and mountains the cold air formed at night is heavy and flows down the slopes like water, piling up in valley bottoms with warmer air above. This is air drainage and inversion in the valley. It protects plants on the slopes from frost (NCERT). Leong's Alpine valley example makes the same point: a hot day followed by a calm, cloudless night leaves the valley colder than the slopes (PDF p180).

## The ionosphere and the sun's activity

The ionosphere (80 to 400 km) contains electrically charged particles (ions) and reflects radio waves back to earth (NCERT kegy207, PDF p3). This is why disturbances of the upper atmosphere affect communication.

## Solar storms (standard knowledge, verify; not in the extracts)

A solar flare or the ejection of charged particles from the sun is a **solar storm**. If a major one reaches the earth, plausible effects include: interference with GPS and navigation; damage to power grids; stronger auroras over much of the globe; disturbed orbits of satellites; and interrupted short-wave radio for aircraft over polar regions. Tsunamis and forest fires are not caused by solar storms. This list is what the 2022 Prelims question needs (module 10).

## Auroras (standard knowledge, verify)

An **aurora** is a glow in the sky seen mainly at high latitudes: the **aurora borealis** (northern lights) in the north and the **aurora australis** (southern lights) in the south. Charged particles from the sun, travelling in the solar wind and in storms, are steered by the earth's magnetic field towards the polar regions, where they strike oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere at heights of roughly 100 km and above, which then give out light, commonly green or red from oxygen. Stronger solar activity brings brighter auroras reaching lower latitudes. The earth's magnetic field is linked to the liquid outer core (Day 18 lesson GS-096).

## Lapse rate and stability

An inversion is the extreme case of a stable atmosphere: the air near the ground is cooler and denser than the air above, so it does not rise, and vertical mixing stops.

### Notes: Core terms

- Insolation: incoming solar radiation in short wavelengths. Albedo: the share of incoming radiation reflected back to space (35 units of 100 in NCERT).
- Terrestrial radiation: long-wave energy radiated by the earth; absorbed by greenhouse gases and so heats the atmosphere from below.
- Heat budget: the account showing that heat received equals heat lost, so the earth neither warms nor cools.
- Conduction, convection, advection: contact transfer; vertical transfer (troposphere only); horizontal transfer by air movement.
- Normal lapse rate: fall of temperature with height, 6.5 degrees Celsius per 1,000 m (NCERT). Inversion: a rise of temperature with height.
- Isotherm: a line joining places of equal temperature. Annual range: warmest month mean minus coldest month mean. Diurnal range: day's highest minus lowest.
- Aphelion: earth farthest from the sun (152 million km, about 4 July). Perihelion: nearest (147 million km, about 3 January).

- NCERT kegy207 p3; Leong p160.

### Notes: Numbers to remember

- 99 per cent of the atmosphere's mass lies within 32 km (NCERT).
- Solar energy at the top of the atmosphere: 1.94 cal/cm2/min (about 1,353 W/m2).
- Surface insolation about 320 W/m2 in the tropics, about 70 W/m2 at the poles; ratio 4.6.
- Water vapour up to about 4 per cent by volume in the tropics, below 1 per cent in cold deserts and polar areas.
- Heat surplus between about 40 degrees north and south; deficit near the poles.
- Greatest annual range over 60 degrees Celsius in north-eastern Eurasia; least about 3 degrees between 20 south and 15 north.
- January: 20, 10 and 0 degree Celsius isotherms along 35, 45 and 60 degrees south.

### Notes: Heating and cooling of the atmosphere

- Sunlight passes through clear air, is partly scattered and absorbed, and warms the surface.
- The surface radiates long waves; greenhouse gases absorb them and warm the air above.
- Conduction warms the lowest air layer; convection carries heat up through the troposphere; advection moves it sideways.
- At night the surface loses heat; clouds return part of it, clear skies let it escape.

### Notes: Factors controlling temperature

- Higher latitude: slant rays and thicker atmosphere, so lower insolation and lower temperature.
- Higher altitude: farther from the heated surface, so temperature falls by about 6.5 degrees Celsius per km.
- Nearness to the sea: slower heating and cooling, so a smaller range.
- Warm current or warm air mass: higher temperature on that coast; cold current: lower.
- Cloud cover: lowers day maxima and raises night minima, narrowing the daily range.
- Continentality: larger annual range inland (over 60 degrees Celsius in north-eastern Eurasia).

### Notes: Worked examples

- Lapse rate: sea level 27 degrees Celsius, town at 1,370 m: 27 - 1.37 x 6.5 = 18.1; by 164 m per degree, 18.6; Leong prints 18.
- Equatorial tropopause: 27 - 18 x 6.5 = minus 90; by 165 m per degree, minus 82; NCERT minus 80.
- Perihelion effect: (152 / 147)^2 = 1.069, about 7 per cent more energy in early January.
- Atmospheric heating share: 34 / 48 = 71 per cent from the earth; 14 / 48 = 29 per cent direct from the sun.
- Relative humidity (Leong): air at 35 degrees Celsius holds 39.6 g/m3; with 10 g/m3 present the relative humidity is 10 / 39.6 = 25 per cent.
- Delhi (NCERT): January mean 14.2, May mean 32.75 degrees Celsius.
- Fahrenheit: 20 degrees Celsius = 1.8 x 20 + 32 = 68 degrees Fahrenheit.
- Daily mean = (maximum + minimum) / 2. Leong: (31 + 23) / 2 = 27 degrees Celsius.
- NCERT's Delhi example: January mean (21.1 + 7.3) / 2 = 14.2; May mean (39.6 + 25.9) / 2 = 32.75; the printed range 32.75 - 14.2 = 18.55. This uses only the two months shown, so it is a worked illustration, not the true annual range, which needs the warmest and coldest of all twelve months.
- Leong's Mukden: warmest month 25, coldest minus 13, range 38 degrees Celsius. The twelve monthly values sum to 86, a mean of 7.2, whereas Leong prints 6.6; the March entries also disagree (1 degree Celsius against 30 degrees Fahrenheit, which is minus 1), so treat the printed mean as approximate.

### Notes: Easily confused pairs

- Insolation (short waves from the sun) versus terrestrial radiation (long waves from the earth).
- Heat versus temperature.
- Normal (environmental) lapse rate of about 6.5 degrees Celsius per km versus the faster dry adiabatic cooling of a rising parcel (about 9.8 per km, standard knowledge, verify) and the slower saturated rate.
- Albedo (reflection) versus absorption.
- Ionosphere (defined by electric charge) versus thermosphere (defined by temperature): NCERT's text treats the 80 to 400 km zone as both.
- Troposphere (weather, 13 km mean) versus stratosphere (ozone, to 50 km).
- Shade temperature (air) versus temperature in the sun.

### Core concepts

- The sun's short waves warm the surface; the surface's long waves warm the air from below.
- The heat budget balances at the top, at the surface and in the atmosphere.
- Temperature is controlled by latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, air masses and currents, and local aspects.
- The troposphere holds the weather and is thickest at the equator because of convection.
- Inversion is a rise of temperature with height, favoured by long, clear, still winter nights.

**A.** Troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere (ionosphere in NCERT's text) and exosphere.

**Q.** What is insolation?

**A.** Incoming solar radiation, mostly in short wavelengths.

**Q.** Which process carries heat vertically through the troposphere?

**A.** Convection.

**Q.** What is an isotherm?

**A.** A line joining places of equal temperature.

**Q.** Why is the earth's atmosphere heated mainly from below?

**A.** The air is transparent to short waves; the surface absorbs them and radiates long waves that greenhouse gases absorb.

**Q.** Why does the heat surplus of the tropics not make them hotter each year?

**A.** The surplus is carried poleward, so the budget balances over the whole earth.

**Q.** Why is a desert night colder than an equatorial night?

**A.** Clear skies let the earth's radiation escape, while equatorial clouds blanket it.
