### Bridge: Flux and solid angle: the two geometric ideas behind Gauss's law

## Flux through a small patch

Picture water flowing through a hoop. How much passes depends on the speed, the area of the hoop, and how the hoop is tilted: face-on catches the most, edge-on catches nothing. For a field g and a small flat patch of area dA with unit normal n̂, the **flux** is

$$ g · n̂ dA = g cos α dA $$

where α is the angle between g and n̂. On a **closed** surface (one with an inside and an outside, like a balloon) we always take n̂ pointing **outward**, so flux is positive when the field points out and negative when it points in. Writing dA = n̂ dA, the total flux is ∮ g · dA; the circle on the integral sign means the surface is closed. Unit: (m s⁻²)(m²) = m³ s⁻².

## Solid angle

In a plane, an angle in radians is arc length divided by radius. In space, the **solid angle** of a patch seen from a point O is the area of its shadow on a sphere of radius r around O, divided by r². For a patch whose outward normal makes an angle α with the ray from O,

$$ dΩ = cos α dA / r² $$

measured in steradians (sr), a pure number. dΩ is **signed**: it is negative where the outward normal points back toward O (a ray entering the surface). The unsigned size of a thin cone is written |dΩ|. The whole sphere is 4πr²/r² = **4π sr**, and so is any closed surface that surrounds O, however it is shaped. A hemisphere seen from its centre is 2π sr. One face of a cube seen from the cube's centre is 4π/6 sr, because the six faces are identical and together surround the centre.

### Bridge: Divergence, the Laplacian and the divergence theorem

## The operator ∇

∇ (read 'del') is the vector of derivatives (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z). Acting on a scalar Φ it gives the **gradient** ∇Φ (PH-008 bridge B3). Dotted with a vector field F it gives the **divergence**

$$ ∇ · F = ∂F_x/∂x + ∂F_y/∂y + ∂F_z/∂z $$

## Why divergence means outflow per volume

Take a small box with sides dx, dy, dz. Through the face at x + dx, flux F_x(x + dx) dy dz leaves; through the face at x, flux F_x(x) dy dz enters. The net outflow through this pair of faces is [F_x(x + dx) − F_x(x)] dy dz = (∂F_x/∂x) dx dy dz. Add the y and z pairs: net outflow = (∇ · F) dτ, with dτ = dx dy dz the box volume.

## The divergence theorem

Fill any region with such boxes. Each inner face is shared by two boxes and counted outward from both, with opposite normals, so inner faces cancel. Only the outer surface survives:

$$ ∮_S F · dA = ∫_τ (∇ · F) dτ $$

## The Laplacian

The divergence of a gradient is the **Laplacian**: ∇²Φ = ∇ · ∇Φ = ∂²Φ/∂x² + ∂²Φ/∂y² + ∂²Φ/∂z².

## A worked check

F = (x, y, z) has ∇ · F = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. On a sphere of radius a, F points outward with size a, so the flux is a × 4πa² = 4πa³. The volume integral is 3 × (4/3)πa³ = 4πa³. The two sides agree. (The curl, ∇ × F, was defined in PH-001 module 6.)

### Bridge: Cylindrical and spherical coordinates, their Laplacians, and ∫ ds/s = ln s

## Three symmetric descriptions

- **Spherical**: r is the distance from a centre. If Φ depends only on r,

$$ ∇²Φ = (1/r²) d/dr (r² dΦ/dr),   ∇ · g = (1/r²) d(r² g_r)/dr $$

- **Cylindrical**: s is the perpendicular distance from an axis. If Φ depends only on s,

$$ ∇²Φ = (1/s) d/ds (s dΦ/ds),   ∇ · g = (1/s) d(s g_s)/ds $$

- **Planar**: z is the height above a plane. If Φ depends only on z, ∇²Φ = d²Φ/dz².

These are the radial parts of the full Laplacians; the angular parts are zero when nothing depends on angle.

## A consistency check

Φ = r² = x² + y² + z². The Cartesian form gives 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. The spherical form gives dΦ/dr = 2r, r² · 2r = 2r³, d/dr(2r³) = 6r², divided by r² gives 6. Both agree.

## Logarithms you will need

∫ ds/s = ln s + constant, so ∫ from s₀ to s of ds′/s′ = ln(s/s₀). ln 1 = 0, ln(s/s₀) is positive for s > s₀, and it grows without limit as s → ∞, but very slowly. This slow but unbounded growth is why the potential of an infinite line cannot be set to zero at infinity (module 6).

### 1. Orientation: what Gauss's and Poisson's equations say, and what earlier lessons already did

*Objective:* See what Gauss's and Poisson's equations say, which results earlier lessons already derive, and the notation and sign conventions used here.

## The topic in one paragraph

Wrap any closed surface around some matter: a balloon of any shape. **Gauss's law for gravity** says that the total gravitational field passing inward through the balloon is fixed by the mass inside it. The balloon's size and shape do not matter, and neither does any mass outside:

$$ ∮ g · dA = −4πG M_enc $$

**Poisson's equation** is the same statement shrunk to one point. It ties how the potential curves at a point to the density there:

$$ ∇²Φ = 4πG ρ $$

Where there is no matter (ρ = 0) it becomes **Laplace's equation**, ∇²Φ = 0. Together with boundary conditions, these equations contain all of static Newtonian gravity.

## Why the examiner sets it

When there is symmetry, Gauss's law turns a three-dimensional integral into a two-line argument. The verified 2018 Paper I question Q2(a) (15 marks) begins *Use Gauss's theorem to calculate…*. When the question names the method, an answer that adds up shells does not follow the instruction. The syllabus phrase (quoted in PH-008 module 1) is *Gravitational field and potential due to spherical bodies, Gauss and Poisson equations, gravitational self-energy*. ### 2. Foundation: field, potential, flux and the statement of Gauss's law

*Objective:* Fix the definitions and sign conventions of g, Φ and flux, and state Gauss's law with its three features.

## Assumptions for the whole lesson

- Newtonian gravity.
- Static (time-independent) distributions of mass.
- An **inertial frame**, because Newton's laws in their simple form hold only there (Upadhyaya §2.4; PH-001 module 2).
- G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². NCERT quotes 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ (eq. 7.8).

The force between two particles acts along the line joining them, so it is a **central** force (Mathur §6.6 and §12.23).

## Field and potential, with the signs fixed

Field and potential of a point mass are in PH-008 module 2 (bridge B3); only the signs to keep are repeated here. The field g is the force per unit mass on a small test mass. For point masses m_i at positions r_i, superposition (Mathur §12.25) gives

$$ g(r) = −Σ G m_i (r − r_i)/|r − r_i|³ $$

The minus sign makes each term point **toward** its mass. The field is curl-free (PH-001 module 6; Mathur §12.20 starts from curl E = 0), so g = −∇Φ with

$$ Φ(r) = Φ(ref) − ∫_ref^r g · dr $$

For a bounded body the reference is infinity, with Φ(∞) = 0, so Φ is negative everywhere. The additive constant is a free choice and changes no force (NCERT, Points to Ponder 5). Module 6 meets bodies of infinite extent, for which Φ(∞) = 0 is impossible.

## Flux

The flux of g through a closed surface S is ∮ g · dA. At each patch, take the component of g along the outward normal, multiply by the patch area, and add (bridge B1). Its unit is m³ s⁻².

A helpful picture: think of g as the velocity of a fluid. A mass is then a **sink**: field lines arrive from far away and end on it. Flux counts the net flow **out** of S, so a surface around a sink has negative flux.

## Gauss's law for gravity

The total flux of g over any closed surface equals −4πG times the total mass enclosed (Mathur §12.19):

$$ ∮_S g · dA = −4πG M_enc,   M_enc = ∫_inside ρ dτ $$

Units: the left side is m³ s⁻²; the right side is (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg) = m³ s⁻². ✓

Three features to state in every answer:

1. **Only enclosed mass counts.** A mass outside S changes g at points of S, but adds nothing to the total flux.
2. **The shape and size of S do not matter**, provided the enclosed mass stays the same.
3. **The sign is negative**, because field lines end on mass. In electrostatics the law is ∮ E · dA = +Q/ε₀. The translation is E ↔ g, Q ↔ m and 1/(4πε₀) ↔ −G. Mathur §12.16 makes the same swap for spheres: M becomes Q, G becomes 1/k (CGS), and the sign reverses.

## A law about the field, not a formula for it

Gauss's law is always true, but it gives only one number: the total flux. It gives the **field** only when symmetry tells you, before any calculation, that g has constant magnitude on the surface and is normal to it (or lies in parts of it, which then carry zero flux). A ring, a disc or a cube has no such surface; PH-006 module 4 has to integrate instead.

### 3. Core: proof of Gauss's law from Newton's law, and why it needs the inverse square

*Objective:* Prove Gauss's law from Newton's law with solid angles, for masses inside and outside, and show where the inverse-square law is essential.

## Setting

A point mass m sits at O; S is any closed surface with a well-defined outward normal (Mathur's condition). A small patch dA of S, at distance r from O, has outward normal n̂ at angle α to the ray r̂ from O.

## Step 1: a mass inside S

By Newton's law the field at the patch is g = −(Gm/r²) r̂. Its flux through the patch is

$$ g · n̂ dA = −(Gm/r²) cos α dA $$

By bridge B1, cos α dA / r² is the solid angle dΩ that the patch subtends at O. So

$$ g · dA = −Gm dΩ $$

The distance r has cancelled. Seen from an interior point, the patches together subtend the full solid angle 4π:

$$ ∮ g · dA = −Gm ∮ dΩ = −4πG m $$

The surface may be folded so that a ray from O crosses it three times (out, in, out). On the inward crossing the outward normal points back toward O, so dΩ there is negative. With |dΩ| the size of the thin cone, the three crossings give −Gm|dΩ|, +Gm|dΩ| and −Gm|dΩ|, net −Gm|dΩ|, so the result still holds.

*Source note.* Mathur (§12.19, PDF 665-666) proves Step 1, the enclosed case, and asserts zero flux when Σm = 0 without the external-cone argument; Step 2 is not in 12.19 (compare exercise 9, PDF 709). In the extracted text the middle step of Step 1 reads ∫ mG dω = GωΣm, with the minus sign missing, before the correct final value −4πGΣm. Treat this as a print or extraction slip and check the page image.

## Step 2: a mass outside S

Draw a thin cone of solid angle dΩ from m. It cuts S an even number of times; take a near patch 1 and a far patch 2. At both patches g points back along the cone, toward m.

- At patch 1 the outward normal faces m, so g · n̂ > 0 and the flux is +Gm|dΩ|.
- At patch 2 the outward normal faces away from m, so the flux is −Gm|dΩ|.

The two cancel. Summing over all cones,

$$ ∮ g · dA = 0   (mass outside) $$

This is where the inverse-square law does the work. For the same |dΩ| the far patch is larger by (r₂/r₁)², and the field there is weaker by exactly (r₁/r₂)². Mathur's exercise 9 (PDF 709) uses the same double-cone idea to show that the field inside a shell is zero.

## Step 3: many masses and continuous matter

Flux is linear in g and g obeys superposition, so the total flux is −4πG times the sum of the **enclosed** masses; for continuous matter M_enc = ∫ρ dτ. This completes the proof.

## Step 4: why the inverse square is essential

Suppose instead that g = −K r̂/rⁿ. Through a sphere of radius r centred on the mass,

$$ ∮ g · dA = −(K/rⁿ)(4πr²) = −4πK r^(2−n) $$

This is independent of r only when n = 2. For n ≠ 2 the external-cone cancellation also fails, because the two patches give K dΩ r^(2−n) at different values of r. Mathur §12.23 writes the general force F = C r̂/rⁿ; only n = 2 obeys a Gauss law.

The converse also holds: Gauss's law with curl g = 0 and spherical symmetry gives back g = GM/r² (module 5).

## A mass exactly on S

The solid-angle method shows that such a mass contributes in proportion to the solid angle the inside of S subtends at it: one half for a point on a smooth face, one eighth at a corner of a cube (module 10).

## Check

For a sphere centred on m, cos α = 1 and the flux is −(Gm/r²)(4πr²) = −4πGm directly; Gm dΩ is in m³ s⁻² because dΩ is a pure number.

### 4. Core: the divergence theorem, Gauss's law at a point, and Poisson's and Laplace's equations

*Objective:* Derive ∇ · g = −4πGρ and Poisson's and Laplace's equations, and interpret divergence and the Laplacian.

## Recap: from Gauss's law to Poisson's equation

PH-008 module 5 derives this in full, and D2 is the exam layout. In three lines: Gauss's law holds for every region τ, ∮_S g · dA = −4πG ∫_τ ρ dτ; the divergence theorem (bridge B2) rewrites the left side as ∫_τ (∇ · g) dτ; so ∫_τ (∇ · g + 4πGρ) dτ = 0 for **every** region. Both sides of the final equations have units s⁻².

## What is new here: from "every region" to "every point"

Suppose the integrand, taken as continuous, were positive at some point P. Then it would be positive throughout a small ball around P, and the integral over that ball would be positive, not zero. The same argument rules out a negative value. Hence, at every point,

$$ ∇ · g = −4πG ρ   (Gauss's law, differential form) $$

With g = −∇Φ we have ∇ · g = −∇²Φ, so

$$ ∇²Φ = ∂²Φ/∂x² + ∂²Φ/∂y² + ∂²Φ/∂z² = 4πG ρ   (Poisson) $$

$$ ∇²Φ = 0  where ρ = 0   (Laplace) $$

This is Mathur's route in §12.20 (PDF 667). The field equations of static Newtonian gravity are the pair

$$ ∇ · g = −4πG ρ,   ∇ × g = 0 $$

Together with Φ → 0 far from a bounded body, they fix g uniquely (module 7).

## What the operators mean

- **∇ · g** is the sink strength per unit volume. Wherever ρ > 0, field lines converge. In empty space, as many lines leave a small box as enter it.
- **∇²Φ** compares Φ at a point with its average over a tiny sphere around the point. ∇²Φ > 0 means the point lies **below** its surroundings, like the bottom of a bowl: matter digs potential bowls. In empty space Φ equals the average of its surroundings exactly (module 8).
- **The sign.** Electrostatics has ∇²V = −ρ/ε₀; gravity has **+4πGρ**. Carrying the wrong sign across is the most common slip in this topic.

## Four quick uses

1. **Point mass.** g_r = −Gm/r². The spherical divergence (bridge B3) is (1/r²) d(r² g_r)/dr = (1/r²) d(−Gm)/dr = 0 for r ≠ 0. Yet the flux is −4πGm, so all the divergence sits at the origin: ∇ · g = −4πGm δ³(r), where δ³ is zero except at the origin and integrates to 1.
2. **Inside a uniform sphere.** In vector form g = −(GM/R³) r (PH-008 module 4), so ∇ · g = −3GM/R³. With ρ = 3M/(4πR³) this equals −4πGρ. ✓ (PH-008 module 5 checks Poisson on Φ instead; the content is the same.)
3. **Density from a measured field.** If a planet's interior field is g = −k r with k constant, then ∇ · g = −3k, so ρ = 3k/(4πG): the planet is uniform. Numerical N4 uses this.
4. **The inverse square again.** For g_r = −K/rⁿ, (1/r²) d(r² g_r)/dr = −K(2 − n)/r^(n+1). For n ≠ 2 this is non-zero in empty space, so Poisson's equation would report matter where there is none.

## Closing the loop to Newton

For a point mass, ∇²(1/r) = −4π δ³(r), so Φ = −Gm/r gives ∇²Φ = 4πGm δ³(r), the delta form found in use 1. Superposing point masses, the potential of any density is Φ(r) = −G ∫ ρ(r₁)/|r − r₁| dτ₁, the integral solution of Poisson's equation: Newton's law summed over the matter. (Standard results, not in the extracts; flagged in completeness.)

## Cylindrical and planar forms

When the field depends only on the distance s from an axis, ∇²Φ = (1/s) d/ds (s dΦ/ds). When it depends only on the height z, ∇²Φ = d²Φ/dz² (bridge B3). Module 6 uses both.

### 5. Core: spherical symmetry by Gauss's theorem: the 2018 potential outside and inside a sphere

*Objective:* Use Gauss's theorem to obtain the potential of a sphere outside and inside from its field, with the symmetry argument and every sign.

## The three moves in every Gauss solution

1. **Argue the symmetry first.** For a spherically symmetric body, g at P must be radial, with a magnitude that depends only on r. A sideways component would have to point some particular way around the radius. Rotating the body about OP changes nothing, so no such direction can be picked out.
2. **Choose the Gaussian surface to match**: a sphere of radius r, centred on O, passing through P.
3. **Evaluate the flux** as (g_r)(area). On every patch g is parallel to n̂, so ∮ g · dA = g_r(r) · 4πr².

## The field in three cases: recap

PH-008 module 5 works these through a Gaussian sphere (Mathur §12.19 applications, PDF 666-667), so only the results are listed. With 4πr² g_r = −4πG M_enc: outside any spherically symmetric body, g_r = −GM/r² (the density need not be uniform); inside a uniform sphere, M_enc = M r³/R³ gives g_r = −GMr/R³; for a thin shell, M_enc = 0 gives g = 0 inside, although a shell does not shield its interior from other bodies (NCERT, Points to Ponder 9).

## Outside: the potential (what the 2018 question asks for)

Gauss's law gives the field; the potential needs one integral from the reference point. With Φ(∞) = 0 and dΦ = −g · dr,

$$ Φ(r) = −∫_∞^r g_r(r′) dr′ = −∫_∞^r (−GM/r′²) dr′ = GM [−1/r′]_∞^r = −GM/r $$

Watch three signs: the minus in dΦ = −g · dr, the minus in g_r, and the lower limit ∞, where −1/r′ is zero. Outside, the potential of the sphere is that of a point mass M at O.

## Inside a uniform sphere: the potential

Integrate the inside field inward from the surface value Φ(R) = −GM/R:

$$ Φ(r) = Φ(R) − ∫_R^r (−GMr′/R³) dr′ = −GM/R + (GM/(2R³))(r² − R²) $$

$$ Φ(r) = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³) $$

This is the result PH-008 module 4 obtains by adding shells. For a thin shell, Φ = −GM/R everywhere inside.

## The second part of the 2018 question, in one line

Raising m from R to 3R/2 needs W = m[Φ(3R/2) − Φ(R)] = mgR/3. PH-006 module 6 derives the general W = mgh/(1 + h/R); module 9 of this lesson works the 2018 calculation in full, with numbers. Mathur exercise 17 (PDF 710) is the same problem as a textbook exercise; its printed answer is a textbook answer, not an official key.

## Checks

- **Continuity:** the interior and exterior formulas both give Φ = −GM/R at r = R. g_r is continuous for the solid sphere (−GM/R²) but jumps for the shell (module 7).
- **Dimensions:** GM/R is in J kg⁻¹.
- **Limit:** far from the sphere the potential tends to zero, as chosen.

### 6. Core: cylindrical and planar symmetry: line, cylinder, sheet and slab

*Objective:* Apply Gauss's law with cylindrical and planar symmetry, and explain why Φ(∞) = 0 fails for an infinite line or plane.

## Infinite uniform line of mass (λ kg m⁻¹)

**Symmetry.** At distance s, g points along the perpendicular to the line, with magnitude g(s). There is no component along the line, because the line looks the same upward and downward. There is no component around it either.

**Gaussian surface.** A coaxial cylinder of radius s and length L. On the curved face g is parallel to n̂. On the flat ends g lies in the face, so g · n̂ = 0 there. Then

$$ g_s · 2πsL = −4πG λ L  ⇒  g_s = −2Gλ/s $$

The field falls as **1/s**, not 1/s², because the symmetric surface grows only in proportion to s.

**Potential.** dΦ/ds = −g_s = 2Gλ/s, so

$$ Φ(s) = Φ(s₀) + 2Gλ ln(s/s₀) $$

The logarithm grows without limit, so Φ(∞) = 0 cannot be imposed: an infinite line has infinite mass. Choose a reference radius s₀ and quote only differences.

## Uniform solid cylinder (radius a, density ρ)

Inside, λ_enc = ρπs², so g_s = −2πGρ s. Outside, g_s = −2Gλ/s with λ = ρπa². Both give 2πGρa at s = a. Taking Φ(a) = 0:

$$ Φ = πGρ(s² − a²)  for s ≤ a,    Φ = 2πGρa² ln(s/a)  for s ≥ a $$

Poisson check (bridge B3). Inside, s dΦ/ds = 2πGρ s²; its derivative is 4πGρ s; dividing by s gives 4πGρ. ✓ Outside, s dΦ/ds = 2πGρa² is constant, so ∇²Φ = 0. ✓

## Infinite plane sheet (σ kg m⁻²)

**Pillbox.** A short cylinder with faces of area A straddles the sheet. By mirror symmetry the field is perpendicular to the sheet, of the same size g on both sides, and points toward the sheet on both sides. Each face therefore carries flux −gA, and the curved side carries none:

$$ −2gA = −4πG σ A  ⇒  g = 2πGσ  toward the sheet, at any distance $$

This agrees with Mathur §12.18 (PDF 665), who reaches 2πσG as the limit of a disc. The potential is Φ(z) = Φ(0) + 2πGσ|z|: linear and unbounded.

**Source slip.** Mathur prints the plane's potential as −2πσGr, which decreases away from the plane and would mean repulsion. It comes from his disc formula V = −2πσGr(1 − cos α), which rests on *potential = intensity × r*, a step that fails for a disc. The standard disc result is −2πGσ(√(R² + z²) − z) (PH-006 module 4); the two agree far from the disc but not near it. Check the page image.

## Uniform slab (−t/2 < z < t/2, density ρ)

Take a pillbox from −z to +z, with 0 < z < t/2. Its top face has n̂ = +ẑ. Its bottom face has n̂ = −ẑ, and by symmetry g_z(−z) = −g_z(z). So the flux is 2g_z(z)A, and the enclosed mass is ρ(2z)A:

$$ g_z = −4πGρ z  for |z| ≤ t/2 $$

Outside, g_z = −2πGρt above the slab and +2πGρt below it. Taking Φ(0) = 0, the inside potential is Φ = 2πGρz², so d²Φ/dz² = 4πGρ (Poisson). ✓

## Summary: the shape of the symmetric surface sets the law

| Symmetry | Gaussian surface | Field outside | Field inside (uniform) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Point or sphere | concentric sphere | GM/r² | GMr/R³ |
| Line or cylinder | coaxial cylinder | 2Gλ/s | 2πGρs |
| Plane or slab | pillbox | 2πGσ, or 2πGρt | 4πGρ × distance from mid-plane |


**Limits:** shrinking a → 0 at fixed λ turns the cylinder into a line; t → 0 with ρt = σ turns the slab into a sheet. A real rod looks like a line only when s is much smaller than its length and the point is far from its ends. From far away, any finite rod looks like a point mass.

### 7. Master: solving Poisson's equation: interface conditions, uniqueness and density from potential

*Objective:* Solve Poisson's equation with boundary and interface conditions, justify uniqueness, and obtain a density from a given potential.

## The recipe

1. Write ∇²Φ in the coordinates that match the symmetry (bridge B3).
2. Integrate Poisson's equation in each region where ρ is known. Each region brings two constants.
3. Fix the constants. **Regularity:** Φ and g stay finite at the centre or on the axis. **Reference:** Φ → 0 at infinity for a body of finite mass. **Interface conditions** hold at every surface where ρ jumps (derived next).

## The interface conditions

Let a surface carry surface density σ (σ = 0 at an ordinary boundary such as a planet's surface), with unit normal n̂ pointing from side − to side +.

- **Normal component.** Use a pillbox of face area A whose height shrinks to zero. The flux through its side tends to zero, and so does the volume mass. What remains is (g_n⁺ − g_n⁻)A = −4πGσA, that is, g_n⁺ − g_n⁻ = −4πGσ.
- **Tangential component.** Use a thin rectangular loop across the surface. ∮ g · dl = 0 because g is curl-free, and the short sides contribute nothing, so g_t⁺ = g_t⁻.
- **Potential.** g is finite, so Φ cannot change across zero thickness: Φ⁺ = Φ⁻.

Checks. Shell: g_r(R⁺) − g_r(R⁻) = −GM/R² − 0, and 4πGσ = 4πG M/(4πR²) = GM/R². ✓ Sheet: (−2πGσ) − (+2πGσ) = −4πGσ. ✓

## Worked: the uniform sphere straight from Poisson

Inside, (1/r²)(r²Φ′)′ = 4πGρ. Multiply by r² and integrate: r²Φ′ = (4πGρ/3) r³ + C₁. Hence

$$ Φ′ = (4πGρ/3) r + C₁/r²,   Φ = (2πGρ/3) r² − C₁/r + C₂ $$

Outside, (r²Φ′)′ = 0 gives Φ = −D₁/r + D₂. Now fix the four constants:

- **Regularity at r = 0:** C₁ = 0, otherwise g would blow up like 1/r².
- **Φ(∞) = 0:** D₂ = 0.
- **Normal g continuous at R** (σ = 0): (4πGρ/3)R = D₁/R², so D₁ = (4πρR³/3)G = GM. Gauss's law has come out of the matching.
- **Φ continuous at R:** (2πGρ/3)R² + C₂ = −GM/R. Since (2πGρ/3)R² = GM/(2R), C₂ = −3GM/(2R).

Result: Φ = GMr²/(2R³) − 3GM/(2R), as in module 5. The same recipe inside a thick hollow sphere gives a constant potential −2πρG(R₂² − R₁²) in the cavity (Mathur exercise 15, PDF 709; PH-006 module 5).

## Why the answer is the only one (uniqueness)

Let Φ₁ and Φ₂ both satisfy Poisson's equation with the same ρ and the same boundary values (or both fall to zero like 1/r at infinity). Their difference ψ obeys ∇²ψ = 0 and vanishes on the boundary. From ∇ · (ψ∇ψ) = |∇ψ|² + ψ∇²ψ and the divergence theorem,

$$ ∫ |∇ψ|² dτ = ∮ ψ ∇ψ · dA − ∫ ψ ∇²ψ dτ = 0 − 0 $$

So ∇ψ = 0 everywhere and ψ is constant; it is zero on the boundary, so it is zero everywhere. Any trial solution that satisfies the equation and the boundary conditions is therefore **the** solution, and that is what justifies guessing.

## The inverse problem: density from a potential

Given Φ(r) = −GM/√(r² + b²), with b a constant length:

1. Φ′ = GM r (r² + b²)^(−3/2).
2. r²Φ′ = GM r³ (r² + b²)^(−3/2).
3. Differentiate: GM[3r²(r² + b²)^(−3/2) − 3r⁴(r² + b²)^(−5/2)] = 3GM r² b² (r² + b²)^(−5/2).
4. Divide by r²: ∇²Φ = 3GMb²(r² + b²)^(−5/2) = 4πGρ, so

$$ ρ(r) = (3M/(4πb³)) (1 + r²/b²)^(−5/2) $$

Gauss check: M(r) = r²|g_r|/G = M r³/(r² + b²)^(3/2), which tends to M at large r. ✓ Half the mass lies inside r = 1.305 b. In stellar dynamics this smooth cluster is called the Plummer model (the name is quoted from standard references; verify).

### 8. Master: self energy as field energy, no stable equilibrium in empty space, and the mean-value property

*Objective:* Express self energy as field energy and check it, and explain why a test mass has no stable equilibrium in a matter-free region.

## Self energy as energy stored in the field

PH-008 module 6 writes the self energy of a body as U = ½∫ρΦ dτ, taken over the body. Replace ρ using Poisson's equation, ρ = ∇²Φ/(4πG):

$$ U = (1/(8πG)) ∫ Φ ∇²Φ dτ $$

Now use ∇ · (Φ∇Φ) = |∇Φ|² + Φ∇²Φ and the divergence theorem over a very large sphere of radius S:

$$ ∫ Φ∇²Φ dτ = ∮ Φ ∇Φ · dA − ∫ |∇Φ|² dτ $$

Far away, Φ ≈ −GM/S and |∇Φ| ≈ GM/S², and the area is 4πS². So the surface term is about −4πG²M²/S, which tends to zero as S → ∞. With |∇Φ| = g:

$$ U = −(1/(8πG)) ∫_all space g² dτ $$

Because ρ = 0 outside the body, ½∫ρΦ over the body equals the same integral over all space, which is why a large sphere may be used above. The final integral, however, is of g² and runs over **all space**, including empty space where g ≠ 0. Read −g²/(8πG) as an energy density for bookkeeping only; where gravitational energy is located is a subtle question beyond this syllabus.

**Check on the uniform sphere.**

- Outside: ∫_R^∞ (GM/r²)² 4πr² dr = 4πG²M²/R.
- Inside: ∫_0^R (GMr/R³)² 4πr² dr = 4πG²M²/(5R).
- Total: 4πG²M²(6/5)/R. Multiply by −1/(8πG):

$$ U = −(6/5)(GM²/(2R)) = −(3/5) GM²/R  ✓ $$

This matches PH-008 module 7, where it is obtained by assembling shells. A new fact follows: **five-sixths of the field energy lies outside the sphere**. For a thin shell g = 0 inside, so U = −(1/(8πG))(4πG²M²/R) = −GM²/(2R), PH-008's shell value. At the Earth's surface, −g²/(8πG) = −(9.81)²/(1.677 × 10⁻⁹) = −5.74 × 10¹⁰ J m⁻³.

## No stable equilibrium in empty space

Mathur §12.24 (PDF 671-672) argues from equipotentials that bodies cannot rest in stable equilibrium under inverse-square forces. Laplace's equation makes this precise.

A test mass is stable only where Φ has a strict minimum. In a region with no matter ∇²Φ = 0, and then Φ at any point equals its average over any small sphere around that point. This is the mean-value property of solutions of Laplace's equation, a standard theorem (flag). A point cannot be lower than all its neighbours if it equals their average. Hence **in a matter-free region a static field has no potential minimum**. A test mass can be in equilibrium there, but never in stable equilibrium.

Example: equal masses m at x = ±d. At the midpoint g = 0. Expanding there gives Φ_xx = −4Gm/d³ and Φ_yy = Φ_zz = +2Gm/d³. The sum is zero, as Laplace's equation demands, and the point is a **saddle**: stable sideways, unstable along the line.

Inside matter ∇²Φ = 4πGρ > 0, so a minimum is allowed. The centre of a uniform sphere is a true minimum, which is why the tunnel motion in PH-006 module 7 is simple harmonic. Mathur's sentence *no static equilibrium is possible in an inverse square law force field* is correct for systems of point masses (or charges), which is the Earnshaw setting. It does not extend to a test mass inside continuous matter; read it as applying to a test body in a region free of the attracting matter, in a static field and an inertial frame.

## The shell theorem in one line

Put a point mass m at distance D from the centre of an imaginary sphere of radius a < D. Inside that sphere the potential of m is harmonic, so its average over the sphere is its central value −Gm/D. Now use reciprocity. The potential at m of a uniform shell of mass M is Φ_shell = −G ∫ σ dA/|r − r_m| = (M/m) × (sphere average of Φ_m) = (M/m)(−Gm/D) = −GM/D, because σ = M/(4πa²) is uniform. From outside, the shell therefore acts as a point mass at its centre (compare PH-008 module 3).

### 9. Exam: the 2018 question, likely variants and the traps

*Objective:* Plan and write the 2018 Q2(a) answer, and recognise the likely variants and traps.

## The verified question

**2018 Paper I Q2(a), 15 marks:** *Use Gauss's theorem to calculate the gravitational potential due to a solid sphere at a point outside the sphere. Calculate the amount of work required to send a body of mass m from the Earth's surface to a height R/2, where R is the radius of the Earth.*

The notes below are analysis only; the project holds no official answer key.

## The 2018 work calculation, in full (the one full working in this lesson)

Raise m slowly from r = R to r = 3R/2 (Earth non-rotating, no air), so that the work equals the rise in potential energy. With Φ = −GM/r outside:

$$ W = m[Φ(3R/2) − Φ(R)] = GMm(1/R − 2/(3R)) = GMm/(3R) = mgR/3 $$

using GM = gR². For m = 1 kg, g = 9.81 m s⁻² and R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m, W = 2.083 × 10⁷ J. The school formula mgh with h = R/2 gives mgR/2 = 3.125 × 10⁷ J, which is 50 % too high, because g falls with height (the general result W = mgh/(1 + h/R) is in PH-006 module 6; PH-006 module 9 outlines the same question). For h = 10R the same method gives (10/11) mgR. Mathur exercise 17 (PDF 710) prints mgR/3, (10/11)mgR and (1000/1001)mgR for such heights; these are textbook answers, not an official key.

## A model layout (about 14 lines)

1. Figure: sphere (centre O, radius R), point P at r > R, a dotted Gaussian sphere through P, and field arrows pointing to O.
2. Statement: ∮ g · dA = −4πG M_enc, with outward normal, and Φ(∞) = 0.
3. Symmetry: g is radial and of equal magnitude on the Gaussian sphere, so the flux is g_r · 4πr².
4. Gauss: 4πr² g_r = −4πGM, so g_r = −GM/r², for any spherically symmetric density.
5. Potential: Φ(r) = −∫_∞^r g_r dr′ = −GM/r. Box it.
6. One line of meaning: outside, the sphere acts as a point mass at its centre.
7. Work: W = m[Φ(3R/2) − Φ(R)] = GMm/(3R).
8. With GM = gR², W = mgR/3. Box it.
9. Checks: units are joules; the h ≪ R limit gives mgh; mgh at h = R/2 gives mgR/2, 50 % too large.
10. Assumptions: slow raising, non-rotating Earth, no air.

The first part carries most of the marks and asks for Gauss *by name*. An answer by shell integration does not follow the instruction, however correct it is.

## Traps examiners exploit

- Writing ∮ g · dA = +4πGM or ∇²Φ = −4πGρ, by carrying over the electrostatic signs.
- Using Gauss without first arguing the symmetry, or claiming the method works for a disc or a cube.
- Integrating from the wrong reference point (from 0 or from R) when the question asks for the potential.
- Treating a line or a plane as if Φ(∞) = 0 were possible.
- Forgetting that a mass outside changes g on the surface even though it adds no flux.
- Taking R/2 as the final radius: it is 3R/2.

## Guided problem 1: flux through a cube

*A 1000 kg point mass sits (a) at the centre of a cube of side 2 m, (b) at one corner of the cube, (c) 5 m outside it. Find the flux through the whole cube, and, in case (a), through one face.*

Thinking: Gauss's law counts only enclosed mass, and the solid-angle proof (module 3) handles the corner.

- (a) −4πGM = −4π(6.674 × 10⁻¹¹)(1000) = −8.387 × 10⁻⁷ m³ s⁻². By symmetry each face carries one sixth: −1.398 × 10⁻⁷ m³ s⁻².
- (b) The inside of the cube subtends π/2 sr at a corner (one eighth of 4π), so the flux is −πGM/2 = −1.048 × 10⁻⁷ m³ s⁻². The three faces that meet at the corner carry zero flux, because g lies in them; the other three share the flux.
- (c) Zero. The field on the cube is not zero, but the net flux is.

## Guided problem 2: a rock layer (planar symmetry)

*A broad horizontal layer of rock is 100 m thick, with density 2670 kg m⁻³. Estimate its contribution to g just above it.*

Thinking: a wide, uniform layer has slab symmetry (module 6). The field outside is 2πGρt, independent of height while the layer still looks infinite. 2π(6.674 × 10⁻¹¹)(2670)(100) = 1.120 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻², about 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ of g. Geophysicists quote such small changes in milligal (1 mGal = 10⁻⁵ m s⁻²): 11.2 mGal, or 0.112 mGal per metre of rock. This is the Bouguer slab correction of gravity surveys: 2πG(1000 kg m⁻³)(1 m) = 0.04193 mGal, so 0.04193 × 2.67 = 0.112 mGal per metre. The 2670 kg m⁻³ convention is standard practice to verify.

## Guided problem 3: a buried ore body (cylindrical symmetry)

*A long horizontal cylinder of radius 50 m and excess density 500 kg m⁻³ has its axis 200 m below level ground. Find the extra vertical g directly above it, and 200 m to the side.*

Thinking: long means line symmetry. The excess mass per length is λ = 500 × π × 50² = 3.927 × 10⁶ kg m⁻¹. At distance ℓ from the axis the extra field is 2Gλ/ℓ, toward the axis. At horizontal offset x its vertical component is (2Gλ/ℓ)(z₀/ℓ) = 2Gλz₀/(z₀² + x²).

- Directly above: 2Gλ/z₀ = 2.621 × 10⁻⁶ m s⁻² = 0.262 mGal.
- At x = z₀ = 200 m: half of that, 0.131 mGal.

The curve falls to half its peak at x = ±z₀, so the half-width of a measured profile gives the depth of the axis.

## Guided problem 4: density from a potential

*Inside a model planet Φ = Ar² + B, with A = 7.7 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻² and radius R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m. Find ρ, g at the surface, M and B.*

Thinking: use Poisson's equation (module 4).

- ∇²(Ar²) = 6A, so ρ = 6A/(4πG) = 5.51 × 10³ kg m⁻³.
- g_r = −2Ar, so g(R) = 2AR = 9.81 m s⁻².
- By Gauss's law, M = gR²/G = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg.
- Continuity with −GM/R = −gR = −2AR² gives B = −3AR² = −9.38 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹, so Φ(0)/Φ(R) = 3/2.

### Notes: Definitions

- Gravitational field g: force per unit mass on a small test mass; unit N kg⁻¹ = m s⁻².
- Gravitational potential Φ: potential energy per unit mass, defined by dΦ = −g · dr; Φ = 0 at infinity for bounded bodies; unit J kg⁻¹ (written V in PH-006 and PH-008).
- Flux of g through a closed surface: ∮ g · dA, with dA = n̂ dA and n̂ the outward normal; unit m³ s⁻².
- Solid angle of a patch seen from O: dΩ = cos α dA / r²; a closed surface around O subtends 4π sr.
- Gaussian surface: an imaginary closed surface chosen so that g has constant magnitude and is normal to it (or lies in it) on each part.
- Divergence ∇ · g: net outward flux per unit volume. Laplacian ∇²Φ = ∇ · ∇Φ.
- Harmonic function: a solution of Laplace's equation; it equals its average over any sphere inside its region (mean-value property).

### Notes: Key equations

- Integral Gauss law: ∮ g · dA = −4πG M_enc.
- Differential form: ∇ · g = −4πGρ, together with ∇ × g = 0 and g = −∇Φ.
- Poisson: ∇²Φ = 4πGρ. Laplace: ∇²Φ = 0 where ρ = 0.
- Radial Laplacians: spherical (1/r²) d/dr (r² dΦ/dr); cylindrical (1/s) d/ds (s dΦ/ds); planar d²Φ/dz².
- Point mass: ∇ · g = −4πGm δ³(r).
- Interface conditions: g_n⁺ − g_n⁻ = −4πGσ; g_t continuous; Φ continuous.
- Line: g_s = −2Gλ/s, Φ = Φ(s₀) + 2Gλ ln(s/s₀). Sheet: g = 2πGσ toward it, Φ = Φ(0) + 2πGσ|z|. Slab: g_z = −4πGρz inside.
- Self energy: U = ½∫ρΦ dτ = −(1/8πG)∫ g² dτ over all space; −(3/5)GM²/R for a uniform sphere.
- Potential from the field: Φ(r) = −∫_∞^r g_r dr′ (module 5); the 2018 work calculation is in module 9.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: Gauss's law from Newton's law

- 1. Point mass m at O, closed surface S, patch dA at distance r, angle α between n̂ and r̂.
- 2. g · n̂ dA = −(Gm/r²) cos α dA = −Gm dΩ (the inverse square cancels r).
- 3. Mass inside: ∮ dΩ = 4π, so the flux is −4πGm (rays that cross S several times cross an odd number of times; extra pairs cancel).
- 4. Mass outside: each cone cuts S an even number of times; entry gives +Gm dΩ and exit gives −Gm dΩ; net zero.
- 5. Superposition: ∮ g · dA = −4πG Σ m_inside; continuous matter gives M_enc = ∫ρ dτ.
- 6. For g ∝ 1/rⁿ the flux through a sphere varies as r^(2−n): Gauss's law needs n = 2.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: Poisson's equation from Gauss's law

- 1. For any region: ∮ g · dA = −4πG ∫ρ dτ.
- 2. Divergence theorem: ∮ g · dA = ∫ (∇ · g) dτ.
- 3. ∫(∇ · g + 4πGρ) dτ = 0 for every region, so ∇ · g = −4πGρ at every point (continuous integrand).
- 4. g = −∇Φ (curl-free field), so ∇ · g = −∇²Φ.
- 5. ∇²Φ = 4πGρ; where ρ = 0, ∇²Φ = 0.
- 6. Check: both sides have units s⁻²; for a uniform sphere ∇ · g = −3GM/R³ = −4πGρ.

### Notes: The three symmetries

- The field law is set by how the area of the symmetric Gaussian surface grows with distance: as r², s and a constant respectively.

### Notes: Gravity versus electrostatics

- The mathematics is identical apart from signs and constants; gravity has only one sign of source and cannot be shielded.

### Notes: Concepts that are easily confused

- Gauss's law is always true, but it yields the field only when symmetry fixes the field's direction and magnitude on the Gaussian surface; for a disc, a ring or a cube, integrate instead (PH-006 module 4).
- Zero net flux through a surface does not mean zero field on it: an outside mass gives g ≠ 0 on the surface but zero net flux.
- Zero field at a point does not mean stable equilibrium: in empty space such points are saddles.
- Gauss's law gives the field; the potential needs an integral from a stated reference point.
- Poisson's equation for gravity has +4πGρ; electrostatics has −ρ/ε₀.
- An infinite line or plane cannot have Φ(∞) = 0; quote potential differences.
- A shell has zero field inside from its own mass, but it does not shield its interior from external masses.

  - The proof uses solid angles; the inverse-square law makes each patch's flux −Gm dΩ, independent of distance.
  - The divergence theorem turns Gauss's law into ∇ · g = −4πGρ, and g = −∇Φ turns that into Poisson's equation.
  - Symmetry plus Gauss gives the field; the potential then needs an integral from a reference point.
  - Poisson's equation with boundary and interface conditions has a unique solution.
  - In a matter-free region Φ is harmonic and has no minimum, so there is no stable equilibrium there.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - ∮ g · dA = −4πG M_enc (negative: field lines end on mass).
  - ∇²Φ = +4πGρ for gravity, the opposite sign to electrostatics.
  - Sphere outside: g = GM/r², Φ = −GM/r; uniform sphere inside: g = GMr/R³.
  - Infinite line: g = 2Gλ/s; infinite sheet: g = 2πGσ; slab inside: g = 4πGρ × distance from mid-plane.
  - Normal g jumps by −4πGσ across a surface layer; Φ and tangential g are continuous.
  - Self energy = −(1/8πG)∫g² dτ; five-sixths of it lies outside a uniform sphere.
  - Potential outside any spherically symmetric body: Φ = −∫_∞^r g_r dr′ = −GM/r (module 5); the 2018 work calculation is in module 9.
- **criticalDistinctions:**
  - Flux (one number for a whole surface) versus field (a vector at each point).
  - Enclosed mass (sets the flux) versus outside mass (changes the field on the surface, not the flux).
  - Gauss's law always holds versus Gauss's law is useful only with symmetry.
  - Equilibrium (g = 0) versus stable equilibrium (Φ a minimum).
  - Bounded body (Φ(∞) = 0) versus infinite line or plane (needs a finite reference).
- **formulae:**
  - ∮ g · dA = −4πG M_enc
  - ∇ · g = −4πGρ;  ∇ × g = 0;  g = −∇Φ
  - ∇²Φ = 4πGρ;  ∇²Φ = 0 (ρ = 0)
  - ∇²Φ = (1/r²)(r²Φ′)′;  (1/s)(sΦ′)′;  Φ″(z)
  - g_s = −2Gλ/s;  Φ = Φ(s₀) + 2Gλ ln(s/s₀)
  - g = 2πGσ (sheet);  g_z = −4πGρz (inside slab)
  - g_n⁺ − g_n⁻ = −4πGσ
  - U = −(1/8πG) ∫ g² dτ
  - Φ(r) = −∫_∞^r g_r dr′ = −GM/r outside a spherical body
- **traps:**
  - Writing +4πGM for the flux or −4πGρ in Poisson's equation (electrostatic signs carried over).
  - Using Gauss's law for a disc, a ring or a cube as if symmetry gave a constant field.
  - Setting Φ(∞) = 0 for an infinite line or plane.
  - Integrating the potential from the wrong reference point.
  - Using mgh for a large height, or taking R/2 as the final radius (module 9).
  - Integrating the field energy over the body only instead of all space.
- **blankPrompts:**
  - - **q:** ∮ g · dA = ______
    - **a:** −4πG M_enc
  - - **q:** ∇²Φ = ______ inside matter and ______ in empty space.
    - **a:** 4πGρ; 0
  - - **q:** The field of an infinite line of mass per length λ at distance s is ______.
    - **a:** 2Gλ/s, toward the line
  - - **q:** Across a surface of density σ, g_n⁺ − g_n⁻ = ______.
    - **a:** −4πGσ
  - - **q:** The solid angle of a patch is dΩ = ______, and a closed surface subtends ______ at an interior point.
    - **a:** cos α dA / r²; 4π sr
  - - **q:** U = ½∫ρΦ dτ can be rewritten as ______.
    - **a:** −(1/8πG)∫ g² dτ over all space
- **shortQuestions:**
  - - **q:** Why does a mass outside a closed surface give zero net flux?
    - **a:** Each thin cone from it enters and leaves the surface; the two patches give +Gm dΩ and −Gm dΩ, equal in size because of the inverse-square law.
  - - **q:** Which mathematical theorem turns Gauss's law into Poisson's equation?
    - **a:** The divergence theorem, ∮ g · dA = ∫(∇ · g) dτ, followed by g = −∇Φ.
  - - **q:** Why can't the potential of an infinite line be zero at infinity?
    - **a:** Φ grows as 2Gλ ln s without limit; the line has infinite mass. Use a finite reference radius.
  - - **q:** Why is a solution of Poisson's equation that meets the boundary conditions unique?
    - **a:** The difference ψ of two solutions is harmonic and zero on the boundary; ∫|∇ψ|² dτ = 0 forces ψ = 0.
  - - **q:** What fraction of a uniform sphere's field energy lies outside it?
    - **a:** Five-sixths (outside 4πG²M²/R against inside 4πG²M²/(5R)).
  - - **q:** Give one point where a test mass is in equilibrium but not stable equilibrium.
    - **a:** The midpoint between two equal masses: g = 0 there, but it is a saddle (Φ_xx < 0, Φ_yy, Φ_zz > 0).
- **mnemonics:** - Sphere, Line, Plane: 2, 1, 0. The field falls as distance to the power 2, 1 and 0, because the symmetric Gaussian surface grows as r², s and a constant.

    - **a:** An imaginary closed surface, chosen to exploit symmetry so that g has constant magnitude and is normal to it (or lies in it) on each part.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Gauss's law holds for a cube enclosing a sphere. Why can it not give the field at a corner of the cube?
    - **a:** On a cube g is neither constant in magnitude nor normal to the faces, so the single equation for the total flux cannot be solved for g at a point. Choose a concentric sphere instead.
  - - **q:** Why does the field of an infinite line fall as 1/s, while that of a sphere falls as 1/r²?
    - **a:** The enclosed mass (per length, for the line) is fixed, and the area of the symmetric Gaussian surface grows as s for a coaxial cylinder but as r² for a sphere. The field is the flux divided by that area.
  - - **q:** If gravity followed a 1/r³ law, would Gauss's law hold?
    - **a:** No. The flux through a sphere would vary as 1/r, and the cones from external masses would not cancel. Gauss's law in its mass-only form needs the inverse square.
  - - **q:** A planet has a concentric empty cavity at its centre. What does Laplace's equation say about the cavity?
    - **a:** With spherical symmetry Φ = −D/r + C there. Regularity at r = 0 forces D = 0, so Φ is constant and g = 0 in the cavity.

  - - **q:** Find the field 1 m from an infinite line of 1000 kg m⁻¹.
