### Bridge: The three organs of government and what each does

Any government has three jobs, and a Constitution gives each job to a different body, called an **organ**.

- The **legislature** makes laws and controls money. In India at the Centre it is **Parliament** (the President, the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha); in a State it is the State legislature.
- The **executive** carries out the laws and runs the administration. Formally it is the **President** (Centre) or **Governor** (State); in real terms it is the **Council of Ministers** headed by the **Prime Minister** or **Chief Minister**, backed by the permanent civil service. NCERT says the executive is the body that takes policy decisions and supervises their day-to-day implementation.
- The **judiciary** interprets the law, settles disputes (between citizens, between citizens and the government, and between governments), and protects rights. It consists of the Supreme Court, the High Courts and the subordinate courts.

**A small example.** Suppose the legislature passes a law fixing a speed limit. The traffic police and the transport department (executive) enforce it and issue fines. If a driver says the fine is unlawful, a court (judiciary) decides. If the court finds the law itself violates the Constitution, it can declare the law invalid.

Two words to keep ready. **Head of state** is the formal, symbolic head (India: the President). **Head of government** is the person who actually leads the executive (India: the Prime Minister). In the American presidential system one person, the President, is both; in India they are different persons. Everything else in this lesson is about how these three organs are kept apart, and how they are made to watch each other.

### Bridge: What 'check' and 'balance' mean, with an everyday example

A **check** is the ability of one body to review, restrain or correct another. A **balance** is the overall condition in which no single body can do whatever it likes, because each has some power and each is limited by some other.

**An everyday example: a housing society.** The secretary spends the society's money (executive). The general body of residents must approve the yearly budget and can remove the secretary (a legislature-like body). An independent auditor examines the accounts and reports to residents (a watchdog). If residents dispute a decision, they go to a registrar or a consumer court (judiciary). Notice three things. The secretary cannot approve his own budget. The auditor does not run the society, only examines it. And the court does not manage the society; it intervenes only when a rule is broken. Remove any of these safeguards and the secretary's power becomes unchecked.

NCERT's Class 11 book states the logic for a nation: well-designed constitutions **fragment power** across institutions so that no single institution acquires a monopoly, and even if one wants to subvert the Constitution, others can check its transgressions. NCERT adds that India's design spreads power not only across the legislature, executive and judiciary but also to independent bodies such as the Election Commission.

**Two cautions for the exam.** First, a check is not the same as taking over: a court striking down a law is a check; a court writing a replacement law is a different, debatable thing. Second, checks work only if the checking body is itself independent; that is why Indian lessons on this topic always ask who guards the guards.

### 1. The big idea: why split state power, and what Montesquieu said

*Objective:* State the doctrine of separation of powers, its three possible meanings, and why a constitutional government is a limited government.

## The problem the idea solves

If one person or body can make the laws, carry them out, and also judge every dispute about them, nothing stops that body from abusing power. The doctrine of **separation of powers** answers this by dividing the work of government among three organs: the legislature, the executive and the judiciary (see bridge B1 if these words are new).

## Montesquieu

The doctrine is linked to the French thinker **Montesquieu**, who set it out in *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748). His argument, in plain words: liberty is endangered when legislative, executive and judicial power sit in the same hands, so each should be placed in different hands. (Montesquieu and the date are standard textbook knowledge; the NCERT and Laxmikanth extracts used here do not discuss him, so treat the attribution as a point to verify.)

## Three meanings of 'separation'

Students often use the phrase loosely. Keep three versions apart:

| Version | What it demands | Does any real system have it in pure form? |
|---|---|---|
| Separation of **functions** | Each organ does only its own job | No; every organ does a little of the others' work |
| Separation of **personnel** | No person serves in two organs at once | Strongly in the USA; not in parliamentary systems |
| **Checks and balances** | Each organ can restrain the others | Yes, in every working constitutional system |

## Constitutional government is limited government

A government that must act inside rules written in a constitution, and can be restrained when it steps outside them, is a **constitutional government**, which in effect means a **limited government**. Separation of powers is one of the main devices for keeping government limited. Two earlier Prelims questions (2020 and 2021) test exactly this equation, and another (2021) asks which factor best safeguards liberty in a liberal democracy.

## Why this matters in India

NCERT's concluding chapter on how the Constitution works says that a well-crafted constitution fragments power intelligently, horizontally across the legislature, executive and judiciary and independent bodies, and that such a system of checks and balances has helped the Indian Constitution succeed. The rest of the lesson asks: how far is power actually separated in India, and what are the checks?

> Memory hook: separation = who does the job; checks = who can stop the job being abused.

### 2. Pure separation does not exist: the USA, the UK and India compared

*Objective:* Explain, with the American presidential and British parliamentary models, why no country has separation of powers in pure form, and place India between them.

## Two models, two different answers

Laxmikanth states the contrast in one sentence: the **parliamentary system** rests on cooperation and co-ordination between legislature and executive, while the **presidential system** rests on the doctrine of separation of powers between the two. India chose the parliamentary system. NCERT adds why: the framers had experience of it under the Acts of 1919 and 1935, wanted an executive that was answerable to representatives of the people, and wanted safeguards against a personality cult around a single chief executive.

| Feature | USA (presidential) | UK (parliamentary) | India (parliamentary + written constitution) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head of executive | President is head of state **and** government (NCERT) | PM heads government; monarch is nominal head | PM heads government; President is formal head |
| Executive from legislature? | Separate election; cabinet members are not legislators (standard knowledge) | Ministers sit in Parliament | Ministers sit in Parliament and are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha |
| Can the legislature end the executive's term? | By impeachment only (standard knowledge) | By losing confidence | By no-confidence in the Lok Sabha |
| Courts and laws | Supreme Court strikes down laws (Marbury v Madison, 1803) | Parliament historically sovereign; courts do not strike down Acts (standard knowledge, verify) | Courts can strike down laws that violate the Constitution |
| Label | Rigid separation | Fusion of powers | Functional overlap with checks |

## Why even the USA is not pure

The American system is the textbook case of separation, yet it is full of checks. The President's executive power is checked by the legislature's control of money and law-making; the Supreme Court reviews laws (Marbury v Madison, 1803, as Laxmikanth records). So the doctrine becomes **checks and balances**, not a wall.

## Why the UK is the opposite

In the UK the executive is drawn from Parliament and depends on its confidence. The only strong separation is the independence of judges. (The UK moved judicial functions into a separate Supreme Court in 2009 under the Constitutional Reform Act 2005; this is standard knowledge, not in the extracts, so verify. Under the Human Rights Act 1998, UK courts may also issue declarations of incompatibility against Acts of Parliament without striking them down; verify.)

## Where India sits

Laxmikanth calls India a **synthesis**: the British idea of parliamentary supremacy and the American idea of judicial supremacy, each present to a limited degree. Parliament is not sovereign (a written Constitution, federalism, Fundamental Rights and judicial review limit it), and the Supreme Court's review power is narrower in text than in the USA because Article 21 uses 'procedure established by law' rather than 'due process of law'; since Maneka Gandhi (1978), however, courts require fair, just and reasonable procedure, and they do not judge the wisdom of policy (see GS-173 module 5).

> Examiner's phrase to remember: India has a *parliamentary* executive, so the executive and legislature are fused in personnel, yet the *judiciary* is independent and powerful. Neither the American wall nor the British fusion describes it.

### 3. India's position: overlap of functions, Article 50 and the key cases

*Objective:* Argue, with constitutional features and cases, that India follows limited separation with checks and balances, and name where separation is strongest.

## The answer in one line

NCERT describes the Indian Constitution as based on a **delicate principle of limited separation of powers and checks and balances**: Parliament is supreme in making laws and amending the Constitution, the executive in implementing them, and the judiciary in settling disputes and deciding whether laws match the Constitution.

## Where functions overlap (the 'not strict' evidence)

- **Executive in the legislature**: ministers sit in Parliament; the Cabinet decides the substance and timing of bills, and NCERT notes that no major bill is introduced without Cabinet approval.
- **Executive legislating**: the President can promulgate **ordinances** when Parliament is not in session (Article 123, and the Governor under Article 213).
- **Legislature judging**: Parliament removes a judge or impeaches the President, and each House punishes breaches of its privileges.
- **Judiciary legislating or administering**: through PIL the courts issue directions to agencies, and tribunals decide disputes outside regular courts (module 5 and 7).

## Where India separates strongly: the judiciary

Laxmikanth lists the safeguards of judicial independence, which include security of tenure, fixed service conditions, expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund, a bar on discussing judges' conduct in legislatures, a ban on practice after retirement, the Court's power to punish for contempt, and **separation of the judiciary from the executive**. That last item is **Article 50**, which sits in Part IV: the State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services. Because Part IV is a set of Directive Principles, Laxmikanth stresses that it is not enforceable by courts. Because it is a Directive Principle it is not a Fundamental Right. Article 50 has been given effect through ordinary legislation: the Code of Criminal Procedure 1973 separated judicial magistrates (under High Court control) from executive magistrates (standard knowledge; verify). Two companion walls (standard knowledge): Articles 121 and 211 bar legislatures from discussing judges' conduct, and Articles 122 and 212 stop courts from questioning proceedings of the legislature on grounds of procedural irregularity.

## The cases

- **Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab (1955)**: as commonly summarised, the Court said the Constitution does not recognise separation of powers in its absolute rigidity, but the functions of the organs are sufficiently differentiated that one organ cannot usurp the essential functions of another. *(Not in the extracts; verify.)*
- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**: the Court held Parliament cannot alter the **basic structure** under Article 368 (Laxmikanth, NCERT). Commonly cited summaries list separation of powers among the basic features. *(Verify the exact judges' lists.)*
- **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975)**: the Court tested a constitutional amendment against the basic structure (Laxmikanth cites it as requiring each amendment to be judged on its effect). As commonly summarised, it struck down an amendment clause by which Parliament had itself decided the validity of the Prime Minister's election, because that mixed up judicial and legislative power. *(Verify.)*

For the later fight over this boundary see GS-173 (landmark judgments).

### 4. Legislature and executive: the parliamentary web, committees and the party-discipline caveat

*Objective:* List how Parliament checks the executive and how the executive controls Parliament, and judge how real each check is.

## NCERT's starting point

In a parliamentary system the executive and legislature are **interdependent**: the legislature controls the executive and is in turn controlled by it. The Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**; if it loses the House's confidence it must resign. NCERT adds that a no-confidence vote against even one minister leads to the resignation of the whole Council. Collective responsibility is Article 75(3).

## The legislature's instruments (NCERT)

1. **Deliberation and discussion**: Question Hour (every sitting day), Zero Hour, half-an-hour discussions, adjournment motion.
2. **Approval or refusal of laws**: a Bill becomes law only with Parliament's approval; NCERT notes that when the government lacked a Rajya Sabha majority (1977, 2000) it had to make concessions, and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill 2002 was rejected by the Rajya Sabha, but was then passed at a joint sitting of the two Houses (Article 108) and became the Prevention of Terrorism Act 2002 (standard knowledge; verify).
3. **Financial control**: the budget must be approved; the House can examine misuse of funds on the basis of the CAG's reports and the Public Accounts Committee.
4. **No-confidence motion**: the most powerful weapon, but NCERT says that when a government has a stable majority the power is 'fictional rather than real'; after 1989 several governments fell when coalition partners withdrew.

## Parliamentary committees

Parliament meets only in sessions, so committees do the detailed work. NCERT says India has developed a system of parliamentary standing committees since 1983; the **departmentally related standing committees** were set up in 1993 (Laxmikanth; verify). NCERT puts them at over twenty in number (verify the present number), and these committees examine each ministry's budget, expenditure and Bills. **Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs)** are set up for a particular Bill or to investigate financial irregularities, with members from both Houses. NCERT's judgement: committees have reduced Parliament's burden and Parliament rarely rejects their suggestions. (Verify the present number of standing committees; recommendations are advisory and not binding on the government.)

## The executive's instruments over the legislature

The Cabinet controls the agenda and drafting of bills; a disciplined majority passes them; members' private Bills seldom succeed; ordinances let the executive legislate between sessions; and the President's assent is required for every Bill. NCERT's Post Office Bill example (1986) shows the assent power has no time limit in the Constitution: the then President never decided, and the Bill effectively died.

## The honest limits

NCERT lists real weaknesses: declining sittings, absence of quorum, walkouts and boycotts which deprive the House of its power to question, and Bills drafted by the bureaucracy under minister supervision.

## Party discipline: a cross-link only

When a legislator who defies the party whip can lose the seat, the floor checks on a government with a majority weaken. That is the anti-defection law (the Tenth Schedule), taught in a later topic; here note only that it is one reason the legislature's control of the executive is weaker in practice than on paper.

### 5. The judiciary in the web: review, appointments, removal, money, tribunals and contempt

*Objective:* Show how the judiciary checks the other organs, how they check it, and why the collegium and tribunals raise separation-of-powers questions.

## Judiciary over legislature and executive: judicial review

**Judicial review** is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of laws and executive orders and declare them void if they violate the Constitution (Laxmikanth). India's Constitution itself confers it on the Supreme Court and High Courts (Articles 13, 32, 226 and others), and the Court has held it a basic feature, so even a constitutional amendment cannot remove it. In text the scope is narrower than in the USA ('procedure established by law'), though since Maneka Gandhi (1978) courts require fair, just and reasonable procedure under Article 21, test reasonableness of restrictions under Article 19 and arbitrariness under Article 14; courts do not judge the wisdom of policy (see GS-173 module 5). Quadri's three kinds: review of constitutional amendments, of legislation and subordinate legislation, and of administrative action. See GS-186 for jurisdiction.

## Legislature over judiciary

- **Removal**: a Supreme Court or High Court judge can be removed only for proven misbehaviour or incapacity, by a motion passed by a special majority in both Houses. NCERT's 1991 case against Justice V. Ramaswami shows how hard this is: the motion got two-thirds of those voting but not a majority of the whole House because one party abstained.
- **Amendment and jurisdiction**: NCERT recounts the 1967 to 1973 conflict over property, fundamental rights and Parliament's amending power, finally settled in principle by Kesavananda.
- **Money and talk**: judges' salaries are not put to legislative approval, and conduct cannot be discussed in Parliament except in a removal proceeding. These are protections, not checks.

## Executive over judiciary: appointments

NCERT says the Constitution made the President appoint judges after 'consulting' the CJI, so in practice the Council of Ministers had the final say. Convention that the senior-most judge becomes CJI was broken in 1973 (A. N. Ray) and January 1977 (M. H. Beg superseded H. R. Khanna); NCERT prints 1975, which is a slip (verify). Between 1982 and 1998 the Court turned 'consultation' into the principle of **collegiality**, giving greater weight to senior judges. The 99th Amendment and NJAC Act (2014) tried to replace it and were struck down in 2015 (Laxmikanth). **Accountability debate:** supporters say the collegium protects independence; critics say it is opaque and self-appointing. Link: GS-186, modules 2 and 8.

## Tribunals and contempt

**Tribunals** are specialised bodies, often staffed partly by administrators, to decide disputes (administrative, tax and others). They raise a separation question: when the executive's own officials adjudicate, is the judiciary-from-executive principle of Article 50 stretched? Articles 323A and 323B (added in 1976) authorise them; in *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) the Supreme Court held their decisions remain open to High Court review. *(Standard knowledge; verify. Laxmikanth cites the case only for a quote on judicial review.)* See GS-187, module 8, and GS-188 modules 2 to 5 (sourced from Laxmikanth chapter 35).

Contempt power protects judges from unfair criticism (NCERT) but critics ask whether it also chills legitimate criticism: the independence-accountability tension again.

### 6. Independent bodies as institutional checks: EC, CAG, UPSC and Lokpal in outline

*Objective:* Name the independent constitutional bodies Laxmikanth lists, explain what each checks, and distinguish them from statutory bodies.

## Why a fourth set of institutions

Laxmikanth says the Constitution creates, apart from the three organs, certain independent bodies as 'bulwarks' of democratic government. NCERT's concluding chapter also counts independent bodies like the Election Commission among the institutions that fragment power so no one organ dominates.

| Body | What it checks (source wording simplified) | Article (standard knowledge) |
|---|---|---|
| **Election Commission** | Ensures free and fair elections to Parliament, State legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President | 324 |
| **Comptroller and Auditor-General (CAG)** | Audits accounts of Centre and States; 'guardian of the public purse'; comments on legality and propriety of expenditure | 148 |
| **Union Public Service Commission** | Conducts exams for all-India and higher central services; advises the President on disciplinary matters | 315 |
| **State Public Service Commission** | The same for State services, advising the Governor | 315 |

Independence is secured through security of tenure, fixed service conditions and expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund. NCERT adds that members of public service commissions have a fixed term and describes removal as following an inquiry by a Supreme Court judge. The precise rule (Article 317; verify): for misbehaviour the President removes a member only after the Supreme Court, on reference, inquires and reports; other grounds (insolvency, outside paid employment, infirmity of mind or body: Article 317(3)) need no inquiry. NCERT also presents the UPSC as the device that protects merit-based, non-partisan civil service recruitment.

## How they connect to the three organs

The CAG's reports feed the **Public Accounts Committee** of Parliament (NCERT mentions the link), so the CAG helps the legislature check the executive's spending. The Election Commission checks the political executive's most dangerous temptation, manipulating elections. The UPSC checks patronage in the bureaucracy. None of them makes laws, runs ministries or decides private disputes, which is why they are called watchdogs outside the three organs.

## Lokpal in outline

The **Lokpal** is a statutory (not constitutional) anti-corruption body at the Centre, created by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 (standard knowledge; NCERT notes only that the earlier Lok Pal Bill failed to be enacted). Details of its working belong to a later topic; verify its current status.

## Traps

- NCERT calls the Election Commission an 'independent statutory body' in one line, but it is created by **Article 324 of the Constitution**, so in the exam treat it as a **constitutional body**. Statutory = created by an Act of Parliament.
- Constitutional bodies are not free from the question 'who appoints them?' Recent appointment-law changes for the Election Commission (the 2023 Act and the judgment before it) are post-2024 sensitive: verify before using in Mains.

### 7. Judicial activism, restraint, overreach, PIL and 'judicial legislation'

*Objective:* Define activism, restraint and overreach, explain how PIL works and its guard-rails, and judge the claim that 'judicial legislation' violates separation of powers.

## Definitions

- **Judicial review**: striking down unconstitutional laws and actions (module 5).
- **Judicial activism**: the proactive role of the judiciary in protecting rights and promoting justice, in effect pushing the other organs to perform their constitutional duties. Laxmikanth says it began in India in the mid-1970s, associated with Justices Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Chinnappa Reddy and Desai; the term was coined in 1947 by the American historian Arthur Schlesinger Jr.
- **Judicial restraint**: the self-control of the judiciary; it defers to the elected organs on policy.
- **Judicial overreach**: critics' label (not a precise legal term) for activism that moves from enforcing rights into running the administration or making policy.

Laxmikanth adds that activism is a form of judicial review in which judges also exercise policy preferences, and that PIL is the most popular form of activism.

## PIL: how it works

Traditionally only the person whose rights are violated can approach a court (**locus standi**). In a **Public Interest Litigation**, any public-spirited person or organisation with 'sufficient interest' can petition for the poor or disadvantaged, who cannot reach courts themselves. India took the idea from the USA of the 1960s and introduced it in the early 1980s. NCERT's early examples: *Hussainara Khatoon v Bihar* (1979, undertrial prisoners) and *Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration* (1980, a letter from Tihar). Justice Bhagwati in *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984) said the poor cannot enforce rights through an ordinary adversarial procedure.

**Guard-rails**: Laxmikanth lists categories ordinarily accepted (bonded labour, neglected children, prison harassment, environment, riot victims) and those not (landlord-tenant, service matters, admission to colleges, early-hearing requests). It also lists the Court's own cautions: the Court 'will not ordinarily transgress into a policy', petitions must be bona fide, credentials of the petitioner can be checked, and frivolous petitioners can be fined. (The Laxmikanth extract prints 1998 for the first guidelines but then says they were 'modified in 1993 and 2003', so the year is probably 1988 and looks like a printing slip; verify.)

## Benefits and costs (NCERT)

Benefits: wider access to courts, executive accountability, more meaningful rights, and electoral reforms such as candidates' affidavits. Costs: overburdened courts and a blurring of lines between the judiciary and the executive and legislature.

## 'Judicial legislation'

When courts issue detailed guidelines that fill a legal vacuum, critics call it **judicial legislation**, contrary to separation of powers. A balanced test (this is a study framework, not a source quotation): was a right at stake; was there executive or legislative inaction; is the order temporary and tied to constitutional text; and does it dictate resource-heavy policy choices? Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar's Constituent Assembly warning, quoted by NCERT, is that independence must not turn the judiciary into a super-legislature or super-executive. See GS-173 and GS-186.

### 8. Friction in practice: Governors' assent, ordinances, contempt and Delhi (verify every recent item)

*Objective:* Use a repeatable template to analyse current disputes between organs without relying on unverified details.

## Template for any current item

Write four lines: (1) which organ acted; (2) which organ or body checked it; (3) which constitutional principle was invoked; (4) what remains unresolved. Always date the event and add 'verify' when you are not certain.

## Assent and timelines

NCERT shows that the Constitution fixes no time limit for the President's assent to Bills (the 1986 Post Office Bill). The Governor's assent power (Article 200) raises the same worry for State Bills. In 2025 the Supreme Court dealt with prolonged inaction by a Governor in a Tamil Nadu case (State of Tamil Nadu v Governor, reported as 8 April 2025; verify) and the President then sought the Court's opinion under Article 143 on whether courts can fix timelines; the later advisory opinion (reported as November 2025; verify) rejected judicially fixed deadlines and 'deemed assent' while allowing limited review of unreasonable delay. **Everything in this paragraph is post-2024 and must be verified against the judgments.**

## Ordinances

An ordinance under Article 123 (Governor: Article 213) lets the executive legislate when the legislature is not sitting. The legislature must approve it within six weeks of reassembly or it lapses. In *D.C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar* (1987) the Court held that repeatedly re-promulgating ordinances without putting them before the legislature is improper. *(Standard knowledge; verify.)* In *Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar* (2017) the Court held that re-promulgation is a fraud on the Constitution and that ordinances are open to judicial review *(standard knowledge; verify)*. The debate: a safety valve or an executive shortcut that dilutes separation?

## Contempt

Contempt of court (Articles 129 and 215) shields the judiciary but sits uneasily with freedom of speech. Keep the NCERT line: independence does not mean absence of accountability.

## Delhi

The 2018 PYQ asks whether the July 2018 Supreme Court judgment can end the political tussle between the Lieutenant Governor and Delhi's elected government. As commonly summarised, a Constitution Bench held that the Lieutenant Governor must ordinarily act on the Council of Ministers' aid and advice, with limited exceptions. Later developments in 2023 on control over services changed the legal position again; both items are standard knowledge and **must be verified**. For Delhi's constitutional status see GS-200.

## Collegium and appointments friction

Disputes over pending collegium recommendations recur; do not cite numbers or names without a dated source.

### 9. How UPSC tests this topic: Prelims traps and PYQ patterns

*Objective:* Recognise the three families of questions (principle, institutional accountability, judicial role) and the traps in the Prelims versions.

## Prelims: concept, Article and statement questions

The 15 verified PYQs for this topic include five Prelims questions. They test: what 'constitutional government' means (2020, 2021), what judicial review implies (2017), which instrument enjoins the separation of judiciary from the executive (2020), and what safeguards liberty (2021). The approach is definitional: know the textbook term precisely.

**Traps: things that are NOT features of separation of powers in India**

- 'Ministers cannot be members of Parliament.' Wrong: they must be.
- 'India follows the American presidential model.' Wrong: it chose the parliamentary system.
- 'Article 50 is a Fundamental Right.' Wrong: it is a Directive Principle in Part IV.
- 'Judicial review lets courts examine Bills before the President signs them.' Wrong: review applies to enacted laws and executive orders.
- 'Judicial review includes reviewing the court's own earlier judgments.' Confusion with the Article 137 review power.
- 'Judicial activism and judicial review are identical.' They overlap; activism is a policy-leaning form of review.
- 'Independent bodies are statutory.' The Election Commission, CAG and UPSC are constitutional bodies.

## Mains: the three families

1. **Principle**: 'Is separation of powers strictly followed?' (2019, 2026). Structure: define, show overlap, show checks, conclude with India's 'functional separation'.
2. **Institutional accountability**: parliamentary control (2021 Q4), departmental committees (2021 Q15), judicial independence (2023), constitutional morality and the independence-accountability balance (2025).
3. **Judicial role**: judicial legislation and PIL (2020, 2024), India-UK judicial systems (2020 Q4) and the Delhi tussle (2018).

**Directive words**: *Comment*, *Explain*, *Examine*, *Evaluate*, *Justify* each ask for something different. 'Examine' needs both sides plus a verdict; 'Justify' asks you to defend a position with evidence.

## Cross-checking rules

For any statement ask: (1) which organ is acting; (2) what Article or source supports it; (3) is the claim absolute ('only', 'never', 'cannot')? Absolute words in separation-of-power statements are usually wrong because the whole doctrine is about overlap and checks.

## Thinking aloud: a Prelims question

*Question:* 'In India, separation of judiciary from the executive is enjoined by (a) the Preamble, (b) a Directive Principle of State Policy, (c) the Seventh Schedule, (d) the conventional practice.'

The Preamble states ideals and does not contain this; the Seventh Schedule divides law-making subjects between Centre and States; a convention is unwritten, but Article 50 is written. ## A Mains skeleton: 'Is separation of powers strictly followed in India?' (15 marks, 250 words)

- **Introduction (30 words)**: define separation of powers and Montesquieu; say India follows neither rigid American separation nor British fusion.
- **Body 1, overlap (60 words)**: ministers sit in Parliament; ordinances; tribunals; Parliament's removal powers.
- **Body 2, separation (60 words)**: judicial independence safeguards; Article 50; basic structure (Kesavananda); Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain.
- **Body 3, checks (60 words)**: no-confidence and committees; judicial review; independent bodies such as the CAG and Election Commission.
- **Body 4, strains (30 words)**: judicial legislation debate; the collegium; ordinance and assent disputes.
- **Conclusion (10 words)**: 'India practises functional separation with checks and balances, not watertight compartments.'

### Notes: Key terms in one place

- Separation of powers: the doctrine that legislative, executive and judicial power should rest with different organs so no one body can make, enforce and judge the law.
- Checks and balances: powers given to each organ to review, restrain or correct the others so that no organ dominates.
- Fusion of powers: the executive is drawn from the legislature and answers to it (the parliamentary model).
- Constitutional government / limited government: government that acts within the limits set by a constitution and can be restrained when it exceeds them.
- Judicial review: the power of the judiciary to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders and declare them void (ultra vires) if they violate the Constitution (Laxmikanth).
- Locus standi: the traditional rule that only the person whose rights are infringed may move the court; PIL relaxes it.
- Judicial activism: the proactive role of the judiciary in protecting rights and promoting justice, pushing the other organs to perform their duties; judicial restraint is its opposite (self-control).
- Judicial overreach: a critics' label for activism that enters the domain of policy and administration; not a precise legal category.
- Basic structure: features of the Constitution that Parliament cannot alter under Article 368 (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).

### Notes: Cases and judgments in context

- Marbury v Madison (1803, USA): origin of judicial review (Laxmikanth).
- Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab (1955): the Constitution does not recognise strict separation of powers, though functions are differentiated. Standard knowledge; verify.
- Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973): Parliament cannot alter the basic structure; right to property not part of it; the Court reserves to itself the decision on what is basic structure (NCERT).
- Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975): applied basic structure to an amendment; as commonly summarised, struck down the clause that put the Prime Minister's election beyond the courts. Verify.

### Notes: Cause and effect: how PIL and activism grew

- Cause: ordinary litigation required a personally aggrieved party, leaving the poor and unorganised without access to courts.
- Step: around 1979 the Supreme Court relaxed locus standi and began hearing petitions filed on behalf of others.
- Effect 1: rights widened (clean air and water, decent living) and access democratised.
- Effect 2: executive accountability rose (directions to investigating agencies; candidates' affidavits).
- Effect 3: courts overburdened; lines between judiciary and the executive and legislature blurred.
- Counter-reaction: the Court's own guidelines against misuse and its caution not to transgress into policy.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- PIL versus judicial activism: PIL is a procedure; activism is the posture of which PIL is the most important vehicle.
- Judicial review versus judicial activism: review is the power to test laws against the Constitution; activism is the assertive use of that and related powers, with policy preferences.
- Independence versus accountability: independence shields judges from the other organs; accountability is answerability to the Constitution, democratic traditions and the people (NCERT).
- Separation of powers versus division of powers: the first divides functions among organs; the second divides subjects between Centre and States in federalism.
- Constitutional versus statutory body: created by the Constitution versus created by an Act. NCERT's loose use of 'statutory' for the Election Commission is a trap.
- Review of a law (judicial review) versus review of a judgment (Article 137 power of the Supreme Court).
- Parliamentary supremacy (UK) versus supremacy of the Constitution (India).

### Notes: Where India is more and less separated

- Strongest separation: the judiciary, through tenure, finance and Article 50.
- Weakest separation: executive and legislature, which are fused by parliamentary design.
- Executive legislating: ordinances and delegated rule-making.
- Legislature adjudicating: removal of judges, impeachment, breach of privilege.
- Judiciary governing: PIL directions and monitoring of agencies, which is why 'judicial legislation' is debated.
- No organ is above the Constitution: even Parliament's amending power is limited by the basic structure.
