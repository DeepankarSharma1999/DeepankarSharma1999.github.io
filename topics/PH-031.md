## PH-031: Relativistic Doppler effect

Physics Paper I · Physics · Special relativity

### 1. Orientation: what a Doppler shift is and the conventions used

*Objective:* State what the Doppler effect is, set the frames, signs and angle conventions used in every module, and see where this lesson reuses PH-028 to PH-030.

## The question

A source sends out a steady wave of frequency f measured in the source's own rest frame: a star emitting a hydrogen line, or a whistle. An observer who moves relative to the source records a different frequency f′. This change is the **Doppler effect**. For sound it is school physics. For light it needs the relativity of PH-028 to PH-030, because light has no medium and its speed is c for every observer. This lesson derives the light formula by two routes, treats the transverse case, and solves the three verified past questions (2018, 2022, 2024). One of those three is about sound, not light.

## What is reused and not re-derived

- Lorentz transformation and its inverse: PH-028 modules 4 and 5.
- Time dilation Δt = γ Δt₀ and the rule that proper time is read by *one clock present at both events*: PH-029 module 3.
- Aberration, cosθ′ = (cosθ − β)/(1 − β cosθ): PH-030 module 6. Module 5 below shows that it comes out of the same calculation as the Doppler law.

## Words to know first

- **Frequency f**: crests passing a point per second, in hertz (Hz). **Period T** = 1/f. **Wavelength λ**: crest-to-crest distance; for light λf = c.
- **Redshift**: the observed wavelength is longer (frequency lower). **Blueshift**: shorter (frequency higher).
- **Longitudinal Doppler effect**: relative motion along the line joining source and observer. **Transverse**: relative motion at right angles to the line of sight (module 5).
- **z**: the fractional wavelength change (module 6).

## Conventions (same as PH-028 to PH-030)

S is the **source's rest frame** and S′ is the **observer's rest frame**. S′ moves at +v along +x of S, axes parallel and right-handed, origins coinciding at t = t′ = 0. β = v/c and γ = 1/√(1 − β²). The wave has frequency f in S and f′ in S′. The angle θ is the direction of propagation of the light measured in S from +x, and θ′ the same direction measured in S′ (as in PH-030 module 6). **Recession** means the observer moves away from the source along the light's path (θ = 0, light chasing the observer); **approach** is θ = 180°. Light has no medium, so only the relative motion matters: 'source receding at v' and 'observer receding at v' give the same f′ (module 3).

### 2. Foundation: the classical (sound) Doppler effect and what light changes

*Objective:* Derive the classical Doppler formula for sound for a moving source and a moving observer and list the three ways light must differ.

## Sound needs a medium

Sound moves at c_s relative to the air (about 340 m/s; the 2022 question uses 380 m/s). All speeds below are relative to the air. The source emits f.

## Source moving away, observer at rest in the air

In one period T = 1/f the crest travels c_s T from the source's position at emission, but the source has moved a further v_s T away. So the spacing of crests that reach an observer behind it is λ′ = (c_s + v_s)T. These crests move at c_s past the observer, so

$$ f′ = c_s/λ′ = f c_s/(c_s + v_s) $$

## Observer moving away, source at rest in the air

The crest spacing is unchanged, λ = c_s/f, but the observer retreats and meets crests at relative speed c_s − v_o:

$$ f′ = (c_s − v_o)/λ = f (c_s − v_o)/c_s $$

## Both at once

$$ f′ = f (c_s − v_o)/(c_s + v_s) $$

Here v_o is the observer's speed *away from* the source and v_s the source's speed *away from* the observer, both measured relative to the air. For approach, make the speed negative. Brijlal eq. 11.16 writes the same law in the other sign convention (check the printed definitions of its symbols before using it).

## Three things light must change

1. **Source-moving and observer-moving differ for sound.** At v/c_s = 0.5: observer retreating gives 0.5, source retreating gives 1/1.5 = 0.667. The medium picks a special frame. For light there is none, so there can be only one answer.
2. **The wave speed relative to the observer** is c_s − v_o for sound but stays c for light in every frame (PH-030 module 3).
3. **Transverse motion**: classically there is no shift for a source circling a listener at its centre (Brijlal). For light there is one (module 5).

## First-order agreement

For v ≪ c_s both sound results become f(1 − v/c_s), because 1/(1 + x) = 1 − x + x² − …. So the light formula must also reduce to f(1 − β) when β ≪ 1 (checked in module 6).

## Dimensional check

(c_s − v_o)/(c_s + v_s) is a ratio of speeds, a pure number, so f′ has the units of f.

### 3. Core: Route A, time dilation plus the stretching of the pulse gap

*Objective:* Derive f′ = f√((1 − β)/(1 + β)) for recession from the pulse-gap spacing in S and time dilation, and from Mathur's use of the Lorentz transformation.

## Set-up and assumptions

The source sits at the origin of S and emits pulses (crests) a proper period T₀ = 1/f apart: pulse 1 at t = 0 and pulse 2 at t = T₀. The observer O′ is at rest in S′, which moves at +v along +x of S, so O′ moves away from the source; the light travels along +x chasing him. O′ is at x = x₀ > 0 at t = 0. Everything in steps 1 and 2 is measured in S.

## Step 1: the gap between receptions in S

Pulse 1 is at x = ct and reaches O′ at time t₁, so ct₁ = x₀ + vt₁. Pulse 2 is at x = c(t − T₀) and reaches him at t₂, so c(t₂ − T₀) = x₀ + vt₂. Subtract:

$$ c(t₂ − t₁) − cT₀ = v(t₂ − t₁), so Δt = t₂ − t₁ = T₀/(1 − β) $$

Pulse 2 starts a distance cT₀ behind pulse 1 and closes on the retreating observer at rate c − v (both numbers are S-measurements, so the subtraction is legitimate here).

## Step 2: time dilation

Both receptions happen at the same place in S′ (at O′), so his own clock reads proper time T′, and PH-029 module 3 gives Δt = γT′. Hence T′ = Δt/γ = T₀/[γ(1 − β)].

## Step 3: simplify

γ(1 − β) = (1 − β)/√((1 − β)(1 + β)) = √((1 − β)/(1 + β)). So T′ = T₀√((1 + β)/(1 − β)) and, with f′ = 1/T′,

$$ f′ = f √((1 − β)/(1 + β))  (recession),   f′ = f √((1 + β)/(1 − β))  (approach, β → −β) $$

In wavelength, λ′ = c/f′ = λ√((1 + β)/(1 − β)) for recession. Step 1 alone, without the dilation, would give f(1 − β): the classical observer-moving result of module 2. **The relativistic law is that result multiplied by γ.**

## Mathur's version (the photon-pulse route)

Mathur §3.7.6 sends pulses from the origin of S at t = 0 and t = T. The receptions are at one point of S′, so Δx′ = 0 and Δt′ = T′. The inverse Lorentz transformation (PH-028 module 5) gives Δx = γvT′ and Δt = γT′. Pulse 2 leaves T later and must cover the extra distance Δx at speed c, so Δt = T + Δx/c, that is γT′ = T + γβT′, giving T′ = T/[γ(1 − β)]: the same result. Mathur takes v as positive when the observer is receding.

## Why moving the source gives the same law

If the source recedes from a stationary observer, step into the source's frame: the observer recedes at the same relative speed, so f′ is the same. For sound this argument fails because the air frame is special.

## Checks

- Dimensions: β is a pure number, so f′ and f have the same units.
- β = 0 gives f′ = f. β → 1 gives f′ → 0.
- First order: f′ ≈ f(1 − β), the classical value (module 2).

### 4. Core: Route B, the invariant wave phase and the Lorentz transformation

*Objective:* Derive the Doppler law a second way by requiring the phase ωt − k·x to be the same in S and S′, and read the result as a transformation of (ω/c, k).

## The idea

The phase of a plane wave counts crests. Whether a given event is 'on a crest' cannot depend on who looks, so the phase φ = ωt − kx has the same value in S and S′ at every event. The Lorentz transformation turns that equality into the Doppler law. Assumptions: a plane wave in vacuum with ω = kc and ω = 2πf, and the two frames of module 1.

## Wave moving along +x in S

In S, φ = ωt − kx = ω(t − x/c). Put in the inverse Lorentz transformation (PH-028 module 5), t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²) and x = γ(x′ + vt′):

$$ t − x/c = γ[t′ + vx′/c² − x′/c − vt′/c] $$

Collect t′: t′(1 − v/c) = t′(1 − β). Collect x′: x′(v/c² − 1/c) = −(x′/c)(1 − β). So

$$ t − x/c = γ(1 − β)(t′ − x′/c),   φ = ω γ(1 − β)(t′ − x′/c) $$

This has the same form as the S wave, φ = ω′(t′ − x′/c), moving at +x′ with speed c (the second postulate shows up as the *same* form, with no new speed). Therefore

$$ ω′ = γ(1 − β) ω,   f′ = f √((1 − β)/(1 + β)) $$

using γ(1 − β) = √((1 − β)/(1 + β)) from module 3. It is the same law as Route A, now with no pulse-chasing picture at all.

## Reading it as a transformation

Write k_x = ω/c for this wave. The pair (ω/c, k_x) transforms like (ct, x) of PH-028:

$$ ω′/c = γ(ω/c − β k_x),   k′_x = γ(k_x − β ω/c) $$

With k_x = ω/c, the first gives ω′ = γ(1 − β)ω, and the second gives k′_x = γ(1 − β)ω/c = ω′/c, so ω′ = c k′_x, as required for light. This (ω/c, k) pattern is a wave four-vector; its general treatment is a later topic, **to come, not yet written**, and is used here only as a pattern.

## Why this route is worth having

1. It makes no assumption about the observer, the pulses or a clock; one invariant does all the work.
2. It handles **any direction** at once (module 5) and yields the aberration law as a by-product.
3. It makes clear that the Doppler shift and aberration are two parts of one transformation of the wave.

## Checks

- Dimensions: ωt and kx are pure numbers (radians); β is dimensionless.
- Limit β → 0: with t ≈ t′ and x = x′ + vt′, t − x/c = (1 − β)t′ − x′/c, so the t′ coefficient is ω(1 − β) to first order.
- Sign: for light going the other way (−x), the cross term changes sign, giving γ(1 + β): approach (module 5).

### 5. Core: any direction, the link to aberration, and the transverse effect

*Objective:* Derive f′ for a general propagation angle in both frame forms, tie it to the PH-030 aberration law, and state the two transverse cases with the frame of each right angle.

## General angle from the invariant phase

Let the wave in S travel at angle θ from +x: φ = ω[t − (x cosθ + y sinθ)/c]. Substitute t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²), x = γ(x′ + vt′), y = y′:

$$ t − (x cosθ + y sinθ)/c = γ(1 − β cosθ) t′ − γ(cosθ − β) x′/c − y′ sinθ/c $$

(the t′ coefficient is γ − γβ cosθ; the x′ coefficient is γβ/c − γ cosθ/c.) In S′ the wave is φ = ω′[t′ − (x′ cosθ′ + y′ sinθ′)/c]. Match coefficients:

$$ ω′ = γω(1 − β cosθ),   ω′ cosθ′ = γω(cosθ − β),   ω′ sinθ′ = ω sinθ $$

Dividing the second by the first gives cosθ′ = (cosθ − β)/(1 − β cosθ), and the third divided by the first gives sinθ′ = sinθ/[γ(1 − β cosθ)]: exactly the aberration law of PH-030 module 6 (derived there by velocity addition). Doppler shift and aberration are two parts of one result.

$$ f′ = γ f (1 − β cosθ) = f/[γ(1 + β cosθ′)] $$

The two forms agree: 1 + β cosθ′ = (1 − β cosθ + β cosθ − β²)/(1 − β cosθ) = (1 − β²)/(1 − β cosθ), so γ(1 + β cosθ′) = 1/[γ(1 − β cosθ)]. **The first form uses the angle in the source frame S; the second uses the angle in the observer frame S′.** Mixing the two is the most common error. Limits: θ = 0 gives recession and θ = 180° gives approach, and there θ′ = θ.

## The two transverse cases

- **Case A, right angle in the observer's frame** (θ′ = 90°): cosθ′ = 0 gives cosθ = β, so f′ = f/γ, a **redshift**. Physically, the light is emitted at the moment of closest approach as the observer sees it, and the moving source's clock is slow in S′. Mathur's n′ = n√(1 − β²) and Brijlal's eq. 11.18 describe this case; Mathur's wording (right angles to the motion) implies the observer's frame without saying so, while Brijlal's 'line through receiver and source' fixes neither the frame nor the instant (flagged).
- **Case B, right angle in the source's frame** (θ = 90°): f′ = γ f, a **blueshift**, and cosθ′ = −β, so θ′ is more than 90°. The observer, who moves in S, has the slow clock.

| Right angle in | f′/f | The other frame's angle (β = 0.6) |
|---|---|---|
| observer frame S′ (θ′ = 90°) | 1/γ = 0.8 | θ = arccos β = 53.13° |
| source frame S (θ = 90°) | γ = 1.25 | θ′ = arccos(−β) = 126.87° |

## What 'time dilation causes it' really means

Write f′ = f × γ × (1 − β cosθ): a time-dilation factor times a geometric (crest-spacing) factor. The geometric factor is 1 only in the frame where the light is perpendicular to the relative velocity. In that frame the shift is pure time dilation of the clock that moves in that frame, so the sign depends on which clock moves. 'The transverse effect is time dilation' is true, but it does not say whether the shift is red or blue until the frame of the right angle is named.

### 6. Core: limits, redshift z and astrophysical numbers

*Objective:* Expand the law for β ≪ 1 to second order, define z and invert it for β, and compute shifts for stars and galaxies.

## Small β

Write f′/f = (1 − β)/√(1 − β²) ≈ (1 − β)(1 + β²/2), so

$$ f′/f = 1 − β + β²/2 − β³/2 + …  (recession) $$

(checked by series in Python). The first-order term is the classical −β. The second-order term is +β²/2. Classically the observer-moving law has second-order term 0 and the source-moving law 1/(1 + β) = 1 − β + β² has +β²; the relativistic coefficient is exactly half way. Indeed

$$ (f′/f)² = (1 − β) × 1/(1 + β) $$

so **the relativistic factor is the geometric mean of the two classical factors**. At β = 0.5: observer-moving 0.5, source-moving 0.667, relativistic 0.577. For the transverse cases the first-order term is absent: 1/γ ≈ 1 − β²/2, so the shift is second-order only (β = 10⁻⁴ gives about 5 × 10⁻⁹).

## Red and blue, and z

Define the redshift z by λ′ = λ(1 + z), that is z = (λ′ − λ)/λ. For recession (module 3) λ′/λ = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)), so

$$ 1 + z = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)),   β = [(1 + z)² − 1]/[(1 + z)² + 1] $$

(inversion: (1 + z)²(1 − β) = 1 + β, so β[(1 + z)² + 1] = (1 + z)² − 1). Approach gives z < 0 (blueshift). Values:

| z | β = v/c | f′/f = 1/(1 + z) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.0950 | 0.909 |
| 0.5 | 0.3846 | 0.667 |
| 1.0 | 0.6000 | 0.500 |
| 2.0 | 0.8000 | 0.333 |

So z = β only for small β: at z = 1 the speed is 0.6c, not c.

## Astrophysical numbers (all recomputed in Python)

1. A binary-star component receding at 30 km/s emits Hα (656.1 nm): β = 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ and Δλ ≈ 656.1 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.0656 nm. The second-order correction is about 5 × 10⁻⁹ of λ, utterly negligible.
2. A star wobbling at 10 m/s gives β = 3.33 × 10⁻⁸ and Δλ = 656.1 nm × 3.33 × 10⁻⁸ = 2.19 × 10⁻⁵ nm (0.0219 pm): why such shifts need extreme spectrograph stability.
3. A galaxy shows Hα at 700 nm: 1 + z = 700/656.1 = 1.0669, z = 0.0669, β = 0.0647, v = 1.94 × 10⁴ km/s with c = 3.00 × 10⁵ km/s.
4. A line whose wavelength doubles has 1 + z = 2 and β = 0.6.

## A caution about galaxies

Mathur and Brijlal read galaxy redshifts as Doppler recession, supporting an expanding universe. Modern cosmology describes the redshift of distant galaxies as the expansion of space, with general relativity needed beyond small z; the special-relativity β is then only a convenient equivalent number (verify against a cosmology text; not examined in these extracts). Mathur's empirical v = ar and his age and radius of the universe figures are not used here.

## Checks

β, z and f′/f are pure numbers; z = 0 for no shift; z → ∞ as β → 1; β < 1 for every finite z.

### 7. Master: Ives–Stilwell, the trap list and the source slips

*Objective:* Explain what the Ives–Stilwell average tests (flagged: verify), list the traps, and know the printed slips in the sources.

## Ives–Stilwell (flagged: verify details against a reference)

Mathur describes a beam of hydrogen atoms, moving at about 0.005c, whose spectral line is viewed in the forward direction and, by a mirror, in the backward direction, together with the line from atoms at rest. With λ₀ the rest wavelength, forward (atoms approaching the spectrometer) gives λ_F = λ₀√((1 − β)/(1 + β)) and backward gives λ_B = λ₀√((1 + β)/(1 − β)). Their mean is

$$ λ_av = λ₀[(1 − β) + (1 + β)]/[2√(1 − β²)] = γ λ₀ $$

so the mean shift is λ₀(γ − 1) ≈ λ₀β²/2. The first-order terms ±β cancel in the average, leaving a **second-order shift in the same direction for either sign of v**: it tests the time-dilation factor γ rather than the first-order Doppler shift. Number check, assuming λ₀ = 4861 Å (Hβ; Mathur does not name the line, and standard accounts use Hβ, verify) and β = 0.005: 0.0608 Å. Mathur quotes a calculated 0.072 Å and an observed 0.074 Å; 0.072 Å corresponds to β ≈ 0.0054, consistent with his 'about 0.005c'. Dates: Mathur gives 1941 and Brijlal 1938; standard accounts mention papers from both years (verify). Brijlal calls the experiment a proof of the transverse effect; strictly it is a test of the second-order term.

## The trap list

1. **Sign of v.** Mathur takes v positive when receding, and so does Brijlal's light formula (eq. 11.17); only Brijlal's sound formula (eq. 11.16) takes v positive for approach. Always check: receding must give redshift (lower f′, longer λ′).
2. **Frame of the angle.** θ is in the source frame, θ′ in the observer frame; the transverse result is f/γ if the right angle is in the observer's frame and γf if it is in the source's frame (module 5).
3. **'Time dilation causes the transverse effect.'** Only the time-dilation factor remains when the geometric factor is 1, and which clock is dilated depends on the frame of the right angle (module 5).
4. **Sound versus light.** Sound uses the medium formulas of module 2; the relativistic law is for light, which has no medium.
5. **First-order shortcut.** Use f(1 − β) only when β is small enough that the error is tolerable; at β = 0.05 the wavelength difference is about 0.13 per cent.
6. **Wavelength versus frequency.** λ′/λ = f/f′ = inverse of the frequency ratio.
7. **z = β** only for small β (module 6).

## upsc-2022-physics-optional-i-q1-c (10 marks): whistle of a train, 108 km/h, drop of 350 Hz, c_s = 380 m/s

### Notes: Definitions

- Doppler effect: the change of observed frequency of a wave because of relative motion of source and observer.
- Redshift z: z = (λ′ − λ)/λ, so λ′ = λ(1 + z); positive for recession, negative for approach.
- Longitudinal Doppler effect: relative motion along the line of sight. Transverse: right angle to the relative velocity in a stated frame.
- Proper frequency: the frequency in the source's rest frame (called f here); f′ is the observer-frame value.
- Convention: S is the source frame, S′ the observer frame, moving at +v along +x of S; θ is in S and θ′ is in S′.

### Notes: Key equations

- Classical sound: f′ = f(c_s − v_o)/(c_s + v_s), speeds relative to the air, v_o and v_s positive away.
- Recession (light): f′ = f√((1 − β)/(1 + β)); approach: f′ = f√((1 + β)/(1 − β)).
- Wavelength: λ′ = λ√((1 + β)/(1 − β)) for recession.
- General angle: f′ = γ f(1 − β cosθ) with θ in the source frame, or f′ = f/[γ(1 + β cosθ′)] with θ′ in the observer frame.
- Transverse: f′ = f/γ if the right angle is in the observer's frame (θ′ = 90°); f′ = γ f if it is in the source's frame (θ = 90°).
- Redshift: 1 + z = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)) and β = [(1 + z)² − 1]/[(1 + z)² + 1].
- Small β: f′/f = 1 − β + β²/2 − β³/2 + …

### Notes: Derivation skeletons (modules 3, 4, 5)

- Route A: gap in S between receptions Δt = T₀/(1 − β); the receptions are at one place in S′ so Δt = γT′; T′ = T₀/[γ(1 − β)] = T₀√((1 + β)/(1 − β)).
- Mathur's route: Δx = γvT′ and Δt = γT′ from the inverse Lorentz transformation, plus Δt = T + Δx/c, give T′ = T/[γ(1 − β)].
- Route B: phase ω(t − x/c) with t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²) and x = γ(x′ + vt′) becomes γ(1 − β)ω(t′ − x′/c).
- General angle: match coefficients of t′, x′, y′ to get ω′ = γω(1 − β cosθ), ω′ cosθ′ = γω(cosθ − β), ω′ sinθ′ = ω sinθ; the ratio gives the PH-030 aberration law.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Frame of the angle: θ is a source-frame angle and θ′ an observer-frame angle; the transverse shift is red or blue according to which one is 90°.
- Sound versus light: sound has a medium frame (source-moving differs from observer-moving); light has none.
- Doppler shift versus aberration: frequency changes (Doppler) and direction changes (aberration) are two parts of one transformation of the wave.
- z versus β: z = β only for small β.
- Transverse effect versus time dilation: time dilation is the whole shift only when the light is perpendicular in the frame under discussion, and then the sign depends on which clock moves.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- β → 0: f′ → f with first-order shift ∓β; the transverse shift is only second order.
- β → 1 recession: f′ → 0 and z → ∞; approach: f′ → ∞.
- Cosmological redshifts of distant galaxies are described in modern cosmology by expansion of space; the Doppler β is only an equivalent number there (verify).
- A moving medium and gravitational effects are not covered in this lesson.

### Core concepts

- Light has no medium, so the Doppler shift depends only on relative motion.
- Relativistic Doppler factor = time dilation (γ) times a geometric crest-spacing factor (1 − β cosθ).
- The wave phase is invariant and its transformation gives both the Doppler shift and aberration.
