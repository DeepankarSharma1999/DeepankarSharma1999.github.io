### Bridge: Radians, sine and cosine, and their derivatives

SHM is written with sine and cosine functions, so five facts are needed.

- **Radian.** An angle in radians is arc length divided by radius, so a full turn is 2π rad and 180° is π rad. Every formula in this lesson uses radians; a calculator must be set to radian mode.
- **Periodicity.** cos θ and sin θ repeat when θ increases by 2π. The function cos(ωt + φ) therefore repeats when ωt increases by 2π, which happens after a time T = 2π/ω.
- **Derivatives.** With ω and φ constant, d/dt cos(ωt + φ) = −ω sin(ωt + φ) and d/dt sin(ωt + φ) = ω cos(ωt + φ). The chain rule brings out a factor ω each time.
- **Identities.** sin²θ + cos²θ = 1; cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2; sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2.
- **Average over whole cycles.** The average of cos 2θ (or sin 2θ) over a whole number of periods is zero, because positive and negative halves cancel. Hence the average of cos²θ and of sin²θ over a cycle is ½.

Phase relations follow from cos(θ + π/2) = −sin θ and cos(θ + π) = −cos θ.

### Bridge: What it means to solve a differential equation and to fix initial conditions

An equation such as d²x/dt² = −ω²x relates a function x(t) to its own second derivative. To **solve** it is to find functions x(t) that satisfy it for every t.

**Check by substitution.** Propose x = cos ωt. Then dx/dt = −ω sin ωt and d²x/dt² = −ω² cos ωt = −ω² x, so the equation holds. The function x = sin ωt works too, and so does any combination x = a cos ωt + b sin ωt with constants a and b. This is also the general solution: a second-order equation always has two independent constants.

**Initial conditions fix the constants.** The two numbers are usually the position and velocity at t = 0, x(0) = x₀ and v(0) = v₀. For x = a cos ωt + b sin ωt the position at t = 0 is a, and the velocity at t = 0 is ωb, so a = x₀ and b = v₀/ω. Two conditions, two unknowns. The same motion can be written as A cos(ωt + φ); A and φ are then the two constants (module 3).

Whenever a formula is claimed to solve an equation, the habit to build is: differentiate twice, put it back, and see whether both sides agree.

### 1. Orientation: what SHM is asked, and why it appears in Q1

### 2. Foundation: vocabulary, the definition of SHM and why small oscillations are SHM

*Objective:* Define period, frequency, angular frequency, amplitude and equilibrium, define SHM by F = −kx, and explain why any small oscillation about a stable equilibrium is SHM.

**Periodic motion** repeats at regular intervals. The smallest repeat time is the **period T** (SI unit second); the **frequency** is ν = 1/T in hertz (1 Hz = 1 cycle per second). The **angular frequency** is ω = 2π/T = 2πν, in rad/s. **Oscillatory motion** is periodic motion to and fro about an **equilibrium position**, the place where the net force is zero. Every oscillation is periodic but not every periodic motion is an oscillation: circular motion is periodic and not oscillatory (NCERT 13.2). The word 'displacement' is used generally: it can be a distance, an angle, or even a charge or voltage (NCERT 13.2.2).

**Definition (force form).** A particle of mass m moves on a line, with x measured from the equilibrium position. It executes SHM if the net force is proportional to the displacement and directed towards the equilibrium:

$$ F = −k x ,    k > 0 $$

Newton's second law gives m d²x/dt² = −kx, so the **acceleration form** is

$$ d²x/dt² = −ω² x ,    ω² = k/m $$

**Units and dimensions.** k is in N/m, so ω² = k/m has dimensions of T⁻²: (N/m)/kg = s⁻². ✓

**Why the definition is so useful (Mathur 7.2).** A particle in a stable equilibrium sits at a minimum of its potential energy U(x). Expand U about the minimum x = 0 in a Taylor series: U = U₀ + C₁x + ½C₂x² + ..., where the linear term vanishes at a minimum (the force is zero) and C₂ > 0. For small x the higher terms are negligible, so U ≈ U₀ + ½C₂x², a parabola, and the force F = −dU/dx = −C₂x is of the SHM form with k = C₂. **Therefore small oscillations about almost any stable equilibrium are simple harmonic.** The condition 'small' matters: for large amplitude the anharmonic (higher-order) terms change the period (module 7).

**What is not SHM.** A ball bouncing on the ground is periodic and its height–time graph is made of parabolic arcs, not a sinusoid (NCERT 13.2). Mathur's introduction says 'every oscillatory motion is simple harmonic'; that statement is too strong, as the bouncing ball and the large-swing pendulum show.

**Two equivalent definitions (NCERT 13.6).** Either F = −kx, or x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ). Going from the second to the first needs two differentiations; going the other way needs two integrations, with two constants.

### 3. Core: solving d²x/dt² = −ω²x and fixing the constants from initial conditions

*Objective:* Derive x = A cos(ωt + φ) from the equation of motion by the energy-first-integral method, relate A and φ to x₀ and v₀, and show that T is independent of amplitude.

**Setting.** A mass m moves along the x-axis with origin at the equilibrium and x positive to the right. The only force is F = −kx. The equation of motion is d²x/dt² = −ω²x with ω² = k/m. The boundary condition used below is that the speed is zero at the turning point x = A > 0.

**Step 1: first integral.** Multiply both sides by 2 dx/dt: 2 (dx/dt)(d²x/dt²) = −2ω² x (dx/dt). Each side is a time derivative: d/dt[(dx/dt)²] = −ω² d/dt[x²]. Integrating over t:

$$ (dx/dt)² + ω² x² = C $$

At the turning point x = A, dx/dt = 0, so C = ω²A². Hence

$$ v² = ω²(A² − x²) ,    v = ± ω √(A² − x²) $$

This already shows |x| ≤ A (v² cannot be negative) and v_max = ωA at x = 0.

**Step 2: second integration.** Take the minus sign, which describes the half cycle in which x is decreasing (0 < ωt + φ < π): dx/dt = −ω√(A² − x²), so −dx/√(A² − x²) = ω dt. Integrating, cos⁻¹(x/A) = ωt + φ, where φ is the second constant. Therefore

$$ x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) $$

The plus sign gives the other half cycle, where x is increasing. The substitution check in Step 3 confirms that x = A cos(ωt + φ) holds for all t.

**Step 3: check by substitution.** dx/dt = −Aω sin(ωt + φ); d²x/dt² = −Aω² cos(ωt + φ) = −ω²x ✓.

**Equivalent forms.** x = a cos ωt + b sin ωt with A = √(a² + b²), a = A cos φ and b = −A sin φ. Mathur and Upadhyaya write x = a sin(ωt + φ′); the two forms agree if φ′ = φ + π/2.

**Period.** x repeats when ωt increases by 2π, so

$$ T = 2π/ω = 2π √(m/k) $$

The period does not depend on A or φ (isochronism); this is the defining feature of SHM and is stated in NCERT's points to ponder 7.

**Initial conditions.** At t = 0, x₀ = A cos φ and v₀ = −Aω sin φ. Squaring and adding (the identity of bridge module B1):

$$ A = √(x₀² + v₀²/ω²) ,    φ = atan2(−v₀/ω, x₀) $$

Do not use φ = cos⁻¹(x₀/A) alone: it does not fix the sign of φ. The sign of v₀ settles the quadrant. (Upadhyaya's footnote writes φ = sin⁻¹(x₀/a), which has the same two-valued ambiguity.)

**Special cases.** Released from rest at x₀ > 0: v₀ = 0, A = x₀, φ = 0. Struck from the equilibrium with velocity v₀ > 0: x₀ = 0, A = v₀/ω, φ = −π/2, so x = (v₀/ω) sin ωt.

**Number.** x₀ = 3 cm, v₀ = 40 cm/s, ω = 10 s⁻¹: A = √(9 + 16) = 5 cm, cos φ = 0.6, sin φ = −0.8, φ = −0.927 rad. Check: x(0) = 5 × 0.6 = 3 ✓; v(0) = −5 × 10 × (−0.8) = +40 ✓.

### 4. Core: velocity, acceleration, phase, the reference circle and the (x, v) phase diagram

*Objective:* Differentiate x(t) to find v and a, state the phase relations, connect SHM to uniform circular motion, and draw and interpret the (x, v) phase diagram.

**Velocity and acceleration** (differentiate x = A cos(ωt + φ) with respect to t):

$$ v = −ωA sin(ωt + φ) ,    a = −ω²A cos(ωt + φ) = −ω² x $$

Written against position: v = ±ω√(A² − x²) (module 3) and a = −ω²x. The largest speed is v_max = ωA, at x = 0; the largest acceleration magnitude is a_max = ω²A, at x = ±A.

**Phase.** The whole argument θ = ωt + φ is the **phase** of the motion; φ is the **phase constant** (the phase at t = 0). Using cos(θ + π/2) = −sin θ and cos(θ + π) = −cos θ: v = ωA cos(θ + π/2), so the **velocity leads the displacement by π/2**; a = ω²A cos(θ + π), so the **acceleration leads by π** (it is exactly opposite to x). Two SHMs with the same ω are in phase if their phases differ by 0 or 2π, and in antiphase if they differ by π.

**Reference circle (NCERT 13.4).** A point P moves uniformly on a circle of radius A with angular speed ω, starting at angle φ to the +x axis. Its position vector makes angle ωt + φ at time t, and the projection on the x-axis is x = A cos(ωt + φ): the projection executes SHM. The projection of P's velocity (tangential, magnitude ωA) gives v, and the projection of the centripetal acceleration (ω²A toward the centre) gives a = −ω²x. The force on the shadow, however, is −kx, which is different in nature from the centripetal force on P.

**Quarter-cycle table** (A = 0.10 m, ω = 5 s⁻¹, φ = 0):

| ωt | x (m) | v (m/s) | a (m/s²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | +0.10 | 0 | −2.5 |
| π/2 | 0 | −0.50 | 0 |
| π | −0.10 | 0 | +2.5 |
| 3π/2 | 0 | +0.50 | 0 |

**The phase diagram.** Plot position x on the horizontal axis against velocity v on the vertical axis. From v² = ω²(A² − x²):

$$ x²/A² + v²/(ωA)² = 1 $$

an **ellipse** with semi-axes A and ωA. If the vertical axis is v/ω the curve is a circle of radius A: the reference circle again, now seen as the point (x, v/ω). The representative point moves **clockwise**, since at x = A the velocity is zero and then becomes negative. One circuit is one period. Larger energy means a larger ellipse (E = ½mω²A²); the area enclosed is πωA² = 2πE/(mω). A damped oscillator would spiral inward to the origin (PH-036). The phase-space picture is not named in the extracts; it is derived here from the sourced v²-equation.

### 5. Core: energy of the oscillator, conservation and the equality of mean energies

*Objective:* Derive U = ½kx², K = ½k(A² − x²) and E = ½kA², prove that mean K and mean U over a cycle are each E/2, and distinguish time averages from displacement averages.

**Setting.** Mass m, force F = −kx, x from the equilibrium, x = A cos(ωt + φ). Take the potential energy zero at x = 0 and note that F depends only on x, so it is conservative in one dimension.

**Potential energy.** U(x) = −∫₀ˣ F dx′ = ∫₀ˣ kx′ dx′ = ½kx².

**Kinetic energy.** v² = ω²(A² − x²), so K = ½mv² = ½mω²(A² − x²) = ½k(A² − x²) because mω² = k.

**Total energy.** E = K + U = ½k(A² − x²) + ½kx² = ½kA²: **constant, independent of x and t**, proportional to the square of the amplitude. It can also be written E = ½mω²A² = 2π²ν²mA². At x = 0 all of it is kinetic (½mv_max²); at x = ±A all is potential.

**Time forms.** K = ½mω²A² sin²(ωt + φ) = E sin²(ωt + φ); U = ½kA² cos²(ωt + φ) = E cos²(ωt + φ); K + U = E ✓. K and U oscillate at **twice the frequency** of x, because sin² and cos² have period T/2 (NCERT 13.7).

**Mean values over one period (the 2022 question).** Use cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2 with θ = ωt + φ:

$$ ⟨U⟩ = (1/T) ∫₀ᵀ E cos²(ωt + φ) dt = (E/2T) ∫₀ᵀ [1 + cos(2ωt + 2φ)] dt = (E/2T)[T + 0] = E/2 $$

The cosine integrates to zero because 2ωt + 2φ advances by exactly 4π over one period (bridge B1). Similarly with sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2, ⟨K⟩ = E/2. So

$$ ⟨K⟩ = ⟨U⟩ = ½ E = ¼ k A² = ¼ m ω² A² $$

**Shorter argument.** Since K + U = E, ⟨K⟩ + ⟨U⟩ = E; a shift of the time origin by T/4 swaps sin² and cos², so the two averages are equal.

**Trap: average over displacement.** If the oscillator were equally likely to be found at any x in [−A, A], ⟨U⟩ = (1/2A)∫ ½kx² dx = kA²/6 = E/3 and ⟨K⟩ = 2E/3. The 2022 question means the **time average**: SHM spends more time near the turning points, where U is large and the speed small. Check: the fraction of a period with |x| < A/2 is 1/3, although it covers half the range of x.

**Special positions.** K = U at x = A/√2 = 0.707 A. At x = A/2: U = E/4, K = 3E/4.

**Number (NCERT 13.7).** m = 1 kg, k = 50 N/m, A = 0.10 m: at x = 0.05 m, K = 0.1875 J, U = 0.0625 J, E = 0.25 J = ½ × 50 × 0.01 ✓.

**Dimensional check.** k A² has units (N/m) m² = N m = J. ✓

### 6. Core: spring-mass systems - vertical, series, parallel, cut springs and the rubber cord

*Objective:* Show that a vertical spring-mass oscillator has the same ω as a horizontal one, derive the series and parallel spring constants, and use k = YA/L for a cord.

**Horizontal spring.** From module 2, ω = √(k/m) and T = 2π√(m/k). The period increases with the mass and decreases with the stiffness.

**Vertical spring (gravity does not change ω).** Setting: spring hung from a fixed point, mass m at the bottom, x measured **downward from the new equilibrium**. At equilibrium mg = ke (e = static extension). At displacement x below it, the net downward force is mg − k(e + x) = −kx. So m ẍ = −kx and T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(e/g). Gravity only shifts the equilibrium by e = mg/k (Mathur 7.7.3, Upadhyaya 7.5).

**Springs in series (the 2021 question).** Setting: spring 1 (k₁) hangs from the ceiling, spring 2 (k₂) is attached below it and carries the mass; the junction is light. Let x₁ and x₂ be the extra extensions of the two springs when the mass is displaced by x downward from equilibrium, so x = x₁ + x₂. The junction is massless, so the two springs carry the **same force** F: F = k₁x₁ = k₂x₂. Then

$$ x = F/k₁ + F/k₂ = F (k₁ + k₂)/(k₁k₂) ,   so   F = k_s x ,   k_s = k₁k₂/(k₁ + k₂) $$

The restoring force is −k_s x, so the motion is SHM with

$$ f = (1/2π) √[ k₁k₂ / (m(k₁ + k₂)) ] $$

which is exactly the frequency quoted in the question. The figure was not available in the text extract; the arrangement is inferred from the quoted result, which corresponds to springs in series. Series springs are softer: k_s < k₁ and k_s < k₂.

**Springs in parallel (or on either side of the mass).** Both springs have the same extension x, and the forces add: F = (k₁ + k₂)x, so k_p = k₁ + k₂. For two identical springs on opposite sides of a block, one is stretched and the other compressed by x, giving 2k and T = 2π√(m/2k) (NCERT 13.6).

**Cutting a spring.** A spring of constant S cut into pieces of lengths in the ratio 1:3 has constants 4S and 4S/3, because a shorter piece is stiffer: k is inversely proportional to the number of coils (series argument in reverse; Mathur example 7.36).

**Elastic cord (the 2020 question).** A cord of length L, cross-section A and Young's modulus Y behaves as a spring with k = YA/L (PH-021, module 3). Y = 0.05 × 10¹¹ dyne/cm² = 5 × 10⁸ Pa, diameter 1 mm so A = π(0.5 × 10⁻³)² = 7.854 × 10⁻⁷ m², L = 1 m, m = 1 kg:

$$ k = 5 × 10⁸ × 7.854 × 10⁻⁷/1 = 392.7 N/m ,    T = 2π √(m/k) = 0.317 s $$

Cross-check with T = 2π√(e/g): e = mg/k = 0.02496 m, so T = 2π√(0.02496/9.8) = 0.317 s ✓. (Static strain 2.5 %: small enough for a linear model of rubber.) Assumption: the amplitude stays below the static extension e = mg/k = 2.5 cm, so the cord stays taut (a cord can only pull; cord mass neglected). For a larger amplitude the cord goes slack at the top and the motion is no longer SHM.

### 7. Core: the simple pendulum, the small-angle limit and the physical pendulum

*Objective:* Derive T = 2π√(l/g) from the torque equation with the small-angle approximation, quantify its validity, and link the physical pendulum to PH-017.

**Setting.** A point mass m on a light inextensible string of length l, pivot at O. θ is the angle from the downward vertical, positive anticlockwise; torques about O are positive anticlockwise. The tension acts along the string and gives no torque; only the tangential component of the weight does.

**Equation of motion.** The restoring torque is τ = −mgl sin θ (negative for positive θ). With I = ml² about O,

$$ ml² θ̈ = −mgl sin θ   →   θ̈ = −(g/l) sin θ $$

This is **not** the SHM equation: sin θ replaces θ. Expand sin θ = θ − θ³/3! + θ⁵/5! − ... For small θ (radians) keep the first term:

$$ θ̈ ≈ −(g/l) θ ,   ω = √(g/l) ,   T = 2π √(l/g) $$

**Interpretation.** T does not depend on the mass (the weight, which provides the restoring torque, is proportional to m and cancels the inertia) nor, for small swings, on the amplitude. **Dimension:** √(m/(m s⁻²)) = s ✓. For l = 1 m, T = 2.007 s. The length of a seconds pendulum (T = 2 s) is l = gT²/4π² = 0.993 m for g = 9.8 (NCERT rounds to 1 m).

**How small is small?** The table compares θ with sin θ and shows the first correction to the period (Mathur 7.13: T = T₀(1 + θ₀²/16), θ₀ the amplitude in radians; exact values from the complete elliptic integral, computed by Python).

| Amplitude θ₀ | (θ − sin θ)/sin θ | Exact ΔT/T₀ | Formula θ₀²/16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5° | 0.13 % | 0.048 % | 0.048 % |
| 10° | 0.51 % | 0.191 % | 0.190 % |
| 20° | 2.06 % | 0.767 % | 0.762 % |
| 30° | 4.72 % | 1.741 % | 1.713 % |
| 60° | 20.9 % | 7.32 % | 6.85 % |

A 5° swing gives an error under 0.05 % in the period; at 60° the formula underestimates the exact increase (the next term is needed).

**Energy view.** E = ½ml²θ̇² + mgl(1 − cos θ). For small θ, 1 − cos θ ≈ θ²/2, so E ≈ ½ml²θ̇² + ½mglθ²: the same form as ½mv² + ½kx² with 'mass' ml² and 'stiffness' mgl.

**Physical (compound) pendulum.** For a rigid body of mass M pivoted at O with its centre of mass a distance h below it, the same steps give I_O θ̈ = −Mgh sin θ ≈ −Mghθ and T = 2π√(I_O/Mgh) = 2π√(L/g), with L = h + K_g²/h the equivalent length (K_g is the radius of gyration about the centre of mass). The derivation, the interchangeability of the pivot and the centre of oscillation, and numbers are in **PH-017, module 8**; the point mass is the case K_g → 0. The same small-angle condition applies. The torsion pendulum, I θ̈ = −κθ, is exactly SHM for all angles within the elastic limit (PH-017 module 8).

**Accelerating support.** In a lift accelerating upward at a, the effective gravity is g + a, so T = 2π√(l/(g + a)); for l = 1 m and a = 3 m/s², T = 1.756 s (Mathur 7.28).

### 8. Master: the L-C analogy, general reading of SHM and limiting cases

*Objective:* Map the L-C circuit onto the spring-mass oscillator quantity by quantity, list limiting cases and source differences, and recognise where the model stops being linear.

**L-C circuit.** A capacitor C holding charge Q is connected across an inductor L of negligible resistance. The voltage across the capacitor is Q/C and the induced emf in the inductor is L dI/dt; with I = dQ/dt (Mathur's convention) Kirchhoff's loop law gives

$$ L Q̈ + Q/C = 0   →   Q̈ = −(1/LC) Q ,   ω = 1/√(LC) ,   T = 2π√(LC) $$

This is the SHM equation with Q in place of x. The analogy (Mathur 7.10; Upadhyaya 7.7):

| Mechanical | Electrical |
|---|---|
| displacement x | charge Q |
| velocity v = dx/dt | current I = dQ/dt |
| mass m (inertia) | inductance L |
| spring constant k | reciprocal capacitance 1/C |
| ½mv² | ½LI² (magnetic energy) |
| ½kx² | Q²/2C (electric energy) |
| ω = √(k/m) | ω = 1/√(LC) |

The total energy E = ½LI² + Q²/2C = Q₀²/2C = ½LI₀² is constant, and the two stores exchange energy twice per cycle, just as K and U do. **Check:** L = 10 mH, C = 1 μF: ω = 1/√(10⁻² × 10⁻⁶) = 10⁴ rad/s, f = 1591.5 Hz; unit check: henry × farad = second². ✓ **Sign convention difference:** Mathur uses I = dQ/dt, so I leads Q by π/2 (like v leads x); Upadhyaya takes I = −dQ/dt for his choice of current direction and so writes that the current lags by π/2. The physical content is the same: the charge and current oscillate a quarter cycle apart.

**Other systems with the same equation.** A diatomic molecule's relative coordinate obeys μξ̈ = −kξ with the reduced mass (PH-009, module 8). A torsion pendulum obeys Iθ̈ = −κθ (PH-017, module 8). A liquid column in a U-tube also qualifies (Mathur example 7.16); it is not treated here. A body dropped through a frictionless tunnel through the Earth is also SHM, with T = 2π√(R/g) ≈ 84 min (PH-006, module 7); it is linked, not re-derived.

**Limiting cases.** k → ∞ or m → 0: ω → ∞ (very stiff or very light systems oscillate fast). m → ∞: ω → 0. A → 0: the period stays 2π/ω, which is isochronism; E → 0. For the pendulum, l → ∞: T → ∞.

**Where SHM stops.** If the restoring force has an anharmonic term (for the pendulum, the cubic term of the force from sin θ ≈ θ − θ³/6, which is the quartic term of U), the period depends on amplitude (module 7: the pendulum at large θ₀). A resistance in the L-C circuit or friction on the spring makes the oscillation decay: damped oscillations (PH-036). A periodic external drive gives forced oscillations (PH-037). Combining two SHMs gives beats (PH-038) and Lissajous figures; these are linked by ID only.

### Notes: Definitions

- Simple harmonic motion: motion in which the restoring force (hence acceleration) is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and directed towards it, F = −kx; equivalently x = A cos(ωt + φ).
- Period T, frequency ν = 1/T (Hz), angular frequency ω = 2π/T = 2πν (rad/s), amplitude A (maximum displacement), phase θ = ωt + φ, phase constant φ.
- Isochronism: the period does not depend on the amplitude (nor on energy or on φ).
- Equilibrium position: where the net force is zero; for a stable equilibrium the potential energy has a minimum.
- Phase diagram: plot of v (or v/ω) against x; for SHM an ellipse (a circle for v/ω) traversed clockwise.
- Equivalent length of a physical pendulum L = h + K_g²/h, K_g the radius of gyration (PH-017 module 8).

### Notes: Equations

- m ẍ = −kx;  ω² = k/m;  T = 2π√(m/k);  ν = (1/2π)√(k/m).
- x = A cos(ωt + φ);  v = −ωA sin(ωt + φ);  a = −ω²x;  v² = ω²(A² − x²).
- A = √(x₀² + v₀²/ω²);  φ = atan2(−v₀/ω, x₀).
- U = ½kx²;  K = ½k(A² − x²);  E = ½kA² = ½mω²A² = 2π²ν²mA².
- ⟨K⟩ = ⟨U⟩ = ¼kA² = E/2 (time average over a period); displacement averages: ⟨U⟩ = E/3, ⟨K⟩ = 2E/3.
- Series springs k_s = k₁k₂/(k₁ + k₂); parallel k_p = k₁ + k₂; cord k = YA/L; vertical spring T = 2π√(e/g).
- Simple pendulum: θ̈ = −(g/l) sin θ ≈ −(g/l)θ;  T = 2π√(l/g);  T ≈ T₀(1 + θ₀²/16) for amplitude θ₀.
- L-C: Q̈ = −Q/LC;  ω = 1/√(LC);  E = ½LI² + Q²/2C.

### Notes: Derivation summary: solution and initial conditions

- m ẍ = −kx → ẍ = −ω²x.
- Multiply by 2ẋ and integrate: ẋ² + ω²x² = C; ẋ = 0 at x = A gives C = ω²A².
- ẋ = −ω√(A² − x²) → cos⁻¹(x/A) = ωt + φ → x = A cos(ωt + φ).
- Check: ẍ = −ω²x ✓.
- t = 0: x₀ = A cos φ, v₀ = −Aω sin φ → A = √(x₀² + v₀²/ω²), sign of φ from the sign of v₀.

### Notes: Derivation summary: mean energies equal (2022)

- U = E cos²θ, K = E sin²θ with θ = ωt + φ.
- cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2: ⟨cos²⟩ = ½ since ⟨cos 2θ⟩ = 0 over a whole period.
- ⟨U⟩ = ⟨K⟩ = E/2 = ¼kA².
- Alternative: ⟨K⟩ + ⟨U⟩ = E and a quarter-period shift swaps K and U.

### Notes: Phase relations and special points

- For x = A cos θ, θ = ωt + φ.

### Notes: Mechanical and electrical oscillators

- Same differential equation, different symbols.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Periodic versus oscillatory versus simple harmonic: each is a narrower class.
- Angular frequency ω (rad/s) versus frequency ν (Hz) versus the angular speed of the reference point, which is numerically the same as ω.
- Time average versus displacement average of K or U.
- The force on the SHM particle (−kx) versus the centripetal force on the reference point.
- Phase (ωt + φ) versus phase constant φ versus phase difference.
- Spring constant of a specimen k versus the elastic modulus Y (PH-021).

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-OSX1-SHM-X (verified):** Mass on a spring: omega = sqrt(k/m), period T = 2 pi sqrt(m/k), solution x(t) = A cos(omega t + phi); maximum speed v_max = A omega and maximum acceleration a_max = A omega^2.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 15.1 'Simple Harmonic Motion'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-1-simple-harmonic-motion)
- **F-OSX1-SHM-ENERGY (verified):** In simple harmonic motion the total energy E = (1/2) k A^2 is constant, with kinetic energy K = (1/2) m v^2 and potential energy U = (1/2) k x^2.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 15.2 'Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-2-energy-in-simple-harmonic-motion)
- **F-OSX1-PENDULUM (verified):** Simple pendulum of length L: T = 2 pi sqrt(L/g), valid for small angles (sin theta and theta differ by about 1% at 15 degrees, theta in radians); physical pendulum: T = 2 pi sqrt(I/(m g L)).
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 15.4 'Pendulums'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-4-pendulums)

#### Forces on a simple pendulum (draft)

The component mg sin(theta) of the weight along the arc always points toward equilibrium; for small theta sin(theta) is about theta and the motion is SHM.

A pendulum bob displaced to the right by angle theta from the vertical dashed line with tension T along the string toward the pivot, weight mg downward, and the component mg sin theta along the arc pointing back toward the vertical.

1. Draw the pivot, a dashed vertical line, and the string at angle theta to it with the bob at the end.
2. From the bob draw mg straight down and T along the string toward the pivot.
3. Resolve mg: the part along the arc, mg sin(theta), points back toward the vertical; the part along the string is mg cos(theta).
4. Write tau = -mgl sin(theta), I = ml^2, and for small theta theta'' = -(g/l) theta.

### Memory skeleton

- Definition: F = −kx, ẍ = −ω²x, ω² = k/m
- Solve: first integral v² = ω²(A² − x²), then x = A cos(ωt + φ)
- Initial conditions fix A and φ; T = 2π√(m/k), isochronous
- Phase relations: v leads x by π/2, a by π; ellipse in (x, v)
- Energy: E = ½kA²; K = E sin²θ, U = E cos²θ; time averages E/2
- Springs: series, parallel, cord k = YA/L
- Simple pendulum: small angle, T = 2π√(l/g); L-C analogue

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, solve ẍ = −ω²x by the first integral, fix A and φ from x₀ and v₀, then prove ⟨K⟩ = ⟨U⟩ = E/2 over a period.

### Last-week summary

- Multiply ẍ = −ω²x by 2ẋ: ẋ² + ω²x² = ω²A²; integrate again: x = A cos(ωt + φ); T = 2π√(m/k), independent of A.
- A = √(x₀² + v₀²/ω²); sign of φ from the sign of v₀ (atan2), not cos⁻¹ alone.
- E = ½kA²; K = E sin²θ, U = E cos²θ, period T/2; time averages E/2 each (displacement averages E/3, 2E/3).
- Pendulum θ̈ = −(g/l) sin θ ≈ −(g/l)θ for small θ: T = 2π√(l/g); T ≈ T₀(1 + θ₀²/16).
- Series k₁k₂/(k₁ + k₂), parallel k₁ + k₂, cord YA/L; L-C: ω = 1/√(LC), L ↔ m, 1/C ↔ k.
