### Bridge: Words you need: balance of trade, tribute, drain, invisible exports, Home Charges

Economic history questions are a vocabulary test first. Learn these five words.

- **Balance of trade**: the value of what a country sells abroad (exports) minus the value of what it buys (imports). A *favourable* balance means exports exceed imports and gold or claims on other countries flow in.
- **Tribute**: a payment extracted by a stronger power from a weaker one without any return service. Spectrum says earlier invaders did not take India's wealth away as tribute; the British did (PDF p. 602).
- **Drain of wealth (economic drain)**: Spectrum's definition is a portion of India's national product that was not available for the consumption of its people but went to Britain for political reasons, with India getting no adequate economic or material return (PDF p. 609). The key phrase is *no adequate return*: ordinary trade gives something back, a drain does not.
- **Invisible exports**: payments that cross borders without a physical good, such as salaries, pensions, interest, shipping and insurance fees. The word *invisible* is used because customs officers cannot count them. India could show a surplus of visible exports and still be drained through the invisible items.
- **Home Charges**: the sums the Government of India had to pay in Britain every year: interest on loans, pensions of officials, cost of the India Office, and purchases of stores. The term and the full list are standard knowledge, not printed in the extract, so treat the list as verify; Spectrum lists the same items under 'major components of the drain'.

**Worked picture.** A weaver in Bengal sells cloth for 100 rupees. A retired British officer in Britain is paid a pension of 100 rupees out of Indian revenue and spends it in Britain. The weaver's 100 rupees circulated in India; the pension left India and bought nothing from Indian producers. Multiply that by thousands of pensions and salaries and you have the shape of the drain.

### Bridge: Words you need: deindustrialisation, ruralisation, commercialisation, per-capita income

Four more words before the detail.

- **Deindustrialisation**: the decline of a country's existing handicraft and manufacturing sector, here hand-spinning, weaving, shipbuilding and metal work, without a modern industry to replace it (Spectrum PDF p. 603 stresses that no modern industrialisation followed).
- **Ruralisation**: the shift of people from towns and crafts back to villages and farming; many artisans abandoned their trades and took to agriculture, which raised the pressure on land (PDF p. 603). Notice that the word describes *loss*: the economy became more agricultural because the alternatives were shut, not because farming became more attractive.
- **Commercialisation of agriculture**: growing crops for sale in markets (often international) rather than mainly for the family's food (PDF p. 605). Cotton, jute, indigo, tea and groundnut are the standard examples.
- **Per-capita income**: national income divided by the number of people. If the total grows more slowly than the population, per-capita income falls even when the total rises. Reliable figures for colonial India are debated, so this lesson uses the concept and flags every number.

**Examples to attach.** A Dhaka weaver who becomes a farm labourer is deindustrialisation and ruralisation together. A Bombay Deccan peasant who plants cotton because the moneylender is advancing credit for cotton is commercialisation. A district where the sum of all incomes grows by 1 per cent a year while the population grows by 1.5 per cent has falling per-capita income.

### 1. Orientation: the colonial economy on one page

*Objective:* State in a few lines what Spectrum says British rule did to India's economy and name the nine headings of its summary.

Spectrum opens its economic chapter with the key claim: earlier invaders made no structural change in the Indian economy and did not carry India's wealth away as tribute, but British rule turned India into a **colonial economy**, one whose structure and working were shaped by the interests of the British economy (PDF p. 602). It adds a famous figure: according to historians, India held some 23 per cent of the world economy at the start of the eighteenth century and about 3 per cent at independence. This is **as stated by Spectrum; verify** before quoting, because such estimates depend on method and are contested.

Spectrum's own summary (PDF p. 617) names nine headings. Learn them as a chain, not a list:

1. **Deindustrialisation**, the ruin of artisans.
2. **Impoverishment of the peasantry**, ruralisation.
3. **New land relations**, the ruin of old zamindars (GS-029).
4. **Stagnation of agriculture.**
5. **Commercialisation of agriculture.**
6. **Development of modern industry** (Spectrum heading; in substance late and slow).
7. **Rise of an Indian national bourgeoisie.**
8. **Economic drain.**
9. **Famine and poverty.**

Read it as a cause chain. Imports and policy destroyed crafts (1); artisans crowded onto the land (2); the land systems and debt made peasants insecure (3, 4); farmers were pushed to grow for the market (5); modern industry grew late and in foreign hands (6, 7); a part of the surplus left the country (8); and the result was recurrent famine and poverty (9).

**Where this lesson fits.** GS-027 gave the Plassey and Diwani background; GS-029 gave the land revenue systems; GS-036 module 7 listed the Moderates' economic demands. This lesson does three new things: it explains deindustrialisation and the late, lopsided modern industry; it teaches the drain concept properly; and it sets the nationalist critique beside the British defence.

**Two warning notes for a beginner.** First, every statistic in the Spectrum chapter is printed without a source, so each figure in this lesson is labelled 'as stated by Spectrum; verify'. Second, the word *drain* is a nationalist and later historians' term, not the vocabulary of colonial officials; always attribute it ('Naoroji argued') rather than state it as agreed fact.

### 2. Foundation: three stages of colonial exploitation

*Objective:* Describe the three overlapping stages of colonialism that Spectrum takes from Rajni Palme Dutt and place the drain and deindustrialisation in each.

Spectrum borrows a Marxist framework (Rajni Palme Dutt) to show that British rule did not exploit India in one fixed way. The stages **overlap** and old methods continued inside new ones (PDF p. 613). Use the stages as a timeline for everything else in this lesson.

## Stage 1: merchant capital, monopoly trade and direct appropriation (1757 to 1813)

The Company wanted two things: a **monopoly of trade** against rivals, and **direct appropriation of governmental revenue** through control of state power (the Diwani, GS-027 module 8). Spectrum says no basic changes were made in administration, courts, transport, agriculture or industry; the only changes were in military organisation and in the top of the revenue structure.

- **Drain in this stage:** Spectrum says large-scale drain, about 2 to 3 per cent of Britain's national income at the time, which played an important role in financing Britain's industrial revolution (as stated by Spectrum; verify; historians contest the size and the claim of causation).
- **Trade in this stage:** there was no large import of British manufactures; Indian textile exports rose. Weavers were ruined by the Company's monopoly, which forced them to produce at uneconomic prices.
- NCERT supports the mechanism: before 1765 the Company bought Indian goods with gold and silver brought from Britain; after the Diwani, Bengal revenue financed the purchases (hess203 p. 2). Artisans deserted villages because they were forced to sell cheaply, and the famine of 1770 followed (NCERT says ten million died, about a third of Bengal's people; treat the figure as verify).

## Stage 2: colonialism of free trade (1813 to the 1860s)

Industrial capitalists in Britain wanted India as a **market for their manufactures, especially textiles, and a source of raw materials** (cotton, foodgrains). All import duties were removed or reduced to nominal levels; Europeans got free entry to plantations, trade, mining and industry; the Permanent and Ryotwari systems reshaped the countryside (GS-029); the administration spread to villages so that British goods could go in and crops come out. Spectrum says India took 10 to 12 per cent of British exports and nearly 20 per cent of its textile exports (as stated; verify).

## Stage 3: foreign investment and rivalry (from the 1860s)

Britain's industrial lead was challenged by Europe, the United States and Japan. British capital poured into **railways, loans to the Government of India**, and to a lesser extent plantations, coal, jute, shipping and banking. Politics hardened: the liberal promise to train Indians for self-government gave way to the doctrine of permanent 'trusteeship' (PDF p. 617). Drain now meant interest and profits on foreign investment.

The stage table is in the master notes.

### 3. Core: deindustrialisation, ruralisation and the Industrial Revolution question

*Objective:* Explain the causes of the decline of Indian handicrafts and say how far the Industrial Revolution alone explains it (2024 Mains frame).

Spectrum's heading is *Deindustrialisation: ruin of artisans and handicraftsmen* (PDF pp. 602-603). Its explanation has four strands, and a good answer separates them.

## 1. One-way free trade

Cheap machine-made imports flooded the market after the **Charter Act of 1813** allowed free trade for British citizens, while Indian goods found it harder to enter European markets. Spectrum says tariffs of nearly **80 per cent** were imposed on Indian textiles and that after 1820 European markets were virtually closed to Indian exports (as stated by Spectrum; verify the rate, it is not sourced there). From being a net exporter, India became a **net importer** of cloth. Think of it as a one-way gate: British cloth walked in nearly duty-free, Indian cloth met a wall.

## 2. The railway

The new rail network carried European goods to the remotest corners, reaching villages that had been protected by distance. Buchanan, quoted in Spectrum, said the steel rail pierced the armour of the self-sufficient village; a line attributed to Bentinck says the bones of cotton weavers were bleaching the plains of India (the attribution is widely questioned; verify).

## 3. Loss of patronage

Spectrum adds that artisans were already in trouble because **princes and nobles**, now under western influence, lost patronage and taste for local luxury goods. This cause is *not* the Industrial Revolution; keep it for the 'how far' question.

## 4. Coercion in Bengal

Under Company rule artisans were paid low wages and forced to sell at low prices (module 2). This caused decline *before* machine imports mattered.

## Ruralisation and why it hurt

Artisans in decline abandoned their trades, moved to villages and took to farming, so towns declined and pressure on land rose. Spectrum says an overburdened agriculture was a major cause of poverty under British rule. The crucial point is that **no modern industry absorbed the displaced**, unlike the countries that industrialised in the same century (module 4).

## Answering 'how far was the Industrial Revolution responsible?'

A balanced answer places the Industrial Revolution at the centre but not alone:

- **For:** machine-made cloth was cheaper; British industry needed markets; policy removed duties and added tariffs against India; railways gave reach.
- **Against full responsibility:** policy mattered as much as technology (it was *how* Britain treated trade, not machines alone); patronage loss and Company coercion hurt crafts earlier; some crafts survived (a standard point; verify in a text) because local demand and style persisted; and Indian industry had not found a way to modernise because capital, credit and technical education were absent (module 4).

Tharoor, quoted in Spectrum, calls deindustrialisation deliberate policy; others stress technology and the market. State both and give a weighted verdict.

### 4. Core: shipbuilding, steel and the late, lopsided modern industry

*Objective:* Describe what happened to Indian shipbuilding, steel and indigenous capital, and list the limits of modern industry as Spectrum gives them.

Spectrum's heading is *Destruction of industry and late development of modern industry* (PDF pp. 606-608). Take it in three parts.

## The destruction of old industry: textiles and ships

Spectrum says the British stopped paying for Indian textiles in bullion (Spectrum says pounds) and paid instead out of Bengal revenue and at very low rates, which impoverished peasants further (module 2). It then gives the **shipbuilding** story, which is a favourite fact:

- Surat and Malabar on the west coast and Bengal and Masulipatnam on the east were known for shipbuilding.
- British ships hired by the Company got a monopoly on trade routes, and Indian coastal ships paid heavy duties.
- A **1813** law of the British Parliament barred ships below 350 tonnes from sailing between India and Britain, which put many Bengal-built ships out of the Indo-British trade (as stated; verify the tonnage).
- A **1814** law refused to treat Indian-built ships as 'British-registered vessels' for trade with America and Europe.

The result, in Spectrum's words, was that the decline of Indian shipping was ensured. 

## Steel and the Tatas: a claim to handle with care

Spectrum says the British did not let Indian steel grow: firms such as the Tatas, after trouble getting permissions, were forced to make a higher standard of steel for British use and could not sell the lower standard in the larger market, so growth was hampered. This is **as stated by Spectrum; verify**; standard histories debate it, so write cautiously: 'Indian steel makers complained of restrictions and unequal terms'. For balance: TISCO, founded in 1907, received tariff protection in 1924 and large wartime orders (standard knowledge; verify).

## The late start of modern industry

Machine industry arrived in the **second half of the nineteenth century**: the first cotton textile mill in 1853 at Bombay (Cowasjee Nanabhoy; Spectrum; NCERT Class VIII gives 1854, and Fort Gloster near Calcutta, 1818, is often called the first mill; verify) and the first jute mill in 1855 at Rishra, Bengal. Spectrum then makes the points an examiner wants:

- **Most modern industry was foreign-owned**, controlled by British managing agencies. Foreign capital came for high profit, cheap labour and raw material, a ready market and administrative help.
- **Indian-owned industry** appeared in cotton and jute in the nineteenth century and in sugar and cement in the twentieth, but had handicaps: credit problems, **little or no tariff protection before the 1920s** (discriminating protection followed the Fiscal Commission of 1921-22 and covered steel from 1924 and later sugar; standard knowledge; verify), unequal competition from foreign firms, and opposition from British capitalists backed by finance and technology at home.
- **Lopsided pattern:** core and heavy industries and power generation were neglected, and some regions were favoured over others, producing **regional disparities** that Spectrum says hampered nation-building.
- **Thin technical education** meant too few technicians.
- **Indian traders, moneylenders and bankers** were junior partners who fitted the colonial scheme (distributing imports, collecting crops for export, lending to peasants). The colonial situation held back an independent industrial middle class, unlike Germany or Japan.

Spectrum also notes the rise of an **industrial capitalist class** and a **working class** (GS-037 module 8 shows workers striking in 1905-08); the Indian bourgeoisie grew, but its growth was stunted.

**Exam use:** why India did not industrialise: foreign control of capital, little tariff protection before the 1920s, neglect of heavy industry and technical education.

### 5. Core: commercialisation of agriculture, crops and the indigo question

*Objective:* Explain what commercialisation meant, who gained and lost, and why indigo declined (2018 and 2020 Prelims frames).

GS-029 module 8 traced the debt and cotton-boom chain. This module adds what Spectrum's economic chapter says and the indigo detail the 2020 question needs; it does not repeat the settlement material.

## What commercialisation was

In the latter half of the nineteenth century, agriculture, 'a way of life rather than a business', began to respond to commercial considerations (Spectrum PDF p. 605). Specialised crops were grown **for sale** in national and even international markets: **cotton, jute, groundnut, oilseeds, sugarcane, tobacco**, and condiments, spices, fruits and vegetables for wider markets. The most developed form was the European-owned **plantation sector** (tea, coffee, rubber, indigo). NCERT Class VIII adds opium and indigo, then jute in Bengal, tea in Assam, sugarcane in the United Provinces, wheat in Punjab, cotton in Maharashtra and Punjab and rice in Madras (hess203 p. 5).

## Why it spread

Drivers: a money economy, competition and contract replacing custom, a unified national market, internal trade, roads and rail, and **British finance capital** boosting trade.

## Who gained, who lost

For the peasant, Spectrum says, commercialisation looked like a **forced** process: living at subsistence level, he had no surplus to invest in cash crops, while the shift tied him to world price swings. Its example is the cotton boom of the 1860s, which lifted prices but mainly benefited intermediaries; when prices slumped in 1866 the cultivators suffered most, leading to heavy debt, famine and the Deccan riots of the 1870s (details of the cotton boom and Supa 1875 are in GS-029 and GS-031).

For a 2018-style question on 'one economic result of British rule', *commercialisation of agriculture* fits; handicraft exports, Indian-owned factories and urban growth do not (modules 3 and 4).

## Indigo: why did it decline? (2020 Q33 theme)

NCERT hess203 gives three facts. First, indigo planting was oppressive: peasants were tied by advances, got very low prices, had to plant on their best rice land and found the soil exhausted. Second, in **March 1859** thousands of ryots in Bengal refused to plant, in the 'Blue Rebellion', and an Indigo Commission found the planters guilty of coercion and said indigo was not profitable for ryots; after the revolt production collapsed in Bengal and planters shifted to Bihar. Third, the **discovery of synthetic dyes in the late nineteenth century** severely hit the business, though planters still managed to expand, and Gandhi's Champaran visit of 1917 came against indigo planters in Bihar.

**Reading the options:** NCERT ties the *late-nineteenth-century* blow to synthetic dyes, which fits the world-market option (b); resistance (a) belongs to Bengal in 1859-60, and the sources name no national leaders or government control as the cause. Analysis, not an official key.

## A trap

Do not say commercialisation 'improved' farm incomes without qualification: Spectrum's verdict is that the cultivator hardly emerged better off.

### 6. Core: famines, poverty and indentured labour

*Objective:* Give reasons for the spurt in famines in colonial India (2022 Mains frame) and explain why indentured labour was recruited and what happened to it (2018 Mains frame).

## Famines: what the sources say

Spectrum says regular recurrence of famines became a common feature of Indian life, and that they were **not just because of foodgrain scarcity but a direct result of poverty unleashed by colonial forces**. It gives the figure that between 1850 and 1900 about **2.8 crore** people died in famines (PDF p. 605), and elsewhere that severe famines killed **90 lakh** between 1896 and 1900, with plague in the Deccan (PDF p. 304). Both are **as stated by Spectrum; verify**, and the two figures overlap, so never add them. NCERT records the 1770 Bengal famine (ten million said to have died; verify).

## Reasons to give in a 10-mark answer (2022 Mains)

Build the answer as a chain from causes the extracts support:

1. **Extraction of revenue regardless of harvest.** High cash demands under the land systems left peasants with no reserves and pushed them to moneylenders (GS-029 module 8).
2. **Commercialisation.** Land moved to cash crops, and the early Congress said grain exports to Europe created shortages (NCERT hess208 p. 4).
3. **Decline of crafts.** Ruined artisans had no income to buy food (module 3).
4. **Overburdened agriculture and low productivity**, with little state spending on irrigation, technology or education (module 4 and Spectrum PDF p. 605).
5. **Drain of resources** (module 7), a nationalist argument that capital needed for improvement left the country.
6. **Relief policy:** early laissez-faire and slow relief; after the Strachey Famine Commission (1880) Famine Codes (1883) were framed, followed by the Lyall (1898) and MacDonnell (1901) Commissions; defenders cite these, critics note the deaths of 1896-1900 despite them (standard knowledge; verify).
7. **Rainfall failure as the trigger.** A good answer says drought is natural; policy decided whether a drought became a famine.

## Poverty and per-capita income

The nationalists' basic claim was that India was poor and growing poorer because of British rule, and that because the causes were man-made they were removable (Spectrum PDF p. 610). Spectrum gives no per-capita income series and published estimates differ widely, so treat 'stagnant per-capita income' as a flagged position, not a fact.

## Indentured labour (2018 Mains, 15 marks)

The extracts do not explain recruitment, so this is **standard knowledge; verify**. After slavery was abolished in the British empire in the 1830s, plantation colonies (sugar in Mauritius, the Caribbean, Natal, Fiji) needed cheap labour; India supplied it under indenture contracts. **Push factors** were landlessness, debt, famine and ruined crafts; **pull factors** were wages and promised land, though conditions were harsh.

Spectrum tells us what was found in South Africa: indentured labour, mainly from South India, on sugar plantations (Spectrum says they came after 1890; indentured migration to Natal is standardly dated from 1860, so treat 1890 as a slip; verify), merchants, and ex-indentured settlers, who suffered denial of the vote, residence restrictions and a poll tax of three pounds on ex-indentured Indians (PDF p. 360-361). The Moderates also demanded better treatment of Indian labour abroad (PDF p. 299). Cultural identity is not in these sources; answer cautiously (language, religion and festivals persisted in blended forms; verify).

### 7. Core: the drain defined, its components and Home Charges

*Objective:* Define economic drain, list its five components, explain why each was a drain, and apply it to the rupee-sterling ratio of the 2026 Prelims question.

## Definition and the idea behind it

**Economic drain** (Spectrum PDF p. 609): a portion of India's national product not available for consumption by its people but taken to Britain for political reasons, with India getting no adequate economic or material return. Dadabhai Naoroji put the theory forward in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*, which standard sources date to 1901 (the extract gives no year; verify). Spectrum also quotes John Sullivan's sponge image (drawing good things from the Ganges and squeezing them down on the Thames) and Sir George Wingate on taxes spent abroad as an absolute loss.

**Why was it a drain and not trade?** Because the payment bought nothing from India. If a British retiree spends his Indian-paid pension in Britain, no Indian producer receives it; if the same tax were spent in India it would circulate (Wingate's point).

## The five components (Spectrum)

1. **Salaries and pensions** of civil and military officials.
2. **Interest on loans** taken by the Indian government from abroad (including loans for railways and for wars).
3. **Profits on foreign investment** in India.
4. **Stores purchased in Britain** for civil and military departments.
5. **Payments for shipping, banking and insurance services**, which stunted the growth of Indian enterprise in them.

These match the standard idea of the **Home Charges**, the yearly payments the Government of India made in Britain (interest on debt including guaranteed railway capital, pensions and furlough pay, India Office expenses and stores). The Home Charges label and its exact heads are standard knowledge, flagged verify; the Spectrum components are the safe exam list.

## How the drain acted

Spectrum says the drain **checked capital formation** in India while accelerating growth in Britain, and that surplus from Britain's economy re-entered India as finance capital, so that the drain *fed itself*: British capital brought interest and profit claims, which became new outflow. The effect fell on income and employment possibilities in India.

## Size: the estimates

Spectrum says nationalist estimates put the drain at more than total land revenue, or half of government revenue, or one-third of total savings, and in today's terms about 8 per cent of the national product (all as stated by Spectrum; verify). Angus Maddison, quoted there, said a substantial outflow lasted 190 years and that, had the funds been invested in India, they could have helped raise incomes. Maddison is not a nationalist, so his sentence gives the thesis outside support.

## Application to the 2026 Prelims question

The Hilton-Young Commission (1926), the Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance, recommended a fixed rupee-sterling ratio of 1 shilling 6 pence, and the Government of India adopted it by the Currency Act of 1927 (standard knowledge; verify; this is not in the extracts). A *higher* ratio made the rupee dearer in sterling: exports costlier abroad, imports cheaper, and, as nationalists argued, sterling remittances cost fewer rupees. Among the options (remittances and creditworthiness; support to importers; cotton exports; preventing depreciation against gold), the drain concept points to the first. Option (b) needs care: a dearer rupee did cheapen imports, but that was a side effect; nationalist critics such as Purshotamdas Thakurdas (minority dissent) argued that 1s 6d hurt Indian exporters and farmers and helped mainly British imports, while the British case rested on sterling obligations (Home Charges) and India's credit in London (standard knowledge; verify). This is analysis, not an official key; verify the Hilton-Young facts.

### 8. Core: the nationalist critique and its thinkers

*Objective:* State the nationalist critique of the colonial economy and attach the right argument to Naoroji, Dutt, Ranade, Gokhale, Subramania Iyer, Joshi and others.

GS-036 module 7 listed the Moderates' economic demands in one pass. This module goes deeper into the *analysis* behind those demands.

## From hope to disillusion

Early nineteenth-century intellectuals expected British rule to modernise India; **after the 1860s disillusionment began** and the politically conscious probed what it really did (Spectrum PDF p. 609).

## Who said what

| Thinker | Contribution as the extracts give it |
|---|---|
| **Dadabhai Naoroji** ('Grand Old Man of India') | Theory of economic drain in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*; he spent much of his life and income campaigning for India abroad (Spectrum PDF p. 296) |
| **R.C. Dutt** | *The Economic History of India* (standard dates 1901-03; verify); Spectrum quotes him that taxes raised from Indian soil fall as rain on other lands |
| **Ranade, Gokhale, G. Subramania Iyer, Prithwishchandra Ray** | Named by Spectrum among the foremost economic analysts; Gokhale also criticised government finance in the councils (PDF p. 298) |
| **Dinshaw Wacha** | Named with Naoroji and Dutt as an analyst of the political economy (PDF p. 296) |
| **G.V. Joshi** | Said that railway spending was an Indian subsidy to British industry |

Tilak is not named in the economic section of this chapter; link him to the critique only through swadeshi and boycott (GS-037), flagged.

## The essence of the critique

Spectrum's summary of what the thinkers argued:

1. **India was poor and growing poorer because of British rule.** Because the causes were man-made, they could be explained and removed. Poverty became a national problem of raising the productive capacity of the people, which let all sections rally around common economic issues.
2. **Colonialism was a pattern**: India a supplier of foodstuffs and raw materials, a market for British manufactures and a field for British capital.
3. **Development meant industrialisation with Indian, not foreign, capital**, because foreign capital suppressed Indian capital and created vested interests that needed foreign rule.
4. **Trade and railways served Britain.** Trade made India an exporter of raw materials; railways were not coordinated with industrial needs, brought a *commercial rather than an industrial revolution*, and sent orders for steel and machinery to Britain.
5. **One-way free trade and tariffs ruined handicrafts**, exposing them to unequal competition.
6. **Fiscal injustice**: taxes burdened the poor while sparing British capitalists and officials; expenditure served colonial needs.

## Demands

Cut land revenue and military spending, abolish the salt tax, tax the rich through income tax and excise, help plantation labour, give tariff protection and aid to modern industry, and Indianise the services (partly because salaries and pensions remitted home are the drain). The early Congress added that grain exports caused shortages (NCERT hess208 p. 4).

## Why the drain theory mattered politically

Spectrum says the drain theory pulled all threads together and was easily grasped by a nation of peasants (PDF pp. 612-613). It undermined the **moral hegemony** of the rulers by showing that India was poor because it was ruled in British interests, and fed the Moderate phase (Spectrum PDF p. 613 prints 1875-1905; GS-036 uses 1885-1905), 'the seed-time of the national movement'. In the 1900s nationalists moved from asking for a share in power to demanding self-rule; Naoroji was prominent (Calcutta 1906; GS-037).

### 9. Master: nationalist and imperial readings, balanced

*Objective:* Compare the nationalist and imperial interpretations of British economic policy and write a balanced verdict that stays within what the sources support.

A mains answer scores highest when it shows both sides and then *weighs* them. Use this module as the scaffold.

## The nationalist reading (what the extracts give)

India's economy was reshaped to serve Britain: crafts destroyed by free trade and tariffs, agriculture commercialised under pressure, modern industry foreign-owned and lopsided, and a large share of the surplus drained away. The result was poverty and recurrent famine, which made the case against foreign rule.

## The British and imperial defence

The Spectrum extract states the British side only indirectly, and it is important to be exact:

- The nationalists had to **expose the force of British arguments** that foreign trade and railways implied development (PDF p. 610): trade and rail were *progress*.
- The later doctrine of **trusteeship** and the 'White Man's burden' justified rule as civilising a people called immature (PDF p. 617).
- On famines, defenders cite the Famine Codes of 1883 and the Lyall (1898) and MacDonnell (1901) Commissions; critics reply that 1896-1900 killed millions despite them (standard knowledge; verify).
- Other arguments often made by imperial writers (railways unified markets and reduced local famine, irrigation canals raised output, law and order enabled commerce, Britain brought capital, some of the Home Charges paid for real services) are **standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify before using**. State them as 'defenders said', not as established fact.

## Where the critique is strong

- Heavy duty on Indian cloth, nominal duty on British cloth, and the shift from net exporter to importer (module 3).
- Foreign ownership of modern industry and capital (module 4).
- Famines recurred despite grain exports, which the early Congress itself pointed out.

## Where the critique needs care

- **Measurement.** The figures depend on what counts as payment for real services and on a counterfactual (what India would have done with the money). Spectrum presents them as nationalist estimates; Maddison calls the outflow substantial but frames it as lost investment.
- **Causation.** Not every cause of poverty was colonial; weather and earlier famines mattered (standard; verify).
- **Heterogeneity.** Some merchants, bankers and mill owners gained; the damage fell hardest on weavers, small peasants and labourers.
- **Structure versus intention.** Tharoor, quoted by Spectrum, says deindustrialisation was a deliberate policy, while others stress market forces and technology. Both views belong in an essay.

## A balanced verdict, ready to adapt

*Colonial policy and the Industrial Revolution together ruined Indian crafts; policy chose free trade for British goods and gave Indian industry little tariff shelter before the 1920s; and a sizeable share of the surplus left without return, even if its exact size is disputed. Defenders of the Raj could point to railways, irrigation and law and order, but those were designed mainly for British commercial and strategic ends, and the pattern of foreign ownership meant that the gains from them were partly repatriated.*

### Notes: Definitions

- Economic drain (Spectrum PDF p. 609): a portion of India's national product not available for its people's consumption, taken to Britain for political reasons, with no adequate economic or material return.
- Home Charges (standard; verify): the annual payments the Government of India made in Britain for interest on debt, pensions, India Office costs and stores.
- Deindustrialisation: the decline of handicrafts and old industry without modern industry to replace it.
- Ruralisation: artisans leaving crafts and towns for farming, raising the burden on land.
- Commercialisation of agriculture: growing crops for the market (cotton, jute, indigo, tea) rather than mainly for food.
- Colonial economy: one whose structure and working are set by the interests of the ruling economy.

### Notes: Three stages of colonialism (Spectrum, after R.P. Dutt)

- Dates and figures as stated by Spectrum; verify before quoting.

### Notes: Components of the drain (Spectrum)

- Salaries and pensions of civil and military officials.
- Interest on loans taken abroad by the Government of India.
- Profits on foreign investment in India.
- Stores bought in Britain for civil and military departments.
- Payments for shipping, banking and insurance services.
- Mechanism: capital formation checked in India; British surplus returned as finance capital, creating new claims; effect on income and employment in India.

### Notes: Why handicrafts declined

- One-way free trade after 1813: British cloth entered almost free; tariffs (stated as nearly 80 per cent; verify) on Indian cloth.
- Railways carried imports to interior markets.
- Princes and nobles lost patronage and changed tastes.
- Company coercion of Bengal artisans: low wages, forced sales at low prices.
- No modern industry absorbed the displaced; ruralisation increased pressure on land.
- Result: from net exporter to net importer of cloth.

### Notes: Industries and their fate (Spectrum)

- Shipbuilding: laws of 1813 (ships under 350 tonnes barred from the India-Britain route; verify) and 1814 (Indian-built ships not 'British-registered'); decline ensured.
- Steel: Spectrum says Indian firms faced restrictions and unequal terms; contested detail; verify.
- Modern industry: first Bombay cotton mill 1853 (Spectrum, Cowasjee Nanabhoy; NCERT Class VIII gives 1854; verify); first jute mill Rishra 1855.
- Most modern industry foreign-owned and run through British managing agencies.
- Indian-owned industry: cotton and jute (19th century), sugar and cement (20th); handicaps: credit, little or no tariff protection before the 1920s (discriminating protection after 1921-22; verify), unequal competition.
- Pattern: lopsided; heavy industry and power neglected; regional disparities; thin technical education.

### Notes: Nationalist thinkers and arguments

- Naoroji: drain theory, Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901; verify).
- R.C. Dutt: The Economic History of India; taxes raised on Indian soil fall as rain elsewhere.
- Ranade, Gokhale, G. Subramania Iyer, Prithwishchandra Ray, Wacha: named by Spectrum among the economic analysts.
- G.V. Joshi: railway spending an Indian subsidy to British industry.
- Demands: cut land revenue, abolish salt tax, tax the rich, cut military costs, protect industry, Indianise services.
- Political effect: exposed the moral basis of rule; stimulant of the Moderate phase (Spectrum PDF p. 613 prints 1875-1905; GS-036 uses 1885-1905).

### Notes: Nationalist and imperial readings compared

- Imperial arguments are standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Drain versus trade deficit: a drain is outflow without return, which can coexist with a trade surplus (invisible items).
- Deindustrialisation versus slow industrialisation: the first is the decline of old crafts, the second the late start of modern industry; both happened.
- Commercialisation versus plantation: plantation crops (tea, indigo) were one form; peasants also grew cash crops (cotton, jute).
- Land revenue systems (GS-029) versus the drain (this lesson): the first is how revenue was collected, the second where part of it ended up.

### Memory skeleton

- Colonial economy: India's economic structure set by British needs
- Three stages of colonialism (after R.P. Dutt): merchant capital, free trade, foreign investment
- Drain: definition and five components (salaries, interest, profits, stores, services)
- Deindustrialisation: 1813 one-way free trade, railways, lost patronage, coercion; ruralisation
- Commercialisation of agriculture and famine vulnerability
- Late, lopsided modern industry: cotton and jute mills, managing agencies
- Nationalist critique: Naoroji, R.C. Dutt, Ranade, Gokhale, G.V. Joshi; demands and political effect

### Last-week summary

- Drain: part of national product taken to Britain for political reasons without adequate return; S-I-P-S-S = salaries and pensions, interest, profits, stores, services.
- Stages: merchant capital, free trade (from the 1813 Charter Act), foreign investment; deindustrialisation plus ruralisation turned India from cloth exporter to importer.
- Modern industry late and lopsided: first jute mill Rishra 1855; mostly foreign-owned through managing agencies; heavy industry neglected.
- Naoroji: Poverty and Un-British Rule in India; R.C. Dutt: Economic History of India; G.V. Joshi: railway spending a subsidy to British industry; critique exposed the moral basis of rule.
