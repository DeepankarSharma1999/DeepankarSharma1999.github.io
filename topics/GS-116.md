## GS-116: Western and Eastern Ghats

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Reading relief on a physical map: height, slope and direction

A physical map of India uses colour to show height above mean sea level (the average level of the sea, which is the zero line for heights). Green shades usually mean low land, yellow and brown mean higher land, and dark brown or white mean high mountains. The key printed in the corner of the map tells you the metre values for each shade; always read it first.

Directions on a printed map: north is at the top unless an arrow says otherwise, so west is on the left and east is on the right. India's Arabian Sea coast is the left (west) edge of the peninsula and the Bay of Bengal coast is the right (east) edge. This is why this lesson keeps saying 'west to east'.

Four shapes are easy to confuse:

- A **range** is a long, narrow line of hills or mountains (for example the Satpura range).
- A **hill** is a smaller rise of land; names such as 'Nilgiri hills' are used even for large ranges.
- A **plateau** is a raised area of fairly flat or gently rolling land with steep sides on at least one side (the Deccan Plateau).
- A **peak** is the highest point of a range or hill (Anaimudi is a peak, not a range).

A **ghat** in everyday Indian use is a stepped slope or a way up and down a slope; as a physical-geography name it is used for the hill ranges on the edges of the southern plateau. The NCERT extract does not define the word, so remember it as a name, not as a technical term.

Finally, a **cross-section** is a drawing of what you would see if you sliced the land from one side to the other and looked at the cut face: the height is drawn as the up-and-down line and the distance as the left-to-right line. The sketch in module 1 is a cross-section. Drawing it by hand is a fast way to remember an order of features. Day 17 lesson GS-095 (module 8, relief on maps, contours, gradient and section drawing) teaches how to draw a section properly; here only the idea is needed.

### 1. Orientation: the Ghats on one page

*Objective:* Place the Western Ghats, the Deccan Plateau and the Eastern Ghats in the right west-to-east order and say what each is in one phrase.

## Where this topic sits

NCERT divides India into six physiographic divisions (physiography means the physical shape of the land as the result of structure, process and stage of development): the Northern and North-eastern Mountains, the Northern Plain, the Peninsular Plateau, the Indian Desert, the Coastal Plains and the Islands. The Ghats belong to the third division, the **Peninsular Plateau**, which NCERT describes as an irregular triangle rising from about 150 m above the river plains to an elevation of 600-900 m. It is one of the oldest and most stable landmasses of India.

NCERT splits the Peninsular Plateau into three: the **Deccan Plateau**, the **Central Highlands** and the **Northeastern Plateau**. The Ghats are the two edges of the Deccan Plateau.

## The picture to hold

The Deccan Plateau is like a tilted tray. Its **western rim** is a high, nearly continuous wall, the **Western Ghats**, running close to the Arabian Sea. Its **eastern rim** is a lower, broken line of hills, the **Eastern Ghats**, standing back from the Bay of Bengal. The plateau between them slopes down from west to east, which is why the long rivers of the peninsula run eastward. In the far south the two rims meet at the **Nilgiri hills**. In the north the plateau is bordered by the Satpura range, the Maikal range and the Mahadeo hills.

Between each Ghats and its sea lies a coastal plain: a narrow one on the west and a broad one on the east.

## One-line summary of each

- **Western Ghats:** higher, more continuous, average elevation about 1,500 m rising north to south; most Peninsular rivers rise here.
- **Eastern Ghats:** low, discontinuous hills, cut through by big rivers such as the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri.
- **Meeting point:** the Nilgiri hills.

Figure idea for a hand-drawn sketch: a west-to-east cross-section (see the diagram) with five labels in order. Draw it three times from memory today and once tomorrow; the order is the most reusable fact in this lesson.

### 2. Vocabulary you need before the details

*Objective:* Define escarpment, relict mountain, rift valley, orographic rain, rain shadow, delta and emergent and submerged coast, and use each in a sentence.

Every term below is used later in this lesson. Learn each one in one line.

**Plateau.** A raised area of land that is fairly level on top with steep sides on at least one edge. The Deccan Plateau is the standard example.

**Scarp or escarpment.** A steep face or slope that marks the edge of a raised block. NCERT describes the south of India as a stable table land with highly dissected plateaus, denuded rocks and a developed series of scarps. The Western Ghats are often described as a scarp-edge of the plateau; the extract itself does not use the word for them, so hold it as a descriptive idea.

**Relict (or residual) mountain.** An old mountain that was once much higher and has been worn down by long erosion; what stands today is the remnant. NCERT calls the Aravali, Nallamala, Javadi, Veliconda, Palkonda and Mahendragiri hills relict and residual mountains of the Peninsula. Satpura is called a classic relict mountain that is highly denuded and forms discontinuous ranges.

**Block mountain and rift valley.** When a block of land is lifted or dropped between faults (cracks in the rock), the raised block is a block mountain and the dropped strip is a rift valley. NCERT names the rift valleys of the Narmada, the Tapi and the Mahanadi and the Satpura block mountains as examples of vertical movement and block faulting in the Peninsula.

**Orographic rain.** Rain caused by a hill or mountain forcing moist air upward. As the air rises it cools, water vapour condenses and rain falls on the side facing the wind.

**Windward and leeward.** The windward side faces the wind that carries moisture; the leeward side is the sheltered side behind the hills.

**Rain shadow.** The dry zone on the leeward side, because the air has already lost most of its moisture on the windward slope and then descends. The NCERT extract does not discuss the rain shadow of the Ghats; it is standard textbook knowledge (verify with your atlas and climate chapter). The extract does show the opposite end: the Meghalaya plateau receives maximum rainfall from the south-west monsoon and has a highly eroded surface.

**Delta.** A fan of land built where a river drops its load of sediment as it meets the sea. NCERT names the deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri on the east coast.

**Submerged coast and emergent coast.** In a submerged coast the sea has covered what was dry land, which usually gives a narrow plain and deep natural harbours. In an emergent coast the land has risen or the sea has withdrawn, which gives a broad, gently sloping plain. NCERT calls the western coastal plain submerged and the eastern coastal plain emergent. Day 17 lessons GS-102 and GS-130 treat coastal landforms in more depth.

**Continental shelf.** The gently sloping, shallow seabed that extends from the coast before the sea floor drops steeply. On the eastern coast, NCERT prints up to 500 km; standard maps show the eastern shelf narrower than the western (verify).

## Two caution words

'Discontinuous' means broken into separate pieces by gaps. 'Dissected' means cut into by rivers. Both describe the Eastern Ghats, which the rivers have cut across.

### 3. The ancient block under the Ghats

*Objective:* Describe the Peninsular Block, its rock and relict mountains, and the boundaries of the Deccan Plateau within which the two Ghats stand.

## Three geological divisions

NCERT divides India, by geological structure, into three: the **Peninsular Block**, the **Himalayas and other Peninsular mountains**, and the **Indo-Ganga-Brahmaputra Plain**. The Ghats belong to the first.

## The Peninsular Block

The northern boundary of the block is described as an irregular line from Kachchh along the western flank of the Aravali near Delhi, then roughly parallel to the Yamuna and the Ganga as far as the Rajmahal Hills and the Ganga delta. The Karbi Anglong and Meghalaya plateau in the north-east and Rajasthan in the west are extensions of this block.

Key points from the extract:

- It is made essentially of a great complex of very ancient **gneisses and granites** (hard crystalline rocks).
- Since the Cambrian period it has stood like a **rigid block**, except that some of its western coast is submerged beneath the sea and some parts have changed through tectonic activity without affecting the original basement.
- As part of the Indo-Australian plate it has been subjected to vertical movements and block faulting, giving the rift valleys of the Narmada, Tapi and Mahanadi and the Satpura block mountains.
- Its mountains are mostly relict and residual (the Aravali, Nallamala, Javadi, Veliconda, Palkonda and Mahendragiri hills among them), and its river valleys are shallow with low gradients.
- Most east-flowing rivers form deltas before entering the Bay of Bengal.

Contrast: the Himalayas and other mountains of the young belt are described as young, weak and flexible, still subject to faults, folds and thrust planes, cut by fast-flowing youthful rivers. This contrast explains why landslide and earthquake questions treat the Himalaya differently from the peninsula (module 7).

## The Deccan Plateau and its frame

The Deccan Plateau is bordered by the Western Ghats in the west, the Eastern Ghats in the east, and the Satpura range, the Maikal range and the Mahadeo hills in the north. The overall slope of the Peninsular plateau is from west to east, which the extract proves by the pattern of river flow. NCERT says the western and north-western part of the plateau has an emphatic presence of black soil. Typical features of the Peninsular plateau include tors, block mountains, rift valleys, spurs, bare rocky structures, hummocky hills and wall-like quartzite dykes that give natural water-storage sites.

The plateau has had recurrent upliftment and submergence with faulting; the Bhima fault is singled out for repeated seismic activity. Standard textbooks also link the black soil to the lava of the Deccan volcanic outpouring; the extract does not say this, so treat it as general knowledge to verify.

## Why this matters for the Ghats

The Western Ghats stand on the **edge of this rigid, tilted block**; the Eastern Ghats are low, eroded relicts at its other edge. Because the block is old and relatively stable, the Ghats are not the high, young fold mountains of the Himalaya; they are worn, flat-topped heights whose steepness is mainly on the western face. How the western edge came to be so steep is explained in some books by faulting or flexuring along the western margin; the extract does not discuss the origin, so a detailed origin theory is outside this lesson (completeness row marked for review).

### 4. The Western Ghats: names, height, peaks and rivers

*Objective:* State the local names, elevation, highest peaks, rivers and ecological character of the Western Ghats, and mark the standard facts that need verification.

## Position and names

The Western Ghats run close to the Arabian Sea coast on the western rim of the Deccan Plateau. NCERT says they are locally known by different names: **Sahyadri** in Maharashtra, **Nilgiri hills** in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, and the **Anaimalai hills** and **Cardamom hills** in Kerala. Treat this as the NCERT list for Prelims; an atlas shows a more detailed naming (for example the Sahyadri name is also used in Karnataka, and the Nilgiris touch Kerala), so verify with an atlas before using the list in a Mains answer.

## Height and continuity

- The Western Ghats are 'comparatively higher in elevation and more continuous' than the Eastern Ghats.
- Their **average elevation is about 1,500 m**, with the height **increasing from north to south** (NCERT figure; other books quote a different average, so quote the book you use).
- Highest peak of the Peninsular plateau: **Anaimudi, 2,695 m**, on the **Anaimalai hills** of the Western Ghats.
- Next: **Dodabetta, 2,637 m**, on the **Nilgiri hills**. (NCERT writes Dodabetta in the text and Dodabeta in the exercise; both mean the same peak.) The two peaks differ by 58 m (2,695 minus 2,637).

Memory link: A for Anaimudi and Anaimalai; N for Nilgiri and next (Dodabetta). Practise the pairing, because exams reward knowing which peak is on which range.

## Rivers

NCERT: 'Most of the Peninsular rivers have their origin in the Western Ghats.' This makes the Western Ghats the main **water divide** of the peninsula. Rivers on the eastern slope flow across the plateau into the Bay of Bengal. Standard facts outside the extract (verify with an atlas): the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri rise in the Western Ghats region; the Narmada and Tapi flow westward in their rift valleys, as the extract's rift-valley reference implies.

## States and passes (outside the extract, verify)

The Western Ghats are commonly described as running from the Tapi valley near the Gujarat-Maharashtra border to Kanyakumari (verify), through six states: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Kerala. A verified PYQ (2017) asked whether they spread over five states only; the count is a standard-knowledge item you should confirm in an atlas. The well-known gaps through the range, used by rail and road, include the **Thal (Kasara) Ghat** and **Bhor Ghat** on routes from Mumbai, and the **Palghat Gap** between the Nilgiri and Anaimalai hills linking Kerala and Tamil Nadu. These three are standard-knowledge items to verify; the extract names no passes.

## Ecology (outside the extract, verify)

The Western Ghats are widely described as a biodiversity hotspot and are listed as a World Heritage site (as of the last standard reference; verify the current list). Their heavy rainfall, steep slopes and varied altitude support evergreen forests, plantation belts (tea, coffee, cardamom, rubber) and numerous endemic species. The extract gives no species or area figure, so none is stated here. The coffee statement in the agriculture extract (GS-123) places Indian coffee in the highlands of the Western Ghats in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, and tea on the lower slopes of the Nilgiri and Cardamom hills: that link is useful for the next lesson.

## What to carry forward

Western Ghats: high, continuous, steep on the western face, source of most peninsular rivers, heavy windward rain, biodiversity-rich, many plantations.

### 5. The Eastern Ghats and the link between the two Ghats

*Objective:* Describe the Eastern Ghats as discontinuous low hills cut by large rivers, name their ranges and explain where and how they connect with the Western Ghats.

## What NCERT says

The Eastern Ghats 'comprise the discontinuous and low hills' that are 'highly eroded by the rivers' such as the **Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri**. They stand on the eastern rim of the Deccan Plateau, back from the Bay of Bengal coast. NCERT names these ranges: the **Javadi hills**, the **Palconda range** (spelled Palkonda in the earlier relict-mountain list), the **Nallamala hills** and the **Mahendragiri hills**. The Veliconda hills are listed with the relict mountains of the Peninsula. All of these appear in NCERT's list of relict and residual mountains, which tells you their nature: old, worn, low, broken.

## Why they are broken

The big east-flowing rivers have low gradients on the old plateau but a great load of water; they have cut across the eastern rim on their way to the sea and built deltas. Each cut leaves a gap in the hills. That is why the Eastern Ghats are discontinuous, whereas the Western Ghats, which the rivers rise on rather than cross, remain a nearly continuous wall.

## Where the Ghats meet

NCERT says: 'The Eastern and the Western Ghats meet each other at the Nilgiri hills.' The Nilgiri hills therefore are the southern junction. Dodabetta (2,637 m), the second-highest named peak, is on the Nilgiri hills.

## Standard-knowledge additions (verify with an atlas)

The extract gives no peak heights for the Eastern Ghats and does not mention several hill groups that exams use. Commonly named additional Eastern Ghats ranges or hill groups include the Seshachalam hills in Andhra Pradesh and the Shevaroy and Kalrayan hills in Tamil Nadu. The Biligirirangan (BR) hills of Karnataka are described in many books as a link between the Western and Eastern Ghats near the Nilgiri junction. The Sathyamangalam Tiger Reserve in Tamil Nadu is usually described as an ecological link between the Eastern and Western Ghats (a 2017 PYQ asked exactly which of four areas is a good link). The Nallamala forest is in the Eastern Ghats region of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Each of these is a 'verify' item.

Highest points and extent (standard knowledge, verify): Jindhagada (also called Arma Konda) in the Visakhapatnam hills of Andhra Pradesh, about 1,690 m (sources differ slightly), is usually named as the highest Eastern Ghats peak, and Mahendragiri in Odisha is about 1,501 m. The Eastern Ghats are usually described as running from the Mahanadi valley in Odisha southward to the Nilgiris. The extract gives no height for any Eastern Ghats peak, so these figures are for the atlas habit, not for NCERT-bound questions.

Rivers that rise in the Eastern Ghats (verify): the Nagavali and Vamsadhara are usually described as rising in the Eastern Ghats of Odisha, while the Subarnarekha and Brahmani are described as rising on the Chotanagpur plateau region rather than in the Eastern Ghats. A 2021 PYQ tested this sorting. The extract does not give river sources, so this fact is taught as standard knowledge with an uncertain-review row.

## Eastern Ghats and the east coast

The broad eastern coastal plain between the Ghats and the Bay of Bengal is an emergent coast with well-developed deltas. NCERT says the plain's emergent nature gives it fewer ports and harbours than the western coast, and links the difficulty of developing good ports to the continental shelf. NCERT prints up to 500 km; standard maps show the eastern shelf narrower than the western (verify). The four named deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) are the fertile rice lands that follow the rivers through the gaps in the Eastern Ghats.

## What to carry forward

Eastern Ghats: low, discontinuous, eroded by great rivers, old relict hills with named ranges, meeting the Western Ghats at the Nilgiri hills, bordering a broad deltaic east coast.

### 6. Rain, rain shadow, rivers and coasts

*Objective:* Explain with a simple mechanism why the Western Ghats create a wet west coast and a drier plateau behind them, and link this to river direction and the two coastal plains.

## The mechanism in five steps

This is the chain that explains most map questions about the Ghats. The first two steps use the extract; steps 3-5 use standard monsoon knowledge that the extract does not give, so verify them in your climate chapter.

1. The south-west monsoon carries moist air from the Arabian Sea towards the peninsula (standard).
2. The Western Ghats form a high, continuous barrier close to the sea, with an average elevation of about 1,500 m (NCERT).
3. The moist air is forced up the western slope, cools, condenses and gives heavy orographic rainfall on the windward side, that is the narrow coastal plain and the western slopes (standard).
4. Having lost moisture, the air descends on the leeward side; the interior of the Deccan Plateau behind the Ghats is therefore drier. This dry zone is the rain shadow (standard).
5. The Eastern Ghats are low and broken, so they do not block rain in the same way; the south-eastern coast of Tamil Nadu is usually described as getting much of its rain in the winter months from the retreating monsoon (standard; verify).

The extract's own example of a rain-fed upland is Meghalaya, which receives maximum rainfall from the south-west monsoon and has a highly eroded, bare rocky surface at Cherrapunji. It shows what intense rain does to a surface and why the Ghats with their heavy rainfall also show strong erosion on the windward side. The extract gives no rainfall figures for the Ghats, so none are stated here.

## Rivers: the tilt decides the direction

Because the plateau tilts from west to east and the Western Ghats are the high rim, the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri start near the western rim and run eastward across the plateau to the Bay of Bengal; the Mahanadi rises on the Chhattisgarh plateau instead and reaches the same sea (verify the sources in an atlas). NCERT names the deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri. The Narmada and Tapi flow in rift valleys; the rivers that cross the narrow western coastal plain are short, and NCERT says they do not form deltas. NCERT also says the river valleys of the Peninsula are shallow with low gradients, unlike fast-flowing youthful Himalayan rivers (the extract invites you to calculate and compare gradients).

## The two coasts

The western coastal plain is narrow, submerged and delta-free, with natural harbours and the Malabar kayals; the eastern plain is broader, emergent and deltaic, with fewer ports (NCERT). The full comparison table, the port list and the four divisions of the west coast are in Day 17 lesson GS-130, module 3 (the two coasts compared and the western coast).

## Why the Ghats are called a climatic and drainage divide

They separate a wet, narrow, steep, short-river western side from a drier, broad, gentle, long-river eastern side. The same wall is also a watershed (the line from which water drains in two directions), an ecological boundary and a landslide-prone slope (module 7).

### 7. Comparing the Ghats and explaining landslides

*Objective:* Build the full comparison of the Western and Eastern Ghats and write a differentiated explanation of landslide causes in the Himalaya and the Western Ghats.

## The comparison, in words

The master notes carry the full table. In words: the Western Ghats are higher, continuous, steep on the west face and the source of most peninsular rivers; the Eastern Ghats are lower, discontinuous and eroded by the east-flowing rivers. The Western Ghats lead to a wet west coast and dry interior; the Eastern Ghats stand behind a broad deltaic coast. The two meet at the Nilgiri hills.

## Geomorphic and ecological significance

- **Drainage divide:** the Western Ghats separate the short western rivers from the long eastward rivers.
- **Climate divide:** barrier to the south-west monsoon (module 6).
- **Biodiversity:** the Western Ghats are widely described as a biodiversity hotspot and World Heritage site (verify the current status); the corridor between the Ghats near Sathyamangalam and the BR hills carries large animals between them (verify).
- **Economy:** plantation crops (tea, coffee, cardamom) on the Ghats' slopes from the agriculture extract (GS-123); river valleys and deltas for rice in the east; hydro sites on the Ghats' rivers (standard).

## Landslides: Himalaya versus Western Ghats

The 2021 Mains question asked to differentiate the causes of landslides in the two regions. A sound framework uses the extract for the structure and standard knowledge (flagged) for the hazard facts.

**Himalaya.** The extract describes the Himalayas as young, weak and flexible, still subject to faults, folds and thrust planes, and cut by fast-flowing youthful rivers with gorges and V-shaped valleys. A land that is still being uplifted, shaken by earthquakes and steeply cut is predisposed to slope failure. Standard additions (verify): seismic shaking, glacial and snowmelt water, cloudbursts, road and tunnel construction on steep, fractured rock.

**Western Ghats.** The extract describes the Peninsular Block as ancient, rigid and stable, so tectonic uplift is not the main driver. The hazard here comes from **very heavy monsoon rain** on steep windward slopes (module 6), deep weathered surface material over the hard rock (standard, verify) and **human changes**: removing forest, laterite quarrying, plantation expansion, road cuts and settlement on slopes (standard, verify).

**Common ground.** Both regions have steep slopes, heavy monsoon rain and human interference with the slope. **Difference.** The Himalaya adds an active tectonic and glacial base; the Western Ghats are primarily rain-and-surface-driven on a stable block. State this as a contrast, not as 'one has human causes and the other natural'; both have both.

## Template for a 10-mark answer

Intro (one line defining landslide), Himalayan causes in two lines, Western Ghat causes in two lines, one shared cause, one difference, and a one-line mitigation (slope stabilisation, land-use control, early warning). Practise it in the Mains task of this lesson.

### 8. The exam lens: map drill and the five PYQs

*Objective:* Use the lesson's map and facts to analyse the five verified PYQs, spot the traps and draw the trio of sketches that these questions test.

## What this topic tests

All five verified PYQs ask for a **location fact** (which area is where, which range is which, which rivers rise where, how many states) or a **geographical reason** (why landslides differ). A Prelims item in this area is almost always a map question in words. Draw the cross-section, the Ghats outline and the river lines until you can reproduce them in two minutes.

## The five questions as reasoning exercises (analysis, not an official key)

**2017 Q4 (link between the Eastern and Western Ghats).** The four options are a tiger reserve, a forest, a national park and a biosphere reserve. The reasoning is to ask which one sits where the Eastern and Western Ghats approach each other, i.e. near the Nilgiri junction in the Tamil Nadu and Karnataka border region. Sathyamangalam is commonly placed there; Nallamala is in the Eastern Ghats region of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana; Nagarhole is on the Western Ghats side in Karnataka; Seshachalam is in the Eastern Ghats of Andhra Pradesh. The lesson's analysis points to Sathyamangalam; confirm in an atlas, because the extract names only the Nilgiri junction.

**2017 Q58 (Himalayas, Western Ghats and Pulicat).** Statement 1 and 2 say 'five states only'. The Himalayas in India are spread across more than five states and union territories, and the Western Ghats are commonly counted across six states (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala), so both 'only five' claims conflict with the standard count; Pulicat lake is shared between Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, two states. The analysis leaves statement 3. Verify all counts.

**2021 Q55 (rivers from the Eastern Ghats).** The sorting in module 5: Nagavali and Vamsadhara are commonly described as Eastern Ghats rivers, whereas the Subarnarekha and Brahmani are described as rising on the Chotanagpur side. The analysis points to the pair Nagavali and Vamsadhara; verify.

**2021 Mains Q4 (landslides).** Module 7.

**2023 Q9 (three hill statements).** Test each against the map. Amarkantak is generally placed where the Vindhya and Satpura meet, not the Sahyadri. The Biligirirangan hills are in the Karnataka region of the Western-Eastern Ghat link, not the eastern end of the Satpura range. The Seshachalam hills are an Eastern Ghats range in Andhra Pradesh, not the southernmost Western Ghats. On this analysis all three statements conflict with standard geography, which is the pattern 'none'. Verify each placement.

## Traps to remember

- The 'only' trap: 'five states only', 'two states only'. Counting is the whole question.
- Swapping the Ghats: Western versus Eastern, windward versus leeward.
- Mixing hill systems: Vindhya, Satpura, Sahyadri and Aravali each have a position.
- Source-of-river mix-ups: Eastern Ghats rivers versus Chotanagpur rivers versus Western Ghats rivers.

## Prelims method for map items

Draw a quick sketch, place two or three anchor points (Nilgiri junction, Anaimudi, Chilika or Mahanadi delta as needed), place the answer options as dots, and eliminate options that lie on the wrong side. Eliminating even one wrong option raises the expected value of a guess above zero because a wrong answer costs one-third of the question's marks (2 marks minus 0.67), while a blind four-way guess has expected value zero.

- Links between the Ghats: Biligirirangan hills; Sathyamangalam Tiger Reserve.
- Biodiversity-hotspot and World Heritage status of the Western Ghats; confirm the current position.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Western Ghats are not the Sahyadri alone: Sahyadri is the Maharashtra stretch in NCERT's list.
- Nilgiri hills are a name for part of the Western Ghats and also the point where the two Ghats meet; do not treat the Nilgiris as an Eastern Ghat.
- Satpura and Vindhya are not the Western Ghats; Satpura is the northern boundary of the Deccan Plateau.
- Chotanagpur plateau rivers are not Eastern Ghats rivers.
- Rain shadow is on the leeward side; the windward side is the wet one.

### Notes: Examples that show the pattern

- Meghalaya plateau (Cherrapunji): heavy south-west monsoon rainfall, highly eroded bare rocky surface.
- Malabar coast kayals: backwaters used for fishing, inland navigation and tourism; Nehru Trophy boat race at Punnamada Kayal.
- Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri deltas on the east coast.

### Core concepts

- The Ghats are the two rims of the tilted Deccan Plateau.
- The Western Ghats are high and continuous; the Eastern Ghats are low and broken.
- The Ghats meet at the Nilgiri hills.
- Windward rain, leeward rain shadow; west-flowing rivers short and without deltas, east-flowing rivers long with deltas.

**Q.** Which coast is submerged and which emergent?

**A.** West submerged; east emergent.

**Q.** Why do east-flowing rivers form deltas while west-flowing rivers do not?

**A.** The plateau slopes west to east, so long rivers carry sediment across it to the broad eastern coast, while the western rivers are short and fall quickly to a narrow, submerged coast (NCERT says they form no delta).

**Q.** Why is the Western Ghats region a climatic divide?

**A.** Its high continuous wall forces moist monsoon air up, giving heavy windward rain and a drier plateau behind (standard knowledge, verify).

**Q.** Why are the Eastern Ghats called relict hills?

**A.** They are old, low, much-eroded hills of the ancient Peninsular Block.

### Memory skeleton

- Ghats as the two rims of the tilted Deccan Plateau; plateau boundaries
- Peninsular Block: ancient, rigid, relict mountains (versus young Himalaya)
- Western Ghats: higher, continuous, rising north to south; Anaimudi and Dodabetta
- Eastern Ghats: low, discontinuous, cut by four big rivers; Javadi, Palconda, Nallamala, Mahendragiri
- Meeting point: the Nilgiri hills
- Rivers: west-to-east tilt gives long deltaic east-flowing and short delta-less west-flowing rivers
