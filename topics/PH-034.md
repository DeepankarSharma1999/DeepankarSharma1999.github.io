### Bridge: What a law is, and three words that are easily confused

A **law of physics** here means an equation relating measurable quantities, for example F = dp/dt or E² = p²c² + m²c⁴. Different observers in different inertial frames (PH-001 module 2) use different coordinates, so the numbers entering the equation differ. The question of this lesson is whether the *equation itself* still holds when every quantity is re-measured in the other frame.

Three words keep apart three ideas.

- **Invariant:** a *quantity* whose value is the same number in every frame. Examples: the speed of light c, the rest mass m₀, the interval c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz² (PH-028 module 8), the proper time (PH-029 module 3).
- **Covariant:** an *equation* that keeps the same mathematical form when you pass to another frame, because each term transforms in the same way. 'Form' means the same symbols with primes: if A = B holds in S then A′ = B′ holds in S′.
- **Conserved:** a *quantity* that does not change with time during a process in one frame. Momentum of an isolated system is conserved, yet its value is not invariant: different frames give different totals.

Mathur and Upadhyaya say 'invariant' for a law that keeps its form (Upadhyaya: 'invariant in form'); modern usage reserves *covariant* for equations. This lesson uses the modern words and marks where the books say 'invariant'.

### Bridge: Scalars, vectors and how an equation survives a transformation

A **scalar** is a single number that does not change under the transformation (a temperature, the interval, the rest mass). A **vector** is a set of components that change together in a fixed linear way. In ordinary space, rotating the axes changes the components (x, y) but leaves the length √(x² + y²) alone. In relativity the Lorentz transformation of PH-028 module 4 plays the part of the rotation, and the **four-vector** (A⁰, A¹, A², A³) changes by the same rule as (ct, x, y, z) (PH-033 modules 1 and 2).

Why this makes laws easy to test: suppose an equation says one vector equals another, A = B, component by component. Under any linear transformation, A′ = ΛA and B′ = ΛB with the same Λ, so A′ = ΛA = ΛB = B′. The equation is covariant **provided both sides are vectors of the same kind** (both four-vectors, or both scalars). An equation that sets a vector equal to something that does not transform like it (a scalar times an ordinary three-vector, say) cannot survive. This is the strategy of the whole lesson: write laws as equations between four-vectors and scalars and covariance is automatic.

Galilean and Lorentz rules are two different Λ. Galilean: x′ = x − vt, t′ = t. Lorentz: x′ = γ(x − vt), t′ = γ(t − vx/c²), y′ = y, z′ = z. An equation can be covariant under the first and not the second, as module 3 shows.

### Bridge: Partial derivatives and the chain rule for changing variables

A function f(x, t) has two **partial derivatives**: ∂f/∂x (change with x, t held fixed) and ∂f/∂t (change with t, x held fixed). If f is really written as a function of new variables x′(x, t) and t′(x, t), the **chain rule** gives

$$ ∂f/∂x = (∂x′/∂x)(∂f/∂x′) + (∂t′/∂x)(∂f/∂t′) $$

and the same with t in place of x. The second derivative is found by applying the same operator twice; when the coefficients are constants (as they are here) the operator can be squared like a number.

Example. Let x′ = x − vt and t′ = t, and take f = sin(x′) = sin(x − vt). Then ∂x′/∂x = 1, ∂t′/∂x = 0, so ∂f/∂x = cos x′; and ∂x′/∂t = −v, ∂t′/∂t = 1, so ∂f/∂t = −v cos x′ + ∂f/∂t′ = −v cos x′ because f does not depend on t′ explicitly. Check against direct differentiation of sin(x − vt): ∂f/∂t = −v cos(x − vt). They agree.

In module 5 the two steps are: write the four operators ∂/∂x, ∂/∂t in terms of ∂/∂x′, ∂/∂t′, then square and combine.

### 1. Orientation: what is already known and what covariance adds

## The idea in one paragraph

The principle of relativity says the laws of physics take the same form in all inertial frames. **Covariance** is how that principle is tested on a given equation: change the variables by the transformation connecting the frames and ask whether the equation keeps its form. Newton's second law keeps its form under the Galilean transformation but not under the Lorentz transformation; Maxwell's equations and the wave equation do the opposite. That is the logical route from the ether story (PH-027) to special relativity.

### 2. Foundation: the principle of relativity and covariance under Galilean and Lorentz rules

*Objective:* State the principle of relativity, summarise Galilean covariance of mechanics and the failure for light, and set up the table of what must be tested.

## The principle

Upadhyaya §6.3 (PDF pp. 328 and 329) states it in steps: the basic laws of physics are identical in all inertial systems; in the form tied to the Galilean transformation, they are 'invariant in form' for frames connected by it; and in special relativity they are invariant in form for frames connected by the Lorentz transformation. Mathur §3.4 (PDF pp. 128 and 129) gives the first postulate: the laws of physics take the same form in all inertial frames, which follows from the absence of an absolute frame (if laws differed, the differences would reveal the frame at rest).

## Galilean covariance of mechanics (recap, linked)

With x′ = x − vt, t′ = t, the acceleration is the same in both frames, a′ = a (PH-001 module 2); with a constant mass and F = ma, Upadhyaya §6.5 (PDF p. 330) concludes F′ = ma′ = F. The same follows for conservation of momentum and energy (Upadhyaya §6.4, PDF p. 329). Mathur's Example 9 (PDF p. 137) reaches the same conclusion. Newton's laws are covariant under the Galilean rule.

## Where it breaks: light

Upadhyaya §6.8 (PDF p. 334) says the laws of mechanics are invariant under Galilean transformations while the laws of electrodynamics (Maxwell's equations) are not, and that light speed would become c ± v. The experiment of PH-027 found no such change. Something must give: Newton or Maxwell. The history is that Maxwell's equations were kept and the transformation was changed to the Lorentz one (PH-028); this forced a revision of mechanics (module 4). The wave-equation test is made explicit in module 5.

## The table to carry

| Law | Galilean transformation | Lorentz transformation |
|---|---|---|
| F = m₀a (constant m₀) | covariant | **not** covariant (module 3) |
| F = dp/dt with p = γm₀u, or dP/dτ = K | not covariant in general; reduces to F = m₀a (Galilean covariant) only for u much less than c | covariant (module 4) |
| Wave equation for light, c fixed | **not** covariant (module 5) | covariant (module 5) |
| Maxwell's equations | not covariant (Upadhyaya §6.8) | covariant (module 7, standard; verify) |
| E² − p²c² = m²c⁴ | not meaningful | covariant (PH-033 module 4) |

## Why both transformations are candidates

Both reduce to the identity when v is zero, and the Lorentz transformation reduces to the Galilean one when v ≪ c (PH-028 module 5). Which one nature obeys at speeds near c is a matter of experiment, and the null result of PH-027 plus the later tests decide for Lorentz.

### 3. Core: Newton's second law fails under the Lorentz transformation (derivation 1, part one)

*Objective:* Derive the transformation of acceleration, find the instantaneous force in the second frame, and show F = m₀a with constant m₀ is not covariant.

## Acceleration under the Lorentz transformation

Frames as in module 1. Let a particle have velocity u_x along x in S and acceleration a_x = du_x/dt. From PH-028 module 7, the velocity in S′ is

$$ u′_x = (u_x − v)/(1 − v u_x/c²) $$

Differentiate with respect to u_x (the quotient rule), v constant:

$$ du′_x/du_x = [(1 − vu_x/c²) + (u_x − v)(v/c²)]/(1 − vu_x/c²)² = (1 − v²/c²)/(1 − vu_x/c²)² $$

The time interval transforms as dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²) = γ dt(1 − vu_x/c²). Divide:

$$ a′_x = du′_x/dt′ = (1 − v²/c²) a_x/[γ(1 − vu_x/c²)³] = a_x/[γ³(1 − vu_x/c²)³] $$

using 1 − v²/c² = 1/γ². (Python confirms the algebra.) Unlike the Galilean case a′ = a, **the acceleration changes**, and by a factor depending on u_x.

## A particle momentarily at rest in S

Let the particle be at rest in S at the instant considered, u = 0, under a force F along x, so that F = m₀a_x with constant rest mass m₀ in S. In S′ the particle moves at −v.

*Acceleration:* a′_x = a_x/γ³.

*Force:* use the transformation of momentum and energy (PH-033 module 5): p′_x = γ(p_x − vE/c²). Differentiate, and divide by dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²):

$$ F′_x = dp′_x/dt′ = γ(dp_x − v dE/c²)/[γ(dt − v dx/c²)] = [F − (v/c²) dE/dt]/(1 − vu_x/c²) $$

At u = 0, dx/dt = 0 and dE/dt = F·u = 0, so F′_x = F.

## The mismatch

Take the Newtonian form in S′ with the same constant m₀: m₀a′_x = m₀a_x/γ³ = F/γ³. But the force in S′ is F′ = F. The equation F′ = m₀a′ would need γ³ = 1. So **F = m₀a with constant m₀ cannot hold in both frames.**

Numbers: v = 0.6c gives γ = 1.25 and γ³ = 1.953125. For m₀ = 1 kg and a = 10 m s⁻² (F = 10 N): a′ = 10/1.953 = 5.12 m s⁻²; m₀a′ = 5.12 N against the actual F′ = 10 N. The 'Newtonian' force in S′ is 49 per cent too small.

```
 S : particle at rest, force F →, a = F/m₀
 S': particle moves at −v, force F′ = F (instant), a′ = a/γ³
 Newton in S': m₀a′ = F/γ³  ≠  F′
```

## What repairs it

If the longitudinal 'mass' is γ³m₀ for a particle at speed v (the S′ view of the particle), then γ³m₀a′ = F′ holds: exactly the relativistic F = γ³m₀a of module 4. Checks: a′ and a in m s⁻²; as v → 0, γ → 1 and a′ → a (Galilean); a′ < a because γ³ > 1.

### 4. Core: the relativistic force, its transformation and the four-force (derivation 1, part two)

*Objective:* State F = dp/dt with p = γm₀u, derive dE/dt = F·u and the transformation of force, and show dP/dτ = K is covariant.

## The relativistic second law

Mathur §3.8 (PDF pp. 164 and 165) defines force as the rate of change of momentum, F = dp/dt, with p = γm₀u, because this reduces to Newton's law for u ≪ c and, with the third law, keeps momentum conservation valid in relativity. For motion along the force, differentiate p = m₀u(1 − u²/c²)^(−1/2) with respect to u:

$$ dp/du = m₀(1 − u²/c²)^(−3/2)[(1 − u²/c²) + u²/c²] = γ³m₀,   so F = γ³m₀ a $$

(Mathur prints the same factor as (1 − v²/c²)^(−3/2).) This is the 'mass' γ³m₀ that module 3 needed.

## Work and energy

From E² = p²c² + m₀²c⁴ (PH-032 module 4), E dE = c² p·dp, so dE/dt = (c²p/E)·F = u·F, using u = pc²/E. Energy changes at the rate of work done.

## Transformation of force (derivation)

Let F = dp/dt in S and F′ = dp′/dt′ in S′. Use p′_x = γ(p_x − vE/c²), p′_y = p_y, p′_z = p_z (PH-033 module 5) and dt′ = γ dt(1 − vu_x/c²):

$$ F′_x = γ(dp_x − v dE/c²)/[γ dt(1 − vu_x/c²)] = (F_x − v F·u/c²)/(1 − vu_x/c²) $$

$$ F′_y = F_y/[γ(1 − vu_x/c²)],   F′_z = F_z/[γ(1 − vu_x/c²)] $$

Check: for a particle at rest in S these give F′_x = F_x and F′_y = F_y/γ.

*Number.* v = 0.6c, particle with u = (0.8c, 0, 0), force (0, 10 N, 0). Then F·u = 0, F′_x = 0 and F′_y = 10/[1.25(1 − 0.6 × 0.8)] = 10/(1.25 × 0.52) = 15.38 N (200/13, Python agrees). 

## The four-force

Differentiate the four-momentum with respect to proper time: K = dP/dτ. Since dτ = dt/γ_u, with γ_u the Lorentz factor of the particle's speed u,

$$ K = γ_u (F·u/c, F) $$

(PH-033 module 9 gives the same). **The equation dP/dτ = K is covariant** because P and K are four-vectors and dτ is invariant: in S′, K′ = dP′/dτ with the same τ, and K′ = ΛK by the transformation of module 5 of PH-033.

*Consistency check.* The transverse component gives K′_y = K_y, i.e. γ_u′F′_y = γ_uF_y. The speeds are related by γ_u′ = γγ_u(1 − vu_x/c²). Hence F′_y = F_y/[γ(1 − vu_x/c²)], the result derived above from F = dp/dt. The two routes agree.

## What is and is not a four-vector

The three-force F is not the space part of a four-vector: its components transform with the extra factors above. K is a four-vector, F is not. Covariance of the *law* is a statement about K = dP/dτ. The physics enters through the form of K (electromagnetic force, standard, verify: K = qγ_u(E·u/c, E + u × B)).

Check: for u ≪ c, γ_u → 1 and K → (F·u/c, F), F = dp/dt → m₀a, Newton's law; and K·U = γ_u²(F·u − F·u) = 0 as in PH-033 module 9.

### 5. Core: the wave equation is Lorentz invariant, chain rule step by step (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Show that the Galilean transformation changes the form of the wave equation and the Lorentz transformation leaves it unchanged, writing every chain-rule step.

## The statement

For light in vacuum, each Cartesian component ψ of the electric or magnetic field satisfies (standard, derived from Maxwell's equations with no charges; not in the extracts, flagged)

$$ ∂²ψ/∂x² + ∂²ψ/∂y² + ∂²ψ/∂z² − (1/c²) ∂²ψ/∂t² = 0 $$

Write the operators as D_x = ∂/∂x, D_t = ∂/∂t, D′_x = ∂/∂x′, D′_t = ∂/∂t′. The left side is ◻ψ with ◻ = D_x² + D_y² + D_z² − D_t²/c². We want ◻ in terms of primed derivatives. Assumptions: S′ moves at +v along +x, ψ is a function of position and time, v is constant.

## Galilean transformation: the form changes

With x′ = x − vt, y′ = y, z′ = z, t′ = t: ∂x′/∂x = 1, ∂t′/∂x = 0, ∂x′/∂t = −v, ∂t′/∂t = 1. So D_x = D′_x and D_t = D′_t − vD′_x. Square: D_t² = D′_t² − 2vD′_xD′_t + v²D′_x². Hence

$$ ◻ = (1 − v²/c²) D′_x² + (2v/c²) D′_xD′_t − (1/c²) D′_t² + D′_y² + D′_z² $$

There is a new cross term and a changed coefficient: **the wave equation is not Galilean covariant** (Python check with ψ = exp[i(3x′ − 5t′)] confirms the cross term). A plane wave would move at c − v in S′, but the equation in S′ would no longer be the same equation.

## Lorentz transformation: the form is unchanged

With x′ = γ(x − vt), t′ = γ(t − vx/c²), y′ = y, z′ = z:

∂x′/∂x = γ, ∂t′/∂x = −γv/c², ∂x′/∂t = −γv, ∂t′/∂t = γ. By the chain rule

$$ D_x = γ(D′_x − (v/c²)D′_t),   D_t = γ(D′_t − vD′_x),   D_y = D′_y,   D_z = D′_z $$

Square (γ and v constants, so the operators commute):

$$ D_x² = γ²[D′_x² − 2(v/c²)D′_xD′_t + (v²/c⁴)D′_t²] $$

$$ D_t²/c² = γ²[D′_t²/c² − 2(v/c²)D′_tD′_x + (v²/c²)D′_x²] $$

Subtract:

$$ D_x² − D_t²/c² = γ²[D′_x²(1 − v²/c²) − (D′_t²/c²)(1 − v²/c²) + (−2v/c² + 2v/c²) D′_xD′_t] $$

The cross terms cancel. Since γ²(1 − v²/c²) = 1,

$$ D_x² − D_t²/c² = D′_x² − D′_t²/c²,   so ◻ = ◻′ $$

The equation ◻ψ = 0 becomes ◻′ψ = 0: same form, same constant c. The speed of light is c in S′ as well: the Lorentz transformation, built on the second postulate, is exactly the one that leaves the wave equation unchanged, so the two are consistent.

```
 ∂x = γ(∂x′ − (v/c²)∂t′)     ∂t = γ(∂t′ − v ∂x′)
         |  square and subtract (cross terms cancel)  |
 ∂x² − ∂t²/c²  =  ∂x′² − ∂t′²/c²      (times γ²(1 − v²/c²) = 1)
```

## Components of E and B

The transformed field components are linear combinations of the old components with constant coefficients (standard, module 7), and each old component obeys ◻ψ = 0, so each new component obeys ◻′ψ = 0 as well. The same argument holds for a scalar ψ.

## Checks

- **Dimensions:** every term has dimension ψ/length². **Limit v → 0:** γ → 1, ◻′ = ◻.
- **Plane wave:** ψ = cos(kx − ωt), ω = ck, β = 0.6, k = 1: ω′ = γ(ω − vk) = 0.5c and k′ = γ(k − vω/c²) = 0.5, so ω′ = ck′ again.

### 6. Core: the list of invariants, the phase of a wave and the wave four-vector (derivation 3)

*Objective:* List the standard invariants, prove the transformation of (ω/c, k) from invariance of the phase, and show the invariant ω²/c² − k².

## Invariants: the 'scalars' of covariant equations

| Speed of light c | postulate 2 | PH-028 module 3 |
| Interval c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz² | cross terms cancel | PH-028 module 8 |

Electric charge is also taken as invariant (standard; verify). Note the difference between an invariant (a number) and the covariant equation that uses it: **the equation E² − p²c² = m₀²c⁴ is covariant because both sides are invariants.**

## Phase invariance and the wave four-vector (general k and ω)

PH-031 module 4 does this for light along +x. Here it is general. A wave crest is an event, and whether a given event lies on a crest cannot depend on the frame, so the phase φ = kx − ωt has the same value in S and S′ at every event. Put in x = γ(x′ + vt′), t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²) (PH-028 module 5):

$$ φ = γk(x′ + vt′) − γω(t′ + vx′/c²) = γ(k − vω/c²)x′ − γ(ω − vk)t′ $$

Matching with φ = k′x′ − ω′t′ gives

$$ k′ = γ(k − vω/c²),   ω′ = γ(ω − vk),   k′_y = k_y,   k′_z = k_z $$

Divide the second by c: ω′/c = γ(ω/c − βk), k′ = γ(k − βω/c). This is exactly the rule for (ct, x) of PH-028: **(ω/c, k) is a four-vector** (the 'wave four-vector'). Its norm is invariant by the algebra of PH-028 module 8 (the cross terms cancel and γ²(1 − β²) = 1):

$$ ω′²/c² − k′² = ω²/c² − k² $$

Numerical check: ω = 2.0 × 10¹⁵ rad s⁻¹, k = ω/c, β = 0.6 gives ω′ = 1.25(1 − 0.6)ω = 1.0 × 10¹⁵ rad s⁻¹ and k′ = ω′/c; for the event x = 3 m, t = 2 × 10⁻⁸ s the phase is −2.0 × 10⁷ rad in S, and in S′ (x′ = −0.75 m, t′ = 1.75 × 10⁻⁸ s) it is also −2.0 × 10⁷ rad.

For light the invariant is zero (ω = ck in every frame). For a matter wave with E = ħω and p = ħk (standard, verify) the invariant equals m₀²c²/ħ²; the speed relation v_p v_g = c² for such waves is worked in PH-041 module 7, which uses the same phase convention kx − ωt.

## The four-volume

For the boost along x, the Jacobian of (x, t) → (x′, t′) is the determinant of [[γ, −γv], [−γv/c², γ]], which is γ² − γ²v²/c² = 1, with y and z unchanged. So dx′dy′dz′dt′ = dx dy dz dt. Mathur Example 6 (PDF p. 135) and Upadhyaya Example 3 (PDF p. 367) reach this (Mathur's Example 6 is heuristic, dx′ = γdx and dt′ = dt/γ; the Jacobian is the proof); the three-volume alone is not invariant. (As extracted, Mathur's line reads dt′ = dt although the next line divides by γ: an apparent extraction slip; verify.)

### 7. Core: Maxwell's equations and the conservation laws in outline

*Objective:* Explain, without the full tensor machinery, why Maxwell's equations are Lorentz covariant, quote the field transformation with its invariants, and relate the conservation laws.

## What the sources say, and what they do not

Upadhyaya §6.6 and §6.8 (PDF pp. 331 and 334) say that Maxwell's equations play the role in electrodynamics that Newton's laws play in mechanics, that the principle of relativity requires them to keep the same form between inertial frames, that they are *not* invariant under Galilean transformations, and that this is one of the shortcomings of the Galilean rule. Upadhyaya names further four-vectors without developing them (PDF p. 365). The extracts therefore do not contain a proof of the Lorentz covariance of Maxwell's equations, nor the transformation of the fields. What follows is standard knowledge, flagged for verification, and the **full tensor form F^{μν} is beyond the minimum** needed here.

## The statement (standard, verify)

The microscopic Maxwell equations in vacuum, with sources ρ and J (∇·E = ρ/ε₀, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t, with c² = 1/μ₀ε₀) keep the same form under the Lorentz transformation, provided E, B, ρ and J are transformed by rules in which E and B mix. For a boost with S′ moving at +v along x:

$$ E′_x = E_x,  E′_y = γ(E_y − vB_z),  E′_z = γ(E_z + vB_y) $$

$$ B′_x = B_x,  B′_y = γ(B_y + vE_z/c²),  B′_z = γ(B_z − vE_y/c²) $$

The wave equation of module 5 follows from these equations in empty space, which is why its Lorentz invariance is a consequence of the covariance of Maxwell's equations and of the second postulate.

## Python-verified invariants and an example

Using the rules above, sympy confirms that E² − c²B² and E·B are unchanged by the boost.

*Number.* S has only E = (0, 3.0 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹, 0) and B = 0; S′ moves at β = 0.6 (γ = 1.25). Then E′_y = 1.25 × 3.0 × 10⁵ = 3.75 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹ and B′_z = 1.25(0 − 0.6c × 3.0 × 10⁵/c²) = −1.25 × 0.6 × 3.0 × 10⁵/(3 × 10⁸) = −7.5 × 10⁻⁴ T. Check: E′² − c²B′² = 1.40625 × 10¹¹ − (3 × 10⁸ × 7.5 × 10⁻⁴)² = 1.40625 × 10¹¹ − 5.0625 × 10¹⁰ = 9.0 × 10¹⁰ = E². A pure electric field in one frame is partly magnetic in another; the electric and magnetic fields are two faces of one object.

## Conservation laws

Mathur §3.11 (PDF p. 169) shows that if momentum is conserved in S and the law holds in every frame, then energy conservation follows, using p′_x = γ(p_x − vE/c²); the four-vector form ΣP_in = ΣP_out of PH-033 module 6 is one covariant equation. Charge conservation ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·J = 0 is covariant as the divergence of the four-current (ρc, J) (standard, verify).

### 8. Master: a procedure for checking covariance, with examples

*Objective:* Apply a five-step procedure to decide whether a given equation is covariant, including one equation that fails.

## The procedure

1. **Classify each term.** Is it an invariant scalar (c, m₀, the interval, dτ), a component of a four-vector (ct, x; E/c, p; ω/c, k), a three-vector component with its own rule (acceleration, force), or a derivative operator?
2. **Substitute.** Replace unprimed quantities by primed ones using the inverse transformation (PH-028 module 5), and derivatives by the chain rule (bridge B3).
3. **Simplify** and compare with the original form with primes in place of unprimed symbols.
4. **Decide.** The same form, with the same constants, means covariant under that transformation. An extra term, a changed coefficient or a frame-dependent constant means not covariant.
5. **Test with numbers** in two frames, and check the limit v → 0.

Shortcut: if both sides are scalars, or both sides are components of the same kind of four-vector, covariance is automatic (bridge B2).

## Example A: E² − p²c² = m²c⁴ (covariant)

Both sides are invariants: the left is the norm of P (PH-033 module 4), the right is a constant. The proof is the cross-term cancellation of PH-033 module 5.

## Example B: E = 2pc (not covariant)

Suppose a particle has E = 2pc in S. In S′, E′ = γ(E − vp) and p′c = γ(pc − βE). Setting E′ = 2p′c requires E − βpc = 2pc − 2βE, i.e. E(1 + 2β) = pc(2 + β). With E = 2pc this gives 2 + 4β = 2 + β, so β = 0. The equation holds only for v = 0. Numbers: E = 2 GeV, pc = 1 GeV (so m₀c² = √3 GeV), β = 0.6: E′ = 1.25(2 − 0.6) = 1.75 GeV and p′c = 1.25(1 − 1.2) = −0.25 GeV; 2p′c = −0.5 GeV ≠ 1.75 GeV. Contrast: **E = pc** (the photon) *is* covariant, because it says P·P = 0, an invariant statement: E′ = γ(1 − β)E and p′c = γ(1 − β)pc keep E′ = p′c.

## Example C: F = m₀a

Not covariant under Lorentz (module 3: a′ = a/γ³, F′ = F); covariant under Galilean.

## Example D: the trap E = γm₀c²

In S the particle moves at 0.6c: E = 1.25m₀c². In S′ moving with the particle, E′ = m₀c², *not* 1.25m₀c². The equation is true in every frame only if γ is read as **that frame's** γ for the particle; written as a statement about a fixed number γ it is a statement about components in one frame and is not covariant. The covariant way to say it is E = P⁰c with P a four-vector.

The verdict table is in the master notes.

## Traps

- **Invariance of a quantity versus covariance of an equation.** The interval is invariant; E² − p²c² = m²c⁴ is a covariant equation; a covariant equation can contain non-invariant components such as E.
- **Scalar versus component.** An equation that looks covariant in one component (E = γm₀c²) may hide a frame-dependent γ.
- **Sign of v:** for the inverse transformation, v → −v.

### Notes: Definitions

- Invariant: a quantity with the same value in every inertial frame (c, rest mass, interval, proper time, E² − p²c², phase).
- Covariant equation: an equation that keeps the same form under a transformation because each term transforms in the same way.
- Conserved quantity: a quantity that stays constant in time in one frame during a process (for example total momentum of an isolated system).
- Principle of relativity: the laws of physics take the same form in all inertial frames (Mathur §3.4; Upadhyaya §6.3).
- Four-force: K = dP/dτ = γ_u(F·u/c, F); a four-vector, while the three-force F is not.
- d'Alembertian: ◻ = ∂²/∂x² + ∂²/∂y² + ∂²/∂z² − (1/c²)∂²/∂t².

### Notes: Core equations

- Galilean: x′ = x − vt, t′ = t, a′ = a, F′ = F; Lorentz: x′ = γ(x − vt), t′ = γ(t − vx/c²), y′ = y, z′ = z.
- Acceleration: a′_x = a_x/[γ³(1 − vu_x/c²)³]; at u = 0, a′ = a/γ³.
- Relativistic force: F = dp/dt with p = γm₀u; F = γ³m₀a along the velocity; dE/dt = F·u.
- Force transformation: F′_x = (F_x − vF·u/c²)/(1 − vu_x/c²); F′_y = F_y/[γ(1 − vu_x/c²)].
- Four-force: K = γ_u(F·u/c, F); dP^μ/dτ = K^μ is covariant.
- Chain rule: ∂_x = γ(∂′_x − (v/c²)∂′_t), ∂_t = γ(∂′_t − v∂′_x); ◻ = ◻′.
- Wave four-vector: k′ = γ(k − vω/c²), ω′ = γ(ω − vk); ω²/c² − k² invariant; kx − ωt invariant.
- Fields (standard, verify): E′_y = γ(E_y − vB_z), B′_z = γ(B_z − vE_y/c²); E² − c²B² and E·B invariant.

### Notes: Derivation summary (15-mark version of the wave-equation proof)

- Assumptions: vacuum wave equation for a Cartesian field component; S′ at +v along +x; axes parallel; origins coincide.
- Chain rule: ∂x′/∂x = γ, ∂t′/∂x = −γv/c², ∂x′/∂t = −γv, ∂t′/∂t = γ.
- Square: D_x² = γ²[D′_x² − 2(v/c²)D′_xD′_t + (v²/c⁴)D′_t²]; D_t²/c² = γ²[D′_t²/c² − 2(v/c²)D′_tD′_x + (v²/c²)D′_x²].
- Subtract: cross terms cancel; γ²(1 − v²/c²) = 1 leaves D′_x² − D′_t²/c².
- Conclusion: ◻ = ◻′; the speed of light is c in S′. Galilean contrast: extra cross term (2v/c²)D′_xD′_t.

### Look-alike contrasts

- - **id:** X-INVARIANT-COVARIANT
  - **a:**
    - **topicId:** PH-034
    - **text:** Invariant: a quantity with the same value in every frame (c, interval, proper time, rest mass, phase)
  - **b:**
    - **topicId:** PH-034
    - **text:** Covariant: an equation keeping the same form in every frame (E² − p²c² = m₀²c⁴, ◻ψ = 0, dP/dτ = K)
  - **difference:** Invariance is a property of a number; covariance of an equation. A covariant equation's components may still mix between frames.
