### Bridge: Trade vocabulary: charter, factory, farman and friends

## Words you will meet

- **Charter**: a written grant from a ruler giving a company defined rights. The English charter of 1600 gave the Company the sole right to trade with the East, so no other English group could compete (NCERT).
- **Monopoly**: the exclusive right to trade in a place or a commodity.
- **Factory**: not a place where goods are made. NCERT describes the factory as the base from which the Company's traders, called **factors**, worked: a warehouse for export goods plus offices for officials.
- **Mercantile**: a business that makes its profit mainly by trade, buying cheap and selling dear (NCERT). Mercantile companies of the period tried to earn by excluding competitors.
- **Farman**: a royal edict or order. Mughal farmans gave the Company trading privileges.
- **Dastak**: a pass that exempted the named goods from duty. Its misuse becomes important in Bengal (module 8).
- **Entrepot**: a port that collects goods and sends them on; Calicut's prosperity rested on this role, according to Spectrum.
- **Presidency**: a large administrative unit of Company territory, each under a Governor. NCERT names three: Bengal, Madras and Bombay.

Keep these eight words ready; every later module uses at least four of them.

### Bridge: Order of arrival, old and new place names, and which century is 1600?

## Order of arrival

Spectrum's summary lists the arrivals as Portuguese first, followed by the Dutch, English, Danes and French. Use the memory line **P-D-E-D-F**. The first dates to attach are the Portuguese 1498, the English charter 1600, the Dutch company 1602, the Danish company 1616 and the French company 1664.

## Century arithmetic, one more time

GS-024 bridge B1 explains century counting. Apply it here: the 1st century CE is years 1 to 100, so the 16th century is **1501 to 1600**. The year **1600**, when the English charter was issued, therefore belongs to the *sixteenth* century, and the **first quarter of the 17th century** means **1601 to 1625**. Exam questions use such phrases.

## Names that changed

Sources use older spellings. Calicut is Kozhikode, Cochin is Kochi, Cannanore is Kannur, Bassein is Vasai, Masulipatnam is Machilipatnam, Nagapatam is Nagapattinam, Tranquebar is Tharangambadi, Trichinopoly is Tiruchirappalli, Madras is Chennai, Calcutta is Kolkata and Pondicherry is Puducherry. Spectrum and NCERT use the old forms; UPSC may use either.

### 1. Orientation: why Europeans sailed east and who came

*Objective:* Say in your own words why Europeans wanted a sea route to India and list the five arrivals in order.

## What this topic covers

Between 1498 and the 1760s five European groups reached India by sea: the Portuguese, the Dutch, the English, the Danes and the French. Each came as a trader backed by a king or a charter, built bases on the coast and competed for the same goods. By about 1760 only the English company remained a political contender. This lesson follows that story until trade turns into territory. The next steps are separate topics: GS-026 (Carnatic Wars and Anglo-Mysore conflict, Day 11) and GS-027 (Plassey, Buxar and the rise of the Company, Day 12) are to come and not yet written, so module 7 and module 8 give only the outline the sources support. For the Indian political background read GS-024.

Spectrum opens with a reminder that history is continuous: the story of 'modern' India starts in the fifteenth century, before the Mughal empire was founded.

## Why a sea route?

Spectrum gives a chain of reasons.

1. Indian goods (spices, cotton cloth, silk, precious stones) reached Europe through Arab Muslim middlemen. The Red Sea route was a state monopoly of Islamic rulers, and in **1453** Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks. Buying through middlemen was costly: according to Spectrum, Europeans buying pepper elsewhere would have paid ten times as much as the Portuguese paid with direct access.
2. Europe was changing: Renaissance curiosity, better ships and navigation, a growing farm economy and rising demand for luxury goods. Spices were wanted for cooking and for preserving meat.
3. Venice and Genoa were too small to challenge the Turks. Portugal and Spain took up exploration, helped with money and men by north Europeans and with ships and technical knowledge by the Genoese. Prince Henry of Portugal, called the Navigator, was obsessed with finding the ocean route.
4. Religion and commerce supported each other. Satish Chandra, quoted by Spectrum, says a direct sea link would hit the Muslims, the traditional enemies of Christianity, and displace the Arab and Turkish hold on eastern trade.

## Two corrections for beginners

- Vasco da Gama did not open Asia's trade to the world. The Indian Ocean already had Indian, Arab, African, Chinese and Javanese traders who followed unwritten rules and none sought overwhelming dominance. The Portuguese tried to **monopolise** the trade by force, and that is what changed.
- The five groups did not arrive together. Keep the order in bridge B2 and the three guiding questions: why did each come, what did each leave, and why did only the English remain?


### 2. Foundation: how company trade worked and what India offered

*Objective:* Explain why trading companies could not separate trade from politics and name what they bought in India.

## How the business worked

A European company obtained a royal charter, raised money from shareholders, sent ships east and bought Indian goods at a low price to sell at a higher one in Europe or in Asia. NCERT calls this **mercantile** trading and says the companies made profit mainly by excluding competition. A wider doctrine called **mercantilism** (states grow rich by exporting more than they import and by collecting bullion) is general background that the extracts do not teach; treat the label as standard knowledge to verify.

Spectrum adds that the Dutch, English and French carried Indian textiles on to Malacca and Java to swap them for spices, so Indian cloth served a larger Asian trade.

## What India offered

- **Textiles**: cotton longcloth, salempores (staple cloth), morees (better cloth), painted cloths and prints, silks and dyes. Demand came from Europe and from China, Japan and the Indonesian archipelago, and Indians had traded textiles for centuries before the Europeans came.
- **Spices and others**: pepper, cloves, cardamom and cinnamon (NCERT). Spectrum's list of Indian exports also includes indigo, saltpetre, opium, rice, sugar and precious stones.
- Indian trade usually left India richer in bullion: Spectrum says exports exceeded imports and the balance was paid in silver and gold, so India was called a sink of precious metals.

## A crowded, organised market

The Europeans entered a complex system. Indian merchants ran large fleets; weavers and merchants worked with overland carriers and brokers who worked with exporters and shipowners; agents bargained with local officials for privileges. Newcomers had to learn the rules and work with local partners. The factories of all the companies stood in practically the same places, and early on demand even exceeded what weavers could supply.

## Why trade became politics

NCERT explains the pressure. Every company wanted the same goods, so competition pushed up buying prices and cut profit; the only way to flourish was to remove rivals. Through the 17th and 18th centuries they sank each other's ships, blockaded routes and fortified trading posts. Fortifying a post and carrying arms brought conflict with local rulers. The Company therefore found it difficult to separate trade from politics.

One more link in the chain: Spectrum notes that Asian trade also fed the slave trade. French ships took cowries and Indian textiles to West Africa, where they were exchanged for slaves sent to the Americas.


### 3. Core: the Portuguese, 1498 to decline

*Objective:* Narrate Portuguese arrival, the three governors, the sources of their strength and the reasons for decline.

## Arrival and the first clashes

In **May 1498** three ships under Vasco da Gama, guided by a Gujarati pilot named Abdul Majid (Spectrum's name; identity disputed), reached Calicut. The Zamorin, whose kingdom prospered as an entrepot, welcomed him; Arab traders on the Malabar coast were uneasy. Da Gama sold his cargo at a huge profit. In 1500 Cabral set up a factory at Calicut, but local people attacked it; Cabral seized Arab ships, bombarded Calicut and made treaties with Cochin and Cannanore. Da Gama's next visit (Spectrum's text says 1501, its summary says 1502; the second voyage is usually dated 1502, verify) ended any friendship with the Zamorin. Calicut, Cannanore and Cochin became Portuguese trade centres, and the Portuguese, claiming to protect them, won permission to fortify.

## Three governors

- **Francisco de Almeida** (1505 to 1509): sent as governor for three years with force to protect Portuguese interests and to destroy Muslim trade. His **Blue Water policy** meant mastering the sea instead of relying on forts on land. The combined Egyptian and Gujarat fleets, encouraged by Venetian merchants, challenged him, and he crushed them.
- **Alfonso de Albuquerque** (1509 to 1515): Spectrum calls him the real founder of Portuguese power. He took **Goa** from Bijapur in 1510, built bases overlooking the entrances to the Indian Ocean, introduced a permit system for other ships and encouraged his men to marry local women. He is credited with abolishing sati in Portuguese territory but is also remembered for persecuting Muslims.
- **Nino da Cunha** (1529 to 1538): moved the capital from Cochin to Goa about 1530. Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, hard pressed by Humayun, ceded Bassein in 1534 and promised a base at Diu; relations soured and he was killed in 1537 while negotiating on a Portuguese ship.

## Strength and religion

Indian states had no navy worth the name, the Arabs lacked the Portuguese unity, and Portuguese ships carried cannon. By mid-century they held a long coastal chain: Goa, Bassein to Daman and Diu, ports in Malabar, San Thome and Nagapattinam, later Hooghly in Bengal. Portuguese men settled as landlords and artisans and brought tobacco and cashew nut. Policy towards religion hardened: intolerant to Muslims from the start, tolerant to Hindus at first, until the Inquisition at Goa. Yet Akbar invited Jesuit priests to his court in 1579-80, and Jahangir favoured them at times.

## Why they declined

1. Jahangir's anger at Portuguese piracy and the 1613 seizures; the English naval success of 1612 impressed him. Shah Jahan besieged and took Hooghly in 1632 after the Portuguese seized slave girls of Mumtaz Mahal.
2. The rise of strong neighbours, above all the Marathas, who took Salsette and Bassein in 1739.
3. Resentment of Jesuit activity and conversion.
4. Corruption, piracy and clandestine trade; the shift of colonising energy to Brazil; the union with Spain in 1580-81, which dragged Portugal into Spain's wars; and the rise of the Dutch and English. Goa lost importance after Vijayanagara fell.

The Portuguese were the first Europeans to arrive and the last to leave: Goa, Daman and Diu were recovered only in **1961**. Their lasting legacy lies in naval warfare, cannon, drilled infantry (the model for later sepoy armies) and shipping organisation.


### 4. Core: the Dutch and the Danes

*Objective:* Explain Dutch strengths, why they turned to Indonesia, and what the Danes were known for.

## The Dutch

Dutch merchants reached the East in 1596 when Cornelis de Houtman arrived in Sumatra and Bantam. In **1602** the States-General merged several trading companies into the United East India Company of the Netherlands, empowered to wage war, make treaties, take possession of territory and erect fortresses.

In India the Dutch founded their first factory at **Masulipatnam** in 1605 and then spread along the Coromandel coast and into Gujarat, present-day Uttar Pradesh, Bengal and Bihar. Pulicat followed (Spectrum gives 1609 in its text and 1610 in its summary), and they took **Nagapatam** from the Portuguese as their main southern base. In the 1650s and 1660s they won Cochin and the Portuguese forts of the Malabar coast. Most of their business was the carrying trade: they took Indian goods to the Far East, including Yamuna valley and Central Indian indigo, textiles and silk from Bengal, Gujarat and the Coromandel, Bihar saltpetre, and opium and rice from the Ganga valley. The master notes list the principal factories.

## Why they withdrew from the Indian race

- Their real prize was the Spice Islands of Indonesia, where they monopolised black pepper and spices. Spectrum says the Dutch were not interested in empire building in India.
- Rivalry with the English turned bloody. The climax was **Amboyna** (1623), where the Dutch massacred ten Englishmen and nine Japanese. After prolonged war a **compromise in 1667** left the English withdrawing their claims in Indonesia while the Dutch concentrated on their more profitable trade there. The Dutch also drew away to the Malay archipelago.
- In the third Anglo-Dutch War (1672 to 1674) Surat and Bombay were cut off and the Dutch captured three English ships in the Bay of Bengal. A later Dutch defeat in **November 1759** (Spectrum calls it the Battle of Hooghly in one place and the Battle of Bidara in another, near Chinsura; it came after Mir Jafar had intrigued with the Dutch) dealt a crushing blow to Dutch ambitions in India.
- Spectrum adds that the Dutch company went bankrupt in 1800.

## The Danes

The Danish East India Company dates from 1616; in 1620 it founded a factory at **Tranquebar**, near Tanjore, and its chief settlement was **Serampore** near Calcutta. Spectrum says the Danish factories were never important and were sold to the British in **1845**. The Danes are better known for missionary work than for trade.

A caution for the Prelims: Spectrum does not say who granted the Dutch their east-coast land. Standard accounts (verify): the imperial Gajapati kingdom of Odisha collapsed by 1568, decades before the Dutch reached the Coromandel coast in 1605; the Masulipatnam factory rested on a Golconda (Qutb Shahi) grant, and Pulicat on a grant from the Vijayanagara (Aravidu) court at Chandragiri (see module 10 and the completeness table).


### 5. Core: the English Company and its bases

*Objective:* Trace the English Company from the 1600 charter to the three presidencies and the 1708 merger.

## Charter and motives

Francis Drake's voyage round the world (1580) and the defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588) gave English sailors confidence, and news of Portuguese profits tempted merchants. In 1599 a group of merchants formed a company. On **31 December 1600** Queen Elizabeth I issued a charter to the 'Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies', with exclusive trade rights for 15 years; in 1609 this was extended indefinitely. As the Dutch were concentrating on the East Indies, the English turned to India for textiles.

## West: Surat, Bombay

Captain **Hawkins** reached Surat in 1608 with a letter from James I and was received by Jahangir in 1609, but Portuguese opposition defeated his mission and he left in 1611. After **Captain Best** beat the Portuguese at sea in 1612, Jahangir allowed a factory at **Surat** (1613). **Sir Thomas Roe**, ambassador of James I from 1615 to 1619, won no commercial treaty but secured privileges, including permission for factories at Agra, Ahmedabad and **Broach**. **Bombay** was given to Charles II as the dowry of Catherine of Braganza (1662) and passed to the Company in 1668 for a token annual rent of 10 pounds; in 1687 the western headquarters moved from Surat to Bombay.

## South and east

On the south-east coast the English traded at Masulipatnam from 1611 and set up a factory in 1616 (Spectrum 1616; often given as 1611, verify). The Sultan of Golconda's **Golden Farman** of 1632 allowed free trade in his ports for 500 pagodas a year. In 1639 Francis Day obtained from the ruler of Chandragiri (then the seat of the Vijayanagara (Aravidu) king; the grant came through his nayaka Damarla Venkatadri, verify) permission to build a fortified factory at **Madras**, which became Fort St George and replaced Masulipatnam as headquarters. In the east they opened factories in Orissa in 1633, and in 1651 Shah Shuja, governor of Bengal, allowed trade in return for Rs 3,000 a year; **Hooghly** was the first Bengal factory (NCERT also dates the first factory on the Hugli to 1651).

Customs posts still obstructed trade, so the Company wanted a fortified post. War with the Mughals followed, and Hooghly was sacked in 1686. Job Charnock negotiated a return, and an imperial farman of 10 February 1691 permitted trade on payment of Rs 3,000 a year. In 1696 the English fortified Sutanuti, using a local zamindar's rebellion as a pretext. In 1698 they purchased the zamindari of three villages, Sutanuti, Gobindapur and Kalikata, for Rs 1,200 (NCERT says they bribed Mughal officials to obtain these rights). The fort was named **Fort William** in 1700, seat of the eastern presidency. **Madras, Bombay and Calcutta** were now the three bases.

## Farmans and the merger

An English mission to Farrukhsiyar secured farmans that Spectrum calls the Company's Magna Carta: in Bengal, imports and exports were exempt from extra duties apart from the Rs 3,000 payment, and the Company could issue dastaks. Spectrum prints 1715 for the mission and 1717 for the farman; teach 1717 for the grants and 1715 for the Surman mission. A rival English company was chartered in 1698 (Sir William Norris went to Aurangzeb's court in 1701-02); under Crown and Parliament pressure the two merged in **1708** as the United Company of Merchants of England Trading to the East Indies.


### 6. Core: the French Company in India

*Objective:* Narrate French company origins, the Pondicherry base and the setbacks before 1740.

## A late and state-led company

Spectrum calls the French the last Europeans to come to India for trade. Louis XIV's minister Colbert laid the foundation of the **Compagnie des Indes Orientales in 1664**, with a 50-year monopoly of French trade in the Indian and Pacific Oceans. The company spent heavily on Madagascar without success. Francois Caron led an expedition that set up a factory at **Surat** (Spectrum says 1667; many books give 1668, verify). A Persian, Mercara, who travelled with Caron, founded a factory at **Masulipatnam** in 1669 with a patent from the Sultan of Golconda. In 1673 the Mughal subahdar Shaista Khan allowed the French to establish a township at **Chandernagore** (other books date the actual foundation later, c. 1690, verify).

## Pondicherry, the nerve centre

In 1673 Sher Khan Lodi, the governor of Valikondapuram under the Bijapur Sultan, granted Francois Martin, director of the Masulipatnam factory, a settlement site. **Pondicherry** was founded in 1674 and Martin became French governor. The French also had Mahe, Karaikal, Balasore and Qasim Bazar. Spectrum notes that Pondicherry was finally incorporated into the Indian Union in 1954; that was the de facto transfer, and the de jure transfer was 1962 (verify).

## Setbacks and revival

- The Dutch, allied with the English since the Revolution of 1688, captured Pondicherry in 1693; the Treaty of Ryswick (September 1697) returned it, though the Dutch garrison stayed two more years.
- The War of Spanish Succession forced the French to leave Surat, Masulipatnam and Bantam early in the 18th century, and Martin died on 31 December 1706.
- In **1720** the company was reorganised as the Perpetual Company of the Indies. Two governors, Lenoir and Dumas (1720 to 1742), strengthened it, and French Mauritius and Reunion in the Indian Ocean gave it island bases.

The structural problem recurs in module 9: the French company was controlled and financed by the state, and its fate was tied to wars in Europe. That is why the Anglo-French rivalry in India, taught next, followed every European war between the two powers.


### 7. Core: the Anglo-French struggle, the Carnatic Wars

*Objective:* Narrate the three Carnatic Wars and explain how the French lost their chance of empire.

## Background

**Carnatic** was the Europeans' name for the Coromandel coast and its hinterland. The British and French had come to trade, but both were drawn into Indian politics. Spectrum says the Anglo-French rivalry mirrored their European wars: it began with the Austrian War of Succession and ended with the Seven Years' War. Around 1740 south India lacked a strong ruler. The Nizam of Hyderabad was old and busy with the Marathas, and small states (Madura, Tanjore, Trichinopoly) lay to the south; GS-024 module 6 describes Hyderabad and the Carnatic as successor states.

## First war (Spectrum 1740 to 1748)

An extension of the European war (Spectrum counts 1740-48 from the European war; fighting in India is usually dated 1746-48, verify). French reluctance to fight in India was overcome when the English navy seized French ships; the French governor of Mauritius, La Bourdonnais, took **Madras in 1746**. At the Battle of **St. Thome** a small French army under Captain Paradise defeated the much larger army of Anwaruddin, Nawab of the Carnatic, to whom the English had appealed. The lesson for Europeans was that a small disciplined army could beat a big Indian one. The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) returned Madras to the English.

## Second war (1749 to 1754)

Joseph Dupleix, the French governor, interfered in local succession disputes. When Nizam-ul-Mulk died in 1748, the French backed Muzaffar Jang for Hyderabad and **Chanda Sahib** in the Carnatic; the English backed Nasir Jang and Anwaruddin. At **Ambur** in 1749 Anwaruddin was killed, Muzaffar Jang became subahdar and the French general **Bussy** was posted at Hyderabad at the subahdar's expense. Spectrum calls Dupleix the first European to interfere in Indian internal politics and the originator of the subsidiary alliance practice.

The English recovered through **Robert Clive's** raid on Arcot in 1751 (with 210 men, according to Spectrum) to draw Chanda Sahib away from Trichinopoly. Chanda Sahib's siege of 53 days failed, Mysore, Tanjore and the Maratha Morari Rao helped relieve Trichinopoly, and Chanda Sahib was executed in 1752. The French company, angry at the cost, **recalled Dupleix in 1754**. His successor Godeheu agreed with the English that neither side would meddle in the quarrels of Indian princes (Spectrum gives no year; the treaty of Pondicherry was concluded in December 1754 and took effect in January 1755, verify). Spectrum's lesson: Indian rulers like Muhammad Ali and Salabat Jang were becoming clients of European support.

## Third war (1758 to 1763)

Part of the Seven Years' War. Lally took St David and other English posts in 1758, but English naval and military pressure turned the tide. At **Wandiwash (22 January 1760)** Sir Eyre Coote routed Lally and took Bussy prisoner; Pondicherry surrendered on **16 January 1761**. The **Treaty of Paris (1763)** returned French factories but, Spectrum's summary says, they were for commerce only and fortifications were banned. French political influence in India ended. The Dutch had already been beaten in 1759.

## An interpretive point

The Battle of Plassey (1757) is usually seen as the decisive event, but Spectrum points out a view that Wandiwash was the true turning point, because it left the Company with no European rival.


### 8. Core: from trade to territory in Bengal, 1651 to 1765

*Objective:* Explain how conflict over trade privileges led to Plassey, Buxar and the Diwani, and why that was a hand-off to conquest.

## Why Bengal mattered

Spectrum says Bengal was the Mughal empire's richest province and its exports to Europe included saltpetre, rice, indigo, pepper, sugar, silk and cotton textiles; nearly 60 per cent of British imports from Asia came from Bengal. The Company paid Rs 3,000 a year for duty-free trade while its exports were worth over 50,000 pounds a year. Calcutta's population rose from 15,000 (1706) to 100,000 (1750).

## The quarrel over privileges

NCERT explains the friction. The Mughal farman exempted the Company's goods from duty, but officials of the Company carried on private trade and refused to pay, costing Bengal much revenue. Spectrum adds that servants even sold dastaks to Indian merchants for a commission. After Aurangzeb's death the nawabs of Bengal (Murshid Quli Khan, then Alivardi Khan, then Sirajuddaulah) asserted autonomy, refused concessions, demanded tribute, denied the Company the right to mint coins and stopped its fortification. The Company said unjust demands ruined trade and that it needed more settlements.

## Plassey and Buxar

When Sirajuddaulah became nawab in 1756 the Company tried to help a rival, and the nawab seized the English factory at Kasimbazar and then Calcutta. Spectrum notes the Black Hole story, which historians doubt or say involved far fewer deaths. Clive came from Madras, and at **Plassey (23 June 1757)** the nawab's army was defeated; NCERT and Spectrum agree that the decisive factor was Mir Jafar, who had been promised the nawabship and whose troops did not fight. After Mir Jafar protested, the Company replaced him with Mir Qasim; when Mir Qasim also resisted, in particular by abolishing duties, he was defeated and the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Awadh's Shuja-ud-Daula and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II lost at **Buxar (22 October 1764)** to Major Munro. Spectrum stresses that not only a nawab but the emperor had been beaten.

## The Diwani of 1765

In August 1765, by the treaties of Allahabad, Shah Alam II granted the Company the **diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa for an annual payment, and Awadh was left as a buffer state. NCERT explains why it mattered: until then the Company bought most goods in India with gold and silver brought from Britain; after the Diwani, revenue from Bengal could buy textiles, maintain troops and build forts, and the outflow of gold stopped. NCERT also quotes Clive saying that the Company must itself become nawabs, once puppet nawabs proved unreliable. Under the dual system (1765 to 1772) the Company held revenue and, through its nominee, police and justice; Spectrum calls it disastrous for the people, and Warren Hastings ended it in 1772.

## The hand-off

This is only the outline. Plassey, Buxar and the Company's rise are taught in GS-027 (Day 12); subsidiary alliance and lapse in GS-028 (Day 13); both to come, not yet written. For the Company's wider expansion to 63 per cent of territory by 1857 see GS-024 module 9.


### 9. Master: why the English prevailed

*Objective:* List Spectrum's reasons for English success, test them against dates, and state the accidental versus intentional debate.

## Spectrum's list

Spectrum gives these reasons, which you should group into five families.

1. **Organisation.** The English company was a private enterprise with directors elected annually, and shareholders could influence it; this allowed quick decisions. The French company was a state concern, the king held over 60 per cent of the shares, the state guaranteed dividends, and there was no shareholders' meeting between 1725 and 1765 (Spectrum's claim).
2. **Sea power.** The Royal Navy was the largest and most advanced; it cut the vital sea link between France and French India. Spectrum quotes a view that English forces were supplied from Bengal and Europe, whereas French supplies arrived only slowly by land.
3. **Bases and money.** The English held Calcutta, Bombay and Madras against the French Pondicherry; the French sacrificed commerce to territorial ambition and were short of funds, while the English kept their commercial interests and had sound finances. Spectrum also credits the English use of debt markets and the Bank of England.
4. **Men.** English commanders (Coote, Lawrence, Clive) were many; the French had Dupleix, a brilliant planner who never led an army, and whose policy cost his company heavily.
5. **Wider conditions.** Stability at home against France's revolution and Napoleonic wars; less religious zeal than Portugal, Spain or the Dutch; and, in Spectrum's view, an Industrial Revolution that later strengthened England.

## Testing the list

Check each reason against the date. The Industrial Revolution is the weakest explanation of the 1740s and 1750s: the machines Spectrum names (spinning jenny, power loom, steam engine) are dated mostly to the later eighteenth century in standard accounts (verify), and Spectrum itself places the revolution in the early 18th century and claims it reached other nations late. It helps explain English advantage in the nineteenth century, not Wandiwash in 1760. Similarly, France's revolution (1789) and Napoleon's defeat (1815) come after the Carnatic Wars. Organisation, sea power, bases, finance and the Bengal revenue fit the mid-century.

## Indian factors

The fragmented-polity argument is in GS-024 module 9; here note only that Indian sepoys served in both armies at Wandiwash and that Spectrum adds superior English arms, discipline and salaries. Many rulers imported European arms and employed European officers, but their armies stayed imitators.

## Accidental or intentional?

John Seeley said the conquest was made in a fit of absent-mindedness; the opposing group said it was planned. Spectrum finds both overstated: Company officials first acquired territory to protect trade amid factionalism, but later British politicians and administrators worked to a plan. Judith Brown, quoted in the same place, says motives varied by region and time. Your safe answer: initially reactive, later intentional.

## What the eight PYQs test

Prelims questions on this topic ask for four things: dates of company foundation and factories; named ruler or governor for a Portuguese or English event; items such as plants or exports; and statements on land grants. Their analysis is in the PYQ notes; here are the patterns.

1. **Factory questions.** The 2021 question about factories in the first quarter of the 17th century needs the range 1601 to 1625 and a list of early English posts (Surat 1613, Masulipatnam 1616, factories at Agra, Ahmedabad and Broach under Roe). ## Three facts to learn from standard knowledge (verify)

These are not in the extracts, so check them in a standard book before relying on them.

- St Francis Xavier was a co-founder of the Jesuit order (Society of Jesus). He died in 1552 on an island off the coast of China, not in Goa; his body is enshrined in Goa, and his feast is observed there every year.
- Tobacco, cocoa and rubber were domesticated in the Americas (the 'New World') and spread to the Old World after 1492. Coffee, wheat and sugarcane are Old World crops. Cotton needs care: Old World cottons were grown in India, so a 'New World' claim for cotton is a trap.
- Papaya, pineapple and guava reached India through the Portuguese in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries according to standard accounts (verify).
