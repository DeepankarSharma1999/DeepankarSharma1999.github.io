### Bridge: Wave number, angular frequency and the complex exponential

Two numbers describe a sine wave. The **wavelength λ** is the length of one repeat in space; the **frequency f** is the number of repeats per second at one place (PH-039 bridge B1 revises both). Physicists prefer the *angular* versions: the **wave number k = 2π/λ** (radians of phase per metre) and the **angular frequency ω = 2πf** (radians of phase per second). Their ratio is the wave speed: v = fλ = ω/k.

A wave moving to +x is y = A cos(kx − ωt). Follow a crest: its phase kx − ωt stays constant, so k dx/dt = ω and dx/dt = ω/k (Brij Lal PDF p. 277 does the same for the 'phase velocity'). **Check with a textbook case:** y = 2 sin π(0.5x − 200t), x in cm, t in s (Brij Lal Example 12.2, PDF pp. 284 to 285). Comparing with sin(kx − ωt): k = 0.5π cm⁻¹, ω = 200π s⁻¹, so v = ω/k = 400 cm/s = 4 m/s, λ = 2π/k = 4 cm, f = 100 Hz. Recomputed: k = 2π/0.04 m = 157.08 m⁻¹ and ω = 628.3 s⁻¹ give 628.3/157.08 = 4.0 m/s.

**Complex exponentials, minimally.** Euler's formula says e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ, where i² = −1. Write a wave as ψ = A e^{i(kx − ωt)}; its real part is A cos(kx − ωt). Why bother? Adding waves of different k becomes adding exponentials, which is easier than adding cosines, and the wave equation is linear and real, so the real part of any complex solution is again a solution (Brij Lal PDF p. 277 introduces the complex form). Rule used in this lesson: *do the algebra with e^{i(…)}, and take the real part (or the modulus squared for intensity) at the end*. |ψ|² = ψψ* removes the oscillating phase.

### Bridge: The Gaussian integral, completing the square and rms width

A **Gaussian** is a bell curve e^{−a x²}. Everything about wave packets in this lesson rests on three small facts, all recomputed with sympy.

**Fact 1.** For a > 0, I = ∫ from −∞ to ∞ of e^{−a x²} dx = √(π/a). Why: I² = ∫∫ e^{−a(x² + y²)} dx dy. In polar coordinates (r dr dθ) I² = 2π ∫₀^∞ r e^{−a r²} dr = 2π × 1/(2a) = π/a.

**Fact 2.** ∫ x² e^{−a x²} dx = (1/2a)√(π/a) (differentiate Fact 1 with respect to a: −∫x² e^{−ax²}dx = d/da (√π a^{−1/2}) = −(√π/2) a^{−3/2}).

**Fact 3 (completing the square).** For a wave component we meet ∫ e^{−q²/(4σ²) + i q x} dq. Write −q²/(4σ²) + i q x = −(q − c)²/(4σ²) − σ²x² with c = 2iσ²x. Check: expand −(q − c)²/(4σ²) = −q²/(4σ²) + cq/(2σ²) − c²/(4σ²) = −q²/(4σ²) + i x q + σ²x², so subtracting σ²x² gives the original. The leftover q-integral is Fact 1 with a = 1/(4σ²): √(4πσ²) = 2σ√π. Result: **2σ√π e^{−σ²x²}**. (Shifting the integration line by an imaginary amount is allowed for a Gaussian: standard; verify. Sympy and a numerical integral both give the same function.)

**rms width.** For a non-negative distribution P(x), the root-mean-square width is σ_x = √(∫x²P dx / ∫P dx) about the centre. For P = e^{−x²/(2s²)} Facts 1 and 2 give σ_x = s. *Always say which function's width you mean:* the amplitude, or its square (intensity). The two differ by a factor √2.

### 1. Orientation: pulse, wave train, wave packet and what this topic asks

*Objective:* Say in your own words what a pulse, a wave train and a wave packet are, and where PH-040 stops and PH-041 begins.

A **pulse** is a single, localised disturbance: one jerk of a rope, one clap, one radar blip. NCERT (PDF p. 3) pictures a pulse travelling along a long string after one up-and-down jerk of the hand, and a continuous sinusoidal jerk instead gives a harmonic wave. A **wave train** is a finite stretch of sinusoidal oscillation, a few cycles long (Brij Lal PDF pp. 277 to 279 uses it for light from one atom). A **wave packet** is a localised disturbance built by adding many sinusoidal waves with a spread of wave numbers; Brij Lal says wave train and wave packet are used as synonyms (PDF p. 279). So 'pulse' is the *shape in space or time*, 'wave packet' is the *recipe* (a sum of sine waves), and the recipe explains the shape.

**The big idea.** A single pure sine wave, y = A cos(kx − ωt), has no beginning or end: it fills all space and is perfectly 'monochromatic' (one k). To build something *localised*, you must add waves of different k so that they reinforce in one place and cancel elsewhere. The more sharply you localise, the wider the spread of k you need. That trade-off, σ_x σ_k ≥ 1/2, is the main result of this lesson.

**Map.** Modules 2 to 4: a pulse travels, reflects and carries energy. Modules 5 and 6: Fourier synthesis and the Gaussian packet. Modules 7 and 8: bandwidth, examples, beats, dispersion named. Module 9: question types and traps.

### 2. Foundation: the travelling pulse y = f(x − vt) from the wave equation

*Objective:* Show by the chain rule that any shape f(x − vt) solves the wave equation, and use d'Alembert's form to follow a pulse released from rest.

**Recap, not repeated.** PH-039 module 2 derived the string equation ∂²y/∂t² = v² ∂²y/∂x², with v = √(T/μ), from Newton's law on an element. Here we only *solve* it for pulses. Coordinate: x along the string, y transverse (small slopes).

## Derivation: f(x − vt) is a solution
Let f be any twice-differentiable function of one variable and set s = x − vt, y(x, t) = f(s). The chain rule gives ∂s/∂x = 1 and ∂s/∂t = −v. Then:

1. ∂y/∂x = f′(s) (dy/ds × ∂s/∂x), so ∂²y/∂x² = f″(s).
2. ∂y/∂t = f′(s)(−v) = −v f′(s), so ∂²y/∂t² = −v f″(s) × (−v) = v² f″(s).
3. Hence ∂²y/∂t² = v² ∂²y/∂x². ∎ The same steps with s = x + vt (∂s/∂t = +v) give a solution g(x + vt).

The wave equation is linear, so **y = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt)** is also a solution. This is the d'Alembert form of the general solution (Mathur PDF pp. 493 to 494 states it as f₁(vt − x) + f₂(vt + x)).

## What 'f(x − vt)' means
At t = 0 the shape is f(x). At time t the value at x equals the old value at x − vt: the whole picture has slid to the right by vt. So f(x − vt) is a pulse moving at **+v without changing shape**, and g(x + vt) moves at −v. *Check the argument's units:* x − vt is a length, so f takes a length (or a pure number built from lengths). Harmonic waves are the special case f(s) = A cos(ks).

## Initial-value form (derivation)
Release at t = 0 with shape y(x, 0) = φ(x) and velocity ∂y/∂t(x, 0) = ψ₀(x). Then f(x) + g(x) = φ(x) and −v f′(x) + v g′(x) = ψ₀(x). Integrating the second: g(x) − f(x) = (1/v) ∫ψ₀ dx + C. Solving the pair for f and g and substituting gives

$$ y(x, t) = ½[φ(x − vt) + φ(x + vt)] + (1/2v) ∫ from x − vt to x + vt of ψ₀(s) ds $$

**Release from rest** (ψ₀ = 0): a hump φ splits into two copies of *half the height*, one going each way. For a Gaussian hump of height 1.0 cm each half has height 0.50 cm. Check at t = 0: ½[φ(x) + φ(x)] = φ(x) ✓.

## Alternative method
Put ξ = x − vt and η = x + vt. Then ∂/∂x = ∂/∂ξ + ∂/∂η and ∂/∂t = −v ∂/∂ξ + v ∂/∂η, so the wave equation becomes ∂²y/∂ξ∂η = 0. Integrate: ∂y/∂η is a function of η alone, and a second integration gives y = f(ξ) + g(η). Same result.

## Limits and traps
- **Non-dispersive.** The speed v = √(T/μ) does not depend on the pulse's shape, so every sine component of the pulse moves at v and the shape survives. If the speed depended on wavelength the shape would change: named here, handled in PH-041.
- **Sign trap.** x − vt (v > 0) goes right; x + vt goes left. f(vt − x) is also right-going.
- **Number.** String S of PH-039 (T = 40 N, μ = 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg/m): v = √(40/0.0040) = 100 m/s.

### 3. Core: superposition of pulses and reflection at fixed and free ends

*Objective:* Derive the reflected pulse at a fixed end (inverted) and at a free end (upright) from the boundary condition, and sketch two pulses passing through each other.

## Superposition of pulses (recap with a link)
PH-038 module 2 states the principle of superposition: for a linear equation the sum of two solutions is a solution. For pulses (NCERT PDF p. 10, Fig. 14.9): two pulses in opposite directions overlap, the net displacement is the **algebraic sum**, and afterwards each continues *as if the other were absent*. Two equal and opposite humps cancel completely at the instant of exact overlap (the string is straight), yet they emerge unchanged.

**Energy at the instant of cancellation.** y = 0 everywhere, so ∂y/∂x = 0 and the stored (potential) energy is zero; the energy is entirely kinetic, because ∂y/∂t is not zero (module 4 gives both densities). Check with f even: y = f(x − vt) − f(x + vt) gives ∂y/∂t at t = 0 equal to −2v f′(x), non-zero.

## Derivation: fixed end
Take the wall at x = 0 and the string on x < 0 (so the incident pulse moves toward +x). Incident: y_i = f(x − vt). Reflected pulse goes left: y_r = g(x + vt). Total y = y_i + y_r.

1. Boundary condition (the end cannot move): y(0, t) = 0 for all t.
2. f(−vt) + g(vt) = 0 for all t. Put u = vt (u runs over all values): g(u) = −f(−u).
3. Therefore y_r(x, t) = g(x + vt) = **−f(−x − vt)**.

Meaning: the reflected pulse has the same shape, *mirrored left-right and flipped in sign*; for a symmetric hump this is an inverted copy, a phase change of π (NCERT PDF p. 12 reaches the same result by superposition and by Newton's third law; Mathur PDF p. 498 gives the reflection coefficient −1). Physical reason: the pulse pulls the wall up, so the wall pulls the string down (Newton's third law).

## Derivation: free end
A free end is a massless ring on a smooth vertical rod, so no transverse force can act on it. The transverse force of the string on the ring is −T sin θ ≈ −T ∂y/∂x (small slope), so the condition is ∂y/∂x(0, t) = 0.

1. ∂y_i/∂x = f′(x − vt) → at x = 0: f′(−vt). ∂y_r/∂x = g′(x + vt) → at x = 0: g′(vt).
2. f′(−u) + g′(u) = 0 with u = vt, so g′(u) = −f′(−u).
3. Note d/du[f(−u)] = −f′(−u). Hence g(u) = f(−u) + C, and C = 0 because both vanish far from the pulse.
4. **y_r = + f(−x − vt)**: mirrored but *not* flipped (NCERT PDF p. 12: same phase and amplitude; the maximum displacement at the end is twice that of each pulse). At the wall y(0, t) = 2 f(−vt).

| | Fixed end | Free end |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary condition | y = 0 | ∂y/∂x = 0 |
| Reflected pulse | −f(−x − vt), inverted | +f(−x − vt), upright |
| Displacement at the end | 0 | 2 × incident |
| Phase change | π | 0 |

**Intermediate boundaries.** A ring of finite mass, or a join to a heavier or lighter string, gives partial reflection and partial transmission (Mathur PDF pp. 496 to 498, with Z₂ → ∞ and Z₂ → 0 as the fixed and free limits). Not derived here.

**Energy.** At the wall of the fixed end ∂y/∂t = 0, so the power the string delivers, −T ∂y/∂x ∂y/∂t (PH-039 module 5), is zero: the wall absorbs nothing and the pulse returns with the same energy.

### 4. Core: the energy a pulse carries and where it goes

*Objective:* Derive the energy densities, the total energy E = μv² ∫ f′² ds of a pulse and the statement that energy travels with the pulse at speed v.

**Recap, not repeated.** PH-039 module 5 derives the power across a section, S = −T ∂y/∂t ∂y/∂x, and shows zero net flow for a standing wave. Here we apply the same ideas to one pulse. Sign conventions: x to the right, y up, small slopes, tension T uniform, v² = T/μ.

## Derivation: energy densities
**Kinetic energy per unit length:** an element of mass μ dx moving at ∂y/∂t has ½ μ (∂y/∂t)² dx.

**Potential energy per unit length:** a slice dx lying at slope ∂y/∂x has length ds = dx √(1 + y_x²) ≈ dx (1 + ½ y_x²), an extra length ½ y_x² dx. Stretching against a constant T stores work T × ½ y_x² dx, so the potential energy per unit length is ½ T y_x² (tension taken as unchanged by the small stretch, longitudinal motion neglected; standard, verify) (Mathur PDF pp. 498 to 499 builds the energy of a travelling wave from the same two parts).

For y = f(s), s = x − vt (module 2): y_t = −v f′, y_x = f′. Hence

KE density = ½ μ v² f′², PE density = ½ T f′² = ½ μ v² f′² (using T = μv²).

**For a travelling pulse the kinetic and potential densities are equal at every point and instant.** (Contrast with a standing wave, where they exchange.) Total energy density u = μ v² f′².

## Total energy of the pulse
E = ∫ u dx = μ v² ∫ f′(x − vt)² dx. Substituting s = x − vt (dx = ds, limits unchanged) removes t entirely: **E = μ v² ∫ f′(s)² ds, constant in time**, as it must be for a loss-free string.

## Energy moves with the pulse
Power across a point (PH-039 module 5 form): S = −T y_x y_t = −T (f′)(−v f′) = T v f′² = μ v³ f′² = (μ v² f′²) v = **u v**. So energy density u is carried at speed v: energy travels with the pulse (Brij Lal PDF p. 282 says the group velocity is also the energy velocity of a packet; PH-041 treats that). Check with a harmonic wave: f = A cos(k s) gives ⟨u⟩ = μ v² A² k² ⟨sin²⟩ = ½ μ ω² A², the standard energy density of a travelling harmonic wave (Mathur PDF pp. 498 to 499).

## Number: Gaussian pulse
f(s) = h e^{−s²/w²}, f′ = −(2s/w²) f, so f′² = (4 s²/w⁴) h² e^{−2s²/w²}. Using ∫ s² e^{−a s²} ds = (1/2a)√(π/a) with a = 2/w² (bridge B2):

∫ f′² ds = (4h²/w⁴) × (w²/4)√(π w²/2) = h² √(π/2)/w

$$ E = μ v² h² √(π/2) / w $$

*Dimensional check:* μ v² has units of N (kg m s⁻²); times h²/w (m) gives N·m = J ✓. For string S (μ = 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg/m, v = 100 m/s), h = 1.0 cm, w = 5.0 cm: E = 40 × 10⁻⁴ × 1.2533/0.05 = **0.100 J** (checked numerically: 0.10027 J). *Limit:* E ∝ 1/w: squeeze the same height into a narrower pulse and the energy rises, because the slope is steeper.

## Momentum
The transverse momentum of the string is ∫ μ y_t dx = −μ v ∫ f′ ds = −μ v [f]_{−∞}^{+∞} = 0 for a localised pulse (f → 0 both sides): the elements go up and come back, so the *net transverse* momentum is zero. Energy moves along the string; mass does not. Any momentum carried along the string is a second-order effect not needed here (standard; verify).

### 5. Core: Fourier synthesis, a rectangular pulse and the Fourier pair

*Objective:* Build a pulse from sine waves numerically, see why a sharp edge needs many terms (Gibbs overshoot) and write the Fourier pair with its convention traps.

**Recap, not repeated.** PH-039 module 6 states the Fourier idea in *space* for a string fixed at both ends (a series in sin(nπx/L)). Now we need two further forms: a series for a *periodic* wave and an *integral* for a single non-repeating pulse.

## Periodic waves: the Fourier series
Mathur (PDF pp. 526 to 527): a periodic wave of period T, if finite, single-valued and with finitely many jumps per period, is a sum of sines and cosines with frequencies equal to integer multiples of 1/T. Example (standard result, recomputed): a square wave of height ±1 and period 2π is

$$ y(x) = (4/π) [ sin x + sin 3x / 3 + sin 5x / 5 + … ] $$

Partial sums at x = π/2 (true value 1): one term 1.2732, two 0.8488, three 1.1035, four 0.9216, five 1.0631 (sympy/numpy). They oscillate around 1 and slowly settle. *Gibbs overshoot:* near the jump the partial-sum peak tends to 1.1790 as terms are added (1.1884 with 3, 1.1798 with 10, 1.1790 with 50) and does not fall below it, an overshoot of about 9 percent of the jump of 2 (standard result; the peak moves toward the jump but does not shrink). Sharp edges need high-k terms.

## Non-periodic pulse: the Fourier integral
For a single pulse f(t) that is piecewise continuous with a finite ∫|f| dt, Brij Lal (PDF pp. 279 to 280) writes the pair f(t) = ∫ F(ω) e^{iωt} dω and F(ω) = (1/2π) ∫ f(t) e^{−iωt} dt, and also gives the symmetric form with 1/√(2π) on each side. Source slip: the book then says the factor can be omitted and writes eqs 12.28 to 12.29 with no 1/2π anywhere; that pair does not invert (it is off by 2π). The total 1/2π must appear once, either on one side or split as 1/√(2π) on both. In space, with wave number k:

$$ F(k) = ∫ f(x) e^{−ikx} dx,   f(x) = (1/2π) ∫ F(k) e^{ikx} dk $$

*Traps:* (i) the placement of 1/2π and the sign in the exponent differ between books, so the formula you quote must be a consistent pair; (ii) f in hertz (e^{2πiνt}, Brij Lal PDF p. 280) versus ω in rad/s changes factors of 2π; (iii) the constant in 'width × width' depends on which widths you use (module 6).

## Rectangular pulse
f(x) = 1 for |x| < a/2, 0 otherwise. Then

F(k) = ∫ from −a/2 to a/2 of e^{−ikx} dx = [e^{−ikx}/(−ik)] = 2 sin(ka/2)/k = a · sin(ka/2)/(ka/2)

(sympy confirms). The spectrum vanishes first at ka/2 = π, i.e. **k = 2π/a**, and has a main lobe containing most of the 'power', as Brij Lal PDF p. 281 finds in time form: the book says the intensity falls to zero at ν₀ − Δν/2 and ν₀ + Δν/2, while its sinc sin{π(ν − ν₀)Δt}/(π(ν − ν₀)Δt) vanishes at ν₀ ± 1/Δt (recomputed), so this Δν is 2/Δt exactly. A narrow pulse has a wide spectrum. *Two cautions:* (a) if you take the full distance between the first zeros you get Δν Δt = 2, while Brij Lal states Δν Δt ~ 1 as an order of magnitude (PDF p. 281); say which definition you use; (b) the rms width of this spectrum is **infinite** (∫k² |F|² dk grows without limit: the sinc² tail falls only as 1/k²), so rms widths are not used for sharp-edged pulses, which is why Gaussians (smooth) are used in module 6.

**Check of the limit.** a → ∞: first zero at 2π/a → 0, a spike at k = 0 (an infinite constant wave). a → 0 with the area held fixed (height 1/a): F → a constant, a delta pulse containing all k equally.

### 6. Core: the Gaussian wave packet and σ_x σ_k = 1/2

*Objective:* Derive the Gaussian packet from a Gaussian spread of wave numbers, find its width and show that the product of rms widths is exactly 1/2.

## Set-up
Build the packet at t = 0 by superposing waves e^{ikx} with Gaussian weights:

$$ ψ(x, 0) = ∫ A(k) e^{ikx} dk,   A(k) = exp[ −(k − k₀)² / (4σ_k²) ] $$

k₀ is the central wave number, and (bridge B2) |A|² = exp[−(k − k₀)²/(2σ_k²)] is a Gaussian whose rms width is **σ_k**. (Defining A with 4σ² in the denominator is a choice so that σ_k is the rms width of the *intensity* |A|².) Sign: every component moves right once time is added. (In the module 5 convention A(k) = F(k)/2π; constant factors do not change any width.)

## Derivation
1. Put q = k − k₀: ψ(x, 0) = e^{ik₀x} ∫ e^{−q²/(4σ_k²) + iqx} dq.
2. Completing the square (bridge B2, Fact 3) gives ∫ … dq = 2σ_k√π e^{−σ_k²x²}.
3. So **ψ(x, 0) = 2σ_k√π e^{ik₀x} e^{−σ_k²x²}**: a carrier wave e^{ik₀x} of wave number k₀ inside a Gaussian envelope. (Numerically, σ_k = 2 m⁻¹ gives |ψ(0)| = 7.0898 = 4√π ✓.)
4. Intensity: |ψ|² = 4πσ_k² e^{−2σ_k²x²}. Compare e^{−x²/(2σ_x²)}: σ_x² = 1/(4σ_k²), that is **σ_x = 1/(2σ_k)**.
5. Product: **σ_x σ_k = ½.**

*Numerical confirmation:* with σ_k = 2.0 m⁻¹ the integrals give σ_x = 0.2500 m, product 0.5000. *Dimensional check:* x in m and k in rad/m, so the product is dimensionless ✓.

## Time evolution in a non-dispersive medium
If every component moves at v, then ω = vk and ψ(x, t) = ∫ A(k) e^{i(kx − vkt)} dk = ∫ A(k) e^{ik(x − vt)} dk = ψ(x − vt, 0). So the packet moves at v **with unchanged envelope and width**: it is just the pulse f(x − vt) of module 2 (here f is the Gaussian packet). This is exactly the non-dispersive case; with ω not proportional to k the components fall out of step and the packet changes (module 8).

## Limiting cases
- **σ_k → 0:** σ_x = 1/(2σ_k) → ∞, A(k) is a spike at k₀ and the packet becomes a plane wave filling all space.
- **σ_k → ∞:** σ_x → 0, A(k) is flat (all k equal) and the packet shrinks to a delta pulse at x = 0.

## The inequality and the traps
For any shape the rms widths of |ψ|² and |A|² obey **σ_x σ_k ≥ ½**, with equality only for a Gaussian with no quadratic phase (no chirp) (standard result, proved with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality; verify). Example of a larger product (sympy): ψ = e^{−|x|} has A(k) ∝ 1/(1 + k²), σ_x = 1/√2, σ_k = 1, product 0.7071.

*Trap: amplitude versus intensity.* f(x) = e^{−x²/(2s²)} has transform F(k) = s√(2π) e^{−s²k²/2} (sympy). The rms widths of the **amplitudes** f and F are s and 1/s, product 1; those of the **intensities** |f|² and |F|² are s/√2 and 1/(s√2), product ½. Quote ½ only for intensities. With p = ħk the same inequality is the position–momentum bound of quantum mechanics (name only; verify).

### 7. Master: short pulses, bandwidth, and practical examples with numbers

*Objective:* Convert σ_x σ_k = 1/2 into the bandwidth–duration relation, handle the rms, FWHM and first-zero conventions, and apply it to light, radar and sound.

## Time version
At a fixed place the same mathematics applies with x → t and k → ω. For a Gaussian pulse with no frequency sweep (chirp), with rms duration σ_t of the intensity and rms angular-frequency spread σ_ω of the power spectrum, **σ_t σ_ω = ½**; every other shape gives a larger product (σ_t σ_ω ≥ ½). With f = ω/2π, σ_ω = 2πσ_f gives

$$ σ_f σ_t = 1/(4π) ≈ 0.0796 $$

*Short pulse ⇒ broad band.* Duration ÷ 10 means bandwidth × 10. Brij Lal (PDF p. 281) writes the order-of-magnitude form Δν Δt ~ 1.

## Worked numbers (all recomputed)
**1. A light flash from one atom** (Brij Lal PDF pp. 278 to 279). Δt = 10⁻⁸ s, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s: l = cΔt = 3 m; with λ = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, N = 3/(5 × 10⁻⁷) = **6 × 10⁶** oscillations (the book then says 'about a million', a loose phrase: the arithmetic gives six million). Δν ~ 1/Δt = 10⁸ Hz, ν₀ = c/λ = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz, so Δν/ν₀ = 1.7 × 10⁻⁷ ≈ 1/N, the 'quasi-monochromatic' case Δν/ν₀ ≪ 1 (PDF p. 283). In wavelength, Δλ = λ²Δν/c ≈ 8.3 × 10⁻¹⁴ m.

**2. An ultrashort laser pulse** (standard knowledge; verify the typical values). σ_t = 50 fs ⇒ σ_f = 1/(4π × 5 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.59 × 10¹² Hz; FWHM duration 117.7 fs, FWHM bandwidth 3.75 × 10¹² Hz, product 0.441. At λ = 800 nm, f₀ = 3.75 × 10¹⁴ Hz, so the band is 1.0% of f₀, or Δλ ≈ 8 nm.

**3. Radar** (standard; verify). A rectangular pulse of τ = 1 μs has first-zero full bandwidth 2/τ = 2 MHz (order 1/τ = 1 MHz); the receiver must accept this band. Range resolution is cτ/2 = 150 m (the echo travels out and back).

**4. A clap and a spectrum analyser** (standard; verify). If a click were a smooth Gaussian of rms duration 1 ms, σ_f = 1/(4π × 10⁻³ s) ≈ 80 Hz about its centre. A real clap is a sharp, irregular burst, so its spectrum has a long high-frequency tail and it sounds like a broadband click, unlike a sustained tone. A spectrum analyser displays |F(f)|², the |A|² of this lesson.

**Dimensions and limits.** σ_f σ_t is dimensionless (Hz × s). Duration → ∞ gives a single spectral line; duration → 0 gives a flat spectrum.

### 8. Master: packets and beats, dispersion named, and the group-velocity pointer

*Objective:* Link packets to beats, name dispersion without solving it and state precisely what is deferred to PH-041.

## Beats are an infinite train of packets
PH-038 modules 4 to 6 derive beats from two close frequencies. In space the same algebra with two close wave numbers gives (cos A + cos B identity)

cos(k₁x − ω₁t) + cos(k₂x − ω₂t) = 2 cos(Δk x/2 − Δω t/2) cos(k̄x − ω̄t),

with Δk = k₂ − k₁, k̄ the mean. The slow factor is the envelope; the groups repeat every **2π/Δk** (the beat length λ₁λ₂/|λ₁ − λ₂| of PH-038 module 7). Mathur (PDF pp. 532 to 533) says that in a non-dispersive medium both components and the groups move at the same speed v. *Number (recomputed):* 440 Hz and 444 Hz in air at 340 m/s (standard value; verify) give beat period 1/4 Hz = 0.25 s and group spacing v/Δf = 85 m. **Contrast with module 6:** two lines give a periodic string of groups (never localised to one); a *continuous* Gaussian band of k gives a *single* packet (the spacing 2π/Δk → ∞ as the lines merge into a smooth band).

## Dispersion, only named
In the string of module 2 every wave number moves at the same v. In many media (glass for light, deep water) the speed depends on k, that is ω is not proportional to k: a **dispersive** medium. Then the components fall out of step and a packet changes shape as it travels; Brij Lal (PDF p. 282) and NCERT (PDF p. 22, an end-of-chapter exercise asks the learner to explain why a pulse is distorted in a dispersive medium) state this. *How fast it spreads and why is not derived here.*

## The pointer (stated once)
For a narrow packet the envelope's speed is set by dω/dk at the centre k₀: v_g = dω/dk (Brij Lal eq. 12.35, PDF p. 282; Mathur PDF p. 535). **Derived properly on Day 15 in PH-041 (not yet written).** For ω = vk, dω/dk = v, so v_g = v, which is exactly what module 6 showed directly (the packet moved at v with no change).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: f(x − vt) and the d'Alembert form

- Assumptions: small slopes, uniform μ and T, non-dispersive, one transverse direction.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: reflection at fixed and free ends

- Wall at x = 0, string on x < 0, y = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt).
- Fixed: y(0, t) = 0 ⇒ g(u) = −f(−u) ⇒ y_r = −f(−x − vt): inverted and mirrored.
- Free (massless ring): transverse force −T y_x = 0 ⇒ g′(u) = −f′(−u) ⇒ g(u) = f(−u) ⇒ y_r = +f(−x − vt): upright, mirrored; end displacement 2f.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: Gaussian packet and σ_x σ_k = 1/2
