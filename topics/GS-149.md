## GS-149: Population and associated concerns

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Population and associated concerns and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Theories: Malthus and demographic transition

Malthus held that population rises in geometric progression while food grows only arithmetically, so famine and disease act as 'positive checks' unless people delay marriage or practise restraint. History proved him wrong: from the late nineteenth century European birth rates fell, epidemics were controlled and living standards rose despite population growth. Critics also argued that poverty reflects unequal distribution of resources. Demographic transition theory links population growth to development in three stages: low growth with high birth and death rates, a transitional phase of rapid growth as death rates fall faster than birth rates, and low growth with both rates low. The middle 'population explosion' occurs because disease control spreads quickly but reproductive behaviour changes slowly. NCERT judges India's transition incomplete because mortality has fallen more than fertility.

### India's growth path and regional divergence

Between 1901 and 1951 annual growth stayed at or below about 1.33%, and in 1911–21 it was slightly negative because the influenza pandemic of 1918–19 killed around a twentieth of the population. After 1921 death rates fell through better control of famines and epidemics, aided by irrigation, transport and state relief. Growth rose to about 2.2% in 1961–81 and then eased. Fertility varies widely: five States reached a TFR of 1.7 in 2016 and Kerala was also below replacement, while Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan remained high. According to SRS 2020, India's crude birth rate was 19.5, higher in rural (21.1) than urban (16.1) areas. Economic Survey 2018–19 projections expect Uttar Pradesh and Bihar to contribute about half of additions to population up to 2041.

### Policy: from targets to welfare

India announced an explicit population policy in 1952, implemented through the National Family Planning Programme to slow growth via birth control, better public health and awareness. During the Emergency of 1975–76 the government pushed coercive mass sterilisation, with heavy pressure on low-level officials to bring people to camps; popular opposition led the next government to abandon it. The programme was renamed the National Family Welfare Programme and broadened its socio-demographic aims, with new guidelines in the National Population Policy 2000. National Health Policy 2017 folded many goals into health targets, such as cutting TFR to 2.1 nationally and sub-nationally by 2025. Population momentum, driven by a large cohort of women of reproductive age, means numbers keep rising for a generation even as fertility falls. NCERT's lesson: fertility ultimately follows economic, social and cultural change.

### Worked example

Q: 'Population is a resource, not merely a burden.' Discuss with reference to India. Frame: theories (Malthus critiqued; transition stages); India's growth phases (1911–21 decline, 1961–81 peak); regional TFR divergence (1.7 in five States vs 3.3 in Bihar in 2016); policy evolution (1952, Emergency, Family Welfare, NPP 2000, NHP 2017 TFR 2.1); momentum to about 1.6 billion by 2050; link to demographic dividend; conclude that health, education and women's agency drive fertility decline.

### Recall

- - **q:** What is population momentum?
  - **a:** Continued population growth because a large cohort of women is in reproductive ages, even if each woman has fewer children.
- - **q:** Why did India's population decline slightly in 1911–21?
  - **a:** The 1918–19 influenza epidemic killed about 5% of the population.
- - **q:** What happened to the family planning programme after the Emergency?
  - **a:** Coercive methods were abandoned and it was renamed the National Family Welfare Programme with broader objectives.

### Common trap

Blaming poverty mainly on population growth. NCERT records liberal and Marxist critiques that poverty and starvation stem from unequal distribution of resources, and Amartya Sen's finding that famines reflected 'failure of entitlements', not only falling food output.
