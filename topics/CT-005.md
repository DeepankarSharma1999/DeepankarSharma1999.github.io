### Bridge: Words this lesson uses

Seven words recur. Learn them before module 1.

- **Claim**: a statement put forward as true or as worth accepting. A claim may be a fact claim, an opinion or a prediction.
- **Evidence**: records, measurements or observations that can support or defeat a claim.
- **Verify**: to check a claim against evidence. A claim about the past or present is *verifiable* if checking is possible in principle, even if nobody has done it yet.
- **Attribute**: to say who made a claim ('the union says ...'). An attributed claim is reported, not endorsed.
- **Hedge**: a word that softens a claim ('may', 'probably', 'seems').
- **Forecast**: a statement about a future event, with some degree of confidence.
- **Value judgement**: a statement that calls something good, bad, wise or unfair; it depends on a standard the speaker holds.

Example: 'Officials say the dam is safe.' The passage *attributes* the claim to the officials; it reports it without endorsing it, and a test could *verify* it. 'Safe' needs a standard, so check what standard is meant.

### 1. Two kinds of sentence: what fact-versus-opinion questions test

*Objective:* Name the three labels (fact claim, opinion, prediction) and the three ways these questions appear in a CSAT paper.

## Two kinds of sentence

A comprehension passage mixes sentences that **report** the world with sentences that **judge** it. Many wrong options are built by blurring that line.

Three labels are used throughout.

- **Fact claim**: a statement about the past or present that can, in principle, be checked against records, measurement or observation, whoever does the checking. 'The library has 18,000 books' is a fact claim.
- **Opinion**: a statement whose acceptance depends on values, taste or interpretation, such as 'the library is the finest in the district'. Judgements ('wasteful', 'unfair') and recommendations ('the city should ...') belong here.
- **Prediction**: a statement about the future. However confident, it cannot yet be checked: 'the monsoon will be normal next year'.

One idea matters more than any other: a fact claim may be **false** and is still a fact claim. 'The Moon is larger than the Earth' can be checked and fails; it is a false fact claim, not an opinion.

## How the topic appears

As direct classification, as voice questions (module 5) and as a hidden type trap inside inference options; module 7 lists the patterns.

### 2. The verifiability test and its edge cases

*Objective:* Apply three questions (time, check, disagreement) to any sentence and handle six edge cases without error.

## Three questions

1. **Time question.** Ask this first. Is the sentence about something that has not happened yet? If yes, it is a prediction (or a plan), whatever its tone.
2. **Check question.** Could records, instruments or observation of the past or present settle it, without asking what anyone values? If yes, it is a fact claim.
3. **Disagreement question.** If two honest, well-informed people had identical evidence, could they still differ without either making a factual error? If yes, it is an opinion.

| Sentence | Deciding question | Label |
|---|---|---|
| The Yamuna flows through Delhi. | Map and survey settle it | Fact claim (true) |
| The Moon is larger than the Earth. | Measurement settles it | Fact claim (false) |
| Delhi has the most pleasant winters in India. | 'Pleasant' is a taste | Opinion |
| Rs 9 lakh is too much for a bus shelter. | 'Too much' needs a value standard | Opinion |
| The next monsoon will be normal. | Time question: not yet happened | Prediction |

## True or false is a separate question

'Fact claim' describes the *kind* of sentence, not whether it is right. 'True' and 'false' apply to fact claims after checking.

## Six edge cases

1. **Comparatives.** 'Taller than' or 'faster than' is a fact claim when a measure is understood. 'Better than' needs a value standard, so it is an opinion.
2. **Beliefs.** 'Many residents believe the water is unsafe' is a fact claim about residents. What they believe may be a fact claim or an opinion; judge it separately.
3. **Unrecoverable past claims.** 'The first boat crossed at dawn' is still a fact claim; evidence could in principle settle it.
4. **Widely shared judgements.** 'Cruelty to children is wrong' is accepted almost everywhere but is a value statement, not a measurement.
5. **Probability statements.** 'The department forecasts a 70 per cent chance of rain' is a fact about the forecast; the rain is a prediction.
6. **Standard words** (overcrowded, unsafe, ageing, poorly planned). If the passage gives or implies a measurable standard (design capacity, safety code), the word is checkable; otherwise treat it as an opinion.

### 3. Cue words: value words, hedges, attribution and disguised certainty

*Objective:* Use six families of cue words as clues, and refuse to treat any cue as a verdict.

## Six cue families

| Family | Examples | Usually signals |
|---|---|---|
| Value words | best, worst, wasteful, unfair, wise, alarming | Opinion |
| Future markers | will, going to, expected to, by 2035 | Prediction |
| Hedges | may, might, probably, perhaps, seems | Uncertainty (of what? see below) |
| Attribution verbs | says, claims, argues, alleges, reports, according to | A reported claim: a fact that someone said it |
| Disguised certainty | clearly, obviously, undoubtedly, it is a fact that | Often an opinion dressed as a fact |
| Numerals and dates | 18,000; 2011; 12 per cent | Often a fact claim, but see below |

Value words are also the clues to the author's **tone** (CT-003 modules 2 and 3): the same word does both jobs. 'Claims' and 'alleges' also show the author's distance from a claim (CT-003 module 3).

## Cues are clues, not verdicts

Four cases show why.

1. **'It is a fact that the scheme is a failure.'** The phrase announces a fact, but 'failure' still needs a value standard. Opinion.
2. **'Rs 9 lakh is far too much.'** There is a number, but the claim is about 'too much'. Opinion.
3. **'The tragic fire of 2019 killed twelve people.'** One value word ('tragic') sits beside two checkable claims. This is a *mixed* sentence (module 4).
4. **'Rainfall was probably about 800 mm.'** The hedge reflects doubt about a measurement. The sentence is still a fact claim, only a less certain one.

## What is the hedge hedging?

Ask what 'may', 'likely' or 'probably' is attached to. A past or present fact gives an *uncertain fact claim*; a future event gives a prediction; a judgement ('this may be unwise') gives a softened opinion. The hedge shows confidence, not the label.

### 4. Mixed statements, quoted opinions and false facts

*Objective:* Split mixed sentences into parts, separate a report from the thing reported, and classify a false claim correctly.

## Mixed statements: split into atoms

Break a multi-claim sentence into **atoms**, the smallest pieces that can be labelled separately.

> The poorly planned flyover, which opened in 2018, has four lanes.

- 'opened in 2018': fact claim.
- 'has four lanes': fact claim.
- 'poorly planned': opinion.

The whole sentence is **mixed**. If the stem asks for 'a statement of fact', a mixed option fails because one atom is an opinion; if it asks 'which contains an opinion', the same option qualifies. Check whether the stem wants a *pure* label or a *containing* label.

## Quoted opinions: three layers

A passage often reports what somebody said. Peel the layers.

> The minister called the scheme a triumph.

1. *Layer 1:* that the minister said this is a fact claim (a person said it).
2. *Layer 2:* 'the scheme is a triumph' is an opinion.
3. *Layer 3:* whether the **author** agrees is a separate matter. The sentence only reports.

As a whole, 'X called Y a triumph' is a fact claim (that X said it), not a mixed sentence; only the embedded content carries the opinion label.

If the quoted content is a number, as in 'The minister says twelve lakh houses were built', Layer 2 is a fact claim, but it is **unsettled**: the passage records the statement, not its truth. An option that drops the attribution ('Twelve lakh houses were built') promotes a reported claim to the author's own claim, which the passage may not support.

## False and unsettled facts

'Forty per cent of the village wells run dry before May' may turn out true or false. Until tested, it is a **checkable claim**. Its being disputed does not turn it into an opinion. Equally, a claim being widely accepted does not turn an opinion into a fact.

'Which is factually incorrect?' asks about the *truth* of fact claims; 'which is an opinion?' asks about *kind*.

### 5. How fact and opinion interact with inference and author's-view questions

*Objective:* Find the type trap in an inference option and answer author's-view questions by separating the author's voice from reported voices.

## Voice first

Every sentence speaks in a **voice**: the author's, a quoted person's, or neutral report. Ask whose voice an option echoes. CT-002 modules 3 to 6 judge *strength*; this lesson adds a check on *type and voice*.

## Five rules

1. **A quoted view supports only 'X holds this view'.** If a critic calls a plan wasteful, 'the plan is wasteful' goes beyond the passage unless the author says so too.
2. **Opinion promoted to fact.** The commonest type trap: 'the editor says the data show the new fare is fair' becomes 'the data show the new fare is fair'. The first is a report; the second claims the evidence supports a value judgement.
3. **Prediction promoted to certainty.** 'The board sees a strong chance of a power cut tonight' becomes 'there will be a power cut tonight'. Keep the forecast, its source and its hedge.
4. **A true fact that is not in the passage still fails** (the 'true but not supported' trap, CT-002 module 5).
5. **An opinion option can be right** when the passage states it or leaves no other reading: for example, the author says, without attribution, that the policy has been a failure.

## Author's-view questions

'Which expresses the author's view?' asks for the evaluative sentence that is not attributed to anyone else. Distractors are of three kinds: a reported fact, a view the author attributes to others, and a fact the author uses as evidence. Look for evaluative words with no 'they say': 'it is hard to avoid the conclusion', 'unfortunately'.

## The type check

For a fact claim, find the line that states it; for an opinion, the voice that holds it; for a prediction, the forecaster and hedge. An option fails if the passage has nothing to match, only a different voice, or is more certain than the passage's hedge allows. Never supply your own values.

### 6. The Voice-Split-Label-Support method and two worked analyses

*Objective:* Run a four-step method on a classification question and on an inference question.

## Four steps

1. **Voice.** Mark each sentence: author, quoted source or neutral report.
2. **Split.** Break mixed sentences into atoms.
3. **Label.** Label each atom by the module 2 questions.
4. **Support.** For the option you test, find the passage line that supports its type, voice and strength.

## Worked analysis A (direct classification)

> The Sundar canal, completed in 1962, is 84 km long. Tail-end farmers say it is the best thing to happen to the district. The department estimates that silt removal next year will raise flow by a tenth. A visiting engineer found the lining 'in a shocking state'.

*Question.* Which one of the following statements is a prediction?
(a) The canal is 84 km long. (b) It is the best thing that has happened to the district. (c) Silt removal will raise flow by a tenth. (d) The lining is in a shocking state.

- (a) fact claim. (b) farmers' opinion. (c) the department's estimate about next year: prediction. (d) the engineer's judgement.
- Choose (c); attribution does not change a prediction's kind.

## Worked analysis B (inference with a type trap)

> In the school, 61 of the 80 Class X students scored above 60 per cent this year. The principal describes the result as 'outstanding'. The district education officer has asked for a comparison with other schools before drawing any conclusion.

*Question.* Which one of the following is the most logical inference?
(a) The school's result is outstanding. (b) More than half the school's Class X students scored above 60 per cent. (c) The school is better than other schools in the district. (d) The district education officer disagrees with the principal.

- 'Outstanding' is the principal's opinion. 61 of 80 is a fact claim in the author's voice, and 61 exceeds half of 80 (40; shares as in CT-004-B2).
- (a) promotes a quoted opinion, which the officer leaves open. (c) is a comparison the passage lacks. (d) reads a request for comparison as disagreement. (b) restates the passage's own fact claim (stated in the author's voice). Choose (b).

After choosing, re-read the stem: a *kind* stem ignores the truth of the claim; a *follows* stem uses the voice and strength rules.

### 7. Exam layer: general patterns, time and the practice protocol

*Objective:* Know the general ways fact and opinion are tested, budget time, and follow a practice protocol with an error log.

## General patterns (analysis, not a claim about any paper)

- **Kind questions** ask for the fact, the opinion or the prediction among four statements. Wrong options are usually the other two kinds, plus a mixed sentence.
- **Voice questions** ask for the author's view or what a critic claims. The trap is the reported view.
- **Numbered-statement forms** ('which of the above are opinions?') need each statement judged alone.

## Time and risk

A practice target, not an official allotment: about one minute for a short passage with a kind question. Marking rules: CT-001-B2 (verify against the notification). If you can eliminate even one option with confidence, the gamble shifts in your favour.

## Practice protocol

1. Do the first four original passages untimed, using Voice-Split-Label-Support in writing.
2. Read the explanation for **every** option, including those you rejected correctly.
3. Do the rest at one minute each, about 1.5 for the inference and voice passages (P5, P6); practice targets.
4. Log each error in one of seven categories: *cue trap* (acted on a cue word), *mixed sentence* (called a mixed option pure), *attribution dropped*, *true but not in passage*, *fact equals true* (confused kind with truth), *hedge* (misread what the hedge covers), or *type promotion* (opinion promoted to fact, prediction to certainty).
5. Repeat the failed passage after seven days, answering before reading the explanation again.

Most mistakes are habits: reacting to a word instead of reading the sentence. If one category dominates your log, drill it next. Completing the passages is not mastery; correct, explained answers on fresh passages after a delay are the evidence.

### Notes: Definitions

- Fact claim: a statement about the past or present that can in principle be checked against records, measurement or observation; it may be true or false.
- Opinion: a statement whose acceptance depends on values, taste or interpretation; includes judgements and recommendations.
- Prediction: a statement about a future event; it cannot yet be checked.
- Mixed statement: a sentence whose atoms carry more than one label.
- Atom: the smallest part of a sentence that can be labelled separately.
- Attribution: naming who made a claim; an attributed claim is reported, not endorsed.
- Hedge: a word that softens confidence ('may', 'probably'); it does not decide the label.

### Notes: The labels at a glance

- Use the questions in order: time, check, disagreement.

### Notes: Cue words and their exceptions

- A cue is a clue; test it against the sentence.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Fact versus true: fact is a kind of statement, true is a result of checking.
- Opinion versus belief: a belief may be about a fact claim; an opinion is a judgement.
- Opinion versus prediction: 'will be bad' is both a prediction and a judgement; split the atoms.
- Report versus endorsement: 'X says' establishes only that X said it.
- Uncertain fact versus opinion: a doubtful measurement is still a fact claim.
- Widely held versus factual: wide agreement does not turn a value statement into a measurement.

### Notes: Edge cases tested against the test

- Comparatives: 'taller' is a fact claim; 'more beautiful' is an opinion.
- Past claims that can no longer be checked are still fact claims in principle.
- Probability statements: the forecast is a fact; the event is a prediction.
- A recommendation ('the city should widen roads') is an opinion even without value words.
- Statements of intention ('the state plans to ...'): that the plan exists is a fact claim; whether it happens is a prediction.
- A definition ('a hectare is 10,000 square metres') is a convention, checkable in a reference and unlikely to be tested; treat it as a fact claim.
- Quoted opinion: 'The minister called the scheme a triumph' is a fact claim as a whole (the minister said it); only the embedded content is an opinion. It is not a mixed sentence.
- Standard words (overcrowded, unsafe, ageing, poorly planned): checkable if the passage gives or implies a measurable standard (capacity, safety code); otherwise treat as opinions.
- Causal claims ('due mainly to X') are fact claims in principle but often contested interpretations; in a kind stem they are rarely the keyed 'fact'. Judge them by the voice that holds them; causal check in CT-004 module 4.

### Notes: Splitting mixed sentences: patterns

- 'The overcrowded hostel houses 640 students.' Atoms: overcrowded (opinion unless a capacity is stated), 640 students (fact claim).
- 'Officials say the dam, built in 1974, is unsafe.' Atoms: built in 1974 (fact claim), unsafe (an opinion unless a safety standard is stated; module 2, edge case 6), officials said so (fact claim).
- 'The tragic fire of 2019 killed twelve people.' Atoms: tragic (opinion), 2019 and twelve deaths (fact claims).

### Notes: Stem families and what each demands (general pattern)

- Kind stems ('which is a statement of fact / opinion / prediction') ask for the label; the passage's truth does not matter.
- Truth stems ('which is factually incorrect') ask whether a fact claim is true; opinions cannot be answers.
- Voice stems ('author's view', 'critic claims') ask for the speaker; reported views are distractors.
- Inference stems ask what follows; check the option's type, voice and strength.
- Numbered-statement stems need each atom judged alone before the code is chosen.

      - The reading room is too noisy to be of much use to students.
      - The library committee has seven members.
    - **answer:** 3
    - **explanation:** Option D is a fact claim: the membership of a committee can be counted from its records. Option A is an opinion ('finest' needs a standard of excellence, and the passage attributes the view to residents). Option B is a prediction about the future that the passage says has not been tested. Option C is the teacher's judgement ('too noisy', 'much use'), which depends on a standard of tolerable noise. Only D can be settled without asking anyone's values.

    - **passage:** The new Kosi footbridge, completed in 2022 at a cost of Rs 14 crore, is an extravagant project. It is 310 metres long and carries about 2,000 pedestrians a day. Opponents say that the money should have gone to schools. The district engineer argues that the bridge has cut the walking distance from the riverside villages to the town market from six kilometres to one, and that it will therefore pay for itself in time saved within a decade. Local shopkeepers report that more customers now arrive on foot from across the river.

    - **options:**
      - It is entirely a statement of fact, because it gives a year and a cost.
      - It is entirely an opinion, because it contains the word 'extravagant'.
      - It joins verifiable facts (the year and the cost) to an opinion ('extravagant').
      - It is a prediction about the cost of the bridge.
    - **answer:** 2
    - **explanation:** Option C is correct: 'completed in 2022' and 'at a cost of Rs 14 crore' can be checked, while 'extravagant' needs a standard of reasonable spending, so the sentence is mixed. Option A ignores the value word. Option B reverses the error: a cue word is present, but two atoms of the sentence are still fact claims, so calling the whole sentence an opinion discards checkable content. Option D is wrong because the cost and the completion lie in the past and nothing in the sentence is about the future.

    - **options:**
      - Seventy per cent of the packaged juices sold in the city contain more sugar than their labels declare.
      - The consumer group has made a claim about sugar content that laboratory tests can check.
      - The consumer group's report is misleading.
      - The juice makers do not follow the labelling rules.

    - **explanation:** Option B is correct: the passage reports the group's 70 per cent figure and says tests will show whether it is correct, so it is a checkable fact claim whose truth is unsettled. ### Memory skeleton

- Fact claim, opinion, prediction are kinds of statement; true or false comes after checking
- Test in order: time, check, disagreement
- Cue words are clues, not verdicts
- Voice: author, quoted source or neutral report
- Split mixed sentences into atoms; peel quoted opinions into three layers
- Label each atom; support the option's type, voice and strength from a passage line

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, write the three test questions in order, the four method steps, the three layers of a quoted opinion and the three type traps.

### Last-week summary

- Fact claim: checkable about past or present, may be false. Opinion: informed people could differ on the same evidence. Prediction: future.
- Cues mislead: a number can serve a judgement ('too much'); 'will' can state a law; 'clearly' can disguise an opinion.
- A hedge shows confidence, not the label; a recommendation is an opinion even without value words.
- Quoted opinion: that it was said is a fact claim; what was said keeps its own label; agreement is separate.
- Type traps: opinion promoted to fact, prediction promoted to certainty, attribution dropped.
