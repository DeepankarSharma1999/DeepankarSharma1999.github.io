### Bridge: State, Union Territory, asymmetric, autonomy, Schedule: the five words this topic rests on

**State.** In India a State is a full member of the federation: it has its own elected legislature, its own Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister, a Governor as constitutional head, and a constitutionally guaranteed share of law-making power (the State List). Article 1 says India 'shall be a Union of States' and the **First Schedule** names each State and its territory.

**Union Territory (UT).** A UT is a piece of India's territory that is **not** a State. It is administered directly by the Union (central) government through an **administrator** appointed by the President. Some UTs (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu and Kashmir) have been given their own assemblies by law, but even then Parliament keeps full power over them.

**Asymmetric.** 'Symmetric' means every unit is treated identically. 'Asymmetric federalism' means some units get different powers or protections because of their history, geography or population. NCERT calls this differential treatment 'the most extra-ordinary feature' of India's federal arrangement.

**Autonomy.** The power of a unit to govern itself in its own sphere without outside interference. NCERT distinguishes autonomy (more self-rule inside India) from secession (leaving India). Special provisions are a way of granting extra autonomy without allowing secession.

**Schedule.** A list attached to the end of the Constitution. The First Schedule lists States and UTs; the Fifth Schedule deals with Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in most States; the Sixth Schedule gives autonomous district councils to tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram; the Seventh Schedule has the Union, State and Concurrent Lists.

**Article, Part, Amendment.** Articles are the numbered rules; Parts group them (Part VIII covers Union Territories, Articles 239-242; Part XXI, titled 'Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions', holds Articles 371-371J). A Constitutional Amendment Act (for example the 13th of 1962) adds or changes Articles; inserted Articles carry letters (371A, 371B ...) so the numbering stays intact.

### 1. Why one Constitution treats some States differently

*Objective:* Grasp the purpose of asymmetric federalism and what Part XXI contains.

## The one-sentence meaning

**Special provisions** are Articles 371 to 371J of Part XXI that give particular States arrangements the other States do not have: a Governor with special duties, laws of Parliament that do not apply automatically, development boards for backward regions, or a guaranteed minimum size of assembly.

## Why a federation of equals is not fully equal

NCERT puts it plainly: the division of powers is common to all States, 'and yet, the Constitution has some special provisions for some States given their peculiar social and historical circumstances'. It calls this differential treatment the most extraordinary feature of Indian federalism. Representation is already asymmetric (small States get a minimum of Rajya Sabha seats, large ones more), so unequal treatment is built into the design.

Laxmikanth lists four purposes behind the Part XXI provisions:
1. to meet the aspirations of people of **backward regions** within a State (Vidarbha, Marathwada, Saurashtra, Kutch, Hyderabad-Karnataka);
2. to protect the **cultural and economic interests of tribal people** (Nagaland, Mizoram, Assam, Manipur);
3. to deal with **disturbed law and order** in parts of a State (Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh);
4. to protect the **interests of local people** (Andhra Pradesh and Telangana local cadres, Sikkim's sections of population).

## How they entered the Constitution

None of the present special provisions (Articles 371A to 371J, and the present text of Article 371) was in the original Constitution; the original Article 371 dealt with Part B States and was replaced. Each present provision was inserted by a later amendment at the moment of a **reorganisation of States** or the **conferment of statehood on a Union Territory**: the price of agreeing to join or to be reorganised was a constitutional promise. That is why most special provisions concern the north-east, whose States were carved out of Assam or elevated from Union Territory status, and whose 'sizeable indigenous tribal population with a distinct history and culture' NCERT highlights. NCERT also notes the limit of the device: these provisions 'have not been able to stem alienation and the insurgency in parts of the region'.

## Where Union Territories fit

A Union Territory is the opposite answer to diversity: instead of more autonomy, direct central administration. Laxmikanth quotes the view that UTs are a 'conspicuous departure from federalism', the Union being 'plainly unitary' towards them. So this topic spans two ends of a single spectrum: extra autonomy (Part XXI) and no autonomy (Part VIII).

## A picture to hold

Think of the First Schedule as a classroom roll. Most students follow one rulebook. A few have written accommodations (Part XXI). A few are taught directly by the principal (Part VIII). Jammu and Kashmir moved in 2019 from the first group to the third.

### 3. Regional equity provisions: Maharashtra, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Goa

*Objective:* Explain the development-board and local-cadre model of special provisions.

## Article 371: Maharashtra and Gujarat

The President may provide that the Governors of Maharashtra and Gujarat have a **special responsibility** for:
1. separate **development boards** for Vidarbha, Marathwada and the rest of Maharashtra, and for Saurashtra, Kutch and the rest of Gujarat;
2. an annual **report** on the boards' working placed before the State Assembly;
3. **equitable allocation of funds** for development across these areas;
4. equitable arrangements for **technical education, vocational training and State employment** in these areas.

The pattern is 'fairness between regions of one State', enforced through the Governor rather than the elected government alone.

## Articles 371D and 371E: Andhra Pradesh and Telangana

Inserted by the **32nd Amendment, 1973**, after agitation over jobs and education between regions. In **2014** the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act extended Article 371D to the new State of **Telangana**. Under 371D the President may:
1. provide **equitable opportunities in public employment and education** for people of different parts of the State, with different rules for different parts;
2. require the State to organise civil posts into **local cadres**, allow **direct recruitment** to them, define **local areas** for admission to educational institutions, and fix the extent of **preference or reservation**;
3. establish an **Administrative Tribunal** for disputes about appointment, allotment and promotion to civil posts. The tribunal works **outside the purview of the State High Court**; no court other than the **Supreme Court** has jurisdiction over matters within its remit. The President may abolish it when no longer needed (it was set up by the Andhra Pradesh Administrative Tribunal Order, 1975). Verify current status: this describes the text of Article 371D as the source gives it; later case law (P. Sambamurthy, 1987; L. Chandra Kumar, 1997) qualified the ouster of High Court review, and the tribunal is reported to have been abolished around 2019-21 (uncertain).

Article **371E** empowers Parliament to establish a **Central University** in Andhra Pradesh.

## Article 371J: Karnataka

Inserted by the **98th Amendment, 2012**, after both Houses of the Karnataka legislature passed resolutions in 2010 seeking special provisions for the **Hyderabad-Karnataka region** (six backward northern districts: Gulbarga, Bidar, Raichur, Koppal, Yadgir and Bellary). The President may give the Governor special responsibility for:
1. a separate **development board** for the region;
2. an annual **report** to the Assembly;
3. **equitable fund allocation**;
4. **reservation of seats** in educational and vocational training institutions for students of the region;
5. **reservation in State government posts** in the region for persons of the region.

The aim, in Laxmikanth's words, is an institutional mechanism for equitable funds and for human resources and employment through local cadres and reservations, to reduce inter-district disparities. (The region has reportedly been renamed Kalyana-Karnataka; this is not in the source and is unverified.)

## Article 371I: Goa

The shortest provision: the Goa Legislative Assembly must have **not less than 30 members** (56th Amendment, 1987, when Goa became a State).

## What to notice

Maharashtra-Gujarat and Karnataka share the same five-part template; Andhra Pradesh adds the unusual tribunal that displaces the High Court. Prelims statements often test exactly that displacement.

### 4. Protecting tribal identity: Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh and Sikkim

*Objective:* Explain the customary-law and Governor-responsibility model and the Tuensang arrangement.

## Article 371A: Nagaland (13th Amendment, 1962)

1. **Acts of Parliament do not apply** to Nagaland on (i) religious or social practices of the Nagas, (ii) Naga customary law and procedure, (iii) civil and criminal justice according to Naga customary law, and (iv) **ownership and transfer of land and its resources**, unless the Nagaland Assembly so decides.
2. The **Governor has special responsibility for law and order** so long as internal disturbances caused by hostile Nagas continue; after consulting the Council of Ministers he exercises **individual judgement** and his decision is final; the responsibility ceases when the **President** so directs.
3. The Governor ensures that **Central money for a specific purpose** goes into the demand for a grant for that purpose and no other.
4. A **regional council of 35 members** for the **Tuensang district**, with the Deputy Commissioner as ex-officio chairman; the Governor makes rules for its composition and functioning.
5. For **ten years** from statehood (extendable on the council's recommendation), Tuensang is **administered by the Governor**, who distributes Central money between Tuensang and the rest of the State, decides whether Nagaland's Acts apply there, may make Regulations that can even amend Acts of Parliament for the district, and takes the final decision on Tuensang matters in his discretion. There is a **Minister for Tuensang affairs**, and, during this transitional period, Tuensang's members of the Assembly are chosen **by the regional council, not directly by the people** (Laxmikanth, PDF p. 812).

**Important time limit.** Items 4 and 5 describe a **transitional arrangement**: it ran for ten years from the formation of Nagaland (statehood in December 1963, so roughly 1963-73), extendable by the Governor on the regional council's recommendation. It is not a permanent feature. Today the Tuensang constituencies of the Nagaland Assembly are filled by direct election like the rest of the State (verify the present position against the Election Commission's constituency list and Article 371A as currently printed). Read any Prelims statement about Tuensang for its tense and its time frame: 'for the first ten years members were chosen by the regional council' is true; 'Tuensang members are chosen by the regional council today' is not.

## Article 371B: Assam (22nd Amendment, 1969)

The President may provide for a **committee of the Assam Assembly** of members elected from the **Tribal Areas** (the Sixth Schedule areas: North Cachar Hills, Karbi Anglong and the Bodoland Territorial Areas District) plus other members he specifies.

## Article 371C: Manipur (27th Amendment, 1971)

1. A **committee of the Manipur Assembly** of members from the **Hill Areas** (areas the President declares by order).
2. The President may give the Governor **special responsibility** for the committee's proper functioning.
3. The Governor submits an **annual report** to the President on Hill Areas administration.
4. The **Central Government may give directions** to the State on Hill Areas administration.

## Article 371F: Sikkim (36th Amendment, 1975)

The 36th Amendment made Sikkim a full State and added 371F: Assembly of **not less than 30**; **one Lok Sabha seat**, the whole State one constituency; Parliament may reserve Assembly seats and delimit constituencies for **different sections** of the population; the Governor has special responsibility for **peace and equitable social and economic advancement** of different sections, acting in his discretion subject to the President's directions; the President may extend any law in force in a State to Sikkim with modifications.

## Article 371G: Mizoram (53rd Amendment, 1986)

Mirrors Nagaland's first clause: Acts of Parliament on Mizo religious or social practices, Mizo customary law, justice according to it, and ownership and transfer of land do not apply unless the Assembly decides. Assembly of **not less than 40**.

## Article 371H: Arunachal Pradesh (55th Amendment, 1986)

Governor's special responsibility for **law and order** (individual judgement after consulting ministers; final; ceases when the President directs). Assembly of **not less than 30**.

## The common thread

Two protections recur: identity (customary law, land) and security (Governor's law-and-order role). Sikkim adds a third, protecting the balance between its population sections. Note the Prelims-grade detail differences: Nagaland's land clause says 'land and its resources'; Mizoram's says only 'land'; Mizoram's minimum is 40 while others are 30.

### 5. Article 370 in outline, the 2019 reorganisation, and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules

*Objective:* Narrate the Jammu and Kashmir story exactly as the sources give it and place the Schedules in outline.

## The origin (NCERT)

Jammu and Kashmir was a large princely State with the option of joining India or Pakistan or staying independent. In **October 1947** Pakistan sent tribal infiltrators; Maharaja Hari Singh asked for Indian help and **acceded** to India. Because of these circumstances the Constitution gave it 'much greater autonomy' through **Article 370**.

## What Article 370 did (NCERT)

- The **concurrence of the State** was required for Parliament to make laws on matters in the Union and Concurrent Lists; for other States the three lists apply automatically.
- The President, with the State government's concurrence, could specify which parts of the Union List applied. Two Constitutional orders made large parts of the Constitution applicable, so Parliament's power over Union List subjects came to be 'fully accepted', though the State kept a **separate constitution and a flag**.
- Remaining differences: no emergency on grounds of **internal disturbance** without the State's concurrence; **no financial emergency** could be imposed; the **Directive Principles did not apply**; constitutional amendments under Article 368 applied only with the State's concurrence.

## 2019: the change (NCERT and Laxmikanth)

NCERT states that the special status under Article 370 'no longer exists' and that by the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** the State was bifurcated into two Union Territories, **Jammu and Kashmir** and **Ladakh**, effective **31 October 2019**. Laxmikanth records the government's stated reasons when introducing the Bill: Ladakh is large, sparsely populated and difficult in terrain, with a long-standing demand for UT status, so it became a UT **without a legislature**; the internal security situation fuelled by cross-border terrorism justified a separate UT of Jammu and Kashmir **with a legislature**. Laxmikanth gives the J&K Assembly as **83 members** (the source-edition figure, which reflects the pre-delimitation position) and lists both UTs with a **Lieutenant Governor** and the common **Jammu and Kashmir High Court**.

## Later developments (not in the sources read; verify each against official sources)

- **Delimitation (2022).** After the Delimitation Commission's 2022 order, the Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly is reported to have **90 elected seats** (with 24 further seats notionally reserved for Pakistan-occupied Kashmir and kept vacant), plus members nominated by the Lieutenant Governor under the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation (Amendment) Act, 2023. Use 83 only as the figure printed in the Laxmikanth edition; the 90-seat figure is the one to verify and use for current answers.
- **Supreme Court, December 2023.** A Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court is reported to have upheld the abrogation of Article 370 (December 2023), recorded an assurance on behalf of the Union that statehood would be restored, and asked for Assembly elections to be held by a stated date in 2024.
- **Elections, 2024.** Assembly elections were reported to have been held in September-October 2024 for the first time since 2019, after which an elected government took office. Verify the dates and the present constitutional status (including any restoration of statehood) before using them.

The constitutional mechanics of the abrogation (the Presidential orders of August 2019, the role of Article 367, Article 35A) are **not covered by the sources read here**; treat them as items to verify before using in an answer.

## Fifth and Sixth Schedules (outline only; separate topics)

- The **Fifth Schedule** governs **Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes** in States other than the four Sixth Schedule States; it works through the Governor's reports and Tribes Advisory Councils.
- The **Sixth Schedule** creates **autonomous district and regional councils** with law-making and judicial powers for tribal areas in **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram**. Laxmikanth's note names Assam's Sixth Schedule areas (North Cachar Hills, Karbi Anglong, Bodoland Territorial Areas District), the same areas whose members form the Article 371B committee. NCERT's exercise reproduces a news item on Sixth Schedule status for the Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council, showing that the Schedule is a live tool of accommodation.

Together, Part XXI, the Fifth and Sixth Schedules and Article 370 (historically) form India's toolkit of asymmetry, ranging from mild (development boards) to deep (a separate constitution).

### 6. Union Territories: why they exist and how they are run (Articles 239-241)

*Objective:* Explain the origin, list, administration, legislation and judiciary of Union Territories.

## Article 1 and the First Schedule

The territory of India has three categories: territories of the **States**, **Union Territories**, and territories that may be **acquired**. States are federal members sharing power with the Centre; UTs are under the **direct control and administration** of the Central government, hence 'centrally administered territories'. Laxmikanth's edition counts 29 States and nine UTs; after the 2020 merger of Dadra and Nagar Haveli with Daman and Diu the UT count is commonly given as eight and States as 28 (not in the source; verify against the current First Schedule).

## How UTs came to be

In 1874 the British created **'scheduled districts'**, later **chief commissioners' provinces**. After independence they became **Part C States and Part D Territories** (the 1950 Constitution had Parts A, B, C and D). The **7th Amendment Act and the States Reorganisation Act, 1956** turned them into Union Territories. Some were later elevated to statehood (Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Tripura, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa). Territories acquired from the Portuguese (Goa, Daman and Diu, Dadra and Nagar Haveli) and the French (Puducherry) became UTs.

Laxmikanth's list with year of creation: Andaman and Nicobar Islands 1956; Delhi 1956 (National Capital Territory since 1992); Lakshadweep 1956 (called Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands till 1973); Dadra and Nagar Haveli 1961; Daman and Diu 1962; Puducherry 1962 (Pondicherry till 2006); Chandigarh 1966; Jammu and Kashmir 2019; Ladakh 2019.

Reasons for creating UTs: **political and administrative** (Delhi, Chandigarh); **cultural distinctiveness** (Puducherry, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Daman and Diu); **strategic importance** (Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep); **special care of backward and tribal people** (Mizoram, Manipur, Tripura, Arunachal Pradesh, which later became States).

## Administration: Article 239

Every UT is administered by the **President through an administrator** he appoints. The administrator is an **agent of the President, not a head of State like a Governor**. The President fixes the designation: **Lieutenant Governor** (Delhi, Puducherry, Andaman and Nicobar, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh) or **Administrator** (Chandigarh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Daman and Diu, Lakshadweep). The President may make a State's Governor the administrator of an adjoining UT, in which capacity he acts **independently of his Council of Ministers** (Punjab's Governor for Chandigarh).

## Legislatures and Parliament's power

Puducherry (1963), Delhi (1992) and Jammu and Kashmir (2019) have a **Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister**; the other UTs do not. Creating such institutions does **not diminish the supreme control of the President and Parliament**. **Parliament can legislate on any subject of all three Lists** for every UT, including those with assemblies. Within that umbrella: Puducherry's Assembly may legislate on the **State and Concurrent Lists**; Delhi's on the same **except public order, police and land**; Jammu and Kashmir's on the same **except public order and police**.

## Regulations: Article 240

The President may make **regulations for peace, progress and good government** of Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Daman and Diu and Ladakh; for Puducherry only when its Assembly is **suspended or dissolved**. A regulation has the force of an Act of Parliament and may repeal or amend one in relation to those UTs.

## High Courts: Article 241

Parliament may create a High Court for a UT or place it under an adjacent State's High Court. **Delhi is the only UT with a High Court of its own (since 1966); Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh share a common High Court (since 2019); the other UTs are under adjacent States' High Courts.** Bombay High Court covers Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu; Andaman and Nicobar is under Calcutta, Chandigarh under Punjab and Haryana, Lakshadweep under Kerala, Puducherry under Madras; Jammu and Kashmir High Court is common to J&K and Ladakh.

## Advisory committees

The Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal ministry for UTs. UTs without legislatures have a **Home Minister's Advisory Committee** (chaired by the Union Home Minister) or **Administrator's Advisory Committee** (chaired by the Administrator), with the local MP and elected members of local bodies, to discuss social and economic development.

### 7. Delhi's hybrid: the 69th Amendment and Article 239AA

*Objective:* Describe Delhi's special arrangement precisely and contrast it with Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir.

## The 69th Amendment Act, 1991 (effective 1 February 1992)

It gave the Union Territory of Delhi a special status: renamed it the **National Capital Territory of Delhi**, designated its administrator the **Lieutenant Governor**, and created a **Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers**. Before this Delhi had a metropolitan council and an executive council.

## The Assembly

**70 members**, directly elected; elections conducted by the **Election Commission of India**. It may legislate on all **State List and Concurrent List** matters **except public order, police and land**. **Parliament's laws prevail** over the Assembly's.

## The Council of Ministers

Strength fixed at **ten per cent of the Assembly**, that is **seven** (one Chief Minister and six ministers). The **Chief Minister is appointed by the President**, not by the Lieutenant Governor; other ministers by the President on the Chief Minister's advice; they hold office during the President's pleasure and are **collectively responsible to the Assembly**.

## Aid and advice, and the escape valve

The Council aids and advises the Lieutenant Governor **except where he is required to act in his discretion**. On a **difference of opinion** between the Lieutenant Governor and his ministers, he **refers the matter to the President** and acts on the President's decision. This clause is the constitutional root of the recurring Delhi disputes. The Supreme Court has interpreted it twice at Constitution Bench level; the following dated notes are **not in the sources read** and must each be verified against the judgments and Acts before use (see module 9 on the 2018 PYQ).

- **July 2018 Constitution Bench ruling (State (NCT of Delhi) v Union of India).** Reported to hold that the Lieutenant Governor is bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except where he refers a matter to the President, that he has no independent decision-making power, and that a reference should not be made routinely.
- **May 2023 services judgment (Constitution Bench, Government of NCT of Delhi v Union of India).** Reported to hold that the elected Delhi government controls 'services' other than the subjects excluded from its competence (public order, police, land).
- **Gazette follow-up.** The Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2021 (defining 'Government' in the Delhi context as the Lieutenant Governor) and the Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023 (created a National Capital Civil Services Authority for transfers and postings, with the Lieutenant Governor having the final say where the members differ) are reported to have shifted the balance back; challenges to them were reported as pending. Verify the current legal position before writing.

## Failure of constitutional machinery: Article 239AB

If administration cannot be carried on under these provisions, the **President may suspend them** and make incidental provisions, on the Lieutenant Governor's report or otherwise; this resembles **Article 356** for States.

## Ordinances: Article 239B

The Lieutenant Governor may promulgate ordinances **during recess of the Assembly** with the same force as an Act of the Assembly; each must be approved by the Assembly **within six weeks of reassembly**; he may withdraw one at any time; he **cannot** promulgate one while the Assembly is **dissolved or suspended**; and **no ordinance may be promulgated or withdrawn without the President's prior permission**.

## Three legislatures compared

| Feature | Puducherry (1963) | Delhi (1992) | Jammu and Kashmir (2019) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal source | Article 239A and Act of Parliament | 69th Amendment, Article 239AA | J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 |
| Assembly size | 30 | 70 | 83 in the Laxmikanth edition (pre-delimitation); 90 elected seats after the 2022 delimitation plus nominated members (verify) |
| Can legislate on | State and Concurrent Lists | Same, except public order, police, land | Same, except public order and police |
| President's regulations | Only when Assembly suspended or dissolved | Not listed in Article 240 | Not listed in Article 240 |
| Head | Lieutenant Governor | Lieutenant Governor | Lieutenant Governor |

Delhi's design is called a hybrid because it has an elected government like a State but excludes the three subjects most tied to the national capital's security and land, keeps Parliament supreme and routes disagreements to the President.

### 8. Master view: State versus Union Territory, and the spectrum of asymmetry

*Objective:* Consolidate the whole topic into one comparison and one evaluative argument.

## State versus Union Territory (Laxmikanth Table 40.2, expanded)

| Feature | State | Union Territory |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship with Centre | Federal | Unitary |
| Power | Shares a constitutional distribution of power | Under direct control and administration of the Centre |
| Autonomy | Has autonomy | No autonomy of its own |
| Uniformity | Uniform administrative set-up | No uniformity (some have assemblies, some do not) |
| Executive head | Governor | Administrator, Lieutenant Governor or Chief Commissioner |
| Status of head | Constitutional head | Agent of the President |
| Parliament and the State List | Cannot legislate except in extraordinary circumstances | Can legislate on any subject of the three Lists |
| Judiciary | Own High Court or shared by law | Delhi is the only UT with its own High Court; J&K and Ladakh share a common High Court; the other UTs are under adjacent States' High Courts |

## The spectrum of asymmetry

Arrange the instruments from least to most autonomy:
1. **Union Territory without legislature** (Ladakh, Chandigarh): President's regulations, Parliament's laws, advisory committee.
2. **Union Territory with legislature** (Puducherry, Delhi, Jammu and Kashmir): elected government, but excluded subjects, Parliament supreme, disputes referred to the President.
3. **Ordinary State**: full State List, Governor as constitutional head.
4. **State with Part XXI provisions**: plus development boards, Governor's special duties, or protection from Parliament's laws on customary matters (Nagaland, Mizoram).
5. **Sixth Schedule areas within States**: autonomous district councils.
6. **Former Article 370 position**: separate constitution and flag, Parliament's power conditional on concurrence; ended in 2019.

NCERT's evaluation gives you both sides for Mains. For: differential treatment recognises 'peculiar social and historical circumstances' and keeps diverse regions within one Union; the conclusion says a polity 'sensitive to diversities and to the demands for autonomy' is the basis of cooperative federalism. Against: the provisions 'have not been able to stem alienation and the insurgency' in parts of the north-east, and NCERT's own exercise asks whether special provisions 'create resentment among other States'.

## Confused pairs to settle now

- **Governor versus Administrator**: constitutional head versus agent of the President; a Governor acting as administrator of an adjoining UT acts without his Council of Ministers.
- **Article 371A (Nagaland) versus 371G (Mizoram)**: same customary-law clause; Nagaland adds Governor's law-and-order role, Tuensang and 'land and its resources'; Mizoram adds the 40-member minimum.
- **Article 371B (Assam) versus 371C (Manipur)**: Assam's committee covers Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas; Manipur's covers presidentially declared Hill Areas and adds Central directions and the Governor's annual report.
- **Puducherry versus Delhi regulations**: the President may legislate by regulation for Puducherry only when its Assembly is suspended or dissolved; Delhi is not in the Article 240 list at all.
- **Special provisions versus Fifth/Sixth Schedules**: Part XXI works State-wide through Governor and Assembly; the Schedules work area-wide through councils.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Special provisions: Articles 371-371J in Part XXI ('Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions') giving twelve States arrangements others do not have.
- Asymmetric federalism: differential treatment of federal units because of their social, historical or geographical circumstances; NCERT calls it the most extraordinary feature of India's federal arrangement.
- Union Territory: a territory under the direct control and administration of the Central government, administered by the President through an administrator (Article 239).
- Administrator: the President's agent in a UT, designated Lieutenant Governor, Chief Commissioner or Administrator; not a constitutional head like a Governor.
- Governor's special responsibility: a duty given to a Governor under Part XXI to be discharged in his individual judgement or discretion, often ceasing when the President so directs.

### Notes: Articles 371-371J at a glance

- 371 Maharashtra and Gujarat: development boards for Vidarbha, Marathwada, Saurashtra, Kutch; annual report; equitable funds; technical education and employment.
- 371A Nagaland: Parliament's Acts on Naga religion, customary law, justice and land do not apply unless Assembly decides; Governor's law-and-order responsibility; Tuensang regional council of 35.
- 371B Assam: committee of Assembly members from Tribal Areas (Sixth Schedule areas).
- 371C Manipur: committee for Hill Areas; Governor's special responsibility and annual report; Central directions.
- 371D Andhra Pradesh and Telangana: equitable public employment and education; local cadres; Administrative Tribunal outside High Court purview. 371E: Central University in Andhra Pradesh.
- 371F Sikkim: Assembly of at least 30; one Lok Sabha seat; seats for sections; Governor's responsibility for peace and advancement of sections; President may extend laws.
- 371G Mizoram: Parliament's Acts on Mizo religion, customary law, justice and land do not apply unless Assembly decides; Assembly of at least 40.
- 371H Arunachal Pradesh: Governor's law-and-order responsibility; Assembly of at least 30.
- 371I Goa: Assembly of at least 30.
- 371J Karnataka: development board for Hyderabad-Karnataka; reservations in education and State posts for the region.

### Notes: Inserting amendments

- 7th Amendment 1956 and Bombay Reorganisation Act 1960: Article 371 (Andhra Pradesh removed by the 32nd Amendment 1973).
- 13th Amendment 1962: Article 371A (Nagaland).
- 22nd Amendment 1969: Article 371B (Assam).
- 27th Amendment 1971: Article 371C (Manipur).
- 32nd Amendment 1973: Articles 371D and 371E (Andhra Pradesh); 371D extended to Telangana by the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014.
- 36th Amendment 1975: Article 371F (Sikkim becomes a full State).
- 53rd Amendment 1986: Article 371G (Mizoram). 55th Amendment 1986: Article 371H (Arunachal Pradesh). 56th Amendment 1987: Article 371I (Goa).
- 98th Amendment 2012: Article 371J (Karnataka).
- 69th Amendment 1991 (effective 1 February 1992): Article 239AA for Delhi.

### Notes: Union Territories chronology

- 1874: scheduled districts under British rule; later chief commissioners' provinces.
- 1950: Part C States and Part D Territories in the four-fold classification (Parts A, B, C, D).
- 1956: 7th Amendment and States Reorganisation Act create Union Territories; Andaman and Nicobar, Delhi, Lakshadweep created as UTs.
- 1961 Dadra and Nagar Haveli; 1962 Daman and Diu, Puducherry; 1963 Puducherry gets a legislature; 1966 Chandigarh; 1966 Delhi High Court.
- 1973: Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands renamed Lakshadweep. 1992: Delhi redesignated National Capital Territory with a legislature. 2006: Pondicherry renamed Puducherry.
- 31 October 2019: Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 creates the UTs of Jammu and Kashmir (with legislature) and Ladakh (without).
- Former UTs now States: Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Tripura, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa.

### Notes: State versus Union Territory

- Laxmikanth Table 40.2 is the Prelims and Mains reference for this contrast.

### Notes: Legislatures in Union Territories

- Three UTs have assemblies; their competence differs by excluded subjects.

### Notes: Article 370 and 2019: cause and effect

- Princely State with option to join either dominion + tribal invasion of October 1947 -> accession under pressure -> Article 370 gave greater autonomy (State concurrence for Union and Concurrent List laws).
- Presidential orders with State concurrence -> large parts of the Constitution applied -> Parliament's Union List power accepted while separate constitution and flag remained.
- Residual differences: no internal-disturbance emergency without concurrence; no financial emergency; Directive Principles inapplicable; Article 368 amendments needed concurrence.
- 2019 Reorganisation Act (effective 31 October 2019) -> two UTs: Jammu and Kashmir with legislature (security rationale), Ladakh without (terrain and long-standing demand).

### Notes: Exceptions and fine print

- Tuensang: for ten years from statehood (extendable by the Governor on the regional council's recommendation) the district was administered by the Governor and its Assembly members were chosen by the regional council, not directly elected. This was a transitional arrangement (c. 1963-73); Tuensang's members are directly elected today (verify against the current Election Commission list).
- The Governor's law-and-order responsibility in Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh ceases when the President so directs; the validity of his individual judgement cannot be questioned.
- The Andhra Pradesh Administrative Tribunal is outside the High Court's purview; only the Supreme Court has jurisdiction over its matters.
- A Governor acting as administrator of an adjoining UT acts independently of his Council of Ministers.
- The Delhi Lieutenant Governor cannot promulgate an ordinance when the Assembly is dissolved or suspended, and needs the President's prior permission to promulgate or withdraw one.
- Creating a legislature for a UT does not reduce Parliament's power to legislate on State List subjects for it.

### Notes: Commonly confused pairs

- Governor (constitutional head of a State) versus Administrator (agent of the President in a UT).
- Article 371A Nagaland ('land and its resources', Tuensang, law and order) versus Article 371G Mizoram ('land', 40-member minimum).
- Article 371B Assam (Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas committee) versus Article 371C Manipur (Hill Areas committee, Central directions, Governor's report).
- Delhi's three excluded subjects (public order, police, land) versus Jammu and Kashmir's two (public order, police).
- Part XXI special provisions (State-wide, via Governor and Assembly) versus Fifth and Sixth Schedules (area-based councils).

### Last-week summary

- Part XXI 'Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions': 371-371J, twelve States; letters in insertion order: A Nagaland 1962, B Assam, C Manipur, D-E Andhra, F Sikkim 1975, G Mizoram, H Arunachal, I Goa, J Karnataka 2012.
- Nagaland and Mizoram: Parliament's Acts on religion, customary law, justice and land apply only if the Assembly decides; minimum Assembly Mizoram 40, Sikkim/Arunachal/Goa 30.
- UTs (Arts 239-241): administered by the President through an administrator, his agent; Parliament legislates on any List; Delhi only UT with own High Court (1966).
- Delhi 239AA (69th Amendment 1991, in force 1 Feb 1992): 70 members, CM appointed by President, excludes public order, police, land; J&K excludes public order, police.
- J&K Reorganisation Act effective 31 Oct 2019: UT of J&K with legislature, Ladakh without.
