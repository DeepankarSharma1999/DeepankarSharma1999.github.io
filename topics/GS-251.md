## GS-251: Farm subsidies direct and indirect

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Agriculture and food

*Goal:* Explain Farm subsidies direct and indirect and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why subsidies were introduced

When HYV seeds arrived, farmers viewed the new package as risky. Government provided subsidised fertiliser and low-interest loans so that small farmers could also buy inputs. NCERT credits this state role for preventing the Green Revolution from favouring only rich cultivators: output on small farms eventually matched that on large farms. Research institutes set up by the state also cut the risk that a pest attack would ruin small adopters. Their original rationale was diffusion of technology, which raises the question of whether they should continue once adoption is widespread.

### Direct support: PM-KISAN and related transfers

A direct subsidy hands farmers cash instead of cheapening one input. PM-KISAN, a Central Sector scheme that began on 24 February 2019, credits ₹6,000 a year, in three equal instalments every four months, to the bank accounts of landholding farmer families through DBT. Its 2020 operational guidelines treat husband, wife and minor children owning cultivable land in state records as one family, and shut out institutional landholders and better-off households such as income-tax payers, holders of constitutional posts, most government employees and practising professionals. Over ₹4.09 lakh crore had been paid across 21 instalments (Economic Survey 2025-26). Because eligibility follows land records, tenants who rent land may not benefit directly. Credit support is a hybrid: under MISS, crop loans cost 7 per cent, with another 3 per cent off for prompt repayment.

### Indirect subsidies: fertiliser, power and water

Fertiliser support is indirect: the Department of Fertilisers reimburses companies for quantities sold to farmers through PoS machines. NCERT notes that much of the gain goes to industry and prosperous regions, while defenders say poor farmers still need help, making targeting the middle path. Cheap urea also distorts nutrient choice. The N:P:K ratio slid from 4:3.2:1 in 2009-10 to roughly 10.9:4.1:1 in 2023-24, eroding soil organic matter and crop response (Economic Survey 2025-26). Nutrient-based pricing, neem-coated urea, Aadhaar-linked sales and iFMS tightened control but did not change relative prices, so the Survey proposes a modestly higher urea price offset by an equal per-acre transfer. NCERT's price-signal logic is the same for free power and water, which push water-hungry crops into dry regions.
