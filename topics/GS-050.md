### Bridge: Words of constitutional change

Ten words carry this topic.

The **franchise** is the right to vote; a **restricted franchise** means only people with property, tax-paying capacity or education could vote. A **constituency** is the group of voters that elects one member. An **electorate** is the set of voters allowed to choose a representative. In a **general electorate** all voters in the area vote together. In a **separate (communal) electorate** voters of one community alone choose a member of that community, so a Muslim candidate faces only Muslim voters. In a **joint electorate with reserved seats** everyone votes together but some seats are set aside for a group. **Weightage** means giving a community more seats than its share of the population would produce.

**Responsible government** means the executive answers to an elected legislature and can be removed by it; the opposite is an executive appointed from above. **Dyarchy** (double rule) split provincial government into two halves, one answerable to the legislature and one not. **Bicameral** means a legislature of two houses; **unicameral** means one. An **ordinance** is an emergency order issued by the executive without the legislature.

Keep one question beside every Act: who votes, who rules, and who can be removed?

### 1. Orientation: from the 1909 council to the Wavell Plan on one page

*Objective:* Place every Act, commission and plan between 1909 and 1947 on one timeline and name the one idea each tried.

Between 1909 and 1947 Britain issued a chain of reforms, commissions and offers. Each answered a pressure from Indian politics, and each stopped short of handing over real power. Learn the chain as three stages.

**Stage 1, reform by instalments (1909 to 1919).** The Morley-Minto reforms of 1909 added elected members and introduced separate electorates for Muslims. The Montagu declaration of August 1917 promised responsible government by stages; the Government of India Act 1919 delivered dyarchy in the provinces.

**Stage 2, the search for a constitution (1927 to 1935).** The Simon Commission was appointed in November 1927 and boycotted. Indians answered with the Nehru Report of 1928, and the League with Jinnah's Fourteen Points in 1929. Round Table Conferences, the Communal Award and the Poona Pact led to the Government of India Act 1935.

**Stage 3, war-time offers and the endgame (1940 to 1947).** The August Offer (1940), the Cripps proposals (1942) and the Wavell Plan (1945) tried to win support for the war and keep control; the Cabinet Mission and the Mountbatten plan close the story.

### 2. Foundation: the councils from 1861 to 1909 and the birth of separate electorates

*Objective:* State what the Acts of 1861, 1892 and 1909 each added and why the 1909 Act is the most debated.

GS-033 module 2 and GS-158 module 4 teach the Acts of 1858 and 1861; here is only the recap needed to read 1909.

## 1861 and 1892 in four lines each

**1861.** Spectrum says the principle that non-officials sit in law-making bodies was accepted; Canning's portfolio system laid the base of cabinet government; legislative powers returned to Bombay and Madras, starting devolution. The councils still could not discuss finance without prior approval, had no budget control, and the Secretary of State could disallow a law. The Viceroy's ordinance power is in GS-033.

**1892.** The Congress (founded 1885) called council reform the root of all reforms. The Act enlarged the councils and let universities, district boards, municipalities, zamindars and chambers of commerce recommend members. The word *election* was avoided, but indirect election came in. Members could discuss the budget and put questions after six days' notice (Spectrum).

## The 1909 Act, the Morley-Minto reforms

Context: the partition of Bengal (GS-038), the Swadeshi movement, the Simla Deputation of 1906 and the League's demand for separate electorates. Provisions (Spectrum, with GS-158 where marked):

- The Imperial Legislative Council was enlarged (GS-158 says from 16 to 60 members; verify).
- Elected members were added to the councils, still indirectly through electoral colleges. GS-158 notes an official majority kept at the centre and a non-official majority allowed in the provinces (verify).
- The powers of the councils were increased: Spectrum notes more discussion, but the real power stayed with the government, leaving the councils little but criticism.
- For the first time an Indian joined the Viceroy's Executive Council: **Satyendra Prasad Sinha** as law member.
- **Separate electorates for Muslims**, with representation above their population share and a lower income qualification for Muslim voters.

## Why 1909 is debated

Spectrum calls the system very indirect and the people's representation remote and unreal, and says separate electorates created new problems. GS-038 shows Spectrum judging the intention as dividing nationalists and NCERT noting the incentive for politicians to win favours for their own community. Keep the claims apart: the Act's intention (as Spectrum reads it), its effect (a political wall between communities), and the nationalist reaction (the Congress accepted separate electorates later at Lucknow in 1916; GS-040 module 7).

## The slip to avoid

Spectrum's chapter summary lists an 'Indian Councils Act of 1882'. No such Act belongs to this series; the constitutional Acts are 1861, 1892 and 1909 (1882 is the year of Ripon's local-government resolution, GS-033 module 5; standard, verify).

### 3. Core: the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms and the Government of India Act 1919

*Objective:* List the main provisions of the 1919 Act at the centre and in the provinces, and its limits.

## From declaration to Act

On 20 August 1917 the Secretary of State, Edwin Montagu, declared that the goal was the gradual introduction of **responsible government** as an integral part of the British Empire (GS-040 module 5). Spectrum adds the sting: the British Parliament, not Indian self-determination, would decide the time and manner of each step. The Act is named after Montagu and the Viceroy, Lord Chelmsford. It was passed in December 1919 and came into operation in 1921 (Spectrum speaks of the Constitution 'set up in 1921'; the exact passing date is standard knowledge, verify). GS-042 module 2 explains why the same year also brought the Rowlatt Act, the stick beside this carrot.

## Provisions

- **A two-house centre.** The Imperial Legislative Council was replaced by a **Council of State** (upper house) and a **Legislative Assembly** (lower house). Each was to have a majority of directly elected members. Direct election was introduced, but the franchise was restricted by property, tax or education.
- **Communal electorates extended** to Sikhs, Christians and Anglo-Indians as well as Muslims (Spectrum).
- **Dyarchy in the provinces** (module 4). The provincial legislature was a single house, the legislative council.
- **Separate budgets.** For the first time provincial and central budgets were separated, and provincial legislatures could make their own budgets. The central legislature could vote supplies.
- **A High Commissioner for India** in London, holding office for six years, to look after Indian trade in Europe and take over some functions of the Secretary of State.
- **The Secretary of State's salary** was now paid by the British Exchequer, undoing the injustice of the Charter Act of 1793, which charged it to Indian revenue (Spectrum).
- **A review clause.** A Royal Commission was to be appointed after ten years; this is the root of the Simon Commission (module 5). The Act also contemplated a statutory public service commission (Spectrum, in its note on the Lee Commission).

## The limits

The central legislature could not remove the government, and its control over law and finance was subject to the **Governor-General's overriding powers**: he could veto a bill, reserve it for the King's pleasure, or secure the enactment of laws he thought essential for the safety, tranquillity or interests of British India. Spectrum, quoting Subhash Kashyap, calls the legislature a non-sovereign law-making body, powerless before the executive. The structure stayed **unitary and centralised** even though subjects were divided between the centre and provinces. Indian leaders did gain administrative experience, but there was no responsible government.

In one line: 1909 added elected members to councils that governed nothing; 1919 gave elected Indians a share of provincial government but no control of the centre.

### 4. Core: dyarchy, how it worked and why it failed

*Objective:* Define dyarchy, name typical reserved and transferred subjects and give the reasons it failed.

## The idea

**Dyarchy** (double rule) was the device of the 1919 Act for the provinces. The provincial subjects were divided into two lists. **Transferred subjects** were administered by the Governor with **ministers** chosen from the elected members of the legislative council and answerable to it. **Reserved subjects** were administered by the Governor with his **executive councillors**, who were not answerable to the legislature. Spectrum describes dyarchy as a substantial step towards transfer of power, and also says it failed. Both statements are part of the answer.

## The two lists (standard; verify)

The extract does not list the subjects, so the following is standard textbook knowledge to be checked against the Act's schedule. The one sourced case is local self-government, which Spectrum says was made a transferred subject.

| Half | Typical subjects |
|---|---|
| Transferred (ministers) | Local self-government; education (with some exceptions); public health and sanitation; agriculture; excise |
| Reserved (Governor and executive councillors) | Law and order including police; administration of justice; land revenue; irrigation; finance of the reserved half |

A memory aid: the **hard powers** (police, justice, land revenue, the purse) stayed reserved; the **service subjects** (local bodies, schools, health) were transferred.

## Why it failed

1. **Finance was reserved.** Spectrum's own example: local self-government was transferred, but because finance was a reserved subject under an executive councillor, the Indian ministers could not do much for lack of funds. This is the single best example to quote.
2. **Divided responsibility.** One government with two halves, one answerable and one not, produced friction rather than a clear chain of accountability (standard; verify).
3. **The Governor's overriding powers** over ministers and legislation limited what ministers could achieve (standard; verify).
4. **A restricted franchise and communal seats** meant the legislatures did not truly represent the people.
The Muddiman Committee (module 5) records the verdict: its non-official Indian minority called for dyarchy to be abolished immediately.

## Two traps for Prelims

- **Dyarchy is not the Centre and the states.** It is the division of subjects delegated to the provinces into two categories. Option wording such as 'double government at the centre and in the provinces' is the typical wrong answer. Do not call it 'two rulers, in London and Delhi' either.
- **The 1935 Act ended provincial dyarchy but planned dyarchy at the centre.** GS-047 teaches this; Spectrum notes that dyarchy, rejected by the Simon Commission, was provided for in the federal executive.

## Women and the vote (standard; verify)

The 1919 Act did not give all women over twenty-one the vote. Standard books say it left the decision to each provincial legislature (Madras acted in 1921) and kept property or educational qualifications; verify. The 1935 Act then extended electorates and weightage to women (Spectrum).

### 5. Core: reviewing the 1919 Act, from Muddiman and Butler to the Simon Commission

*Objective:* Order the post-1919 inquiries and explain why the Simon Commission was appointed early, boycotted and overtaken.

## The inquiries

Spectrum lists several bodies set up because the 1919 Act did not produce a stable arrangement.

- **Lee Commission (appointed 1923, reported 1924; Spectrum heads it 1924, PDF p566; verify).** On the superior civil services: it asked for the statutory public service commission to be set up without delay (GS-033 module 6).
- **Muddiman Committee (Reforms Enquiry Committee, 1924).** On the working of the 1919 scheme. The majority suggested minor changes; the non-official Indian minority wanted dyarchy abolished at once and the constitution democratised.
- **Butler Committee (1927), standard; verify.** Official name, Indian States Committee, under Sir Harcourt Butler. Its object was the relationship between the paramount power (the Government of India) and the princely states. A verified Prelims question asks about it, and it is not in the project extracts, so check it in a standard text.

## The Simon Commission

The 1919 Act provided for a commission after ten years. In **November 1927** (8 November, Spectrum) the Conservative government under Baldwin announced the **Indian Statutory Commission**, two years early. Spectrum gives the motive: the Conservatives feared losing the coming election and did not want to leave India to Labour. Lord Birkenhead, the Secretary of State, who had often declared that Indians could not agree on a constitution, was responsible.

It had seven members, all British: four Conservatives, two Labour and one Liberal, with **Sir John Simon** as chairman. Spectrum says it was under the joint chairmanship of Simon and Clement Attlee, which is a slip: Attlee was a member (verify). The offence was the exclusion of Indians, since foreigners would judge India's fitness for self-government, which Indians saw as an insult and a denial of self-determination.

**Responses.** The Congress, at Madras in December 1927 under M. A. Ansari, resolved to boycott it at every stage and in every form. The majority League faction under Jinnah and liberal Hindu Mahasabha leaders joined the boycott; the Shafi group of the League, the Punjab Unionists and the Justice Party did not. The Commission landed at Bombay on 3 February 1928 to a countrywide hartal and black flags. The agitation, the death of Lala Lajpat Rai and the youth movement are told in **GS-044 (Day 15, not yet written)**.

## The report (Spectrum: May 1930; published June 1930, verify)

A two-volume report proposed abolishing dyarchy and giving the provinces responsible government and more members, with the Governor holding special powers over internal security and minority protection. It rejected responsible government at the centre (the Governor-General would pick the cabinet), kept communal electorates until communal tension eased and offered no adult franchise. It accepted federation in principle but not soon, and suggested separating Burma from India. By the time it appeared events had overtaken it.

Dr Ambedkar's evidence to the Commission, which asked for adult franchise and reserved seats for the depressed classes, is in GS-052 module 6.

### 6. Core: the Nehru Report, the Fourteen Points and the Irwin declaration

*Objective:* Narrate the Indian reply to Birkenhead's challenge and the communal and political reactions of 1928 and 1929.

## Birkenhead's challenge and the Nehru Report

Lord Birkenhead had repeatedly said Indians could not frame a constitution acceptable to all. An **All Parties Conference** in February 1928 appointed a committee under **Motilal Nehru**, to draft one (Spectrum: the February 1928 conference appointed the committee; standard accounts place the appointment at the Bombay session of 19 May 1928, verify). Spectrum calls it the first major Indian attempt to draft a constitutional framework. It was finalised in August 1928.

**Main recommendations (Spectrum).** Dominion status on the lines of the self-governing dominions; rejection of separate electorates in favour of joint electorates with seats reserved for Muslims at the centre and in provinces where they were a minority, not in Punjab and Bengal; linguistic provinces; nineteen fundamental rights including equal rights for women, the right to form unions and universal adult suffrage; responsible government at the centre and in the provinces; a 500-member House of Representatives and 200-member Senate; full protection of Muslim cultural and religious interests; and complete separation of State from religion. The report confined itself to British India, linking it to the princely states on a federal basis. On the goal there was one dissent: a section wanted complete independence, with liberty of action.

## The communal rift

Spectrum dates the **Delhi Proposals** to a meeting of Muslim leaders at Delhi in December 1927; standard accounts date them to 20 March 1927, with the Madras Congress of December 1927 accepting them (verify). There were four proposals: joint electorates with reserved seats, one-third Muslim seats in the Central Legislative Assembly, proportional representation for Muslims in Punjab and Bengal, and three new Muslim-majority provinces (Sindh, Baluchistan, NWFP). The Hindu Mahasabha opposed the new provinces and the reservation in Punjab and Bengal, and demanded a unitary structure. Facing a dilemma, the Nehru Report made concessions to the Hindu side: reservation only where Muslims were a minority, Sindh separated only after dominion status, and a broadly unitary structure.

At the all-party meeting at Calcutta in December 1928, Jinnah for the League proposed three amendments: one-third Muslim seats in the central legislature, reserved Muslim seats in Bengal and Punjab in proportion to population, and residual powers to the provinces. They were not accepted. In **March 1929** he put forward the **Fourteen Points**, which Spectrum says became the basis of the League's later propaganda: a federal constitution with residual powers to the provinces, one-third Muslim representation at the centre and in cabinets, separate electorates, safeguards against majority laws, no territorial change reducing Muslim majorities in Punjab, Bengal and NWFP, separation of Sindh, and religious and cultural protections.

## Calcutta Congress and the Irwin declaration

The Calcutta Congress of December 1928 approved the Nehru Report, but younger leaders (Jawaharlal Nehru, Bose, Satyamurthy) pressed for complete independence. A compromise gave the government a year to accept dominion status, failing which the Congress would demand independence and launch civil disobedience. On **31 October 1929** Viceroy Irwin announced, on behalf of the government, that dominion status was the natural issue of the 1917 declaration. Spectrum notes no time scale was given and nothing new was offered. Irwin's refusal to promise dominion status as the aim of the coming conference led on to Lahore and the Salt March (GS-045).

### 7. Core: from the Round Table Conferences to the Government of India Act 1935 (a bridge summary)

*Objective:* Place the Round Table Conferences, the Communal Award, the Poona Pact, the White Paper and the 1935 Act in order, without retelling GS-046, GS-047 and GS-052.

## The path in six steps

1. **Round Table Conferences.** Three were held: November 1930 to January 1931 (without the Congress), September to December 1931 (Gandhi the Congress's sole representative) and a third in late 1932 (GS-046, with the Gandhi-Irwin Pact).
2. **Communal Award, 16 August 1932.** Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald's award continued separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans and extended them to the depressed classes (GS-052 module 6).
3. **Poona Pact, 24 September 1932.** After Gandhi's fast, separate electorates for the depressed classes were replaced by reserved seats in a joint electorate (GS-052 module 6 has the figures and flags). This 'joint electorate plus reserved seats' formula is the one independent India kept (GS-158).
4. **White Paper, March 1933.** Spectrum says it proposed a federal set-up and provincial autonomy.
5. **Joint Committee under Lord Linlithgow, report 1934.** It said a federation would come into being only if enough princely states were ready to join; Spectrum says at least 50 per cent of them (the exact test, by states and by population or seats, is standard; verify).
6. **The Act of 1935,** passed on the basis of that report.

## The 1935 Act in one table (details in GS-047)

| Feature | Content |
|---|---|
| Size | 321 sections and 10 schedules (Spectrum prints 'articles'; an Act has sections) |
| Federation | An All-India Federation of provinces and acceding states, never created because the states and parties opposed it |
| Provinces | Dyarchy abolished; provincial autonomy with ministers responsible to legislatures, subject to the Governor's safeguards; provincial autonomy began on 1 April 1937 |
| Legislatures | Bicameral in six provinces (Madras, Bombay, Bengal, United Provinces, Bihar, Assam) |
| Electorates | Communal electorates and weightage extended to depressed classes, women and labour |
| Franchise | About 10 per cent of the population |
| Court | Federal Court, established 1937, under the shadow of the Privy Council |

## What to take from it

Spectrum calls the Act an attempt to give India a written constitution, but one in which Indians had no hand, rigid, with amendment reserved to the British Parliament, and condemned by nearly all sections. The Congress unanimously rejected it and demanded instead a **Constituent Assembly elected on adult franchise**. The extension of communal electorates promoted separatist tendencies, in Spectrum's view culminating in partition.

## Two cautions

- Spectrum (PDF p562) says the operative part of the Act stayed in force till 15 August 1947. GS-047 gives the safer wording: under the Indian Independence Act 1947 the Act, as adapted, served as the constitution of the Dominion of India until 26 January 1950, when Article 395 of the Constitution repealed it (standard; verify).
- The federal part of the Act never operated. The central government carried on under the 1919 Act with minor amendments until the transfer of power.

### 8. Core: the August Offer of 1940 and the Cripps proposals of 1942

*Objective:* State the content of the August Offer and the Cripps proposals, the responses and why each failed.

GS-048 modules 3 and 4 tell the war-time story and the failure of Cripps; this module compares the constitutional content.

## The August Offer, 1940

After the fall of France Britain was in a conciliatory mood. The Congress asked for an interim government during the war, and the government refused. Viceroy **Linlithgow** announced the **August Offer in August 1940** (8 August is standard; verify). It proposed:

- **dominion status** as the objective for India;
- expansion of the Viceroy's Executive Council with a **majority of Indians** drawn from major parties;
- a **constituent assembly after the war**, in which Indians would mainly decide the constitution, subject to the government's obligations on defence, minority rights, treaties with the states and all-India services;
- **no future constitution without the consent of minorities.**

The Congress rejected it; Nehru said the dominion-status idea was 'dead as a doornail' and Gandhi said it widened the gulf. The League welcomed the assurance that gave it a veto and repeated that partition was the only solution. It left two legacies: an Indian-framed constitution and the minority veto.

## The Cripps proposals, March 1942

Sir **Stafford Cripps**, a left-wing Labour member of the War Cabinet, came in March 1942 because Japan's advance made Indian support crucial and the Allies pressed for a settlement. Spectrum's proposals:

1. An **Indian Union with dominion status**, free to decide its relation with the Commonwealth and to join international bodies.
2. A **constituent assembly after the war**, partly **elected by the provincial assemblies through proportional representation** and partly **nominated by the princes**.
3. Britain would accept the constitution on two conditions: a province unwilling to join could have a separate constitution and form a separate union, and the assembly and the British government would conclude a **treaty** to effect the transfer of power and safeguard minorities.
4. Meanwhile **defence would remain with the British** and the Governor-General's powers would remain intact.

New compared with 1940 (Spectrum): the constitution would be framed solely by Indians; there was a concrete plan for the assembly; any province could stay out, which Spectrum calls a blueprint for partition; free India could leave the Commonwealth; and Indians would have a larger share in the interim administration.

## Why it failed

| Party | Main objection |
|---|---|
| Congress | Dominion status, not independence; princely states represented by nominees; the right of provinces to secede; no immediate transfer of power and no real share in defence |
| League | A single Union; the assembly's machinery; Pakistan not clearly conceded |

A province's exit was to be decided by a 60 per cent vote of its legislature, failing which by a plebiscite of adult males. Talks broke on the Viceroy's veto and the Governor-General's supremacy. Churchill, Amery, Linlithgow and the Commander-in-Chief are said to have torpedoed the effort. Gandhi's phrase for the scheme was 'a post-dated cheque'. GS-048 follows what came next.

The C. Rajagopalachari Formula (March 1944) and the Desai-Liaqat proposal are in the master notes.

### 9. Master: the Wavell Plan and Shimla, and how the pieces fit

*Objective:* Explain the Wavell Plan, why it failed and the through-lines across all the Acts and offers.

## The Wavell Plan, June 1945

With the war in Europe over (May 1945) and Japan still fighting, Churchill's government told Viceroy **Lord Wavell** to negotiate, and Congress leaders were released in June 1945. Spectrum gives three reasons for the new keenness: a British general election was due in mid-1945, the Allies pressed for further Indian cooperation, and the government wanted to divert Indian energy into channels more profitable to Britain. Wavell convened a conference at **Shimla** in June 1945 (the dates, about 25 June to 14 July, are standard; verify).

**Proposals (Spectrum).**

- Except for the Governor-General and the Commander-in-Chief, all members of the Executive Council were to be Indians.
- **Caste Hindus and Muslims were to have equal representation.**
- The reconstructed council would act as an interim government within the 1935 Act, not responsible to the Central Assembly.
- The Governor-General would exercise his veto on the advice of ministers.
- Party representatives would send a joint list of nominees to the Viceroy, or separate lists if they could not agree.
- Negotiations on a new constitution would stay open after the war.

**Why it failed.** The League wanted every Muslim member to be a League nominee, fearing that other minorities with the Congress's aims would reduce it to a one-third minority, and it claimed a communal veto: decisions opposed by Muslims would need a two-thirds majority. Wavell wished to include Khizr Hyat Khan as the Muslim representative of western Punjab. The Congress objected to being painted as a purely caste Hindu party and insisted on its right to name members of all communities. Wavell then announced a breakdown, which Spectrum calls his mistake: it gave the League a virtual veto, strengthened its position (as the 1945-46 elections showed) and boosted Jinnah.

## Prelims patterns

- **Definition items** (2017, dyarchy). Spectrum's wording: the division of the subjects delegated to the provinces into two categories. Options that speak of double government at the centre and in the provinces, or rulers in London and Delhi, describe other ideas.
- **List items** (2022, reserved subjects). Spectrum makes local self-government a transferred subject. Standard books (verify) put police, justice and land revenue in the reserved half. The reasoning is elimination: any combination containing local self-government conflicts with the source.

### Notes: Definitions to hold

- Dyarchy: division of the provincial subjects into transferred (ministers answerable to the legislative council) and reserved (Governor and executive councillors) under the 1919 Act.
- Separate electorate: voters of one community alone elect that community's members; introduced for Muslims in 1909 and extended in 1919 and 1935.
- Joint electorate with reserved seats: all voters choose, but certain seats are set aside for a group; the Nehru Report proposed it for Muslims, and the Poona Pact used it for the depressed classes.
- Weightage: more seats for a community than its population share alone would give.
- Responsible government: an executive answerable to and removable by an elected legislature.
- Dominion status: self-government within the Empire; offered as an objective in 1929 and 1940, rejected by the Congress as insufficient after Lahore 1929.
- Provincial autonomy: ministers alone in the provinces under the 1935 Act, from 1 April 1937, with Governors' safeguards.

### Notes: Who is who

- John Morley (Secretary of State) and Lord Minto (Viceroy): 1909 reforms.
- Edwin Montagu (Secretary of State) and Lord Chelmsford (Viceroy): 1919 Act; Montagu made the 20 August 1917 declaration.
- Satyendra Prasad Sinha: first Indian on the Viceroy's Executive Council (1909, law member).
- Lord Birkenhead: Secretary of State who challenged Indians to produce a constitution; responsible for the Simon Commission.
- Sir John Simon: chairman of the Indian Statutory Commission; Clement Attlee was a member (Spectrum calls him joint chairman: verify).
- Motilal Nehru: chairman of the committee that drafted the Nehru Report.
- Lord Irwin: Viceroy who declared dominion status the natural issue of the 1917 declaration (31 Oct 1929).
- Lord Linlithgow: Viceroy who announced the August Offer, and chaired the Joint Committee on the 1935 Act.
- Stafford Cripps: left-wing Labour minister who led the 1942 mission.
- Lord Wavell: Viceroy who convened the Shimla Conference of June 1945.

### Notes: Cause and effect: why Britain conceded reform

- 1861: after the Revolt, the policy of association, to win Indian cooperation and give the executive the look of consultation.
- 1892: Congress pressure after 1885; the council was the first demand.
- 1909: Swadeshi unrest and the League's demand; reform paired with a policy of separate electorates (Spectrum reads it as dividing nationalists).
- 1919: war-time sacrifices and Home Rule pressure; the 1917 declaration; paired with the Rowlatt Act as carrot and stick (GS-042).
- 1935: the failure of dyarchy, the Simon report, the Round Table Conferences and civil disobedience.
- 1940-45: the war; each offer tried to buy cooperation without surrendering defence or the Governor-General's powers.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Dyarchy (provinces, 1919) versus federation (1935): not the same; the 1935 Act abolished provincial dyarchy and planned dyarchy at the centre, which never operated.
- Simon Commission (all-white, 1927) versus Nehru Report (Indian answer, 1928) versus Irwin declaration (1929).
- Cripps (1942, provinces may stay out) versus Cabinet Mission (1946, GS-051) versus August Offer (1940).
- Delhi Proposals (Muslim leaders, Mar 1927; Spectrum Dec 1927, verify) versus Delhi Manifesto (leaders, 2 Nov 1929) versus the Fourteen Points (Jinnah, Mar 1929).
- Separate electorate versus reserved seats in a joint electorate: the Poona Pact replaced the first by the second.

### Last-week summary

- 1909 separate electorates for Muslims, Sinha on Executive Council; 1919 Council of State + Legislative Assembly, provincial dyarchy (local self-government transferred), electorates extended to Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians.
