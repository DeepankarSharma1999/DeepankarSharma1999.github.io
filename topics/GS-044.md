## GS-044: Swarajists and Simon Commission

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words of council politics: legislature, boycott, obstruction, 'end or mend'

A **legislative council** or **assembly** was the law-making body created by the British Acts. After the 1919 Act there was a Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi and a council in each province, with some members elected by a small number of voters and others nominated. (Details are in GS-050; here you only need the idea.)

To **boycott** is to refuse to take part. In 1920 the Congress boycotted the new councils, as part of Non-Cooperation. To **contest an election** is the opposite: to stand for a seat. A party that wins seats and then uses them to stop the government's business is practising **obstruction**: voting down the budget, rejecting bills, walking out, or making speeches that expose the government. This is different from **cooperation**, where members accept the rules and try to pass useful laws.

**'End or mend'** was the slogan of the council-entry school: if the government granted the nationalists' demand for self-government, the councils would be mended; if not, they would be ended by deadlock. **Dyarchy**, in one line, was the 1919 plan that split provincial subjects into a transferred half run by elected ministers and a reserved half kept by the Governor; the Swarajists said it was a sham, which is why they wished to expose it.

Finally, a **No-Changer** was a Congressman who wanted no change in the Non-Cooperation programme: keep the boycott of the councils and prepare quietly for the next struggle.

### Bridge: Words of the constitutional debate: dominion status, purna swaraj, joint and separate electorates

**Dominion status** meant self-government within the British Empire on the model of Canada or Australia: the country governs itself, the Crown stays the head of state, and the country is free in its internal and (in practice) external affairs. **Purna swaraj** means complete independence. In the 1920s the older Congress leaders were prepared to ask for dominion status; younger leaders wanted purna swaraj.

An **electorate** is the group of voters who elect a seat. In a **separate electorate** only members of a community (for example, Muslims) vote for the seats reserved for that community. In a **joint electorate** all voters vote together, but some seats may be **reserved**, so that candidates from a given community must be elected. **Weightage** gave a community more seats than its population share would suggest. Nationalists generally disliked separate electorates because they separated voters by religion; many Muslim leaders valued them as a protection against being outvoted. This difference is the thread of the lesson.

A **Royal Commission** or **Statutory Commission** is a body appointed by the British government (here under a statute, the 1919 Act) to study a question and report. A **residual power** is a power not listed for either centre or province; whoever holds residual powers is the stronger partner. The Nehru Report gave them to the centre; the League wanted them with the provinces. A **safeguard** is a legal protection written into a constitution for a minority.

### 1. Orientation: the 1920s on one page

*Objective:* Hold the whole 1922-1929 story as two linked arcs: the Swarajists and the Simon Commission.

## Where we start

GS-043 ended in February 1922 with the withdrawal of Non-Cooperation after Chauri Chaura, and with Gandhi's arrest in March, when he was sentenced to six years. Read its modules 8 and 9 first. This lesson starts at that point: the movement is suddenly switched off, the leaders are divided, and nobody knows what to do next.

## Arc one: what to do while the movement sleeps (1922 to 1929)

Congressmen split. One school, led by C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru, wanted to enter the councils and obstruct them from inside; they became the **Swarajists**. The other, led by C. Rajagopalachari, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and M. A. Ansari, wanted constructive work and a continued boycott; they were the **No-Changers**. After the Gaya session (December 1922) the Swarajists formed their own party; in September 1923 a Delhi compromise let them contest elections as a group inside the Congress. They did well in November 1923, and Gandhi, released in February 1924, came round to them. They faded after 1925.

## Arc two: the Simon Commission and a constitution of our own (1927 to 1929)

In November 1927 Britain appointed an all-white commission to review the 1919 Act. Indians were angered by the exclusion, and the Congress and most others boycotted it. The commission landed in Bombay on 3 February 1928 to hartals and black flags. Police charged protesters, and **Lala Lajpat Rai** died on 17 November 1928 after blows received in Lahore the month before. The challenge of the Secretary of State, Birkenhead, to produce an agreed constitution led to the **Nehru Report** (August 1928). Communal differences, voiced in the Delhi Proposals and **Jinnah's Fourteen Points** (March 1929), blocked a common scheme. The road then leads to the Calcutta ultimatum and the Lahore Congress of December 1929, which GS-045 teaches.

Both arcs are about method: the Swarajists tested whether a legislature could be a weapon; the Simon boycott showed that a clear insult could unite nearly everyone; the Nehru Report showed that unity broke on one question, the shape of representation.

### 2. Foundation: the vacuum after the withdrawal and the Gaya split

*Objective:* Explain why the end of Non-Cooperation produced a debate over council entry and how the Swarajya Party was born.

## The mood in 1922

Spectrum describes disintegration, disorganisation and demoralisation among nationalists after Gandhi's arrest in March 1922 (PDF p388). A mass movement had been called off at its height, its leader was in prison and the boycott of the councils, schools and courts was fading. A question followed: what should Congressmen do in the **transition period**, the passive phase between two mass struggles? (GS-043 module 9 gives Gandhi's reasons for withdrawing.)

## Two answers

**Answer one: enter the councils.** C. R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Hakim Ajmal Khan wanted to end the boycott of the legislative councils so that nationalists could go in, expose their basic weaknesses and use them as an arena of political struggle. Their slogan was 'end or mend': if the government did not respond to nationalist demands, they would obstruct the councils. Those who held this view were called **Swarajists**.

**Answer two: stay out.** C. Rajagopalachari, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and M. A. Ansari argued for constructive work, continuation of boycott and non-cooperation, and quiet preparation for the resumption of civil disobedience. They were the **No-Changers**.

## Gaya, December 1922

At the Congress session at **Gaya** (December 1922) the Swarajists' proposal to 'end or mend' the councils was defeated. C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru resigned from the presidentship and secretaryship of the Congress and announced a new body, the **Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party**, usually called the **Swaraj** or **Swarajist Party**, with Das as president and Motilal Nehru as one of the secretaries (Spectrum, PDF p389).

**Date caution.** Spectrum ties the formation to the Gaya session of December 1922. Many standard accounts give 1 January 1923 as the date the party was announced (verify). For Prelims, 'December 1922 to January 1923, after Gaya' is the safe statement.

## Why the split did not become a break

Spectrum notes that both sides wanted to avoid a **1907-type split** (the Surat split of GS-038 module 7) and kept in touch with Gandhi in jail. Both realised that a united front was needed for a mass movement to force reforms, and both accepted Gandhi's leadership of a united nationalist front. This restraint is the key to the story: the Swarajists and No-Changers argued about method, not about the goal.

## Reading the debate

Both groups wanted swaraj; they differed about what to do in the gap. The Swarajists did not intend to use the councils for gradual change but as a political arena (Spectrum, PDF p389). The No-Changers feared neglect of constructive work, loss of revolutionary zeal and corruption.

**Label caution.** The Swarajists were a group and later a party within the Congress, not a synonym for it, and not to be confused with the 1920 promise of 'swaraj in one year' (GS-043).

### 3. Core: the Swarajists' case, the Delhi compromise and the manifesto

*Objective:* State the Swarajist and No-Changer arguments, the September 1923 compromise and the content of the election manifesto.

## The Swarajists' reasons (Spectrum, PDF p389)

1. **A new front, not a surrender.** Entering the councils did not negate non-cooperation; it carried on the struggle by other means.
2. **Filling a political vacuum.** In a quiet phase, council work could keep up the masses' morale. It would also prevent the government from filling the councils with undesirable people who could be used to give its measures legitimacy.
3. **A political arena.** The councils were to be used as a battlefield, not as an instrument for gradual transformation of colonial rule.

## The No-Changers' reasons

Parliamentary work would lead to neglect of constructive work, a loss of revolutionary zeal and political corruption. Constructive work would prepare everyone for the next phase of civil disobedience. Module 5 sets out what that work was and its critique.

## The Delhi compromise, September 1923

A meeting in Delhi in **September 1923** reached a compromise (Spectrum, PDF pp389-390). The Swarajists were allowed to contest elections as a group within the Congress. They accepted the Congress programme with one difference: they would join the legislative councils. The elections to the newly constituted Central Legislative Assembly and the provincial assemblies were due in **November 1923**. The deal gave Das and Motilal an electoral platform without forcing a split in the Congress.

## The manifesto, October 1923

The Swarajists released a strongly anti-imperialist manifesto in October 1923. Its points, in Spectrum's list:

- The guiding motive of the British in ruling India was selfish interest.
- The so-called reforms were a blind: the real objective was to keep on exploiting the country's resources while holding Indians in permanent subservience.
- The Swarajists would present the demand for self-government in the councils.
- If the demand was rejected they would adopt a policy of **uniform, continuous and consistent obstruction** to make government through councils impossible.
- The councils would be wrecked from within by creating deadlocks on every measure.

**Obstruction** meant voting against the government, defeating its bills and budget demands, and using debates to expose its claims: a legal use of the 1919 machinery (bridge GS-044-B1).

## Gandhi's position

Gandhi was initially opposed to the Swarajist proposal of council entry. After his release on health grounds in February 1924 he moved gradually towards reconciliation (Spectrum, PDF p390):

- Public opposition to council entry would be counter-productive.
- In November 1923 the Swarajists won 42 elected seats (see the seat-total caution in module 4) and a clear majority in the Central Provinces assembly.
- Their uncompromising conduct convinced him they would not become another limb of the administration.
- A crackdown on revolutionaries and Swarajists in late 1924 angered him, and he yielded to their wishes.

## The Belgaum agreement, December 1924

The two sides reached an agreement in 1924, endorsed at the **Belgaum** session of the Congress in December 1924, when Gandhi presided (the only time he did, as Spectrum notes, PDF p391): the Swarajists would work in the councils as an integral part of the Congress. This closed the Gaya split.

## A rule to remember

Three steps: argue (Gaya), bargain (Delhi 1923), merge (Belgaum 1924).

### 4. Core: the Swarajists in the councils, achievements, drawbacks and decline

*Objective:* List what the Swarajists achieved in the legislatures, what limited them and the steps of their decline, with dates.

## The 1923 and 1926 elections

In the elections of November 1923 the Swarajists won 42 elected seats in the Central Legislative Assembly and a clear majority in the Central Provinces assembly. With Liberals and independents such as Jinnah and Malaviya they formed working majorities (Spectrum, PDF pp390-391).

**Source caution.** Spectrum prints '42 out of 141 elected seats'. Many standard accounts say 42 of the **101** elected seats in the Central Legislative Assembly (verify). Learn '42 seats' and avoid quoting a total in an examination unless the question gives it.

In 1926 a party 'in disarray' won 40 central seats and some in Madras but was routed in the United Provinces, Central Provinces and Punjab (PDF pp391-392).

## Achievements (Spectrum, PDF p392)

1. With coalition partners they outvoted the government several times, even on budget grants, and passed adjournment motions.
2. Powerful speeches argued for self-government, civil liberties and industrialisation.
3. **Vithalbhai Patel** was elected Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly in **1925**.
4. They defeated the **Public Safety Bill in 1928**, aimed at deporting undesirable and subversive foreigners. The government feared socialist and communist ideas and thought Comintern-sent British and foreign activists were playing a crucial role.
5. They filled the political vacuum, exposed the hollowness of the Montford scheme and showed that the councils could be used creatively.

## Drawbacks

- No policy to **coordinate** militancy inside the legislatures with mass struggle outside; they relied on newspaper reports to reach the public.
- Obstruction can block but cannot build, and allies with conflicting ideas could not be held for long.
- Some failed to resist the perks of office.
- They did not support Bengal's tenants against the zamindars and so lost many Muslim members, since most tenants were Muslims.

## The decline, step by step

1. **Communal riots** in the mid-1920s and a split among the Swarajists on communal and Responsivist versus non-Responsivist lines. Government strategy to divide the militant from the moderate and Hindus from Muslims worked.
2. **Death of C. R. Das in 1925.** He had been the party's president and its main strength.
3. **The Responsivists** (Lala Lajpat Rai, Madan Mohan Malaviya and N. C. Kelkar) wanted cooperation with the government and the holding of office wherever possible (Spectrum grouping; standard accounts name Jayakar, Kelkar and Moonje as Responsivists, with Lajpat Rai and Malaviya founding the Independent Congress Party in 1926; verify). Spectrum adds that communalists accused Motilal Nehru of being anti-Hindu while Muslim communalists called the Swarajists anti-Muslim.
4. **March 1926**: the main leadership, reasserting faith in mass civil disobedience, **withdrew from the legislatures**; another section contested the 1926 elections in disarray.
5. **1930**: the Swarajists finally walked out as a result of the Lahore Congress resolution on purna swaraj and the beginning of Civil Disobedience (GS-045).

## One-line evaluation

The Swarajists proved the councils could be a platform but not a weapon strong enough to wreck imperial rule. They are best seen as a bridge between Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience. A later echo: the Congress left of 1935-36 proposed council entry only to create deadlock, the older Swarajist strategy (PDF p458; GS-047).

### 5. Core: constructive work and the new forces of the 1920s

*Objective:* Describe what the No-Changers did, and map the new currents (socialist, youth, labour, caste, revolutionary) that Spectrum links to the decade.

## The No-Changers' constructive work (Spectrum, PDF p393)

The No-Changers connected themselves to the masses through four activities:

1. **Ashrams** where young men and women worked among tribal and lower-caste communities, especially in the Kheda and Bardoli areas of Gujarat, and popularised the charkha and khadi.
2. **National schools and colleges**, where students were trained in a non-colonial framework.
3. Work for **Hindu-Muslim unity**, removal of untouchability, boycott of foreign cloth and liquor, and flood relief.
4. These workers became the backbone of civil disobedience as its organisers.

**The critique.** National education mainly benefited the urban lower middle classes and rich peasants and faded with the movement; khadi cost more than imported cloth; the anti-untouchability work neglected the economic grievances of landless labourers, mostly untouchables. Spectrum stresses that both groups kept on the best of terms and united when a new struggle came.

## A watershed decade

Spectrum calls the third decade a watershed: the masses had entered the national movement, and the main political currents crystallised, partly in positive or negative reaction to Gandhian satyagraha (PDF pp393-394). Six currents are listed:

- **Marxist and socialist ideas.** A Congress left wing formed around **Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose**, inspired by the Soviet Revolution. They criticised both Swarajists and No-Changers, wanted a more consistent anti-imperialist line (purna swaraj), and linked nationalism to social justice and to the question of class oppression by capitalists and landlords. Spectrum dates the Communist Party of India to 1920 at Tashkent (M. N. Roy and others) and its formal foundation to Kanpur in 1925; the Meerut Conspiracy Case followed in 1929.
- **Youth.** Students' leagues spread; Nehru presided over the All Bengal Students' Conference in 1928.
- **Peasants.** Tenancy and rent agitations in several regions, and the **Bardoli satyagraha of 1928** under Vallabhbhai Patel (GS-031).
- **Trade unionism.** The **AITUC** was founded in 1920 with Lala Lajpat Rai as first president; a Bombay textile strike of 150,000 workers lasted five months; strikes in 1928 involved about five lakh workers.
- **Caste movements.** The Justice Party, Periyar's Self-Respect movement (1925), Ambedkar's Mahars and the Punjab Unionists (GS-052).
- **Revolutionary activity with a turn towards socialism.** After the 1922 withdrawal many young nationalists, drawn neither to council work nor to constructive work, turned to violent methods: the HRA (October 1924), Kakori (August 1925), the HSRA (September 1928), Saunders' killing and the Assembly bomb (1929), and Surya Sen's group. GS-039 modules 7-9 teach these; do not re-learn them here.

## The point to carry forward

Three answers to the 1922 question existed at once: parliamentary (Swarajists), constructive (No-Changers) and revolutionary (HRA/HSRA). A fourth answer, socialist, took shape in the Congress left. The Simon boycott of 1927-28 was the occasion on which these streams, the youth and the left in particular, came into the open.

### 6. Core: the Simon Commission, the boycott and the lathi-charge

*Objective:* Explain why the Commission was appointed, who boycotted it, how the protest spread and what happened to Lala Lajpat Rai.

## Appointment

The 1919 Act provided that a commission would review the working of the scheme ten years after the Act. In **November 1927** (8 November, Spectrum, PDF p406), two years early, the Conservative government under **Stanley Baldwin** announced the **Indian Statutory Commission**, known as the **Simon Commission** after its chairman, Sir John Simon. It had seven members, all British: four Conservatives, two Labour and one Liberal. Its task was to report whether India was ready for further constitutional reform and on what lines (GS-050 module 5 teaches the Lee, Muddiman and Butler bodies and the report's content).

**Why early?** Spectrum says the Conservatives feared defeat by Labour and did not want to leave India to 'irresponsible Labour hands'. **Lord Birkenhead**, the Secretary of State, who kept saying Indians could not agree on a scheme, was responsible.

**Source slip.** Spectrum says the Commission was under the joint chairmanship of Simon and Clement Attlee. Attlee was a member (verify); Simon was chairman.

## Why Indians were angry

The exclusion of Indians implied that foreigners would decide India's fitness for self-government, which Spectrum calls a violation of self-determination and a deliberate insult (PDF pp407-408). The response was 'immediate and nearly unanimous'.

## Who boycotted and who did not

- **Congress**, Madras, December 1927, M. A. Ansari presiding: boycott 'at every stage and in every form'. Nehru also got a snap resolution on complete independence passed; it was not a call to action (see Lahore 1929, GS-045 module 3).
- **Supporting the boycott:** liberals of the Hindu Mahasabha and the **majority faction of the Muslim League under Jinnah**. Spectrum says the League held two sessions in 1927: Jinnah's at Calcutta opposed the Commission; **Muhammad Shafi's** at Lahore supported the government.
- **Not boycotting:** the **Unionists in Punjab** and the **Justice Party** in the south.
- **Dr Ambedkar** appeared before the Commission in October 1928, asking for adult franchise and safeguards for the depressed classes (GS-052 module 6).

## The protest

The Commission landed at **Bombay on 3 February 1928**. A countrywide hartal, rallies and black-flag demonstrations followed, with the slogan 'Simon Go Back' (widely credited to Yusuf Meherally; PDF p408). Standard accounts add that the Commission made two visits, February-March 1928 and October 1928 to April 1929 (verify).

The youth first tasted political action and gave the protest a militant flavour; **Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Bose** emerged as their leaders.

## Police repression and Lajpat Rai

Spectrum says there were lathi-charges that spared not even senior leaders (PDF pp409-410). Nehru and G. B. Pant were beaten at **Lucknow**. **Lala Lajpat Rai** received severe blows on the chest in **October 1928** and died on **17 November 1928**. The anti-Simon procession was at **Lahore**, and the date of the charge is standard 30 October 1928 (verify). His words, quoted by Spectrum, said the blows would be the last nails in the coffin of British imperialism. Spectrum links his death to the HSRA killing of J. P. Saunders at Lahore in December 1928 (GS-039 module 8).

## What the Simon Commission did for the movement

Spectrum gives a two-fold impact (PDF p410): it stimulated radical forces demanding complete independence and socialist reforms, and gave a Congress with no active programme an issue for mass action; and the Birkenhead challenge to produce an agreed constitution was taken up, so prospects for unity briefly seemed bright (module 7 shows how they faded). Gandhi gave his blessing but did not himself take part (NCERT, PDF p10).

### 7. Core: the challenge, the Nehru Report, the Delhi Proposals and the Fourteen Points

*Objective:* Compare the Nehru Report with the Delhi Proposals, Jinnah's amendments and the Fourteen Points, and explain why agreement failed.

GS-050 module 6 already gives the provisions and the dates in a compact form. This module deepens the **comparison**: who asked for what, and where the gap was.

## The challenge accepted

Birkenhead had said Indians could not frame a scheme with wide support. An **All Parties Conference** in February 1928 appointed a committee under **Motilal Nehru** (members included Tej Bahadur Sapru, Subhas Bose and Ali Imam); the report was finalised by August 1928 (Spectrum, PDF p411), the first major Indian attempt to draft a constitution. Standard accounts place the appointment at the Bombay conference of May 1928 (verify).

## The four positions on representation

| | Delhi Proposals (Muslim leaders) | Nehru Report (Aug 1928) | Jinnah's three amendments (Calcutta, Dec 1928) | Fourteen Points (Mar 1929) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electorates | Joint, with reserved seats for Muslims | Joint everywhere; reserved seats for Muslims only where they were a minority (not in Punjab and Bengal) | Reserved Muslim seats in Bengal and Punjab in proportion to population, until adult suffrage | Separate electorates |
| Centre | One-third Muslim seats in the Central Assembly | Responsible government at the centre; broadly unitary (residuary powers at the centre) | One-third Muslim seats in the central legislature | One-third Muslim seats; one-third of cabinet posts |
| Provinces | Punjab and Bengal seats in proportion to population | No reservation for Muslims in those provinces | Residuary powers with the provinces | Federal constitution with residuary powers to provinces; no change that reduces a Muslim majority |
| New provinces | Sindh, Baluchistan and NWFP as Muslim-majority provinces (Spectrum's words) | Sindh to be separated only after dominion status, with weightage for the Hindu minority | Not stated | Sindh separated; reforms in NWFP and Baluchistan |

Sources: Spectrum PDF pp411-415. Spectrum dates the Delhi Proposals to December 1927, the month the Madras Congress accepted them; standard accounts date the Delhi meeting to 20 March 1927 (verify; as in GS-050).

## The dilemma

Spectrum (PDF pp412-413): the League dissociated itself and held to reserved seats. The Hindu Mahasabha opposed new Muslim-majority provinces and reservation in Punjab and Bengal, and wanted a strictly unitary structure. Granting one side's demands would lose the other. The Report leaned towards the Mahasabha on three counts: reservation only where Muslims were a minority, Sindh only after dominion status, and a broadly unitary form.

## Calcutta and the Fourteen Points

At Calcutta in December 1928 Jinnah's three amendments (table) were not accepted. In **March 1929** he gave **fourteen points**, which Spectrum says became the basis of all future League propaganda. Besides the table entries they included: provincial autonomy; adequate Muslim representation in every province without reducing a Muslim majority to a minority or equality; no bill to pass if three-fourths of a minority community consider it against its interests; no territorial redistribution affecting the Muslim majority in Punjab, Bengal and NWFP; reforms in NWFP and Baluchistan; and religious freedom and protection of Muslim culture, education and language.

## The other critics

The Report also displeased the Sikh communalists and the younger Congress section under Nehru and Bose, who saw dominion status as a step backward and jointly set up the **Independence for India League** (Spectrum, PDF p415; date not given, verify).

## Reading the failure

Two lessons: a unitary or federal form was itself a communal question, because it decided who held residuary powers; and Punjab and Bengal were the pivotal provinces, a theme that returns in 1946-47 (GS-051). Spectrum's later judgement (PDF p536) that bargaining with the League gave its politics legitimacy is one reading, not a settled finding.

### 8. Master: how the strands connect, and what historians debate

*Objective:* Link the Swarajist and Simon episodes to the road to Lahore and to later constitutional history, and handle three evaluative debates with balance.

## The connecting thread

The Swarajists, the anti-Simon protest and the Nehru Report are three answers to one question: how far could the colonial constitution be used or replaced from within? The sequence runs on as follows.

1. **1922-26: use the councils.** The Swarajists test obstruction and find its limits.
2. **1927-28: reject a commission and offer an alternative.** The boycott shows unity of response; the Nehru Report shows the limits of unity of proposal.
3. **December 1928, Calcutta:** the Congress approves the Report but sets a deadline (the version of the story in GS-045 module 2). If dominion status is not accepted by the end of 1929, the Congress will demand independence and begin civil disobedience.
4. **31 October 1929:** Irwin's declaration of dominion status as the natural issue of 1917, with no time scale; **December 1929, Lahore:** purna swaraj becomes the goal, and the Swarajists leave the legislatures in 1930 (GS-045).
5. **1930 onwards:** the Simon report (Spectrum: May 1930; June 1930 in standard accounts, verify) proposed abolishing dyarchy; with the Round Table Conferences it fed into the 1935 Act (GS-046, GS-047, GS-050).

## A difference worth noticing

Madras (December 1927) passed a snap resolution on complete independence, yet Calcutta (December 1928) approved a report based on dominion status. The first stated a goal; the second was a negotiating position; the change to a programme came at Lahore in 1929. In an examination, the standard answer for purna swaraj as the Congress goal is Lahore.

## Debate one: were the Swarajists a success?

- **For:** they kept political life going, used the legislatures as a platform, exposed dyarchy and defeated the Public Safety Bill.
- **Against:** obstruction changed little in how India was governed; there was no link with the people; some were drawn by office; the party split on communal lines.
- **Balance:** success depends on the yardstick: as a political bridge, yes; as a lever against the Raj, limited. The No-Changers' constructive base is what Spectrum calls the backbone of civil disobedience.

## Debate two: did the Simon boycott succeed?

It united most politically active India, produced a youth leadership and gave the Congress an issue. It did not stop the Commission, and the report was overtaken by events. The unity was real but not total (the League split).

## Debate three: who is responsible for the failure of the Nehru Report?

Spectrum supports several readings: communal leaders on several sides made compromise hard (PDF pp412-413); the Report leaned towards the Hindu side (PDF pp413-414); bargaining with the League legitimised separate politics (PDF p536); British divide and rule encouraged division (PDF p532); and the Congress left found dominion status a step backward (PDF p415). Present them side by side, give your own judgement in one sentence last, and avoid blaming a single community.

## Looking forward

The questions of Punjab, Bengal and NWFP and of residuary powers return in the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 (GS-051). Treat this as a theme, not as proof that partition was inevitable; GS-051 module 9 takes that debate.

## Prelims discriminators

1. **Who founded what, and with which post?** Das was president and Motilal Nehru a secretary of the Swarajist party. Both had resigned posts in the Congress at Gaya.
2. **Which session?** Gaya (1922), the Delhi meeting of September 1923 (a special Congress session in standard accounts; verify), Belgaum (Dec 1924, Gandhi president), Madras (Dec 1927, Ansari, boycott), Calcutta (Dec 1928, Nehru Report), Lahore (Dec 1929, Nehru president, purna swaraj).
3. **Who boycotted?** Congress and the Jinnah faction; not the Unionists, Justice Party or Shafi faction.
4. **Dates of the Commission.** Announced 8 November 1927; landed 3 February 1928; report 1930.
5. **Nehru Report.** Dominion status, joint electorates with reserved seats where Muslims were a minority, 19 fundamental rights, no state religion, linguistic provinces.
6. **Fourteen Points** belong to Jinnah and to March 1929, not to the Delhi Proposals or the Nehru Report.

Typical traps: reversing Swarajists and No-Changers; giving Gandhi's presidency to Gaya instead of Belgaum; saying the Nehru Report accepted separate electorates; calling Attlee joint chairman of the Commission; giving Lajpat Rai's death as the day of the lathi-charge. Quick eliminations: the Swarajists entered the councils; the Commission was all-white; Gaya (1922), Delhi (1923) and Belgaum (1924) are in that order.

## A 150-word frame (10 marks): 'Examine the role of the Swarajists in the national movement'

- Introduction (20 words): after Chauri Chaura, Das and Motilal Nehru led a council-entry school against the No-Changers.
- Body: programme of obstruction; the 1923 results; the Belgaum agreement; achievements (Public Safety Bill 1928, the Speaker, outvoting the government); decline (Das's death, Responsivists, communal riots, withdrawal 1926 and 1930).
- Balance: filled a vacuum, limited by lack of mass link.
- Conclusion (20 words): a bridge between Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience, not a substitute for mass struggle.

## A 250-word frame (15 marks): 'Discuss the significance of the Simon Commission for the national movement'

- Context: ten-year review, early appointment, Conservative fears of Labour.
- Why it angered: exclusion, self-determination.
- Responses: boycott, splits (League, Unionists, Justice Party).
- Impact: youth leadership, Nehru and Bose, radical ideas, Lajpat Rai's death and the Saunders killing, revival of mass politics.
- Constitutional outcome: Birkenhead's challenge, Nehru Report, communal split, Fourteen Points; report overtaken.
- Evaluation and conclusion: a catalyst for Lahore 1929; the communal rift foreshadowed later difficulties.

### Notes: Key definitions

- Swarajist: a Congressman who favoured council entry after 1922, later a member of the Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party.
- No-Changer: a Congressman who wanted continuation of the boycott and constructive work.
- Responsivist: a Swarajist who favoured cooperation with the government and holding office where possible (Spectrum names Lajpat Rai, Malaviya, Kelkar; standard accounts name Jayakar, Kelkar, Moonje; verify).
- Obstruction: voting down and exposing the government's business from within a legislature.
- Statutory Commission: a body appointed under the 1919 Act to review the working of the constitution.
- Residuary powers: powers not listed for either centre or province.
- Weightage: more seats than a community's population share would give.

### Notes: Who is who

- C. R. Das: president of the Swarajist party; died 1925.
- Motilal Nehru: secretary of the party; chaired the Nehru Report committee.
- Vithalbhai Patel: elected Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly in 1925.
- Sir John Simon: chairman of the Indian Statutory Commission.
- Lord Birkenhead: Secretary of State whose challenge to Indians led to the Nehru Report.
- M. A. Ansari: president at the Madras session of December 1927.
- Lala Lajpat Rai: first president of the AITUC (1920); injured in the Lahore anti-Simon procession; died 17 November 1928.
- Muhammad Shafi: leader of the League faction that supported the Commission.
- Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Bose: leaders of the youth and the Congress left.

### Notes: Cause and effect

- Withdrawal of Non-Cooperation (1922) led to demoralisation and to the debate over council entry.
- Gaya's defeat of council entry led to the Swarajist party; the Delhi compromise (1923) kept it inside the Congress.
- The 1923 election success, Gandhi's release (Feb 1924) and the late-1924 crackdown led to the Belgaum agreement.
- Das's death, communal riots and the Responsivist split led to decline and withdrawal (1926, 1930).
- Early appointment of an all-white commission (1927) led to boycott, youth mobilisation and a revived mass mood.
- Birkenhead's challenge led to the Nehru Report; the communal differences led to the Fourteen Points; the failure of the Report fed the Calcutta ultimatum and Lahore.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Swarajists versus Swaraj in one year: the first is a 1922-23 party; the second was a 1920 promise.
- Gaya (council entry rejected, 1922) versus Belgaum (Swarajists accepted into Congress work, 1924).
- Delhi Proposals (Muslim leaders) versus Nehru Report (Congress-led committee) versus Fourteen Points (Jinnah, March 1929).
- Lahore Muslim League session of 1927 (Shafi, pro-government) versus the Lahore Congress of December 1929 (Nehru, purna swaraj).
- Lajpat Rai's lathi-charge at Lahore (October 1928) versus Saunders' killing at Lahore (December 1928).

### Core concepts

- After the 1922 withdrawal the Congress split over council entry into Swarajists and No-Changers.
- The Swarajists entered the councils to obstruct them and fill a political vacuum, working as a group inside the Congress after 1923-24.
- The all-white Simon Commission, appointed early in November 1927, was boycotted by most groups and brought youth, the left and a mass mood back into politics.
- The Nehru Report was the first Indian attempt to draft a constitution; the communal question blocked agreement, leading to the Fourteen Points.

### Practice

**Q.** Define a Swarajist and a No-Changer in one line each.

**A.** A Swarajist wanted to enter the councils and obstruct them; a No-Changer wanted to keep the boycott and do constructive work.

**Q.** What is obstruction?

**A.** Voting down and exposing the government's business from inside a legislature.

**Q.** When was the Simon Commission announced and who chaired it?

**A.** 8 November 1927; Sir John Simon.

**Q.** What does dominion status mean?

**A.** Self-government within the Empire on the model of Canada or Australia, short of independence.

**Q.** Why did the Swarajists argue that council entry did not violate non-cooperation?

**A.** They said it was non-cooperation by other means: a new front, filling the vacuum and keeping out undesirable elements, while using the councils as an arena.

**Q.** Why was the exclusion of Indians from the Commission so offensive?

**A.** It implied that foreigners would decide India's fitness for self-government, a violation of self-determination and an insult to self-respect.

**Q.** Why did Gandhi change his stand on the Swarajists in 1924?

**A.** He feared public opposition would be counter-productive, saw their uncompromising conduct and, after the late-1924 crackdown, showed solidarity.

**Q.** How did the Nehru Report differ from the League's Fourteen Points on electorates?

**A.** The Report proposed joint electorates with reserved seats where Muslims were in a minority; the Fourteen Points demanded separate electorates.

**Q.** A newspaper in 1926 says the Swarajists have achieved nothing. Write four sentences giving evidence for and against.

**A.** For: no change in governance, no mass link, communal split. Against: Speaker in 1925, outvoting the government, exposure of dyarchy, the Public Safety Bill defeat in 1928.

**Q.** Use the Simon boycott to explain why the youth were important in the late 1920s.

**A.** They played the most active part in the protests, gave them a militant flavour and found leaders in Nehru and Bose; youth leagues and socialist groups grew.

**Q.** Write one paragraph linking the Fourteen Points' point on Punjab and Bengal to the later constitutional debate.

**A.** Point 10 asked that no territorial change reduce the Muslim majority in Punjab, Bengal and NWFP; the status of these provinces recurs in the Cabinet Mission Plan and partition (GS-051).
