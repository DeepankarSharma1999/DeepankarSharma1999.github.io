### Bridge: Geometry kit: right triangles, sine, congruence and perpendicular lines

Every proof in this lesson is school geometry, so the facts are fixed here once.

- **Right triangle and sine.** In a triangle with one angle of 90 degrees, the longest side (opposite the right angle) is the hypotenuse. For an acute angle θ, sin θ = (side opposite θ) / (hypotenuse). It is a pure number between 0 and 1. If the hypotenuse is 10 and the side opposite θ is 5, sin θ = 0.5 and θ = 30°.
- **Congruent right triangles (RHS).** Two right triangles that share the same hypotenuse and have one other side equal are congruent: all their angles and sides match. The matching angle sits opposite the matching side.
- **Perpendicular pairs.** If line p is perpendicular to line q, and line m is perpendicular to line n, then the angle between p and m equals the angle between q and n. In optics this turns an angle between two wavefront lines into an angle between two rays, and an angle with a surface into an angle with the normal.

Notation: a point such as A is a point on the drawing; AC means the straight distance from A to C; ∠BAC means the angle at A between the lines AB and AC.

### Bridge: Inverse sine, degrees mode and why sin θ cannot exceed 1

Snell's law gives you a number for sin r, and you must turn it into an angle. On a scientific calculator, set the mode to degrees (DEG), then use the inverse-sine key (sin⁻¹ or arcsin). Example: sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30°; sin⁻¹(0.6) = 36.87°.

The sine of any real angle lies between −1 and +1. So an equation such as sin r = 1.2 has no solution: no angle has that sine. In ordinary arithmetic this is an error message; in this lesson it will turn out to carry physical meaning (module 7: no refracted wave exists, and the light is totally reflected).

Two habits protect marks. First, write the ratio before you press any key (sin r = 0.5 / 1.5 = 0.3333). Second, check that the angle is plausible: an angle of refraction must lie between 0° and 90°, and in a denser medium it must be smaller than the angle of incidence.

Do not use the small-angle shortcut (sin θ ≈ θ in radians) in this topic: the angles are large, and the shortcut would give wrong answers.

## The question this lesson answers

Light bounces off a mirror and bends when it enters water. Can both facts be derived from the idea that light is a wave? Yes. The tool is a geometric construction proposed by Christiaan Huygens (NCERT dates his wave theory to 1678) plus school geometry. ## Words to fix now

- **Reflection:** a wave meeting a surface and turning back into the medium it came from.
- **Refraction:** a wave passing into a second transparent medium and changing direction at the boundary (the interface).
- **Normal:** the line through the point of contact at 90° to the interface.
- **Angle of incidence i, angle of reflection r, angle of refraction r** (the same letter r serves both; the module in use says which, and the law of reflection is i = r): always measured from the normal, never from the surface. (NCERT's ray-optics chapter states the convention.) This single habit prevents the commonest error.

## Two results you are heading for

$$ i = r  (reflection)    and    sin i / sin r = v₁ / v₂ = n₂ / n₁  (refraction) $$

Here v₁ and v₂ are the speeds of the wave in medium 1 and medium 2, and n is the refractive index, defined in module 5.

### 2. Foundation: waves, phase, wavefronts and rays

*Objective:* Define wavefront and ray, tell plane, spherical and cylindrical wavefronts apart, and state how rays relate to wavefronts.

## A wave in three numbers

A wave is a disturbance that travels through space carrying energy. A steady wave is described by its speed v (metres per second), its wavelength λ (the distance between successive crests, in metres) and its frequency ν (oscillations per second, in hertz). They are tied by $$ v = ν λ $$ Dimensional check: m/s = (1/s) × m. Light is an electromagnetic wave (NCERT credits Maxwell with this); in vacuum its speed is c, about 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

## Phase and wavefront

Phase says which stage of its cycle an oscillation is in. Points that oscillate exactly in step have the same phase. Drop a pebble in a calm pool: at any instant the points on one circular ring are at the same distance from the splash and oscillate in phase. NCERT defines a **wavefront** as a locus of points oscillating in phase, a surface of constant phase. It moves outward at the speed of the wave, and the energy of the wave travels in a direction perpendicular to it. A wavefront is not only a crest: any surface of constant phase qualifies.

## Three common shapes

- **Spherical:** a point source sending waves equally in all directions through a uniform medium; wavefronts are spheres centred on the source.
- **Plane:** far from the source, a small patch of a huge sphere is almost flat (NCERT Fig. 10.1(b)); wavefronts are parallel planes. Most derivations below use plane waves.
- **Cylindrical:** from a long straight line source, such as a long narrow slit lit from behind; wavefronts are coaxial cylinders. (This case is standard knowledge, not in the supplied extracts; it is flagged in the completeness table.)

## Rays

A ray is a line showing the direction in which the energy travels. In the media used in this lesson (uniform, with the same speed in all directions) rays are perpendicular to the wavefronts. NCERT describes geometrical optics as the case where the finiteness of the wavelength is neglected, with a ray as the path of energy flow in the limit λ → 0.

The two pictures complement each other. The ray picture is quick bookkeeping of direction. The wavefront picture also records phase, which is what later topics on interference and diffraction need.

## Beginner traps

A ray is a construction line, not a physical thin beam. A wavefront is a surface, not a line: on paper we draw its cross-section, which is a line (plane wave) or a circle (spherical wave).

### 3. Core: Huygens' principle, the envelope and the back-wave limitation

*Objective:* State Huygens' principle, carry out the construction for plane and spherical wavefronts, and say precisely what limitation it has.

## The idea

Suppose you know where a wavefront F is now. Where will it be a short time t later? Huygens' principle is a geometrical construction that answers this (NCERT). Treat every point of F as a tiny new source of a **secondary wavelet**. Each wavelet spreads in all directions at the speed v of the wave in that medium, so after time t it is a sphere of radius vt (a circle on paper). The new wavefront G is the surface that touches all these wavelets on the forward side: their **envelope**, meaning a surface tangent to every member of the family. Brij Lal adds that the wavelets have the same frequency and speed as the primary wave.

## Two constructions

**Plane wave.** Every wavelet has radius vt, so the common tangent plane G is parallel to F at distance vt (NCERT Fig. 10.3), and rays perpendicular to F are also perpendicular to G.

**Spherical wave.** If F is a sphere of radius R centred on the source S, the forward envelope is a sphere about S of radius R + vt (proved in derivation PH-042-D5).

## The back-wave limitation

The bare construction produces a second envelope on the side facing the source, a "back-wave" (D1D2 in NCERT Fig. 10.2). No such wave is observed. What the sources say:

- NCERT: Huygens argued that the amplitude of the wavelets is maximum in the forward direction and zero in the backward direction. NCERT calls this assumption ad hoc and unsatisfactory, and says the absence of the back-wave is really justified by more rigorous wave theory.
- Brij Lal: the construction is incomplete because it does not explain the absence of backward-going wavelets; in 1882 Kirchhoff showed that Huygens' principle is a consequence of the differential wave equation.
- Standard knowledge, beyond the supplied extracts (verify): in the Fresnel–Kirchhoff treatment each wavelet is weighted by an obliquity factor that is largest straight ahead and zero directly backward; its usual form is ½(1 + cos χ), where χ is the angle from the forward direction. That treatment belongs to the diffraction topics.

## What the principle gives and what it does not

It gives the position and shape of the next wavefront, hence the ray directions. Used in this plain form it does not give amplitudes or intensities. In the form used here we assume each medium is uniform and isotropic, so wavelets are spheres of radius vt; Huygens also treated anisotropic crystals (calcite) with ellipsoidal wavelets, a later topic (verify). The uniform-medium form is enough for reflection and refraction at a plane boundary. For diffraction and interference, Fresnel's addition of the wavelets with their phases is needed; that is a later topic.

### 4. Core: the law of reflection from Huygens' construction

*Objective:* Derive i = r by Huygens' construction, justifying each geometric step, and state the second law of reflection.

## Setting

A plane wave travels in a medium of speed v and meets a plane reflecting surface MN. Angles are measured from the normal. The reflected wave stays in the same medium, so it has the same speed v.

## The construction, step by step

1. Let AB be the incident wavefront. A is the point where the front is touching MN at the instant t = 0. B is another point of the same front, still at a distance from MN.
2. Rays are perpendicular to AB, so the ray through B is perpendicular to AB: angle ABC = 90°. This ray reaches MN at C after a time t, so BC = vt. (Meanwhile the points of the front between A and C reach MN one after another.)
3. At the moment t the wavelet from A has radius vt. Draw this circle about A. It lies above MN, in the same medium.
4. Draw the tangent from C to the circle, touching it at E. Then CE is the reflected wavefront. A radius is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact, so angle AEC = 90° and AE = vt.
5. The wavelets from the points between A and C are also tangent to CE (derivation D1, step 8), so CE really is the envelope, not just the tangent to one wavelet.

## The proof that i = r

Compare triangles BAC and ECA. Both have a right angle (at B and at E). Both have AC as hypotenuse. And BC = vt = AE. By the RHS test they are congruent, with B matching E and A matching C. So angle BAC = angle ECA.

Now identify these angles. AB (wavefront) and the incident ray are perpendicular, and MN and the normal are perpendicular, so the angle between AB and MN, which is angle BAC, equals the angle between the incident ray and the normal: that is i. In the same way angle ECA, between the reflected wavefront CE and MN, equals the angle between the reflected ray and the normal: that is r. Therefore

$$ i = r $$

A trigonometric version agrees: sin i = BC/AC = vt/AC and sin r = AE/AC = vt/AC, so sin i = sin r, and both angles are acute, hence i = r.

## Second law and remarks

The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal lie in one plane. In the construction everything (rays, normal, tangent from C) lies in the plane of the drawing, and the wavefronts meet the surface along lines perpendicular to that plane.

The speed v cancels, so the law does not depend on the speed or wavelength of the wave. Limiting cases: i = 0 returns the wave along the normal; i → 90° (grazing) gives r → 90°.

### 5. Core: Snell's law from Huygens' construction

*Objective:* Derive sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ by Huygens' construction, with every geometric step, and find the direction in which the wave bends.

## Setting and sign convention

An interface PP′ separates medium 1 (speed v₁), above, from medium 2 (speed v₂), below. A plane wave in medium 1 meets it at angle of incidence i. Angles are measured from the normal. This is the problem posed in the 2023 question: medium 1 rarer, medium 2 denser, so v₁ > v₂.

## The construction

1. AB is the incident wavefront. A is on the interface at t = 0. The ray through B meets the interface at C after time t, so BC = v₁t and angle ABC = 90°.
2. In the same time the wavelet from A travels in medium 2 with speed v₂. Draw the circle about A of radius v₂t, below the interface.
3. Draw the tangent from C to this circle, touching it at E. CE is the refracted wavefront, AE ⊥ CE, and AE = v₂t. (Derivation D2, step 10, checks that the wavelets from points between A and C also touch CE.)

## The proof

In right triangle ABC (right angle at B), the angle at A equals i, as in module 4, and the hypotenuse is AC:

$$ sin i = BC / AC = v₁ t / AC $$

In right triangle AEC (right angle at E), the angle at C is r, the angle between the refracted wavefront and the interface, which equals the angle between the refracted ray AE and the normal (perpendicular pairs, module B1):

$$ sin r = AE / AC = v₂ t / AC $$

Dividing, t and AC cancel:

$$ sin i / sin r = v₁ / v₂ $$

## Introducing the refractive index

The refractive index of a medium is n = c/v, where c is the speed of light in vacuum (NCERT eqs. 10.4, 10.5). So v₁ = c/n₁ and v₂ = c/n₂, and v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁. Hence

$$ sin i / sin r = v₁ / v₂ = n₂ / n₁    that is    n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r $$

This is Snell's law, and it is the full statement asked for in the 2023 question. The first law, that the refracted ray lies in the plane of the incident ray and the normal, follows from the same plane symmetry as in module 4.

## Which way does it bend?

If v₂ < v₁ then sin r < sin i, so r < i: the wave bends toward the normal on entering the slower medium. If v₂ > v₁ it bends away from the normal (module 7). At i = 0 the wave goes straight through. Dimensional check: v₁/v₂ is a ratio of two speeds, a pure number, as sin i / sin r must be.

### 6. Core: refractive index, wavelength change and constant frequency

*Objective:* Use n = c/v and relative refractive index, show that frequency is unchanged on refraction while speed and wavelength change, and place the wave-theory prediction beside the corpuscular one.

## Relative and absolute index

The index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 is n₂₁ = n₂/n₁ = v₁/v₂, so Snell's law reads sin i / sin r = n₂₁ (NCERT's ray-optics chapter writes it this way). With a third medium, n₃₁ = n₃₂ × n₂₁, because (v₁/v₃) = (v₂/v₃) × (v₁/v₂). Also n₁₂ = 1/n₂₁. In NCERT's table, water is 1.33 and crown glass 1.52. These are ordinary values for visible light; the index also depends on the wavelength of the light.

## Optical density is not mass density

Medium 2 is called optically denser when n₂₁ > 1, equivalently v₂ < v₁. NCERT warns that this is different from mass density: turpentine has a lower mass density than water but a higher optical density.

## Frequency is unchanged; speed and wavelength change

The secondary wavelets are driven by the incident wave and oscillate at its frequency (Brij Lal's postulate: the wavelets have the same frequency as the primary wave). So the refracted wave has the same frequency ν as the incident one. Since v = νλ,

$$ λ₁ = v₁/ν,   λ₂ = v₂/ν,   λ₁/λ₂ = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ $$

NCERT reaches the same result from the figure: choose BC = λ₁; the crest from A advances to E in the same time, so AE = λ₂, and AE/BC = v₂/v₁. On entering a denser medium (v₂ < v₁) the speed and wavelength both fall in the same ratio, while the frequency stays fixed. A wave in a medium of index n therefore has wavelength λ₀/n, where λ₀ is the wavelength in vacuum.

Example: red light of vacuum wavelength 600 nm entering glass of n = 1.5 has wavelength 600/1.5 = 400 nm, speed 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.5 = 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s and frequency 3.0 × 10⁸ / 600 × 10⁻⁹ = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz, the same as in vacuum.

## Why this settled an old argument

NCERT and Brij Lal record that the corpuscular (particle) model, associated with Newton, predicts that light travels faster in the denser medium when it bends toward the normal, while Huygens' wave model predicts that it travels slower. Foucault's experiment of 1850 showed that light is slower in water than in air, in agreement with the wave model.

## Common slip

Writing sin i / sin r = v₂/v₁ is the most common inversion. Anchor it: the numerator sine belongs to the medium where the wave is incident (medium 1), so v₁ is in the numerator.

### 7. Core: total internal reflection and the critical angle

*Objective:* Derive the critical angle from the wavefront picture, explain why no refracted wave exists beyond it, and compute critical angles.

## When the second medium is faster

Let the wave go from medium 1 into a rarer medium 2: v₂ > v₁, so n₁ > n₂. Snell's law gives sin r = (n₁/n₂) sin i, which is larger than sin i. So r > i: the wave bends away from the normal, and as i grows r grows faster. The largest sine that r can have is 1, so there is a special angle of incidence, the **critical angle** i_c, at which r = 90°:

$$ sin i_c = n₂ / n₁ = v₁ / v₂ $$

At i = i_c the refracted wave grazes the interface. For i > i_c Snell's law cannot be satisfied and there is no refracted wave: the wave undergoes **total internal reflection** (NCERT).

## Why no refracted wave: the picture

In the construction of module 5, AE = v₂t and AC = v₁t / sin i, so v₂t / AC = (v₂/v₁) sin i. A tangent from C to the circle of radius v₂t about A exists only when C lies on or outside the circle, that is when (v₂/v₁) sin i ≤ 1. At equality E coincides with C and the refracted wavefront CE is perpendicular to the interface, so the refracted ray travels along the interface (r = 90°). For larger i the point C is inside the circle, no tangent exists, and no refracted wavefront can be built. The energy is then carried by the reflected wave, constructed exactly as in module 4, with r = i. Wave theory shows a non-propagating (evanescent) disturbance within about a wavelength of the boundary that carries no average energy; this is a later topic (verify), so "no refracted wavefront" means no propagating refracted wave.

NCERT notes a difference from ordinary reflection: in ordinary reflection some fraction is always transmitted, so the reflected ray is always weaker; in total internal reflection no transmission takes place.

## Numbers

- Glass of n = 1.5 to air: sin i_c = 1/1.5 = 0.6667, i_c = 41.81°.
- NCERT Table 9.1 (with respect to air): water 1.33 gives 48.75°; crown glass 1.52 gives 41.14°; diamond 2.42 gives 24.41°. Recomputed values match these three.
- Source slip: the table lists dense flint glass n = 1.62 with 37.31°, but sin⁻¹(1/1.62) = 38.12°; 37.31° is what n = 1.65 gives. Use the formula, not that table entry.
- Prisms that turn light by 90° or 180° need i_c < 45° (NCERT states that crown and dense flint glass meet this); the lesson's own step turns this into n > 1/sin 45° = 1.414. Optical fibres have a core of higher index than the cladding, which makes total internal reflection possible; whether a ray meets the wall at more than the critical angle depends on the launch angle (NCERT: fibres are fabricated so that this happens).

## Checklist for total internal reflection

Both conditions are needed: the wave goes from higher to lower refractive index, and i > i_c. Going from air into glass (v₂ < v₁) it can never happen, because sin r = sin i / 1.5 is always less than 1.

### 8. Master: Fermat's principle as a cross-check, and wavefronts through lenses and prisms

*Objective:* Check Snell's law a second way by minimising travel time, know the limits of Fermat's principle, and see why equal travel time along every ray gives focusing.

## Why a second derivation

A result gains confidence when two independent routes agree. Fermat's principle is such a route. Brij Lal states it in its modern form: a light ray going from one point to another follows a path for which the travel time is stationary compared with neighbouring paths: a minimum, a maximum, or unchanged. It is not always a minimum (Brij Lal notes a maximum for a spherical reflector and equal times for an ellipsoidal one). Its full treatment is the topic PH-043; only the cross-check is done here.

## Snell's law by minimising time

Put the interface along y = 0, medium 1 (speed v₁ = c/n₁) above and medium 2 below. A is at (0, a) with a > 0; B is at (d, −b) with b > 0. A path touches the interface at P = (x, 0). The time is

$$ T(x) = √(x² + a²)/v₁ + √((d − x)² + b²)/v₂ $$

Setting dT/dx = 0 gives x/(v₁ √(x² + a²)) = (d − x)/(v₂ √((d − x)² + b²)). Now x/√(x² + a²) = sin i and (d − x)/√((d − x)² + b²) = sin r, where i and r are the angles of the two segments with the vertical normal. So sin i / v₁ = sin r / v₂, which is Snell's law again. The second derivative is the sum of two positive terms, a²/(v₁ (x² + a²)^(3/2)) + b²/(v₂ ((d − x)² + b²)^(3/2)), so here the stationary time is a minimum.

Numerical check with n₁ = 1.0, n₂ = 1.5, a = 4, b = 3, d = 8 (any one length unit): the optical path n₁√(x² + a²) + n₂√((d − x)² + b²) equals 12.908 for the straight line (x = 4.571), 13.157 at x = 4, 12.619 at x = 6 and 13.444 at x = 8. The minimum is at x = 6.000, where sin i = 6/√52 = 0.8321 and 1.5 sin r = 1.5 × 2/√13 = 0.8321. The two sides of Snell's law agree.

## Wavefront picture of lenses and prisms

NCERT applies the same construction to a thin prism and a convex lens: the part of a plane wavefront that passes through more glass is delayed more, because the speed is less in glass. For the prism this tilts the emerging wavefront; for the lens it curves it into a sphere that converges on the focus. Hence the time from a point on the object to the corresponding image point is the same along every ray; the central ray is shorter but slower in glass.

## Do not mix the methods in an exam

A question that says "using Huygens' principle" must be answered by the Huygens construction. A Fermat derivation, however correct, does not answer it. Use Fermat only as a stated cross-check.

### 9. Exam: reading the 2023 question and writing a 10-mark Huygens answer

*Objective:* Turn the derivations into a complete, well-presented 10-mark answer to the verified 2023 question, and avoid the standard traps.

## The question

UPSC Physics Optional Paper I 2023, Q1(d), 10 marks (verified from the official paper): using Huygens' principle for a plane wave travelling from a rarer medium 1 to a denser medium 2, show that sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = μ₂/μ₁, where i and r are the angles of incidence and refraction, and v₁, μ₁ and v₂, μ₂ are the speeds and refractive indices in the two media. (Indian texts, and this question, write μ for the refractive index that NCERT writes n.) Per the project brief, Paper I has compulsory Q1 and Q5, each with several 10-mark short parts; verify against the paper instructions.

## Decoding it

- "Using Huygens' principle": you must show the wavelets and the envelope.
- "Rarer to denser": v₁ > v₂, so r < i. The figure must show bending toward the normal.
- "Show that": the three-way equality has two links: sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ (geometry) and v₁/v₂ = μ₂/μ₁ (definition μ = c/v).

## Answer plan (practice target about 12 minutes, not an official allotment)

1. State Huygens' principle in two lines (about 1 minute).
2. One neat labelled figure: interface PP′, wavefront AB, ray BC, circle about A of radius v₂t, tangent CE, point E; i and r marked from the normal (3 minutes).
3. Distances: BC = v₁t, AE = v₂t (1 minute).
4. Two right triangles and two sines, then the division (3 minutes).
5. Substitute μ = c/v to reach μ₂/μ₁ (1 minute).
6. A closing line on direction, wavelength and frequency, plus one check (2 minutes).

## Traps

- Measuring i or r from the interface instead of the normal.
- Writing v₂/v₁, or μ₁/μ₂, in the ratio.
- Drawing the tangent from A or B rather than from C to the circle about A.
- Forgetting to say why the tangent line is the envelope (derivation D2, step 10).
- Using Fermat's principle instead of Huygens'.

### 10. Practice: guided numerical problems on refraction and critical angle

*Objective:* Use a fixed method on numericals: identify the direction, write the ratio, check the sine, then compute.

## The method

1. Mark medium 1 (where the wave arrives) and medium 2, and list n₁, n₂ (or v₁, v₂).
2. Decide the direction: slower second medium means bending toward the normal.
3. Write n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r, solve for sin r as a number, and check it is at most 1.
4. Convert to an angle in degrees mode; check that r < i if the second medium is denser.
5. For λ and v: v = c/n, λ = λ₀/n, ν unchanged.

## Guided problem 1

Light of vacuum wavelength 600 nm travels from air (n ≈ 1) into water (n = 1.33) at i = 50°. Find r, the speed and the wavelength in water, and the frequency.

Water is denser, so the ray bends toward the normal. sin r = sin 50° / 1.33 = 0.7660 / 1.33 = 0.5760, so r = 35.17°, smaller than 50°, as expected. v = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.33 = 2.26 × 10⁸ m/s. λ = 600 / 1.33 = 451 nm. ν = c/λ₀ = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 600 × 10⁻⁹ = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz, unchanged. Check: v = νλ = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ × 451 × 10⁻⁹ = 2.26 × 10⁸ m/s.

## Guided problem 2

Light goes from glass (n = 1.5) into air. Does it emerge for i = 40°? For i = 45°?

First compute the critical angle: sin i_c = 1/1.5 = 0.6667, so i_c = 41.81°. At i = 40°, sin r = 1.5 × sin 40° = 1.5 × 0.6428 = 0.9642, so r = 74.6°: the light emerges, bent away from the normal. At i = 45°, 1.5 × sin 45° = 1.0607, which exceeds 1: no refracted wave, so the light is totally reflected. The check agrees: 45° > 41.81°, while 40° < 41.81°.

## Where errors usually hide

Pressing inverse sine on a number above 1 (a signal for TIR, not a calculator fault); using degrees on a radians setting; writing n₂ sin i; forgetting that the wavelength in the medium, not the vacuum wavelength, enters v = νλ.

### Notes: Definitions

- Wavefront: a surface of constant phase; it moves at the speed of the wave, and the energy travels perpendicular to it (NCERT).
- Ray: a line showing the direction of energy flow, perpendicular to the wavefronts in a uniform isotropic medium; in geometrical optics the path of energy propagation in the limit of zero wavelength.
- Plane wave: parallel plane wavefronts. Spherical wave: concentric spherical wavefronts from a point source. Cylindrical wave: coaxial cylindrical wavefronts from a line source.
- Secondary wavelet: the sphere of radius vt that each point of a wavefront is imagined to send out in time t.
- Envelope: a surface tangent to every member of a family of surfaces; in Huygens' construction, the new wavefront on the forward side.
- Refractive index n = c/v: ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the medium; relative index n₂₁ = n₂/n₁ = v₁/v₂.
- Critical angle i_c: the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction is 90°; sin i_c = n₂/n₁ with n₁ > n₂.
- Optical density: how large the refractive index is; not the same as mass density (NCERT: turpentine versus water).

### Notes: Laws and principles

- Huygens' principle: every point of a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets with the speed of the wave; the new wavefront is the forward envelope of the wavelets.
- First law of reflection and refraction: the incident ray, the reflected or refracted ray and the normal lie in one plane.
- Second law of reflection: i = r, angles measured from the normal.
- Snell's law: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r, equivalently sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁.
- Frequency does not change on reflection or refraction; speed and wavelength change in the ratio v₁/v₂ on refraction.
- Total internal reflection occurs only from higher to lower index and only for i > i_c.
- Fermat's principle (modern form, Brij Lal): the actual ray path has stationary travel time compared with neighbouring paths.

### Notes: Key equations

- v = ν λ
- n = c / v
- i = r (reflection)
- sin i / sin r = v₁ / v₂ = n₂ / n₁ (refraction)
- n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r
- λ₁ / λ₂ = v₁ / v₂ = n₂ / n₁; λ in a medium = λ₀ / n
- sin i_c = n₂ / n₁ (n₁ > n₂); for a glass or liquid to air, sin i_c = 1/n
- n₃₁ = n₃₂ × n₂₁; n₁₂ = 1 / n₂₁

### Notes: Derivation outline: reflection (Huygens)

- Draw incident front AB, A on the surface, B with ray BC reaching the surface at C: BC = vt, angle B = 90°.
- Draw circle radius vt about A; tangent from C touches at E: AE = vt, angle E = 90°; CE is the reflected front.
- Triangles BAC and ECA: right angles, common hypotenuse AC, BC = AE, so congruent (RHS).
- Angle BAC = i and angle ECA = r by perpendicular pairs; hence i = r.

### Notes: Derivation outline: refraction (Huygens)

- Same front AB and ray BC: BC = v₁t. Circle radius v₂t about A in medium 2; tangent from C touches at E: AE = v₂t.
- sin i = BC/AC = v₁t/AC; sin r = AE/AC = v₂t/AC.
- Divide: sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂; with n = c/v this is n₂/n₁, so n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r.
- Same frequency in both media gives λ₁/λ₂ = v₁/v₂.

### Notes: Comparison: reflection and refraction at a plane boundary

- Compared on direction, speed, wavelength, frequency and the governing law.

### Notes: Comparison: rarer to denser and denser to rarer

- Direction of bending and the possibility of total internal reflection.

### Notes: Chronology and source-date slips

- 1637: Descartes' corpuscular model and derivation of Snell's law (NCERT).
- 1678: Huygens proposes the wave theory (NCERT; Brij Lal's history section); Brij Lal's later section gives 1679 for the same event. Use 1678 and note the difference.
- 1801: Young's interference experiment (NCERT); Brij Lal gives 1803. The sources differ; no date question depends on this here.
- 1850: Foucault shows that light is slower in water than in air (NCERT, Brij Lal).
- 1882: Kirchhoff shows that Huygens' principle follows from the wave equation (Brij Lal).
- Fermat's life dates: Brij Lal's history gives 1601–1655, its portrait caption gives 1601–1665. Teach 1601–1665 (standard knowledge; verify) and treat 1655 as the slip. His least-time work is dated 1658 in one place and 1650 in another (source inconsistency, flagged).

### Notes: Limitations and exceptions

- The original Huygens construction predicts a back-wave that is not observed; the justification for its absence comes from the wave equation (Kirchhoff), not from the construction.
- The plain construction gives no amplitudes or intensities.
- Total internal reflection does not occur going into a denser medium.
- Fermat's stationary time is not always a minimum (Brij Lal: maximum for a spherical reflector, constant for an ellipsoidal one).
- The refractive index depends on the wavelength of the light (NCERT), so tabulated values are for stated or typical visible light.

### Notes: Frequently confused

- Angle with the surface versus angle with the normal: i and r are always from the normal.
- Wavefront versus ray: perpendicular to each other; a wavefront is a surface, a ray a line.
- Optical density versus mass density.
- v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ (inverse) versus v₁/v₂ = n₁/n₂ (wrong).
- Frequency (fixed by the source) versus wavelength (set by the medium).
- Huygens' construction (wavefront geometry, used in the 2023 question) versus Fermat's principle (time minimisation, a cross-check only).

- **derivations:**
  - - **id:** PH-042-D1
    - **task:** Derive the law of reflection i = r for a plane wave at a plane surface using Huygens' construction. Timed practice target (not an official allotment): about 12 minutes for 10 marks.
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions and coordinates: a plane wave of speed v in a uniform medium (y > 0) meets the plane surface y = 0; the normal is +y. Angles are measured from the normal. The incident direction is the unit vector d = (sin i, −cos i), so the incident ray travels down and to the right.
      - Choose A at the origin, the point where the wavefront touches the surface at t = 0. The wavefront through A is perpendicular to d, so its direction is (cos i, sin i). Take B = L (cos i, sin i) on it, with L > 0.
      - The ray from B along d reaches y = 0 when B + s d has zero height: L sin i − s cos i = 0, so s = L tan i. Hence BC = L tan i, C = (L/cos i, 0) and AC = L/cos i. The time taken is t = BC/v, so BC = vt.
      - Huygens' wavelet from A has radius vt = L tan i at time t, centred on A, in the same medium (reflection does not change the medium).
      - Draw the tangent from C to this circle, touching at E. Since a radius is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact, triangle AEC has a right angle at E, hypotenuse AC = L/cos i and leg AE = vt = L tan i.
      - Therefore sin ∠ECA = AE/AC = (L tan i)(cos i)/L = sin i. The angle ECA is acute because it is an angle of a right triangle; hence ∠ECA = i.
      - Identify r: the reflected ray is along AE (perpendicular to the reflected wavefront CE). The angle between the wavefront CE and the surface is ∠ECA; the angle between its perpendiculars, the ray AE and the normal, is the same. So r = ∠ECA = i. Coordinates agree: E = L tan i (sin i, cos i), so the reflected direction is (sin i, cos i), the incident direction with its normal component reversed; the two rays lie on opposite sides of the normal.
      - Envelope check (every wavelet touches CE): a point P = (s, 0) with 0 < s < AC is reached by the front at time t_P = s sin i / v, because P·d = s sin i is the distance travelled along d from the front through A. Its wavelet has radius v(t − t_P) = vt − s sin i = L tan i − s sin i = (AC − s) sin i. The distance from P to the line CE (which makes angle i with the surface and passes through C) is (AC − s) sin i. The two are equal, so the wavelet from P touches CE; CE is the envelope of the whole family.
      - Second law: the construction uses only the plane containing the incident ray and the normal, and the reflected ray lies in it by symmetry, so the incident ray, reflected ray and normal are coplanar.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensional check: sin i = BC/AC is a ratio of two lengths, so it is a pure number; v cancels, so the law does not depend on speed or wavelength.
      - Limiting cases: i = 0 gives BC = 0 and the wave returns along the normal; i → 90° gives r → 90°.

      - Measuring the angles from the surface.
      - Drawing the tangent from B instead of C, or the circle about C instead of A.
      - Stating congruence without naming the third equal element, BC = AE.

    - **task:** Derive Snell's law sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ for a plane wave going from medium 1 to medium 2 by Huygens' construction, and show that the frequency is unchanged and λ₁/λ₂ = v₁/v₂. This is the verified 2023 question. Timed practice target (not an official allotment): about 12 minutes for 10 marks.
    - **steps:**
      - Setup and sign convention: the interface is y = 0; medium 1 (y > 0) has speed v₁ and index n₁; medium 2 (y < 0) has speed v₂ and index n₂. The incident direction is d = (sin i, −cos i). Angles i and r are measured from the normal (the y axis). Assume plane wave, plane boundary, uniform media.
      - As in D1, A = origin lies on the front at t = 0, B = L (cos i, sin i), the ray from B hits the interface at C = (L/cos i, 0) after time t, with BC = L tan i = v₁t and AC = L/cos i.
      - In the same time t the wavelet from A, travelling in medium 2, has radius v₂t. Draw this circle about A below the interface.
      - A tangent from C to the circle exists only when C is outside or on the circle, i.e. v₂t ≤ AC. Let it touch at E. Then AE = v₂t and AE ⊥ CE; CE is the refracted wavefront.
      - Right triangle ABC (right angle at B): ∠BAC = i, so sin i = BC/AC = v₁t/AC.
      - Right triangle AEC (right angle at E): sin ∠ACE = AE/AC = v₂t/AC. The refracted ray is along AE; it is perpendicular to the wavefront CE, and the normal is perpendicular to the interface, so the angle between AE and the normal equals the angle between CE and the interface, ∠ACE. Call it r. Thus sin r = v₂t/AC.
      - Divide step 5 by step 6: sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂, since t and AC cancel.
      - Refractive index: n = c/v for each medium, so v₁ = c/n₁ and v₂ = c/n₂ and v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁. Therefore sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁, i.e. n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r.
      - Direction: E = v₂t (sin r, −cos r), so the refracted ray continues to the right and downward. If v₂ < v₁ then sin r < sin i, so r < i: bending toward the normal (the case 'rarer to denser' in the question).
      - Envelope check: a point P = (s, 0) between A and C is reached at t_P = s sin i / v₁. Its wavelet in medium 2 has radius v₂(t − t_P) = v₂ (L tan i − s sin i)/v₁ = (v₂/v₁)(AC − s) sin i = (AC − s) sin r (using sin r = (v₂/v₁) sin i). The distance from P to the line CE is (AC − s) sin ∠ACE = (AC − s) sin r. They are equal, so every wavelet touches CE and CE is the true envelope.
      - Frequency and wavelength: each secondary wavelet oscillates at the frequency ν of the incident wave (Brij Lal's postulate), so the refracted wave has the same ν in medium 2. With v = νλ, λ₁ = v₁/ν and λ₂ = v₂/ν, so λ₁/λ₂ = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁. In the figure this is seen by choosing BC = λ₁: the crest from A reaches E in the same time, so AE = λ₂.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensional check: v₁/v₂ is a ratio of speeds and sin i / sin r is a ratio of sines; both are pure numbers.
      - Limiting cases: n₂ = n₁ gives r = i (no bending); i = 0 gives r = 0 (straight through).
      - Numerical check with python: air to glass (n₂ = 1.5), i = 30° gives sin r = 0.5/1.5 = 0.3333, r = 19.47°; with v₁ = 3.0 × 10⁸ and v₂ = 2.0 × 10⁸, sin i / sin r = 1.5 = v₁/v₂.
    - **traps:**
      - Writing v₂/v₁ in the ratio.
      - Marking r as the angle between the refracted wavefront and the normal.
      - Omitting the step v = c/n that connects speeds to the indices.
      - Not stating why CE is the tangent to the circle about A and the envelope of all wavelets.

    - **task:** Cross-check Snell's law by Fermat's principle: minimise the travel time between a point in medium 1 and a point in medium 2. Timed practice target (not an official allotment): about 12 minutes for 10 marks.
    - **steps:**
      - Setup: interface y = 0; medium 1 above (speed v₁), medium 2 below (speed v₂); A = (0, a), B = (d, −b) with a, b > 0; the path crosses the interface at P = (x, 0). Straight segments are used in each uniform medium.
      - Travel time: T(x) = √(x² + a²)/v₁ + √((d − x)² + b²)/v₂.
      - Differentiate: dT/dx = x/(v₁ √(x² + a²)) − (d − x)/(v₂ √((d − x)² + b²)).
      - Fermat (stationary time): set dT/dx = 0, so x/(v₁ √(x² + a²)) = (d − x)/(v₂ √((d − x)² + b²)).
      - Identify the angles with the vertical normal: sin i = x/√(x² + a²) and sin r = (d − x)/√((d − x)² + b²).
      - Therefore sin i / v₁ = sin r / v₂; with v = c/n this is n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r, the same as D2.
      - Nature of the stationary point: d²T/dx² = a²/(v₁ (x² + a²)^(3/2)) + b²/(v₂ ((d − x)² + b²)^(3/2)) > 0, so here T is a minimum.
    - **checks:**
      - Numerical check with python: n₁ = 1.0, n₂ = 1.5, a = 4, b = 3, d = 8: the optical path is 12.908 on the straight line (x = 4.571), 12.619 at x = 6, 13.157 at x = 4 and 13.444 at x = 8; the minimum is at x = 6.000 with sin i = 0.8321 = 1.5 sin r (r = 33.69°, i = 56.31°).
      - Limiting case: n₁ = n₂ gives the straight line from A to B.

      - Treating Fermat's principle as always a minimum.
      - Answering a 'using Huygens' principle' question by this method.

    - **task:** Show that the Huygens envelope of the wavelets from a spherical wavefront of radius R about S consists of two spheres about S, of radii R + vt and R − vt, and identify the back-wave. Timed practice target (not an official allotment): about 12 minutes for 10 marks.
    - **steps:**
      - Setup: S is the origin; the wavefront F is the sphere |x| = R; v is uniform. Each point P of F emits a wavelet that at time t is the sphere of radius ρ = vt centred on P. Assume ρ < R.
      - By rotational symmetry about S, the envelope of the family is a union of spheres centred on S.
      - Take any point Q on the wavelet from P. The triangle inequality gives |SQ| ≤ |SP| + |PQ| = R + ρ, and |SQ| ≥ |SP| − |PQ| = R − ρ.
      - Equality in the first bound holds for Q on the ray from S through P beyond P, at distance R + ρ; equality in the second at the point at distance R − ρ on the same ray, between S and P.
      - So the wavelet lies between the spheres of radii R − ρ and R + ρ and touches each of them. At the touching point the wavelet and the sphere have the same normal (along SP), so they are tangent.
      - This holds for every P on F, so both spheres are envelopes of the whole family: the outer one (radius R + vt) is the forward wavefront G; the inner one (radius R − vt) converges back toward S and is the back-wave of NCERT Fig. 10.2.
      - Interpretation: nothing in the construction chooses the outer envelope. A reason for discarding the inner one has to come from outside, from Huygens' assumption of zero backward amplitude (ad hoc, per NCERT) or from the wave equation (Kirchhoff).
    - **checks:**
      - Limiting case: t → 0 gives R ± vt → R, so both envelopes coincide with F.
      - Limiting case: R → ∞ gives two parallel planes at distance vt from F: the forward plane wave and the unobserved backward plane.
      - Numerical check: R = 10 m, v t = 3 m gives envelopes of radii 13 m and 7 m, differing by 2 vt = 6 m.
      - Dimensional check: R and vt are lengths.
    - **traps:** - Believing that the construction itself excludes the back-wave.

### Memory skeleton

- Wavefront = surface of constant phase; rays perpendicular to it
- Huygens: wavelets of radius vt from every point; new front = forward envelope; back-wave problem
- Reflection: triangles BAC, ECA congruent (RHS) ⇒ i = r
- Refraction: sin i = v₁t/AC, sin r = v₂t/AC ⇒ sin i/sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁
- Frequency fixed; λ = λ₀/n
- Critical angle sin i_c = n₂/n₁ (n₁ > n₂); total internal reflection beyond it

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, state Huygens' principle and draw the constructions that give i = r and n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r, then derive the critical angle.

### Last-week summary

- Huygens: every point of a wavefront sends wavelets at the wave speed; the forward envelope is the new front; back-wave absence justified by Kirchhoff (1882), not by the construction.
- Reflection: BC = vt, circle vt about A, tangent CE; triangles BAC and ECA congruent ⇒ i = r.
- Refraction: circle v₂t about A; sin i/sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁; same frequency ⇒ λ₁/λ₂ = v₁/v₂.
- sin i_c = n₂/n₁ for denser to rarer only; n = 1.5 glass to air: i_c = 41.81°.
