## GS-182: Parliamentary committees

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Union government

### Bridge: What 'scrutiny' means

**Scrutiny** means close, careful and critical examination. When Parliament 'scrutinises' the government, it does not merely listen to a speech and then vote; it asks for papers, questions officials, checks whether money was spent as sanctioned and compares what ministers promised with what was done. You will see the word again and again in this chapter: committees 'scrutinise' Bills, the budget, spending, promises, delegated rules and the welfare of groups.

Three ideas help. First, scrutiny is *about the executive*: the executive (the council of ministers and the administration under it) runs the government, and Parliament's job in a parliamentary democracy is to hold it to account. Second, scrutiny needs *time and detail*, which is exactly what a full House of hundreds of members, meeting for short sessions, lacks. Third, scrutiny can happen *before* a decision (for example, examining a Bill or demands for grants before they are voted) or *after* it (examining accounts after money has been spent). Laxmikanth repeatedly calls the second kind a **post-mortem**: an examination after the event, useful to learn from and to correct, but unable to undo what has been done.

A simple test: if a body only says 'yes' or 'no' at the end, it approves; if it asks questions, hears witnesses and writes a report, it scrutinises.

### Bridge: Parliament's time problem, in plain words

Parliament does not sit all year. NCERT notes that it meets only in **sessions**, so its time is very limited, while the tasks are heavy: making laws (which needs in-depth study), studying the demands for grants of dozens of ministries, looking into the spending of departments and investigating complaints such as corruption. Laxmikanth makes the same point in his first paragraph: Parliament is too unwieldy to deliberate effectively on everything, and it has neither the adequate time nor the necessary expertise to make a detailed scrutiny of all measures.

No figures are needed to see the point, and none are given here because the extracts contain none. Picture a single House of several hundred members. If every Bill, every ministry's budget and every audit report were discussed clause by clause on the floor, a session would be consumed by a handful of items. The practical answer used in all modern legislatures is division of labour: a small group of members examines a subject in detail, hears the minister, the officials and outside experts, and reports back to the House.

NCERT adds a worry: it notes that in the last two decades there has been a gradual decline in the sittings of the Lok Sabha and State assemblies and in the time spent on debates, along with absence of quorum and boycotts. Committees partly compensate for the loss of floor time, but cannot replace it. Treat this as a qualitative claim from NCERT; if you want numbers, take them from Parliament's own records or from PRS Legislative Research and date them.

### 1. Orientation: why committees exist and what counts as a parliamentary committee

*Objective:* Explain why Parliament uses committees and apply Laxmikanth's four-part definition of a parliamentary committee.

## The reason in one paragraph

Parliament has varied, complex and voluminous work, but little time and limited expertise. So it is assisted by **committees**: small groups of members who study a subject in detail and report. NCERT calls committees **miniature legislatures**, because a large part of the discussion on a Bill takes place there, and the committee's recommendation then goes to the House. NCERT also points out that committees play a vital role not only in law-making but in the day-to-day business of the House: studying demands for grants, looking into departmental expenditure and investigating irregularities.

## What the Constitution says

Laxmikanth observes that the Constitution mentions committees at different places but makes no specific provision about their composition, tenure or functions. These matters are handled by the **rules of the two Houses**. (The chapter extract gives no Article numbers for the committees; Article 118, which empowers each House to make rules of procedure, is the standard anchor and should be verified in the Constitution text.)

## The four-part test

According to Laxmikanth a **parliamentary committee** is a committee that:

1. is appointed or elected by the House, or nominated by the Speaker (Lok Sabha) or Chairman (Rajya Sabha);
2. works under the direction of the Speaker or Chairman;
3. presents its report to the House or to the Speaker or Chairman; and
4. has a secretariat provided by the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha.

**Consultative committees**, although they consist of MPs, are *not* parliamentary committees because they do not fulfil these four conditions. Remember this exception; it is a favourite trap.

## Three key terms

- **Standing committee**: permanent, constituted every year or periodically, working continuously.
- **Ad hoc committee**: temporary, created for a specific task and ceasing to exist after finishing it ('ad hoc' means 'for this purpose').
- **Joint committee**: a committee with members of both Houses of Parliament.

### 2. The classification map: standing and ad hoc committees

*Objective:* Reproduce the six categories of standing committees and the two kinds of ad hoc committees, with one example each.

## Standing and ad hoc

Broadly, parliamentary committees are of two kinds. **Standing committees** are permanent (constituted every year or periodically) and work continuously. **Ad hoc committees** are temporary and cease to exist once their task is complete.

## The six categories of standing committees

Laxmikanth classifies standing committees by the nature of their functions:

1. **Financial committees**: Public Accounts, Estimates, Public Undertakings (modules 3 and 4).
2. **Departmental standing committees**: 24 in number (module 5).
3. **Committees to inquire**: Petitions, Privileges, Ethics (module 6).
4. **Committees to scrutinise and control**: Government Assurances, Subordinate Legislation, Papers Laid on the Table, Welfare of SCs and STs, Empowerment of Women, and the Joint Committee on Offices of Profit (module 6).
5. **Committees relating to the day-to-day business of the House**: Business Advisory, Private Members' Bills and Resolutions, Rules, and Absence of Members (module 7).
6. **House-keeping or service committees**: General Purposes, House, Library, and the Joint Committee on Salaries and Allowances of Members (module 7).

## The two kinds of ad hoc committees

1. **Inquiry committees** are constituted from time to time, either by the two Houses on a motion or by the Speaker or Chairman, to inquire into and report on specific subjects. Laxmikanth's examples include the Committee on Draft Five-Year Plan, the Railway Convention Committee, joint committees on the Bofors contract, on fertilizer pricing, on irregularities in securities and banking transactions, on the stock market scam and on allocation and pricing of telecom licences and spectrum. A joint committee of this inquiry type is often called a **Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC)**; NCERT says JPCs can be set up to discuss a particular Bill or to investigate financial irregularities.
2. **Advisory committees** include the select or joint committees on Bills, appointed to consider and report on particular Bills. Their procedure is laid down in the Rules and in the Speaker's or Chairman's Directions.

## A pattern for remembering

Ask three questions of any committee name: Is it permanent or temporary? Does it look at money, a department, a complaint, a promise or housekeeping? Does it have members from one House or both? The answers fix its place on the map. A committee on a *single Bill* is ad hoc and advisory; a committee that reappears every year is standing.

## Date discrepancy to watch

NCERT says India developed a system of standing committees 'since 1983'. Laxmikanth says the departmentally related standing committees were set up in 1993 (and that three subject committees were formed in 1989 before them). Teach 1993 as the date for the DRSC system, noting that the two sources differ (verify against Rajya Sabha or Lok Sabha websites).

### 3. Financial committees I: the Public Accounts Committee

*Objective:* State the composition, chairperson tradition, functions and limits of the Public Accounts Committee and its link with the CAG.

## Origin and composition

The **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** was first set up in **1921** under the Government of India Act of 1919 and has existed since. It consists of **22 members: 15 from the Lok Sabha and 7 from the Rajya Sabha**. Members are elected by Parliament every year from among its own members by the **principle of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote**, so all parties get due representation. The term of members is **one year**. A **minister cannot be elected** as a member.

## The chairperson and the Opposition tradition

The chairman is appointed from among its members by the **Speaker**. Until 1966-67 the chairman belonged to the ruling party; **since 1967 a convention has developed that the chairman is selected invariably from the Opposition**. A convention is an unwritten practice followed by habit, not a rule of law; remember that it is a convention.

## What it examines: the CAG's reports

The PAC examines the annual **audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)**, which are laid before Parliament by the President. The CAG submits three audit reports to the President: on **appropriation accounts**, on **finance accounts** and on **public undertakings**. Appropriation accounts compare actual spending with the amounts sanctioned by Parliament through the Appropriation Act; finance accounts show the annual receipts and disbursements of the Union government.

## Functions

1. Examine the appropriation accounts and finance accounts of the Union government and any other accounts laid before the Lok Sabha.
2. Satisfy itself that money was legally available for the purpose, that the spending conforms to the authority governing it, and that every re-appropriation follows the rules.
3. Examine accounts of state corporations, trading concerns and manufacturing projects and the CAG's audit reports on them (except undertakings allotted to the Committee on Public Undertakings).
4. Examine accounts of autonomous and semi-autonomous bodies audited by the CAG.
5. Consider CAG reports on the audit of receipts and the accounts of stores and stocks.
6. Examine money spent beyond the amount granted by the Lok Sabha.

The PAC examines expenditure not only from the legal and formal point of view but from the standpoint of **economy, prudence, wisdom and propriety**, to expose waste, loss, corruption, extravagance, inefficiency and nugatory expenses.

## The CAG relationship

The CAG assists the PAC and is described as its 'guide, friend and philosopher'. The Estimates Committee lacks this expert assistance (module 4). Laxmikanth quotes Ashok Chanda (himself a former CAG) praising the committee's traditions; NCERT adds that the Lok Sabha can enquire into misuse of funds on the basis of the CAG's and the PAC's reports.

## Limits

It is not concerned with policy in a broader sense; it conducts a **post-mortem** of expenditure already incurred; it cannot intervene in day-to-day administration; its recommendations are **advisory**, not binding on ministries; it cannot disallow expenditure; and it is not an executive body, so it cannot issue orders. Only Parliament can take a final decision on its findings.

### 4. Financial committees II: Estimates, Public Undertakings, and how the three compare

*Objective:* State the composition and functions of the Estimates Committee and the Committee on Public Undertakings and distinguish the three financial committees.

## Estimates Committee

Its origin lies in the standing financial committee of **1921**. The first post-independence Estimates Committee was constituted in **1950** on the recommendation of John Mathai, then finance minister. It began with 25 members, raised to **30 in 1956**. **All 30 are from the Lok Sabha; the Rajya Sabha has no representation.** Members are elected by the Lok Sabha every year by STV, with a one-year term, and a minister cannot be a member. The chairman is appointed by the Speaker and is **invariably from the ruling party** (the opposite of the PAC).

Its job is to examine the estimates in the budget and suggest **economies**; it is called a **'continuous economy committee'**. It reports on economies, improvements in organisation and efficiency consistent with the policy underlying the estimates, suggests alternative policies for efficiency and economy, examines whether money is well laid out within that policy, and suggests the form in which estimates are presented. It need not examine all estimates of one year and may examine them throughout the year. Limits: it examines estimates after they are voted, cannot question the policy laid down by Parliament, its recommendations are advisory, it covers selected ministries by rotation, it lacks the CAG's expert help, and its work is in the nature of a post-mortem.

## Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)

Created in **1964** on the recommendation of the **Krishna Menon Committee**. It began with 15 members (10 Lok Sabha, 5 Rajya Sabha); in 1974 this rose to **22 (15 Lok Sabha, 7 Rajya Sabha)**. Members are elected annually by STV; the term is one year; ministers are excluded. The chairman is appointed by the Speaker **from the Lok Sabha members only**, so Rajya Sabha members cannot chair it.

Functions: examine the reports and accounts of public undertakings and the CAG's reports on them; examine whether their affairs are managed on sound business principles and prudent commercial practices (in the context of their autonomy and efficiency); and exercise functions vested in the PAC and Estimates Committee for undertakings allotted to it by the Speaker. It must not examine major government policy (as distinct from business functions), day-to-day administration, or matters for which special statute provides machinery. Limits: it cannot take up more than ten to twelve undertakings a year, its work is a post-mortem, it does not look into technical matters since members are not experts, and its recommendations are advisory.

## The three compared

| Feature | PAC | Estimates | COPU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength | 22 (15 LS + 7 RS) | 30 (all LS) | 22 (15 LS + 7 RS) |
| Origin | 1921 | 1950 (post-independence) | 1964 |
| Chairman | By convention from the Opposition | From the ruling party | From LS members only |
| Looks at | CAG audit reports and accounts | Budget estimates; economy | Public undertakings |
| CAG help | Yes ('guide, friend and philosopher') | No | CAG reports on undertakings |

## Overlap

The PAC examines the accounts of state corporations and similar bodies *except* those allotted to COPU, and the Estimates Committee must stay away from undertakings allotted to COPU. So the three divide the field: accounts, estimates, undertakings.

### 5. Departmental standing committees (DRSCs)

*Objective:* Describe the history, structure, functions, limits and merits of the departmentally related standing committees.

## History

On the recommendation of the Rules Committee of the Lok Sabha, **17 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)** were set up in Parliament in **1993**. Before them, in 1989, three standing committees (Agriculture, Science and Technology, Environment and Forests) had been formed and were superseded in 1993. In **2004**, seven more committees were added, taking the number from 17 to **24**. (NCERT's 'since 1983' differs from Laxmikanth's 1993; teach 1993 for the DRSCs (verify); the DRSC numbers should be verified against the current Parliament websites, since committees are reconstituted every year.)

## Objective

The main aim is to secure **more accountability of the Executive to Parliament, particularly financial accountability**, and to help Parliament debate the budget more effectively. Laxmikanth's note quotes the Vice-President and Rajya Sabha Chairman K.R. Narayanan at the 1993 inauguration as saying the intention is not to weaken or criticise the administration but to strengthen it with more meaningful parliamentary support.

## Structure

The 24 committees cover all ministries and departments of the Central government. Each consists of **31 members: 21 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha**, the former nominated by the Speaker and the latter by the Chairman. (Till the 13th Lok Sabha each committee had not more than 45 members: 30 and 15. The restructuring of July 2004 made it 31.) **A minister is not eligible** to be nominated, and a member who becomes a minister ceases to be a member. The **term is one year** from the date of constitution. Of the 24, **8 work under the Rajya Sabha and 16 under the Lok Sabha** (Table 23.1 for 2019 lists the Rajya Sabha ones as Commerce; Home Affairs; Human Resource Development; Industry; Science and Technology, Environment and Forests; Transport, Tourism and Culture; Health and Family Welfare; and Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice; check current lists).

## Functions

1. Consider the **demands for grants** of the concerned ministries before they are discussed and voted in the Lok Sabha; their report should not suggest anything in the nature of **cut motions**.
2. Examine **Bills** pertaining to the concerned ministries.
3. Consider the **annual reports** of ministries.
4. Consider **national basic long-term policy documents** presented to the Houses.

## Procedure for demands for grants

After the general discussion on the budget, the Houses are adjourned for a fixed period; the committees consider the demands and report within that period, without asking for more time; a **separate report for each ministry** is made; the House then considers the demands in the light of the reports.

## Limits

They should not consider matters of **day-to-day administration**, and generally not matters considered by other parliamentary committees. Their recommendations are **advisory in nature** and not binding on Parliament.

## Merits (Laxmikanth)

Their proceedings are free of party bias; procedure is flexible; they make parliamentary control over the executive detailed, close, continuous and in-depth; they promote economy and efficiency; all MPs get to participate; they can invite experts or public opinion; and the Opposition and the Rajya Sabha can play a greater role in financial control.

### 6. Committees to inquire, scrutinise and control

*Objective:* Name the function, strength and Houses of each inquire and scrutinise-and-control committee and state which bar ministers.

## Committees to inquire

- **Committee on Petitions**: examines petitions on Bills and on matters of general public importance, and entertains representations from individuals and associations on Union subjects. Lok Sabha committee: 15 members; Rajya Sabha: 10.
- **Committee of Privileges**: its functions are **semi-judicial**; it examines cases of breach of the privileges of the House and its members and recommends action. Lok Sabha: 15; Rajya Sabha: 10. See GS-181 for privileges.
- **Ethics Committee**: constituted in the **Rajya Sabha in 1997 and in the Lok Sabha in 2000**; enforces the code of conduct of MPs, examines misconduct and recommends action, maintaining discipline and decorum. (The extract does not give the rules on who may complain or on sub judice matters; the 2024 Prelims question on this committee needs the committee's own rules, see module 9; verify.)

## Committees to scrutinise and control

- **Committee on Government Assurances** (constituted **1953**): examines assurances, promises and undertakings given by ministers on the floor of the House and reports how far they have been carried out. Lok Sabha: 15; Rajya Sabha: 10.
- **Committee on Subordinate Legislation** (constituted **1953**): examines whether the powers to make regulations, rules, sub-rules and bye-laws, delegated by Parliament or conferred by the Constitution on the Executive, are being properly exercised. Both Houses: 15 members.
- **Committee on Papers Laid on the Table** (**1975**): examines whether all papers laid on the table by ministers comply with the Constitution or the related Act or Rule; it does not examine statutory notifications and orders that fall under Subordinate Legislation. Lok Sabha: 15; Rajya Sabha: 10.
- **Committee on the Welfare of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes**: **30 members (20 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha)**. Functions: consider the reports of the National Commissions for SCs and STs and examine all matters of welfare, including constitutional and statutory safeguards and welfare programmes.
- **Committee on Empowerment of Women** (**1997**): **30 members (20 Lok Sabha, 10 Rajya Sabha)**; considers the reports of the National Commission for Women and examines measures to secure women's status, dignity and equality in all fields.
- **Joint Committee on Offices of Profit**: examines the composition and character of committees and bodies appointed by the Central, State and Union Territory governments and recommends whether holders should be disqualified from being MPs. **15 members (10 Lok Sabha, 5 Rajya Sabha).**

## Which committees bar ministers?

Laxmikanth's note says a minister is **not eligible** for the Financial Committees, the Departmental Standing Committees and the committees on Empowerment of Women, Government Assurances, Petitions, Subordinate Legislation, and Welfare of SCs and STs. The logic: these committees watch the executive, and a minister cannot sit in judgement on his own government.

## Comparison trap

'Subordinate Legislation' examines delegated rules and regulations; 'Papers Laid' examines papers laid on the table but not those rules and notifications. 'Government Assurances' is about promises made on the floor. Tie each to its keyword: rules, papers, promises.

### 7. Day-to-day business, house-keeping, joint, select and consultative committees

*Objective:* Describe the remaining committees and distinguish joint, select and consultative committees.

## Committees on the day-to-day business of the House

- **Business Advisory Committee**: regulates the programme and time-table and allocates time for legislative and other business. Lok Sabha: **15 members including the Speaker as chairman**; Rajya Sabha: **11 members including the Chairman as ex officio chairman**.
- **Committee on Private Members' Bills and Resolutions**: classifies Bills and allocates time for private members' (non-minister) Bills and resolutions. A **Lok Sabha-only** committee with **15 members, the Deputy Speaker as chairman**; the Rajya Sabha has none, and its Business Advisory Committee does this work.
- **Rules Committee**: considers procedure and conduct of business and recommends amendments to the rules. Lok Sabha: 15 members with the Speaker as ex officio chairman; Rajya Sabha: **16 members** with the Chairman as ex officio chairman.
- **Committee on Absence of Members**: considers leave applications and the cases of members absent for **60 days or more** without permission. A **Lok Sabha-only** committee of 15; in the Rajya Sabha the House itself deals with such matters.

## House-keeping (service) committees

- **General Purposes Committee**: advises on affairs of the House not falling in any other committee's jurisdiction; the presiding officer is ex officio chairman, with the Deputy, panel members, chairpersons of departmental committees, leaders of recognised parties and others.
- **House Committee**: residential accommodation and amenities such as food and medical aid in Delhi; Lok Sabha: 12 members.
- **Library Committee**: nine members (6 Lok Sabha, 3 Rajya Sabha).
- **Joint Committee on Salaries and Allowances of Members**: constituted under the 1954 Act; 15 members (10 Lok Sabha, 5 Rajya Sabha); frames rules on salary, allowances and pension.

## Select and joint committees on Bills

When a Bill is under general discussion, the House can refer it to a **Select Committee** (of that House) or a **Joint Committee** (of both Houses). A motion must be adopted; for a joint committee the other House is asked to nominate members. The committee studies the Bill **clause by clause**, may move amendments, can take **evidence** from associations, public bodies or experts, and submits a report; members who disagree can append **minutes of dissent**. These are ad hoc advisory committees.

## Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs)

NCERT says JPCs have a position of eminence: they may be set up to discuss a particular Bill or to investigate financial irregularities, with members from both Houses. Laxmikanth's list of inquiry committees includes joint committees on the Bofors contract, the stock market scam and telecom licences. Treat details about specific JPC reports as dated and verify them.

## Consultative committees

Attached to ministries; members from both Houses; the **Minister or Minister of State in charge is the chairman**; a forum for **informal discussion** between ministers and MPs on policies and their implementation. They are constituted by the **Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs**, which frames the guidelines. Membership is **voluntary**, with a maximum of 30 and a minimum of 10, and they are normally constituted after each Lok Sabha general election and stand **dissolved with the dissolution of every Lok Sabha**. Informal Consultative Committees also exist for Railway Zones. As module 1 explained, they are **not parliamentary committees**.

## Parliamentary forums and groups

Parliamentary forums and groups (for example on water conservation, children and similar subjects) are separate arrangements, not covered in this extract; this lesson only cross-refers to them. Check them in the relevant chapter later.

### 8. Strengths, weaknesses and 'mini-Parliament': weighing the committee system

*Objective:* Evaluate the committee system with sourced strengths and weaknesses and prepare an argument on whether committees are 'mini-Parliaments'.

## Strengths (sourced)

From Laxmikanth's list of merits of the standing committee system: proceedings are free of party bias; procedure is flexible; control over the executive becomes more detailed, close, continuous and comprehensive; ministries are more careful in formulating demands, so economy and efficiency improve; all MPs participate and understand government functioning; committees can call experts and public opinion; and the Opposition and the Rajya Sabha play a bigger role in financial control. NCERT adds that the system has reduced Parliament's burden: many important Bills are referred to committees, and Parliament has largely approved the committees' work with few alterations.

## Weaknesses (sourced)

1. **Advisory, not binding.** The reports of the PAC, Estimates, COPU and DRSCs are advisory; no committee can order an expenditure disallowed or issue directions. Only Parliament can decide.
2. **Post-mortem character.** PAC, Estimates and COPU examine after money has been spent or voted.
3. **Selective coverage.** The Estimates Committee covers selected ministries by rotation; COPU can take up only ten to twelve undertakings a year.
4. **Limited expertise.** COPU members are not technical experts; Estimates lacks the CAG's expert help.
5. **Policy limits.** The financial committees avoid broader policy and day-to-day administration.
6. **One-year terms.** Members, and DRSCs, are constituted for a year, which limits continuity (verify the trend of reappointment).
7. **Referral is not compulsory.** A Bill goes to a committee only if the House or presiding officer so decides. Commentators and research bodies such as PRS Legislative Research have reported that the share of Bills referred to committees has fallen in recent years; the extracts give no figures, so use this only as a qualitative, dated point after verifying (verify).

## Are committees 'mini-Parliaments'?

NCERT calls committees 'miniature legislatures'. The phrase works partly:

- **For:** Bills are studied clause by clause; witnesses are heard; members come from many parties in proportion (STV in financial committees); reports shape later debate.
- **Against:** a committee does not pass laws or sanction expenditure; it reports. Legally no Bill becomes law, and no budget is sanctioned, without Parliament's approval (NCERT). Its reports are advisory and the government may ignore them.
- **Balanced verdict:** committees are Parliament's working arms, extending its scrutiny; they are not a substitute for it.

## Accountability beyond committees

NCERT lists other devices: questions, motions, financial control through budget and audit, and the no-confidence motion (see GS-181 and GS-180). It warns that these work only if there is adequate time, interest and willingness to compromise; it notes a decline in sittings and in time spent on debates, absence of quorum and boycotts. That is why the verified 2021 question asks 'to what extent'.

## How to write with care

Do not quote statistics unless you can give the source and the year. Use 'reportedly', 'according to' and 'as of' with dated claims. Give both sides, then your reasoned judgement.

### 9. How UPSC tests committees: nine verified questions, patterns and the Mains frame

*Objective:* Map the nine verified questions to modules, recognise the Prelims traps and prepare a Mains frame with three task types.

## The nine verified questions

Prelims: **2018 Q20** (committee on delegated rules), **2019 Q69** (who reviews independent regulators), **2024 Q80** (Ethics Committee), **2026 Q59** (Committee on Welfare of SCs and STs). Mains: **2017 Q5** (PAC and accountability), **2018 Q4** (usefulness of committees and the Estimates Committee), **2021 Q4** (extent of Parliament's accountability of the Executive), **2021 Q15** (DRSCs), **2023 Q16** (structure of the committee system and the financial committees). The pyqNotes give concept, trap and approach for each; none gives an official key.

## Prelims traps

1. **Which House(s).** Estimates: only the Lok Sabha. Private Members' Bills and Absence of Members: Lok Sabha only. PAC and COPU: both Houses (15 + 7). DRSC: both (21 + 10). SC/ST Welfare and Women: 30 (20 + 10).
2. **Members versus chairman.** The PAC chairman is by convention from the Opposition; Estimates from the ruling party; COPU from Lok Sabha members only.
3. **Look-alike names.** Subordinate Legislation (delegated rules), Papers Laid (papers, not those rules), Government Assurances (promises), Offices of Profit (disqualification).
4. **Parliamentary versus consultative.** Consultative committees are not parliamentary committees.
5. **Ministers.** Ministers are barred only from the committees listed in Laxmikanth's note 1 (the financial committees, DRSCs, and the committees on Women, Assurances, Petitions, Subordinate Legislation and SC/ST welfare); do not extend the bar to every committee.
6. **Dates and numbers.** 1921, 1950, 1964; 17 in 1993, 24 in 2004; 31 = 21 + 10. Numbers are from the 2020 edition; committees are reconstituted yearly, so a current-year question may need the live list.
7. **Binding or advisory.** Reports are advisory.

## Mains frame: three task types

1. **Concept, 'Are committees mini-Parliaments?'** Define committee; give two functions (Bills clause by clause, scrutiny of budget); say NCERT's phrase; show limits (advisory; no law-making power); give a balanced conclusion.
2. **Institution, 'Role of PAC / Estimates / financial committees'.** Composition and chair tradition; function with the CAG link; limits (post-mortem, advisory); contribution to accountability and institutionalisation.
3. **Evaluation, 'Do DRSCs keep the administration on its toes?'** Origin and aim; structure; merits; limits; reform ideas (mandatory referral, resources, publicity) and a conclusion.

### 10. Guided practice: an MCQ thought aloud and a Mains skeleton

*Objective:* Apply the lesson to a worked MCQ and a Mains skeleton and set up your error log and delayed retention.

## Worked MCQ (thought aloud)

**Q.** Consider the following statements about parliamentary committees:
1. The Estimates Committee consists of members of the Lok Sabha only.
2. A Minister can be a member of the Public Accounts Committee if he is from the Rajya Sabha.
3. The Committee on Public Undertakings has its chairman appointed from among the Rajya Sabha members.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct? (A) 1 only (B) 1 and 2 only (C) 2 and 3 only (D) 1, 2 and 3.

**Thinking.** Statement 1: the Estimates Committee has 30 members, all from the Lok Sabha (module 4). Correct. Statement 2: a minister cannot be elected a member of the PAC at all (module 3), whichever House he belongs to. Wrong. Statement 3: the COPU chairman is appointed from Lok Sabha members only (module 4). Wrong. So only statement 1 is correct: option **A**. The method: attach each statement to a fact card (strength, House, chairman, minister rule).

## Mains skeleton: 'Evaluate the working of Departmentally Related Standing Committees' (15 marks, 250 words)

1. **Introduction:** 1993, 17 committees, 24 from 2004; aim: accountability of the Executive and better budget debate.
2. **Structure:** 31 members, 21 + 10, ministers barred, one-year term, 8 under the Rajya Sabha and 16 under the Lok Sabha.
3. **Functions:** demands for grants, Bills, annual reports, long-term policy documents.
4. **Strengths:** close, continuous, non-partisan scrutiny; experts heard; the Opposition and the Rajya Sabha involved.
5. **Limits:** advisory, not binding; no day-to-day administration; Bills reach them only on referral (verify trends).
6. **Way forward:** mandatory referral of major Bills, adequate research staff, reports followed up, publicity; conclusion.

### Notes: Classification of parliamentary committees (Laxmikanth)

- Standing committees (permanent, constituted every year or periodically): six categories.
- 1. Financial: Public Accounts, Estimates, Public Undertakings.
- 2. Departmental standing committees: 24.
- 3. To inquire: Petitions, Privileges, Ethics.
- 4. To scrutinise and control: Government Assurances, Subordinate Legislation, Papers Laid on the Table, Welfare of SCs and STs, Empowerment of Women, Joint Committee on Offices of Profit.
- 5. Day-to-day business: Business Advisory, Private Members' Bills and Resolutions, Rules, Absence of Members.
- 6. House-keeping: General Purposes, House, Library, Joint Committee on Salaries and Allowances of Members.
- Ad hoc committees: inquiry committees (e.g. joint committees on Bofors, stock market scam, telecom spectrum) and advisory committees (select or joint committees on Bills).

### Notes: Process: how a Bill reaches a select or joint committee

- Step 1: The Bill comes up for general discussion in a House.
- Step 2: A member moves a motion to refer it to a Select Committee (that House) or a Joint Committee (both Houses); the motion is adopted.
- Step 3: For a joint committee the other House is asked to nominate members.
- Step 4: The committee considers the Bill clause by clause, may amend, and may take evidence of associations, public bodies and experts.
- Step 5: It submits its report to the House; dissenting members may append minutes of dissent.

### Notes: Accountability devices around committees (NCERT)

- Financial control: the legislature can refuse resources, discuss reasons for demands and enquire into misuse of funds on the basis of CAG and PAC reports.
- No-confidence motion: the most powerful device; real when the government lacks majority support, as after 1989 coalitions showed.
- Conditions for control: adequate time, interested members, willingness to compromise; NCERT reports declining sittings and debate time.
- Committees reduce the burden on the House but Parliament remains the legal decision-maker.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Consultative committee (chaired by a minister, informal) is not a parliamentary committee.
- Subordinate Legislation (delegated rules) is not Papers Laid on the Table (papers).
- Estimates (economy in estimates) is not PAC (CAG audit of accounts).
- A select committee (one House) is not a joint committee (both Houses); both on a Bill are ad hoc.
- Standing means permanent and continuous; DRSCs are reconstituted every year but remain standing committees.

### Core concepts

- Committees give Parliament time, expertise and detail; NCERT calls them miniature legislatures.
- A parliamentary committee passes the four-part test; consultative committees do not.
- Standing committees are permanent; ad hoc committees are temporary; joint committees have members of both Houses.
- The three financial committees divide the field: accounts (PAC), estimates (Estimates), undertakings (COPU).
- Committee recommendations are advisory; Parliament decides.

### Practice

**Q.** What are the four conditions that make a committee a parliamentary committee?

**A.** Appointed or elected by the House or nominated by the Speaker or Chairman; working under their direction; presenting its report to the House or the presiding officer; and having a secretariat provided by the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha.

**Q.** What is the difference between a standing and an ad hoc committee?

**A.** A standing committee is permanent and works continuously; an ad hoc committee is temporary and ends when its task is finished.

**Q.** Name the three financial committees.

**A.** Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee and Committee on Public Undertakings.

**Q.** Why are ministers barred from many committees?

**A.** Because the committees scrutinise the executive; a minister cannot judge his own government.

**Q.** Why is the PAC called a post-mortem body?

**A.** It examines accounts of money already spent, so it can recommend but cannot undo the spending.

**Q.** Why do DRSCs examine demands for grants before the House votes?

**A.** So that the House can debate the budget with detailed, expert-informed reports rather than rushed discussion.

**Q.** A report criticising a ministry's spending is placed before the House by the PAC. Can the PAC order the ministry to recover the money?

**A.** No; its recommendations are advisory, and only Parliament can take a final decision on its findings.

**Q.** A Bill is referred to a Joint Committee. Name two things the committee can do.

**A.** Consider it clause by clause and take evidence from experts and public bodies, then report with any dissent.
