### Bridge: A medium, a wave and relative velocity

A **wave** is a disturbance that travels without carrying the material along. Sound travels through air, ripples through water: in each case the material is the **medium**. The wave speed is fixed *relative to the medium*. If the air is still, sound moves at about 340 m s⁻¹ in every direction.

Now add a wind. Suppose a wind blows at 20 m s⁻¹ and you stand on the ground. Sound going with the wind reaches you at 340 + 20 = 360 m s⁻¹, sound going against it at 340 − 20 = 320 m s⁻¹. A boat that points straight across a river is carried downstream, so to land directly opposite the rower must aim a little *upstream*. This everyday rule is the **Galilean addition of velocities** (PH-001 module 2): relative speed = wave speed ± medium speed along the line of motion.

Nineteenth-century physicists reasoned the same way about light. If light is a wave it needs a medium (they called it the **ether**), and a laboratory moving through the ether should feel an 'ether wind' and measure light speeds slightly above or below c, exactly as the boat and the sound do. The Michelson–Morley experiment tests that picture. Notation here: c is the speed of light relative to the ether, v the speed of the apparatus relative to the ether, β = v/c (a pure number).

### Bridge: Interference, path difference and counting fringes

Two beams of the same wavelength λ that start together and later meet add up. If one has travelled **a whole number of wavelengths** more than the other, the crests meet crests and the spot is bright; if the extra path is **an odd number of half wavelengths**, crest meets trough and the spot is dark (Mathur §3.3). The extra distance is the **path difference** Δ.

In an interferometer the beams are slightly divergent, so different points of the field have slightly different Δ and you see alternating bright and dark **fringes**. If something changes Δ by exactly one wavelength, every bright fringe moves into the place of the next bright one: the pattern has shifted by **one fringe**. Hence

$$ fringe shift N = (change in path difference) / λ $$

Check the dimension: metre divided by metre is a pure number, as a count must be. For λ = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m, a change of 2.2 × 10⁻⁷ m in Δ is N = 0.4 of a fringe: the pattern moves two-fifths of the gap between fringes. Sensitivity matters: Mathur says the apparatus could measure about one-hundredth of a fringe (Δ about 5 nm), which is why an expected shift of 0.4 was a 'large' signal.

### Bridge: The binomial approximation used in the derivation

The derivation produces expressions such as 1/(1 − β²) and 1/√(1 − β²) with β² = 10⁻⁸. Evaluating them directly on a calculator needs more digits than the display holds, so we expand. The **binomial series** says (1 − x)ⁿ = 1 − nx + n(n − 1)x²/2 − ..., and when x is tiny only the first two terms matter.

- n = −1: 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + ... ≈ 1 + x.
- n = −1/2: 1/√(1 − x) = 1 + x/2 + 3x²/8 + ... ≈ 1 + x/2.

Put x = β² = 10⁻⁸: 1/(1 − β²) ≈ 1.00000001 and 1/√(1 − β²) ≈ 1.000000005. Each is almost 1, and **the physics is in the tiny difference**: (1 + x) − (1 + x/2) = x/2 = 5 × 10⁻⁹. Two traps follow. First, drop only terms smaller than the one you keep: the next terms are of order x² = 10⁻¹⁶. Second, never subtract the unexpanded numbers on a pocket calculator; the difference is lost in rounding. The first-order result is accurate to about one part in 10⁸ here (relative error 1.25 × 10⁻⁸; Python: exact N = 0.4000000050 against 0.4000000000).

### 2. Foundation: the ether idea and what it predicted

*Objective:* Explain why the ether was proposed, what an ether wind would do to light speeds, and why the effect is of second order in v/c.

## Why a medium for light

Maxwell (1864, per Mathur §3.2) showed that electric and magnetic fields obey wave equations whose speed comes out as the speed of light; light is an electromagnetic wave. Every wave then known (sound, water) needed a material medium, so a medium for light, the **luminiferous ether**, was assumed to fill all space. To carry a *transverse* wave at such a speed the ether had to behave like an extremely stiff solid, and yet planets moved through it without resistance (Mathur's remark that this looks 'utterly incredible'). It was nevertheless attractive because it would supply Newton's **absolute frame**: a frame at rest in the ether, in which light has the same speed c in all directions and in which Newton's laws would hold most simply (Mathur §3.2; Upadhyaya §6.6).

## What Galilean addition predicts

Take the ether frame as the one where light speed is c. For the Earth, which orbits the Sun at about 30 km s⁻¹ (Mathur: 150 million km radius), v ≈ 3 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹ even if the Sun were at rest in the ether, so

$$ β = v/c = 3 × 10⁴ / 3 × 10⁸ = 10⁻⁴,  β² = 10⁻⁸ $$

Light along the motion should travel at c − v one way and c + v on return; across the motion the speed relative to the apparatus is smaller than c by a different amount (module 4). Upadhyaya adds a six-month argument (module 6).

## Why a clever instrument was needed

One-way light speeds cannot be timed over a laboratory distance to a part in 10⁴. Michelson's idea was to let one beam travel along the wind and one across it and compare the **round trips** by interference. The first-order terms in β cancel in a round trip (c − v and c + v average out) and the surviving difference is proportional to **β² = 10⁻⁸**. That tiny number is multiplied by the large ratio 2D/λ (about 4 × 10⁷), which is why the arm was lengthened by repeated reflections.

## Three pictures the experiment could test

| Picture | Light speed in the lab | Predicted shift |
|---|---|---|
| Stationary ether | c ± v along the wind, reduced across it | about 0.4 fringe for the original apparatus |
| Ether dragged by the Earth | c in all lab directions | none |
| Emission (light inherits the source's speed) | c relative to the source, which is fixed to the apparatus | none |

Module 7 shows how the 'none' pictures fared elsewhere. Only the first picture predicted the shift, and it is the one the null result refutes.

### 3. Core: the interferometer and why only a rotation can be read

*Objective:* Describe the arrangement of the interferometer from a diagram, state the purpose of each component, and explain why the observation is the shift on rotation.

## The parts (Mathur §3.3, Upadhyaya §6.7)

A source S gives monochromatic light, a parallel beam that falls on a thin glass plate P, half-silvered on its back face, set at 45° to the beam. At P the beam divides into two beams of nearly equal intensity at right angles: one goes straight on to mirror M2, the other is reflected to mirror M1. Each returns along its own path, and at P they are recombined and pass to a telescope T, where fringes are seen. The mirrors are silvered on the **front** face (to avoid multiple reflections inside glass) and set at nearly equal distances D from P. A **compensating plate** (Mathur's P2), identical to P and parallel to it, is placed in one path because the beam to M1 crosses the glass thrice and the beam to M2 only once; the compensator makes the glass paths equal so the comparison concerns the ether wind, not the glass.

```
                 M1
                 |
                 |  D  (perpendicular arm, along y)
                 |
   S --------->  P  ------------------ M2
                 |          D  (parallel arm, along x)
                 |
                 T   (telescope, sees the recombined beams)

   apparatus moves with velocity v along +x, i.e. the 'ether wind'
   blows from M2 towards P as seen in the apparatus.
```

## Fringes and the role of rotation

If the arms were exactly equal and the beam exactly parallel, the beams would reach T in phase and the field would be bright; with a slightly divergent beam you see alternating bright and dark fringes (module B2). In practice one cannot make two arms equal to better than a fraction of a wavelength, so **the absolute path difference is unknown and unimportant**. What matters is how it *changes*. The ether theory predicts that the wind lengthens the optical path of whichever arm lies along it, so turning the table through 90° exchanges the roles of the arms and moves the fringes. The table floats on mercury and turns slowly (Mathur: about 10 rotations per hour) so that it turns smoothly without strain and the observer watches the fringes while it turns.

## The reading to look for

If a stationary ether existed, the fringe position would vary as the table turned, with a maximum displacement of N fringes between two positions 90° apart. No such variation was found at the sensitivity of about 0.01 fringe (module 6). Repeating at different times of day and year matters because the Earth's velocity relative to the ether, if any, changes as the Earth rotates and orbits (Upadhyaya's six-month argument, module 2).

## Common slips in the description

- Saying 'the beams are in phase so the field is dark' (in phase means bright).
- Forgetting the compensating plate, or saying it compensates for the ether (it compensates for glass thickness).
- Counting the distance D as the physical size of the table rather than the effective arm length.

### 4. Core: deriving the two round-trip times (derivation 1, part one)

*Objective:* Derive t∥ and t⊥ for a stationary ether with every algebra step, by two routes for the perpendicular arm, and check them.

## Assumptions and frame

Work in the ether frame E: light travels at c in every direction. The apparatus moves at +v along x (notation of module 1). Source, plate P and both mirrors move with it. Arms have equal length D. Light is a signal moving at c in E; mirrors reflect it instantly.

## Parallel arm (along x), ether-frame method

*Outward leg.* Light leaves P towards M2 while M2 runs away at v. If the leg takes t₁, the light covers the arm length plus the distance M2 moved:

$$ c t₁ = D + v t₁  ⟹  t₁ = D/(c − v) $$

*Return leg.* Now P runs towards the light, so the light covers the arm length minus the distance P moved: c t₂ = D − v t₂, so t₂ = D/(c + v).

*Total.* Put over a common denominator:

$$ t∥ = D/(c − v) + D/(c + v) = D[(c + v) + (c − v)]/(c² − v²) = 2Dc/(c² − v²) = (2D/c)/(1 − β²) $$

Mathur reaches the same result in the apparatus frame by saying the relative light speed is c − v out and c + v back, the Galilean rule of module B1. Both routes agree because Galilean addition is only a change of frame.

## Perpendicular arm (along y), ether-frame method

```
             M1' (where the mirror is on arrival)
            *
           /|
     c t₁ / | D
         /  |
        P---P'      P' = P + (v t₁) along x
        |<->|
        v t₁
```

While light goes from P to the mirror, the whole apparatus drifts a distance v t₁ along x. In E the light therefore travels the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides D (across) and v t₁ (along x). Pythagoras (Upadhyaya, Fig. 6.3):

$$ (c t₁)² = D² + (v t₁)²  ⟹  t₁² (c² − v²) = D²  ⟹  t₁ = D/√(c² − v²) $$

On the return leg the plate P moves a further v t₂ along +x, so the light again travels a forward-slanting hypotenuse: (c t₂)² = D² + (v t₂)², giving t₂ = t₁. The total is

$$ t⊥ = 2D/√(c² − v²) = (2D/c)/√(1 − β²) $$

*Apparatus-frame check (Mathur).* To go straight across the apparatus, light must also cancel the wind: its component along x equals v and its component along y is √(c² − v²), so the cross speed relative to the apparatus is √(c² − v²) each way, giving the same t⊥.

## Checks

- **Dimensions:** D/c has units of time, so both results are times.
- **Limit v → 0:** t∥ → 2D/c and t⊥ → 2D/c: equal, as for an apparatus at rest in the ether.
- **Ordering:** for 0 < β < 1, 1/(1 − β²) > 1/√(1 − β²), so t∥ > t⊥ even though both beams travel arms of equal length: up-and-back along a stream always takes longer than across-and-back, the rower's rule.

### 5. Core: path difference, rotation through 90° and the factor of 2 (derivation 1, part two)

*Objective:* Obtain N = 2Dβ²/λ with the convention for D, explain exactly where the 2 comes from, and extend to an arm at any angle.

## Path difference before rotation

The optical path difference in orientation A (arm 1 parallel, arm 2 perpendicular) is the distance light covers in the time difference:

$$ Δ_A = c(t∥ − t⊥) = 2D[1/(1 − β²) − 1/√(1 − β²)] $$

Use the binomial approximations of bridge B3, 1/(1 − β²) ≈ 1 + β² and 1/√(1 − β²) ≈ 1 + β²/2:

$$ Δ_A ≈ 2D[(1 + β²) − (1 + β²/2)] = 2D(β²/2) = Dβ² $$

This agrees with Mathur (Δt = D v²/c³, so c Δt = D v²/c²) and Upadhyaya (eq. 3, path difference l v²/c²).

## After rotation

Turn the table through 90°. Arm 2 is now parallel and arm 1 perpendicular, so the roles are swapped and the sign of the difference reverses: Δ_B = −Dβ². The **change** in path difference is

$$ Δ_A − Δ_B = Dβ² − (−Dβ²) = 2Dβ² $$

and the fringe shift (module B2) is

$$ N = 2Dβ²/λ = 2Dv²/(λc²) $$

**Where the 2 comes from:** from the *reversal* of the sign on rotation. The round-trip factor 2 is already inside t∥ and t⊥ (the 2D/c) and was used up when Δ_A became Dβ² (the 2D times the β²/2 left by the binomial difference).

## Convention for D and the usual trap

D is the length of one arm. Some books and questions say 'effective length of each *path*'. Mathur's Example 2 ('effective length of each path be 5 m') uses D = 5 in N = 2Dv²/c²λ. If a question gave the round-trip length L = 2D of each beam, then N = Lβ²/λ. State the convention you use.

A turn of only 45° takes the path difference from Dβ² to zero (angle formula below), a shift Dβ²/λ, half the 90° value.

## Exact result and size of the approximation

Exactly, N = (4D/λ)[1/(1 − β²) − 1/√(1 − β²)]. For D = 11 m, λ = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m, β = 10⁻⁴, Python gives 0.4000000050 against 0.4000000000: the binomial step is safe.

## An arm at an arbitrary angle (standard extension, derived here)

Let the arm direction make angle θ with v. Let light move at speed w relative to the apparatus along the arm (outward). In E its velocity is w n + v with |velocity| = c:

$$ w² + 2wv cos θ + v² = c² $$

The outward speed is w₁ = −v cos θ + √(c² − v² sin²θ) and, for the return direction −n, w₂ = v cos θ + √(c² − v² sin²θ). Their product is c² − v² (the sin² and cos² terms add to 1), so

$$ t(θ) = D(w₁ + w₂)/(w₁w₂) = (2D/c) √(1 − β² sin²θ)/(1 − β²) $$

(θ = 0 reproduces t∥, θ = 90° reproduces t⊥.) For small β, t(θ) ≈ (2D/c)(1 + β² − (β²/2) sin²θ). The second arm is at θ + 90° and has sin² replaced by cos²:

$$ c[t(θ) − t(θ + 90°)] ≈ Dβ² (cos²θ − sin²θ) = Dβ² cos 2θ $$

Python agrees to better than 0.01 per cent at β = 10⁻³. So the fringe position varies as cos 2θ with the table angle: maximum swing from θ = 0 to θ = 90° is 2Dβ², and the pattern repeats every 180°.

### 6. Core: the expected number, the null result and an upper limit

*Objective:* Recompute the expected fringe shift for the source numbers, state the null result honestly, and find the wind speed it excludes.

## The expected shift for the original apparatus

With D = 11 m, λ = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m, v = 3 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹, c = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹:

$$ β² = 10⁻⁸,  N = 2 × 11 × 10⁻⁸/(5.5 × 10⁻⁷) = 2.2 × 10⁻⁷/5.5 × 10⁻⁷ = 0.40 $$

Intermediate values (Python): path difference before rotation Dβ² = 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ m, which is 0.2 λ, and the 90° change 2.2 × 10⁻⁷ m is 0.4 λ.

Printed values recomputed (full table in the master notes): Mathur 0.4, 0.2 and 0.24 are exact; his 0.37 for the cgs data is 0.367 correctly rounded; Upadhyaya's 0.4 for 5800 Å is 0.379 rounded to one figure, his 0.067 and 0.1 are right, and his 0.201 is 0.200.

**Slips noticed:** Mathur's Example 1 calls the path difference Δx 'the fringe shift' (the shift is Δx/λ); Upadhyaya's Example 4 prints 0.201 where the algebra gives exactly 0.200; Upadhyaya prints 1880 for the date (Mathur 1887). Using c = 2.998 × 10⁸ instead of 3 × 10⁸ changes 0.400 to 0.4005, so quote two figures.

## Six months later

If the apparatus happened to be at rest in the ether on one date, then six months later its speed is about 2v = 6 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹ (Upadhyaya). N ∝ v², so the expected shift becomes 4 × 0.40 = 1.6 fringes (1.52 for 5800 Å). The coincidence argument therefore fails; both dates were tested. Upadhyaya (p. 333) reports that no fringe shift was observed, and that the repeat six months later again showed nothing.

## The null result

Mathur reports the observed shift as 'much too small, almost negligible', and the experiment was repeated underground, at altitude, in different seasons and with laser light, always null. The extracts give no tabulated readings, so no reading is quoted here. Commonly quoted (verify): the observed displacement was a small fraction of the predicted 0.4, of the order of the instrumental limit of about 0.01 fringe.

## An upper limit on the wind

Assume only that no shift larger than 0.01 fringe (the stated sensitivity) was seen, with D = 11 m and λ = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m. Since N = 2Dv²/(λc²), the largest v allowed is

$$ v_max = c √(Nλ/2D) = 3 × 10⁸ × √(0.01 × 5.5 × 10⁻⁷/22) = 3 × 10⁸ × 1.58 × 10⁻⁵ = 4.7 × 10³ m s⁻¹ $$

That is about 4.7 km s⁻¹, far below the 30 km s⁻¹ expected. Equivalent check: 0.01/0.40 = 1/40, so v ≤ 30/√40 km s⁻¹ = 4.7 km s⁻¹. This is an illustration of the *method*; the historical bound depended on the actual readings (not in the extracts).

## Interpretation

Upadhyaya concludes that the speed of light relative to the apparatus is c regardless of the Earth's motion; module 8 states what is and is not proved.

### 7. Core: the attempted explanations and the contraction factor (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Explain ether drag, the FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction (with the derivation of its factor) and the emission theory, and say why each was or was not satisfactory.

## 1. Ether drag

Michelson himself suggested that the Earth drags the ether in its neighbourhood, so there is no relative motion at the laboratory. Mathur reports that Lodge (1892) measured light near rapidly rotating bodies and found that at most about half a per cent of c could be communicated to the ether, so a dragged ether could not explain the null result. Two standard objections are not in the extracts and should be flagged (verify): full drag conflicts with stellar aberration (PH-030 module 7), and Fizeau's experiment on light in moving water showed only *partial* drag with the factor 1 − 1/n² (PH-030 module 8), which the relativistic addition law reproduces with no ether.

## 2. The FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction (derivation)

Mathur (PDF p. 126) says FitzGerald and Lorentz proposed in 1892 (commonly cited: FitzGerald 1889, Lorentz 1892; verify) that a body moving through the ether at speed v shortens in the direction of motion. Ask what contraction factor k (length = k × rest length) would remove the shift. Keep the perpendicular arm D unchanged and shorten the parallel arm to kD. The derivation of module 4 holds with D replaced by kD in the parallel arm:

$$ t∥ = 2kD/[c(1 − β²)],   t⊥ = 2D/[c√(1 − β²)] $$

Set t∥ = t⊥:

$$ k/(1 − β²) = 1/√(1 − β²)  ⟹  k = (1 − β²)/√(1 − β²) = √(1 − β²) = 1/γ $$

This matches Mathur's check: replacing D by D√(1 − v²/c²) in t gives the same time as t′. It also removes the shift for any orientation of the table, since after rotation the other arm is the parallel one and contracts instead.

*Size.* Required shortening of the parallel arm for D = 11 m, β = 10⁻⁴: D(1 − √(1 − β²)) ≈ Dβ²/2 = 5.5 × 10⁻⁸ m = 55 nm, about 0.1 λ. For comparison, the Earth's diameter (1.27 × 10⁷ m) would shorten by about 6.4 cm. The effect is small but exactly the size needed.

*Objection (Mathur).* It was put forward only to rescue the ether, and did not follow from any theory. Mathur adds that the contraction factor is indeed √(1 − v²/c²), but for entirely different reasons: in relativity it is a consequence of the Lorentz transformation for observers in relative motion (PH-029 module 5), not a physical squeezing by an ether.

## 3. Emission (ballistic) theory (standard, verify)

If light is emitted with speed c *relative to its source*, a source fixed in the apparatus gives light that moves at c relative to the apparatus in all directions, so the null result is predicted without any ether. It is not in the extracts; it was later disfavoured by observations of fast-moving sources such as double stars (details to verify).

### 8. Master: the relativistic explanation, later experiments and what is proved

*Objective:* Show that special relativity gives equal times in every frame, distinguish it from the contraction hypothesis, and state what the experiment does and does not prove.

## Apparatus frame: equal times, no calculation needed

In the rest frame A of the apparatus the arms have their proper (rest) length D, and by the second postulate light has speed c in every direction (Mathur: 'a statement of the result of Michelson–Morley'; Upadhyaya: it follows from Maxwell's theory with the first postulate). Then

$$ t∥ = t⊥ = 2D/c $$

for every orientation, so the shift is zero and no ether is needed.

## Ether (laboratory) frame E: the same answer through contraction

In E the apparatus moves at v. By the second postulate light still has speed c in E, so the velocity-addition steps of module 4 remain valid. The new ingredient is PH-029 module 5: the arm along the motion is contracted to D/γ, while the perpendicular arm is not changed (PH-029 module 6). Then

$$ t∥ = 2(D/γ)/[c(1 − β²)] = 2γD/c,   t⊥ = 2D/[c√(1 − β²)] = 2γD/c $$

(using 1/γ × γ² = γ). The two times are equal in E too, and both equal γ times the apparatus-frame time 2D/c, which is time dilation.

## How relativity differs from the FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction

The *formula* for the contraction is the same, 1/γ, but its status differs. In the contraction hypothesis there is one true frame (the ether), an arm 'really' shrinks when moving through it, and an observer in that frame would see the effect. In relativity every inertial frame is equivalent, contraction is a relation between the measurements of two frames (reciprocal: each frame finds the other's rods shorter, PH-029 module 7), and the arm has no 'true' shortened length.

## Later experiments (standard knowledge, verify)

- **Kennedy–Thorndike (1932):** an interferometer with *deliberately unequal arms*, observed over months. With contraction alone, unequal arms L₁ and L₂ give a path difference 2(L₁ − L₂)γ that varies with the apparatus speed through the ether; a change of speed from β₂ to β₁ would shift fringes by (L₁ − L₂)(β₁² − β₂²)/λ (derived here with the binomial step). No such shift was seen, which indicates that the source frequency also changes (time dilation) so as to compensate.
- **Ives–Stilwell (1938):** the Doppler shift test of time dilation (PH-031 module 7).

Together they support the full Lorentz transformation, not merely a length contraction.

## What it does and does not show

*Does:* no variation of the round-trip light speed with direction at the 0.01-fringe level, which refutes a stationary ether with Galilean addition and no compensating effect. Ether drag, the contraction hypothesis and the emission theory also predict a null result, so they are judged by other evidence (Lodge, aberration, Fizeau, Kennedy-Thorndike, double stars), not by this experiment alone.

*Does not:* prove time dilation or the Lorentz transformation by itself; prove the one-way speed of light is isotropic (the experiment is a round-trip comparison); prove that no ether-like frame could exist if one adds compensating effects (that route is empirically equivalent but unnecessary); or show how much it influenced Einstein, which historians dispute.

## Derivation traps

- Using c ± v for the perpendicular arm (the cross speed is √(c² − v²) in the apparatus frame).
- Dropping the binomial terms inconsistently: the correct pair is 1 + β² (parallel) and 1 + β²/2 (perpendicular); using the same expansion for both gives Δ = 0, and dropping the β² term from t⊥ gives 2Dβ².
- Forgetting that rotation doubles the path difference, or doubling twice.
- Saying t⊥ > t∥ (it is shorter).

## Numerical traps

- Mixing units: D in cm with c in m s⁻¹; λ in Å (1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m). Convert first.
- Using v in km s⁻¹: 30 km s⁻¹ is 3 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹.

### Notes: Definitions

- Ether: the hypothetical medium for light, defining an absolute frame in which light has speed c in every direction.
- Ether wind: the relative flow of the ether past an apparatus moving through it, of speed v, with v/c = 10⁻⁴ for the Earth's orbital motion.

- Compensating plate: a glass plate identical to the beam splitter placed in one path so that both beams cross equal glass thickness.
- FitzGerald-Lorentz contraction: the ether-based hypothesis (Mathur dates it 1892; commonly FitzGerald 1889, Lorentz 1892; verify) that a body moving at v through the ether is shortened to √(1 − v²/c²) of its rest length along the motion.

      - Limit v → 0: N → 0.

  - - **id:** PH-027-D2
    - **task:** Find the FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction factor that removes the shift and show that relativity gives equal times in the ether frame as well. Practice target, not an official allotment: 10 marks about 12 minutes.
    - **steps:**
      - Let the parallel arm have length kD; the perpendicular arm D. Then t∥ = 2kD/[c(1 − β²)] and t⊥ = 2D/[c√(1 − β²)].
      - Set t∥ = t⊥: k/(1 − β²) = 1/√(1 − β²), so k = √(1 − β²) = 1/γ.
      - Relativistic route: in the ether frame light still has speed c and the parallel arm is D/γ (PH-029 module 5).
      - t∥ = 2(D/γ)/[c(1 − β²)] = 2γD/c, because (1/γ)γ² = γ; t⊥ = 2D/[c√(1 − β²)] = 2γD/c.
      - Both equal γ(2D/c): in the apparatus frame both are 2D/c and the time-dilation factor γ connects them.
      - Conclusion: the same factor 1/γ, but a relation between frames, not an ether squeeze.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimension: k dimensionless.
      - Limit v → 0: k → 1.
      - Numbers: D = 11 m, β = 10⁻⁴ gives a 55 nm shortening, about 0.1 λ.
    - **traps:**
      - Using γ instead of 1/γ for the contraction.
      - Contracting the perpendicular arm.
  - - **id:** PH-027-D3
    - **task:** Derive the round-trip time for an arm making angle θ with the apparatus velocity and the angular dependence of the path difference. Practice target, not an official allotment: 10 marks about 12 minutes.
    - **steps:**
      - Arm direction n at angle θ to v; light speed w relative to the apparatus along n; in the ether frame the velocity is wn + v with magnitude c.
      - w² + 2wv cos θ + v² = c², so w₁ = −v cos θ + √(c² − v² sin²θ) outward and w₂ = v cos θ + √(c² − v² sin²θ) back.
      - w₁w₂ = c² − v² sin²θ − v² cos²θ = c² − v².
      - t(θ) = D(w₁ + w₂)/(w₁w₂) = 2D√(c² − v² sin²θ)/(c² − v²) = (2D/c)√(1 − β² sin²θ)/(1 − β²).
      - Expand: t(θ) ≈ (2D/c)(1 + β² − (β²/2) sin²θ); for the other arm replace θ by θ + 90°, so sin² becomes cos².
      - c[t(θ) − t(θ + 90°)] ≈ Dβ²(cos²θ − sin²θ) = Dβ² cos 2θ; between θ = 0 and 90° the change is 2Dβ².
    - **checks:**
      - θ = 0 gives t∥; θ = 90° gives t⊥.
      - Python check: agreement to better than 0.01 per cent at β = 10⁻³.
      - The pattern period in θ is 180°.
    - **traps:**
      - Taking w = c − v cos θ in both directions.
      - Expanding without keeping the (1 − β²) denominator.
