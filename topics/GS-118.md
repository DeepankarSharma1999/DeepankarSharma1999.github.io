## GS-118: Lakes, wetlands and water bodies

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Bridge: the water balance of a hollow

A **lake** is water standing in a hollow. Whether the hollow stays full depends on a simple balance: **water in** (rain on the lake, rivers, springs and underground seepage) against **water out** (a river leaving the lake, evaporation into the air, and seepage into the ground). If in is greater than out the level rises, if out is greater it falls, and if they match the lake stays steady. Think of a bath with the tap on and the plug partly open.

**Evaporation** is the change of liquid water into vapour. It is fastest where the air is hot and dry. A **basin of inland drainage** is a region whose rivers do not reach the sea: they end in a lake or in a dry salt flat. In such a basin the only exit for water is evaporation, and evaporation takes water away but leaves the dissolved salt behind. Over many years the salt builds up and the lake becomes **saline** (salty). A lake with an outflowing river carries its salts away and stays **fresh**.

Use this bath picture for every lake in this lesson: first ask what made the hollow, then ask what keeps it full and whether it has an exit.

### Bridge: Bridge: treaty, convention, party, signatory and COP

A **treaty** (also called a **convention**, a word used for many environmental agreements) is a written agreement between countries that binds those who join. A **party** or **contracting party** is a country that has joined. A country joins in two usual ways: it **signs** the text and later **ratifies** it (formally confirms it at home), or it **accedes**, which means joining directly after the treaty was adopted. A **signatory** is a country that has signed. The date a text was *adopted* is not the date a particular country joined: the Ramsar Convention was adopted in 1971, and PMF IAS gives India's joining year as 1982.

A **COP** is the **Conference of the Parties** (for Ramsar, the Conference of the Contracting Parties): the meeting of all the parties that reviews progress and adopts decisions. Do not confuse a COP with a party. A **depositary** is the body that keeps the treaty text and records who has joined; PMF names UNESCO as the Ramsar depositary. An **observer** may attend a COP but cannot vote.

Keep this vocabulary on one card: adopt, sign, ratify or accede, party, COP, depositary, observer.

### 1. Orientation: what a lake is and the six ways a hollow is made

*Objective:* Define a lake, tell lakes from ponds, seas and reservoirs, and name the six families of lake origin that organise the lesson.

## Meaning

Leong describes lakes as the hollows of the land surface in which water accumulates, and notes that they vary tremendously in size, shape, depth and mode of formation (PDF p. 118). Tiny ones are no bigger than ponds or pools; large ones are so extensive that they are called seas. Leong's example is the **Caspian Sea**, 1 223 km long, as much as 980 m deep, with a total area of 373 230 sq km (as in the book; verify: maximum depth is usually given as about 1 025 m and the area varies). A lake that fills a small depression after a heavy shower is temporary; a lake that holds more water than evaporation can remove is permanent. Most of the world's lakes are **fresh-water** lakes fed by rivers and with outflowing streams, for example Lake Geneva, Lake Poyang and the Great Lakes of North America.

## Lakes do not last for ever

Leong stresses that lakes are temporary features of the Earth's surface: they are eliminated by **draining** (the outflowing river cuts its outlet lower) and by **silting up** (sediment fills the hollow). In areas of unreliable rainfall they dry up in the dry season, and in hot deserts they vanish by evaporation, percolation and outflow. When a lake dies, its bed becomes fertile alluvium; module 6 gives examples.

## Words to separate

- **Pond:** a small, usually shallow, standing water body; a **lake** is larger and in a natural hollow (general usage; Leong says only that small lakes are no bigger than ponds or pools).
- **Reservoir:** water held behind a dam (module 5). **Lagoon:** shallow water cut off from the sea by a bar or spit (module 5). **Wetland:** a wider class (module 8).

## The six families

Leong sorts lake origins into six groups (PDF pp. 118-123). Learn the hollow-maker for each.

| Family | Hollow-maker | Example (Leong) |
|---|---|---|
| Earth movement (tectonic) | Warping, faulting, rift valley | Caspian Sea, Lake Titicaca, Lake Tanganyika |
| Glaciation | Ice scouring, ice dams, melted ice blocks | Red Tarn, Lake Ullswater, Lake Windermere |
| Volcanic activity | Crater, caldera, lava dam, collapsed lava crust | Crater Lake (Oregon), Lake Toba |
| Erosion | Solution of limestone or salt; wind scooping | Karst lakes, Qattara Depression |
| Deposition | River loops, coastal bars, landslide dams | Ox-bow lakes, lagoons, Lake Gormire |
| Human and biological | Dams, mining, beavers | Lake Mead, Volta Lake |

Leong also warns that in a few cases a lake has more than one cause (PDF p. 118).

### 2. Foundation: fresh and saline lakes and basins of inland drainage

*Objective:* Explain why some lakes are saline, quote Leong's salinity figures with their caveats, and test the rule on edge cases.

## The rule

Use bridge B1. Leong says that in regions of low rainfall and intense evaporation, where few rivers are strong enough to reach the sea, streams drain into a lake and form a **basin of inland drainage**; because of the intense evaporation these lakes are **saline** (PDF p. 118). Deserts commonly have salt lakes, called **playas** (the dry salt flat that remains when the water has gone is also called a playa).

## Leong's numbers (as in the book; verify)

Salinity is measured in **parts per thousand** (per mille, grams of salt in each kilogram of water; see Day 18 lesson GS-109). Leong gives: the **Dead Sea** about 250 parts per thousand, the **Great Salt Lake** of Utah about 220, but the **Black Sea**, into which many large rivers drain, less than 17. Leong's figures are dated and vary with water level; remember the order (Dead Sea highest, Black Sea lowest) and the reason, not the digits. For the ocean's own figure see Day 18 lesson GS-109; the Black Sea is low because of heavy river inflow.

## Lake Chad, a worked example

Leong's example of a lake of inland drainage is **Lake Chad**, north-east of Nigeria. It receives rivers (the Yobe, the Yedseram, the Ngadda and the Shari, which is 1 120 km long) but none flows out. The rain and river inflow is not enough to make up for evaporation, so its size varies with the dry and wet seasons and it has shrunk so much that some geographers call it a big swamp (PDF p. 118).

## Test the rule on edge cases

1. **Dead Sea:** fed by a river, no outlet, hot dry air: saline. The rule fits.
2. **Great Salt Lake:** no outlet, high evaporation: saline. Fits.
3. **Black Sea:** many rivers, an outlet to the sea: low salinity. Fits the 'rivers dilute' side.
4. **Lake Chad:** no surface outflow, yet Leong does not call it saline. Many accounts say some water leaves by underground seepage and carries salt away (standard knowledge; verify), so the no-outlet condition is not fully met. So: *a missing surface outlet is not enough; there must be no surface or underground outlet.*
5. **A lake with an outflow in a dry climate:** the river carries salts away and the lake stays comparatively fresh.

Take-away rule: **saline lake = no surface or underground outlet, so evaporation is the only way water leaves and salt accumulates over a long time.** If evaporation also exceeds inflow, the lake shrinks as well.

### 3. Core: tectonic, rift valley and volcanic lakes

*Objective:* Describe how earth movements and volcanic activity make lakes and give Leong's world examples with the book's caveats.

## Tectonic lakes

When the crust is warped, bent, sagged or fractured, a **tectonic depression** forms and can hold lakes of immense size and depth (Leong, PDF p. 119). Examples: **Lake Titicaca**, in a huge depression of the intermont plateau of the Andes at 3 810 m (12 500 feet) above sea level, which Leong calls the highest lake in the world (usually stated as the highest large navigable lake: check); the **Caspian Sea**, the largest lake, almost five times larger than its nearest rival, Lake Superior (Leong); and **Lake Chad** in a depression in West Africa. **Lake Victoria** lies in the depression between the eastern and western arms of the Rift Valley, but Leong says it is not itself a rift valley lake.

## Rift valley lakes

A **rift valley** is a long, narrow, deep trough formed when the land between two parallel **faults** (cracks along which rock blocks move) sinks. Water collects in the trough, and the floors are often below sea level (Leong's Fig. 9.1).

The best-known case is the **East African Rift Valley**, running through Zambia, Malawi, Tanzania, Kenya and Ethiopia and continuing along the Red Sea to Israel and Jordan, a total distance of 4 800 km (Leong). Its lakes include **Tanganyika** (1 430 m, 4 700 feet deep, which Leong calls the world's deepest; Lake Baikal is generally cited as deeper: check), **Malawi, Rudolf, Edward and Albert**, and the **Dead Sea**, 392 m (1 286 feet) below mean sea level, which Leong calls the world's lowest lake (the figure is dated; verify).

## Volcanic lakes

Volcanic activity makes lakes in three ways (Leong, PDF pp. 120-121).

1. **Crater and caldera lakes.** A volcanic explosion may blow off the top of a cone, leaving a hollow called a **crater**, bounded by steep cliffs and roughly circular. In a dormant or extinct volcano rain falls straight into the crater or caldera; with no outlet, a lake forms. Leong's examples: **Crater Lake** in Oregon (a caldera), **Lake Toba** in northern Sumatra and **Lake Avernus** near Naples. A caldera is generally a larger, collapsed basin; Leong does not separate the two terms (standard distinction; verify). Leong (dated) calls the origin of **Lake Bosumtwi** in Ghana uncertain, a meteorite or a caldera; it is now generally accepted as a meteorite-impact crater (verify).
2. **Lava-blocked lakes.** A lava stream may flow across a valley, solidify and dam the river. Leong's example is the **Sea of Galilee** formed behind a lava flow in the Jordan Valley (other sources stress its rift setting: verify).
3. **Lakes from subsidence of a lava surface.** The crust of a hollow lava flow may collapse and leave a wide shallow depression, as at **Myvatn** in Iceland.

## Quick test

Ask: *Is the hollow long, narrow and steep-sided between faults?* Rift valley. *Circular with steep cliffs and no outlet?* Crater or caldera. *A valley dammed by hardened lava?* Lava-blocked.

### 4. Core: glacial and erosional lakes

*Objective:* Name the glacial lake types and the karst and wind-deflated lakes, with Leong's examples and a one-line hollow-maker for each.

## Glacial lakes (Leong, PDF p. 120)

Glaciers make hollows by scouring and dams by dropping debris. Glacial landforms themselves (cirque, moraine, drumlin, U-shaped trough) are taught in the Day 23 lesson GS-101; here only the lakes matter. Terms: **karst** is limestone country shaped by solution; a **drumlin** is a smooth oval hill of glacial debris; an **outwash plain** is the sand and gravel flat spread by melt-water in front of a glacier. Day 19 lesson GS-111 on glaciers explains how glaciers move.

1. **Cirque lakes or tarns.** A glacier leaves arm-chair-shaped hollows (cirques or corries) at valley heads. Water fills the over-deepened floor: **Red Tarn**, English Lake District. Cirque lakes are small, round and high in the mountains.
2. **Ribbon lakes.** Long, deep lakes in glacial troughs: **Lake Ullswater**.
3. **Kettle lakes.** Depressions in the outwash plain left by melting stagnant ice blocks. They are irregular and never of great size or depth: the **meres of Shropshire**, the kettle lakes of Orkney.
4. **Rock-hollow lakes.** Ice scours hollows in the rock. Finland is the Land of Lakes (Suomi): over 35 000 glacial lakes (as in the book; verify).
5. **Morainic-dammed lakes.** A glacier deposits debris (moraine) across a valley and water collects behind the barrier: **Lake Windermere**.
6. **Drift lakes.** In a drumlin landscape drainage is poor, the hollows between hills are waterlogged and form small lakes, as in County Down in Northern Ireland.

(Leong lists five lettered types, a to e; ribbon lakes appear under the first.)

## Erosional lakes (Leong, PDF pp. 121-122)

- **Karst lakes.** Rain water dissolves limestone and carves solution hollows. When these are clogged with debris, lakes form; collapse of a limestone cavern roof can expose a long narrow lake that was once underground (Lac de Chalillexon). Large depressions called **poljes** have no surface outlet and may hold lakes that shrink in dry weather; Leong's example is **Lake Scutari** in 'Yugoslavia' (the country name is dated).
- **Salt-solution lakes.** Beds of rock salt dissolved underground make the surface sink: many **meres of Cheshire**, England, also helped by salt mining.
- **Wind-deflated lakes.** Wind scoops out hollows in deserts down to the water table; water seeps out as shallow lakes that become salt lakes and playas by evaporation (module 2): the **Qattara Depression** in Egypt and the Great Basin of Utah.

### 5. Core: ox-bow lakes, lagoons, barrier lakes and human-made lakes

*Objective:* Separate ox-bow lakes, lagoons and tectonic lakes by hollow-maker, place and water type, and describe barrier lakes and human-made lakes.

## Lakes made by river deposits: the ox-bow

A **meander** is a bend of a river (Day 18 lesson GS-117). In a flood the river may shorten its course by cutting across the neck of a loop; the abandoned loop, shaped like a horseshoe, becomes an **ox-bow lake**. Leong's examples are the flood plains of the Lower Mississippi and the Rio Grande, Mexico (PDF p. 122). NCERT lists ox-bow lakes among the depositional features of the Himalayan rivers on the plains, together with flat valleys, flood plains, braided channels and deltas, and says the rivers show a strong meandering tendency and shift their courses frequently, with the Kosi as the standard example (kegy103, PDF p. 4).

## Lakes made by marine deposits: the lagoon

Winds and waves build **spits** or **bars** (ridges of sand, mud and shingle) that isolate shallow water along a coast. The enclosed shallow water is a **lagoon**. Because it is enclosed only by a narrow strip of land, a lagoon may drain at low tide. Leong says lagoons are commonly found off the deltas of large rivers such as the Nile and the Ganges; in East Germany and Poland they are called *haffs* (PDF p. 122). Day 18 lesson GS-102 (section on marine deposition, bars, spits and lagoons) explains the sand ridges.

## Barrier lakes

Landslides, screes or avalanches can block a valley and dam a river; the lake behind is a **barrier lake**. These lakes are short-lived: loose fragments give way under the water's pressure and the released water rushes down and causes floods. Leong's examples are Lake Gormire in Yorkshire (landslide) and Ffynnon Frech on Snowdon (scree) (PDF p. 122). Day 19 lesson GS-111 calls the burst of an ice- or debris-dammed lake a glacial lake outburst flood.

## Human-made and other lakes (Leong, PDF pp. 122-123)

- **Man-made lakes (reservoirs):** concrete dams across river valleys: **Lake Mead** above the Hoover Dam on the Colorado, the **Volta Lake** above the Akosombo Dam in Ghana.
- **Animal-made and other:** beaver dams (Beaver Lake, Yellowstone), ornamental lakes, lakes left by tin mining in West Malaysia and fish-culture lakes.

## The testable distinction: ox-bow, lagoon, tectonic

| Question | Ox-bow lake | Lagoon | Tectonic lake |
|---|---|---|---|
| What made the hollow? | River cutting off a meander loop | Bar or spit built by waves and wind | Warping or faulting of the crust |
| Where? | Flood plain of a meandering river | Coast, often off deltas | Plateau or rift valley |
| Shape | Horseshoe or crescent | Long, narrow, parallel to the shore | Rift lakes long, narrow, deep; sag-basin lakes such as Victoria broad and shallow (verify) |
| Water | Usually fresh | Brackish or saline | Fresh or saline |
| Leong example | Lower Mississippi | Off the Nile and Ganges deltas | Caspian Sea, Tanganyika |

Rule: *name the process, not the picture.* A crescent-shaped lake on a flood plain is an ox-bow (a river did it), not a rift lake.

### 6. Core: what lakes do for people - ten uses and the Indian dam examples

*Objective:* List Leong's ten uses of lakes in groups, explain why natural lakes steady river flow, and pair Indian projects with their rivers as the extracts state.

## Leong's ten uses, in four groups (PDF pp. 123-125)

**Transport and trade.** Large lakes carry bulky goods cheaply. The Great Lakes and St Lawrence waterways reach more than 2 740 km (1 700 miles) into the interior. Leong says the annual tonnage through the Sault Ste. Marie (Soo) Canal between Lakes Huron and Superior is more than that of the Suez and Panama Canals combined (an old comparison; verify).

**Water.** Lakes supply towns (Lake Thirlmere supplies Manchester). They also **regulate rivers**: a river with large lakes in its basin seldom floods badly or runs short, because the lake absorbs excess water in heavy rain and releases it in the dry season; lakes also keep back sediment, so the leaving river is clearer. Leong's cases are Poyang and Tung Ting on the Yangtze, Tonle Sap on a tributary of the Mekong and Lake Constance on the upper Rhine. Where lakes are absent, artificial reservoirs do the work, such as the Hoover Dam on the Colorado and the **Bhakra and Nangal dams on the Sutlej in India** (Leong).

**Energy and farming.** For hydroelectric power Leong says natural lakes are preferred to artificial reservoirs because the water flowing from them varies little through the year: the Niagara River from Lake Erie to Lake Ontario has a regular supply, but the Catawba River, which does not flow from a lake, has little water in the dry season. When a lake dries, its bed leaves fertile alluvium, as in the Vale of Pickering and the Red River Valley of Canada (the former Lake Agassiz). Multipurpose dams also supply irrigation.

**Climate, food, minerals, leisure.** Large deep lakes heat and cool more slowly than land and so moderate the climate (the eastern shores of Lakes Erie, Ontario and Huron have milder winters); small lakes have practically no effect. Lakes also give fish (sturgeon in the Caspian), minerals (Dead Sea salt, borax in the Mojave Desert) and tourism.

## Indian projects

The project-river pairs named in NCERT (Bhakra Nangal, Gandhisagar, Damodar Valley, Sardar Sarovar) are taught in Day 19 lesson GS-125, section 'Core: multipurpose projects and the rivers the extracts name'. Here keep Leong's point: the Bhakra and Nangal dams on the Satluj do the work of a lake in regulating flow. NCERT's sentence on the Gandhisagar dam can be read as placing it in Rajasthan; it is in Mandsaur district, Madhya Pradesh (verify).

### 8. Master: wetlands - definition, functions, threats and the 2010 and 2017 rules

*Objective:* State the 2017 definition and exclusions, list functions and threats, and explain how the 2017 rules differ from the 2010 rules as PMF presents them.

## What a wetland is

PMF quotes the Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules 2017: **wetlands** are areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, whether natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, with water that is static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt, including marine water of a depth not exceeding six metres at low tide (PDF p. 132). A **marsh** is waterlogged land with soft-stemmed plants, a **fen** a mineral-rich waterlogged peaty area and **peatland** a landscape of partly decayed plant matter (standard definitions; verify). So a lake can be a wetland, but a wetland need not be a lake.

**Exclusions.** The rules exclude river channels, paddy fields and human-made water bodies such as aquaculture ponds, salt-production pans, recreation and irrigation projects; protected areas under the Indian Forest Act 1927, the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972 and the Coastal Regulation Zone Notification 2019 cannot be notified under these rules (PDF p. 132). The rules apply to Ramsar sites and to wetlands notified by the Central Government or a State or UT administration.

## Functions (standard knowledge; verify)

The PMF extract is about law, so the usual functions are flagged: absorbing floodwater, recharging groundwater, trapping sediment and pollutants, supporting fish, waterbirds and biodiversity, and storing carbon.

## Threats

PMF's list of **prohibited activities** also maps the threats (PDF p. 133): conversion of wetland for non-wetland use; setting up or expanding industry; making, storing or dumping hazardous substances, construction waste and solid waste; discharge of untreated waste and effluents from industries, cities and villages; and encroachment, poaching or permanent construction (except boat jetties). NPCA funds desilting and the removal of invasive weeds such as water hyacinth, and shoreline protection (PDF p. 134).

## From the 2010 rules to the 2017 rules

PMF's account of the **limitations of the 2010 rules**: they did not recognise wetlands' role in livelihoods and domestic water needs; smaller wetlands (jheels, tanks, rural and urban water bodies) were left out; state or district authorities were not mandatory; local communities were not on the central authority; and monitoring was centralised (PDF p. 132). The 2017 rules, notified under the Environment (Protection) Act, **shifted control to states**:

| Feature | 2010 rules | 2017 rules |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Central Wetlands Regulatory Authority (CWRA) | State Wetland Authority in every State/UT, headed by the Environment Minister; National Wetland Committee (advisory) |
| Focus | Central control | State-level management and wise use |
| Inventory | Not covered in the extract | List of wetlands in 3 months, list to be notified in 6 months, digital inventory in 1 year, updated every 10 years |

**Wise use** allows human use (subsistence fishing, harvesting) if compatible with conservation, as decided by the State authority. PMF reports that in late 2024 the Supreme Court ordered all states to complete 'ground-truthing' (marking boundaries on the map and the ground) and describes 'Wetland Mitras', volunteers who report violations (both as in the book; verify). Eco-sensitive zones: generally up to 10 km around a protected wetland (PMF; as in the book).

### 9. Master: the Ramsar Convention, the Montreux Record and India's schemes

*Objective:* State the Ramsar basics as PMF gives them, explain the Montreux Record and the obligations of parties, and recall NPCA, Amrit Dharohar and MISHTI.

## The Convention in brief (use bridge B2)

The Ramsar Convention on Wetlands is an international treaty for the conservation and **wise (sustainable) use** of wetlands. It was adopted on 2 February 1971 at Ramsar, Iran, and the date is observed as **World Wetlands Day**. PMF reports 172 Contracting Parties and over 2,500 Ramsar Sites (as in the book; verify, since numbers change). PMF gives India's joining year as 1982 (PDF p. 134) while p. 133 prints '(1971)' after the Convention's name, which is the Convention's year and not India's: do not read that bracket as India's date. PMF also names the UK (176 sites) and Mexico (144) as having the most, and Brazil as having the largest area, 267,000 sq km (all as in the book; verify). **The count of Indian Ramsar sites is dynamic: the book's list is in a later chapter not extracted. As in the book; verify the current number.**

## Obligations of parties

A party must designate at least one suitable wetland for the List of Wetlands of International Importance (Article 2), promote the wise use of all wetlands in its territory, establish nature reserves on wetlands. A party may in urgent national interest delete or restrict a listed site, but must compensate with additional nature reserves; PMF says no site has ever been deleted. Wetlands that cross a border can be **transboundary Ramsar sites** managed jointly. Listing commits the government to maintain the site's **ecological character**; being a party is not an obligation to protect every wetland (PMF's reprinted 2019 item, module 10; not verified against the official paper).

## Monitoring tools

- **Montreux Record:** a register of Ramsar sites whose ecological character has changed or is likely to change because of pollution or development; it triggers technical help and high-level attention. PMF reprints a 2014 question on exactly this (as printed in PMF IAS; not verified against the official paper). Indian sites on the Record: Keoladeo National Park (Rajasthan) and Loktak Lake (Manipur); Chilika was listed in 1993 and removed in 2002 (all verify).
- **Ramsar Advisory Missions:** on request, international experts help solve a threat to a site.
- **National reports:** parties submit a public report every three years at the COP.

## COP and partners

The **COP** meets every three years to review progress, adopt resolutions, set the budget and amend the text. COP15 (2025) was held at Victoria Falls, Zimbabwe, and adopted the 4th Strategic Plan 2025-2034 (PMF; post-2024; verify). PMF lists five identification criteria, including a waterbird threshold of 20,000 birds (verify the full set). **UNESCO** is the depositary. The six International Organisation Partners are BirdLife International, IUCN, IWMI, Wetlands International, WWF and WWT. **Wetlands International** is a non-governmental organisation, not an intergovernmental one.

## India's schemes (PMF, PDF pp. 133-134)

| Scheme | Key facts |
|---|---|
| National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Eco-systems (NPCA) | Launched 2013-14 by merging NLCP and NWCP; centrally sponsored; 70:30 Centre-State, 90:10 for NE States; funds desilting, weed control, shoreline protection |
| Amrit Dharohar | Wetland programme 2023-2026: carbon assessment and livelihoods at Ramsar sites |
| MISHTI | Mangrove scheme 2023, after India joined the Mangrove Alliance for Climate at COP27 (2022) |

Remember: Amrit Dharohar is about **wetlands**, MISHTI about **mangroves**. India's Ramsar examples in PMF: Chilika Lake, Sundarbans.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Lake: a hollow of the land surface in which water accumulates (Leong).
- Basin of inland drainage: a region whose streams end in a lake or salt flat instead of the sea (Leong).
- Playa: a salt lake or dry salt flat of deserts (Leong).
- Ox-bow lake: horseshoe-shaped channel left when a river cuts across a meander loop (Leong).
- Lagoon: shallow water enclosed from the sea by a bar or spit of mud, sand and shingle (Leong).
- Wetland (2017 rules): areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt, including marine water up to six metres deep at low tide (PMF).
- Ramsar site: a wetland of international importance listed under the Ramsar Convention; listing commits the government to maintain its ecological character (PMF).
- Montreux Record: Ramsar register of sites whose ecological character has changed or is likely to change (PMF).

### Notes: Policies and institutions to remember

- NPCA: centrally sponsored scheme for lakes and wetlands; desilting, weed control, shoreline protection.
- Amrit Dharohar: wetlands, carbon assessment and livelihoods at Ramsar sites (2023-2026).
- MISHTI: mangroves and shoreline habitats (2023).
- Wise use: sustainable human use compatible with conservation, decided by the State authority.
- Eco-sensitive zone: generally up to 10 km around a protected wetland (PMF).

### Notes: Edge cases and exceptions

- Lake Chad has no outflow but is generally fresh because of seepage (verify): 'no outlet' alone does not mean saline.
- Lake Victoria lies between the arms of the Rift Valley but is not a rift valley lake (Leong).
- A caspian-type 'sea' is a lake: size alone does not decide the name.
- Reservoirs are lakes in function but human-made in origin.
- Ramsar listing does not make protection of all wetlands mandatory (PMF's reprinted 2019 item; not verified against the official paper).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Lagoon versus lake: a lagoon is a lake-type water body cut off from the sea by a bar.
- Wetland versus lake: a wetland is broader.
- Party versus COP: the party is a country; the COP is the meeting.
- Adoption year versus joining year.
- Amrit Dharohar (wetlands) versus MISHTI (mangroves).
- Crater versus caldera: crater is a hollow in a cone; caldera is a larger collapse (standard; verify).

### Core concepts

- A lake needs a hollow plus a water balance in which inflow at least balances losses.
- Six families of origin: tectonic, glacial, volcanic, erosional, depositional, human-made.
- Saline lakes: no surface or underground outlet, so evaporation is the only exit and salt accumulates over a long time (if evaporation also exceeds inflow the lake shrinks).
- Wetlands are a broader class than lakes (2017 rules definition).
- Ramsar listing commits a government to maintain a site's ecological character.

### Practice

**Q.** Define lake and list Leong's six origin families.

**A.** A hollow in which water accumulates. Families: earth movement, glaciation, volcanic activity, erosion, deposition, human and biological activity.

**Q.** What is a basin of inland drainage and why are its lakes saline?

**A.** A region whose streams drain into a lake with no outlet to the sea; intense evaporation removes water and leaves salt behind.

**Q.** State the 2017 definition of wetland in one sentence.

**A.** Areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt, including marine water up to six metres deep at low tide.

**Q.** Give the date and place of adoption of the Ramsar Convention.

**A.** 2 February 1971, Ramsar, Iran; observed as World Wetlands Day.

**Q.** Why does Lake Chad, with no surface outflow, not fit the simple rule that such lakes are salty?

**A.** Water also leaves by seepage underground (standard; verify), carrying some salt away, so the no-outlet condition is not fully met; salt accumulates only when evaporation is the sole exit.

**Q.** How can you tell an ox-bow lake from a lagoon on a map described in words?

**A.** An ox-bow lies on a river flood plain as a crescent beside a meandering river; a lagoon lies along the coast behind a bar or spit.

**Q.** Why do natural lakes smooth river flow?

**A.** They absorb the surplus in heavy rain and release water in the dry season (Leong).

**Q.** Why does PMF say Ramsar listing is not an obligation to protect every wetland?

**A.** Parties are expected to maintain the ecological character of listed sites and promote wise use, but the Convention does not mandate protecting every wetland (PMF's reprinted 2019 item; not verified against the official paper).
