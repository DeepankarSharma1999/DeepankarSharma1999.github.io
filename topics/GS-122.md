## GS-122: Natural vegetation and forest belts

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Rainfall in centimetres and what a rainfall band means

This lesson uses numbers such as 'over 200 cm' and '70-100 cm'. Learn to read them before module 5.

**Annual rainfall** is the depth of water that falls on a place in a year, measured in centimetres (cm) or millimetres (mm). 10 mm make 1 cm, so 200 cm is 2,000 mm. If you spread a year's rain on flat ground with no loss, it would stand 200 cm deep at a place with 200 cm of rain.

A **band** is a range. 'Between 100 and 200 cm' includes 100 and 200 in everyday reading, but the textbook does not say which band owns the exact edge. Forests do not change at a sharp line; they blend, so there are belts where one forest type thins into the next. NCERT even says dry deciduous forest has a transition to moist deciduous on its wetter margin and to thorn forest on its drier one.

Three habits help. First, convert to the same unit. Second, expect gaps and overlaps between bands in a book; treat them as soft. Third, rainfall is not the only cause: soil, altitude and human action also matter, which is why the lesson ends with factors.

### 1. Orientation: natural vegetation and forest belts on one page

*Objective:* Define natural vegetation, name India's five forest groups with one clue each and list the world belts from equator to poles.

## What natural vegetation means

NCERT defines **natural vegetation** as a plant community left undisturbed over a long time, so that its species adjust to climate and soil conditions as fully as possible (kegy105 p. 1). A mango orchard you planted is not natural vegetation; the same tree growing wild in a forest is part of it. Leong is stricter: he calls vegetation natural only if humans have not affected it, directly or indirectly, and says most vegetation today is **semi-natural** (Leong, PDF p. 225). Learn the NCERT definition for answers and keep Leong's word 'semi-natural' as the more careful term.

## India's five forest groups

NCERT groups Indian forests by predominant vegetation and climate:

| Group | Rainfall clue | Where (headline) | Sample trees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen | over 200 cm | Western slope of the Western Ghats, north-eastern hills, Andaman and Nicobar | rosewood, mahogany, ebony |
| Tropical deciduous | 70-200 cm | Most widespread; moist and dry types | teak, sal, tendu |
| Tropical thorn | below 50 cm | Semi-arid north-west and centre | babool, ber, khejri |
| Montane | altitude, not rain | Himalaya and southern hills | deodar, pine, sholas |
| Littoral and swamp | tides and salt | Coasts, deltas, islands | mangroves |

## The world on a line

From the equator towards the poles the belts run roughly: **rain forest**, **monsoon forest**, **savanna**, **hot desert**, **Mediterranean scrub and woodland**, **warm temperate forest**, **cool temperate deciduous and mixed forest** with **temperate grass** beside it, **coniferous (taiga) forest** and **tundra**. Climate decides most of it; Day 18 lesson GS-110 gives the climate side of the same belts.

### 2. Foundation: the words and the habitat factors

*Objective:* Define vegetation, flora, evergreen, deciduous, xerophyte, canopy, stratification, climax, succession and forest area against forest cover, and name the habitat factors.

## Words

- **Vegetation**: the community of plants growing together in an area with a general look. Leong says the look comes from the species mix and from how they are arranged, horizontally and vertically.
- **Flora**: the list of plant species of a place; vegetation is more than the list because arrangement matters.
- **Evergreen**: trees that keep green leaves all year because leaf-fall is not tied to a season. **Deciduous**: trees that shed their leaves in a season, usually the dry season, to save water.
- **Xerophyte**: a plant adapted to dry conditions (cactus, thorny shrub). **Hydrophyte**: grows in water. **Mesophyte**: needs moderate water.
- **Canopy** and **stratification**: the canopy is the leafy roof of tall trees; stratification is the layering of tall trees, smaller trees, shrubs and herbs.
- **Succession**: the replacement of one plant community by another over time, from a bare surface through pioneers to a **climax community** matched to the regional climate (Leong, PDF p. 230).
- **Forest area** and **forest cover**: forest area is land officially recorded as forest; forest cover is land actually under tree canopy. NCERT's exercise asks the difference but the extract does not give it; the usual definition is a standard-knowledge item, so **verify** it in the forest survey reports before using it. This matters for the 2019 Prelims item (module 10).

## Habitat factors

Leong lists the factors that decide a plant community: **climate** (light, temperature, precipitation, evapotranspiration), **soil** (the edaphic factor), **relief**, **biotic** factors (other plants and animals) and **human** activity. On a world scale climate counts most; locally rocks, soil, relief, drainage and humans matter more (Leong, PDF pp. 225-226). Day 18 lesson GS-110 teaches this in full; here is what you need for India.

## Temperature and water groups

Leong sorts plants by temperature need into **megathermal** (warmer than about 20 degrees C in most months), **mesothermal** (10-20 degrees C) and **microthermal** (below 10 degrees C for several months). Sun-loving plants are heliophytes and shade-loving ones sciophytes.

## Altitude acts like latitude

On a mountain, temperature falls with height, so vegetation zones stack up: Leong's Fig. 16.3 for a high tropical mountain runs from rain forest at the base, through sub-montane and montane forest, to scrub or grass, snowline and ice. The tree line is higher in the tropics than in temperate latitudes. NCERT's Himalaya story in module 7 follows the same idea.

## A sentence to hold

Climate sets the belt, soil and relief modify it inside the belt, and people change it everywhere. Leong adds that human changes usually take vegetation from a complex form to a simpler one, for example forest to savanna and savanna to scrub (Leong, PDF p. 229).

### 3. Core: world belts, I - rain forest, monsoon forest and savanna

*Objective:* State the climate, trees and adaptations of the equatorial rain forest, the tropical monsoon forest and the savanna.

## Equatorial rain forest (Leong ch. 17)

**Climate:** very heavy rain (over 2,032 mm, which is 203 cm) and uniform high temperature (about 27 degrees C), so growth is continuous; seeding, flowering, fruiting and decay have no seasonal pattern. In the Amazon lowlands the forest is so dense that it has the special name **selvas**.

**Plants and structure:**
1. A great variety of **evergreen** trees yielding tropical hardwood such as mahogany, ebony and greenheart, plus smaller palms, climbers (lianas, rattan), **epiphytes** (plants that grow on other plants for support) and parasites, ferns and orchids.
2. A **layered** structure: a thick canopy, tallest trees about 60 m, smaller trees below and shade-loving ferns on the ground. Undergrowth is sparse because tall trees cut off sunlight.
3. **Many species** and no pure stands. Leong cites as many as 490 tree species per hectare in a Malaysian forest in chapter 17, but chapter 16 says up to 40 species in a quarter of a hectare. These two are not the same measure; do not quote either as a rule. Say only 'very many species, rarely pure stands' (check).
4. Cleared land grows back as less luxuriant **secondary forest** (belukar in Malaysia); brackish coastal swamps carry **mangroves**.

## Tropical monsoon forest (Leong ch. 18)

The vegetation follows the **summer rainfall**. Trees are **deciduous**: they grow fast in the rains, shed leaves in the hot dry season and rest in the cool season. Where rain is heavy (southern Burma, peninsular India, northern Australia, tropical marine coasts) there is forest, more open and less luxuriant than the equatorial type, with far fewer species and a denser undergrowth because more light reaches the ground. **Bamboo** thickets mark intermediate rainfall. With less rain the forest thins into thorny scrub or savanna. **Teak** is the best-known timber; sal and acacia follow. Much of the forest has been cleared for farming.

## Savanna (Leong ch. 19)

A tropical wet-dry belt between the hot desert and the rain forest. Leong says 'tropical grassland' is misleading because trees are always present with tall grass; 'woodland' or 'bush veld' fits better. Equatorwards trees grow larger and denser; desertwards the vegetation becomes scrub and thorny bush (Australian examples: mallee, mulga, spinifex).

**Adaptations to a long dry season:** deciduous leaves; long roots (acacia); thick trunks that store water (baobab, bottle tree); thorns; umbrella-shaped crowns. Grass grows 2-4 m high in tufts, and elephant grass up to 5 m. The landscape is green in the rains and yellow-brown in the dry season.

Leong's chapter 16 gives five structural types of savanna, from dense to open: **woodland** (trees and shrubs, little or no grass), **savanna woodland** (closed canopy over grass), **tree savanna** (scattered trees), **shrub savanna** and **grass savanna** (almost no woody plants).

## Compare in a table

| Belt | Rainfall idea | Tree habit | Typical species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rain forest | very heavy, all year | evergreen, layered | mahogany, ebony |
| Monsoon forest | heavy summer rain, dry season | deciduous | teak, sal, bamboo |
| Savanna | wet-dry, long drought | deciduous, thorny, scattered | acacia, baobab, tall grass |


### 4. Core: world belts, II - desert, Mediterranean, temperate, taiga and tundra

*Objective:* Name the main vegetation of the dry, Mediterranean, temperate, coniferous and polar belts and the trait that marks each.

## Hot and mid-latitude deserts (Leong ch. 20)

Almost all deserts have some plants waiting dormant for rain. The typical vegetation is **xerophytic scrub**: cacti, thorny bushes, long-rooted wiry grasses and dwarf acacias. Trees are rare except **date palms** at oases and along rivers. On western coastal deserts washed by cold currents, as in the Atacama, fog and mist roll inland and feed a thin cover.

## Mediterranean belt (Leong ch. 21)

Winter rain, hot dry summer. Four vegetation types:
1. **Evergreen oak woodland** with cork oak (Spain, Portugal) where rain is over about 635 mm; trees are low, with small leathery leaves, thick bark and wide root systems. In Australia eucalyptus (jarrah, karri) replaces oak; giant sequoia is the Californian giant.
2. **Evergreen conifers** (pines, firs, cedars, cypress) on cooler highlands.
3. **Bushes and shrubs**, the commonest type: laurel, myrtle, lavender, rosemary. Degraded forest becomes scrub with local names: **maquis** (France), **macchia** (Italy), **chaparral** (California), **mallee** (Australia), **garigue** on thin limestone soil.
4. **Grass** is poor because most rain falls in the cool season when growth is slow.

## Warm temperate east, cool temperate west and grasslands (Leong chs. 22-25)

- **Warm temperate eastern margins** are green all year: eucalyptus in eastern Australia (some over 76 m), Parana pine, quebracho and yerba mate in South America, palms and chestnut in Natal, oak, camphor and magnolia in China and Japan; much cleared for fuel and farms.
- **Cool temperate western margin**: **deciduous forest** of oak, elm, ash, birch, beech and poplar, in pure stands; much cleared. Leong says only 4 per cent of the original forest is left in Britain (his figure; verify).
- **Temperate grassland (steppe)**: trees are scarce, except willows or poplars along water; wooded steppe appears poleward; the grass is shorter and sparser towards the desert.
- **Laurentian (cool temperate eastern)**: mixed forest; evergreen conifers predominate north of 50 degrees N and deciduous forest south of it.

## Taiga (Leong ch. 26)

A vast belt of **coniferous** forest of pine, fir, spruce and larch (larch is a hardy deciduous tree among them), in pure stands. Trees reach about 30 m, with needle-shaped, thick leaves to check transpiration and sloping branches that shed snow. The floor is acid, leached and nearly bare. Siberia holds the largest reserve of softwood.

## Tundra (Leong ch. 27)

No trees: **mosses, lichens and sedges**, with stunted birch, dwarf willow and alder in sheltered spots and reindeer moss on coastal lowlands. The warmest month does not exceed 10 degrees C (the tree-survival line), the growing season is under three months and precipitation is about 254 mm.

## The belt order

Equator to pole: rain forest, monsoon forest, savanna, desert, Mediterranean, warm temperate, then cool temperate forest (deciduous and mixed) with grass beside it, taiga, tundra. Temperate grass and temperate forest share latitudes; continental interior against margin decides which (Leong chs. 22-25). The order is not a perfect strip because continents and sea currents bend it; the same sequence also appears going up a mountain.

### 5. Core: India's forest groups and the evergreen forests

*Objective:* State where the tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen forests grow, their rainfall and temperature conditions, structure and trees, and test the 200 cm rule on edge cases.

## Five groups, one idea

NCERT divides Indian forests into five groups by predominant vegetation and climatic region: tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen, tropical deciduous, tropical thorn, montane, and littoral and swamp (kegy105 p. 1). Modules 5 to 7 take them in turn. Remember the simple logic: **rain decides the first three**, altitude decides the fourth, and tides and salt decide the fifth.

## Tropical evergreen forests

**Where:** the western slope of the Western Ghats, the hills of the north-eastern region and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. NCERT's introduction calls the Western Ghats and the Andaman and Nicobar vegetation **tropical rain forest**.

**Conditions:** warm and humid, annual precipitation over **200 cm** and mean annual temperature above **22 degrees C**.

**Structure:** well stratified, with shrubs and creepers close to the ground, short trees above them and tall trees at the top. Trees reach **60 m or more**. There is no fixed time for shedding leaves, flowering or fruiting, so the forest looks green all year.

**Species:** rosewood, mahogany, aini, ebony (NCERT prints 'mahogony').

## Semi-evergreen forests

They occupy the **less rainy parts** of the same regions. They are a mixture of evergreen and moist deciduous trees, and undergrowing climbers give the forest an evergreen look. Main species: white cedar, hollock and kail.

## Why these places?

The west slope of the Ghats faces the south-west monsoon, and NCERT's climate chapter says the highest rainfall is along the west coast, the Western Ghats and the sub-Himalayan north-east, over 200 cm, and in parts of the Khasi and Jaintia hills over 1,000 cm (kegy104 p. 12). The Andaman and Nicobar Islands have convectional rain and equatorial-type vegetation (kegy102 p. 9, Day 21 lesson GS-113). This is the same chain as Leong's equatorial rain forest: heavy rain, no cold or drought, continuous growth, layered evergreen forest (module 3). Details of the Ghats are Day 17 lesson GS-116.

## Test the 200 cm rule on edge cases

A rule is only useful if you know where it bends.

1. **Western Ghats west slope:** rainfall over 200 cm and evergreen. The rule holds.
2. **Brahmaputra valley and adjoining hills (check):** NCERT's climate chapter gives **less than 200 cm** there (kegy104 p. 12), while the vegetation chapter puts evergreen forest in the north-eastern hills. If evergreen patches grow where rain is under 200 cm (standard; verify), the rule is a guide, not a line. The semi-evergreen forests of the less rainy parts (kegy105 p. 1) are the safer textbook example of soft bands.
3. **Eastern slope of the Western Ghats:** a rain-shadow side; NCERT puts moist deciduous forest there, not evergreen (module 6).

State the rule as 'evergreen forests grow where rain is heavy, over about 200 cm, and the dry season is short'.

### 6. Core: the deciduous forests and the thorn forests

*Objective:* Separate moist deciduous, dry deciduous and thorn forests by rainfall, region and trees, and avoid the species traps in the lists.

## Tropical deciduous forests (the monsoon forests)

NCERT calls these the **most widespread forests in India**, also called monsoon forests. They spread over regions with rainfall of **70-200 cm**, and are divided by water supply into moist and dry deciduous (kegy105 p. 3).

### Moist deciduous

- Rainfall about **100-200 cm**.
- **Where:** the north-eastern states along the foothills of the Himalaya, the eastern slopes of the Western Ghats and Odisha.
- **Trees:** teak, sal, shisham, hurra, mahua, amla, semul, kusum and sandalwood.

### Dry deciduous

- Rainfall about **70-100 cm**, covering vast areas.
- **Where:** the rainier parts of the Peninsula and the plains of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. On the wetter margin it merges into moist deciduous, on the drier margin into thorn forest.
- **Landscape:** in the higher-rainfall parts of the Peninsular plateau and the northern plain, a **parkland**, with open stretches where teak and other trees stand among grass. As the dry season begins the trees shed their leaves completely and the forest looks like a vast grassland with naked trees.
- **Trees:** tendu, palas, amaltas, bel, khair, axlewood.
- **Scanty cover:** in western and southern Rajasthan vegetation is very thin because of low rainfall and overgrazing.

## Tropical thorn forests

- Rainfall **below 50 cm**.
- **Where:** semi-arid areas of south-west Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh.
- **Look:** a variety of grasses and shrubs; plants remain **leafless for most of the year**, giving an impression of scrub. Tussocky grass grows up to **2 m** as undergrowth.
- **Trees:** babool, ber, wild date palm, khair, neem, khejri, palas.

## One table to hold

| Forest | Rain (cm) | Clue region | Clue trees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moist deciduous | 100-200 | NE foothills, Odisha, east slope of Ghats | sal, teak, mahua |
| Dry deciduous | 70-100 | Peninsula, UP and Bihar plains | tendu, bel, axlewood |
| Thorn | below 50 | north-west, Gujarat, Rajasthan | babool, khejri, ber |

## Test the rules on edge cases

1. **Species overlap.** Teak is on NCERT's moist deciduous list, but teak also stands in the dry deciduous parkland. Khair and palas appear in both the dry deciduous and the thorn lists. So **a single species cannot fix the forest type**; use rainfall and region together.
2. **A gap in the bands.** NCERT gives dry deciduous 70-100 cm and thorn below 50 cm, and gives no label for 50-70 cm. Read it as a transition belt, as NCERT itself says dry deciduous merges into thorn forest on its drier margin.
3. **A rainfall-versus-people case.** Rajasthan's thin cover in the dry deciduous paragraph is blamed on low rainfall **and overgrazing**, so people shape the map as well as rain.

## Compare with the world

Leong's tropical monsoon forest (module 3) tells the same story; Indian thorn forest matches his thorny scrub on the desert margin.


### 7. Core: montane forests, mangroves and wetlands

*Objective:* Trace the Himalayan altitude sequence, describe the southern montane forests and the littoral and swamp forests with mangrove facts and their dated-number flags.

## Montane forests

In mountains, temperature falls as height rises, and the natural vegetation changes in step. NCERT splits montane forests into **northern** and **southern**.

### Northern mountain forests (Himalaya)

NCERT describes a succession from tropical to tundra with altitude (kegy105 p. 4):

1. **Foothills:** deciduous forests.
2. **1,000-2,000 m:** wet temperate forests; in the higher hill ranges of north-eastern India, hilly West Bengal and Uttaranchal (now Uttarakhand), evergreen broad-leaf trees such as **oak** and **chestnut**.
3. **1,500-1,750 m:** well-developed **pine** forests, with **chir pine** a useful commercial tree. **Deodar**, a valued endemic species, grows mainly in the western Himalaya; its durable wood is used in construction. The **chinar** and **walnut** that sustain Kashmir handicrafts belong to this zone.
4. **2,225-3,048 m:** blue pine and spruce; temperate grasslands in places.
5. **3,000-4,000 m:** silver firs, junipers, pines, birch and rhododendrons, passing into alpine pastures used for transhumance by the Gujjars, Bakarwals, Bhotiyas and Gaddis.
6. **Higher still:** mosses and lichens of the tundra type.

The **southern slopes** carry thicker cover than the drier north-facing slopes because they receive more precipitation.

### Southern mountain forests

These occur in three areas of Peninsular India: the **Western Ghats, the Vindhyas and the Nilgiris**. They stand only about 1,500 m above sea level, but being near the tropics they carry temperate-type vegetation in the higher parts and subtropical vegetation lower down, especially in Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. The temperate forests are called **sholas** in the Nilgiris, Anaimalai and Palani hills. Trees of economic value include magnolia, laurel, cinchona and wattle. Similar forests occur in the Satpura and Maikal ranges.

## Littoral and swamp forests

**Wetlands.** NCERT groups India's wetlands into eight categories, from the southern reservoirs and lagoons, the saline expanses of Rajasthan, Gujarat and the Gulf of Kachchh, and the freshwater lakes (Keoladeo) to the delta lagoons of the east coast (Chilika), the Gangetic marshes, the Brahmaputra floodplains, the lakes of Kashmir and Ladakh and the mangroves of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. The book says wetlands total 3.9 million hectares, about 70 per cent being paddy fields, and names two Ramsar sites, Chilika and Keoladeo. These are the book's figures; **later lists are longer, so verify** (Day 18 lesson GS-131).

**Mangroves** grow along coasts in salt marshes, tidal creeks, mud flats and estuaries. They are made of salt-tolerant species and shelter many birds. Leong adds stilt and aerial roots for support and breathing in waterlogged mud. NCERT prints **4,992 sq. km** and **7 per cent of the world's mangroves** (as printed; dated, verify). The 7 per cent seems carried over from an older edition's larger area (6,740 sq. km; verify); against current global estimates (about 1.47 lakh sq. km; verify) 4,992 sq. km is nearer 3 per cent, so quote the area with its source and year, not the share. Mangroves are best developed in the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the Sunderbans**, with the **Mahanadi, Godavari and Krishna deltas** also important. They are being encroached upon and need conservation.

## A figure worth drawing

A vertical Himalayan strip with 1,000 to 4,000 m marked and the forest type beside each band. The bands overlap: pine (1,500-1,750 m) sits inside the wet temperate range (1,000-2,000 m).

### 8. Master: why India's vegetation is so varied, and what people did to it

*Objective:* Explain the diversity of Indian vegetation through rainfall, altitude, soil and relief, and location, and describe the human changes NCERT and Leong record.

## Four natural causes

1. **Rainfall.** NCERT's climate chapter gives the extremes: Cherrapunji and Mawsynram in the Khasi hills receive over 1,080 cm a year while Jaisalmer rarely gets more than 9 cm; the annual precipitation is under 10 cm in the north-west Himalaya and the western deserts and over 400 cm in Meghalaya (kegy104 p. 3). The average annual rainfall is about 125 cm, with four regions: over 200 cm, 100-200 cm, 50-100 cm and below 50 cm (kegy104 p. 12; Day 21 lesson GS-120). Each band carries a forest type (modules 5 and 6). NCERT's forest bands (dry deciduous 70-100 cm) and its rainfall regions (50-100 cm) are not identical; use each in its own place.
2. **Temperature and altitude.** The Himalaya shows the whole sequence from tropical to tundra with height (module 7), and the southern hills carry temperate sholas.
3. **Soil and relief.** Leong calls soil the edaphic factor; relief modifies soil and climate, and drainage matters. Tidal mud shapes mangroves; waterlogged soils support wetland plants.
4. **Location.** NCERT says the south of India lies in the tropics and the north in the sub-tropical zone (kegy101 p. 2), which adds a latitude layer. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands have equatorial-type vegetation.

NCERT's own summary: Himalayan heights carry temperate vegetation; the Western Ghats and the islands, tropical rain forests; the deltas, tropical forests and mangroves; the desert and semi-desert of Rajasthan, cacti, bushes and thorny vegetation (kegy105 p. 1).

## What people did

- **The British period.** NCERT says the British saw the economic value of Indian forests and began large-scale exploitation. The oak forests of Garhwal and Kumaon were replaced by **chir pine**, needed for railway lines; forests were cleared for **tea, rubber and coffee** plantations; timber was used in construction. The protectional use of forests gave way to commercial use (kegy105 p. 3).
- **Farming and settlement.** Leong's monsoon-land account: much monsoon forest cleared for crops, with hills terraced and soil erosion where land was cleared wantonly.
- **Grazing.** NCERT blames overgrazing, with low rainfall, for thin cover in western and southern Rajasthan.

## What clearing does (Leong, chapter 16)

When natural vegetation is removed: the microclimate becomes drier; the soil is exposed to sun, wind and rain; runoff increases and infiltration falls; eroded sediment silts rivers and lakes; and evapotranspiration is reduced. Leong's six effects give you a ready chain for the climate-change question in module 10. The reverse also holds: forests hold soil and water.

## The answer frame

For 'factors responsible for the diversity of vegetation' write four heads (rainfall, altitude and temperature, soil and relief, latitude), one Indian example each, a fifth on human influence, and end with conservation.

### 9. Master: forest policy, social forestry, wildlife and reserves

*Objective:* State the forest policy aims, the types of social forestry, the reasons for wildlife decline and the conservation tools NCERT lists, with the dated figures flagged.

## Forest policy (kegy105 p. 5)

India adopted a national forest policy in **1952**, modified in **1988**. Its aims in the extract: bring **33 per cent** of the geographical area under forest cover; keep environmental stability and restore forests where balance is lost; conserve biological diversity and the genetic pool; check soil erosion, desert spread, floods and droughts; raise cover through social forestry and afforestation of degraded land; supply timber, fuel, fodder and food to rural people; and build a people's movement, involving women, to plant trees. The 1988 policy is often quoted as one-third overall and two-thirds in hills; that split is not in the extract (verify).

## Social forestry (kegy105 pp. 5-6)

Defined as management and protection of forests and afforestation of barren lands to help environmental, social and rural development. The National Commission on Agriculture (1976) classified it into three kinds:
- **Urban forestry**: trees in and around towns, green belts, parks, avenues.
- **Rural forestry**: **agro-forestry** (trees and crops on the same land) and **community forestry** (trees on common land such as village pasture, temple land, canal banks).
- **Farm forestry**: farmers grow trees for commercial or non-commercial use; state forest departments distribute free seedlings to small and medium farmers.

## Forests and tribal people

Forests are home, food and livelihood for many tribal people; NCERT says they should be growers of minor forest produce and partners in conservation.

## Wildlife

NCERT says about **4-5 per cent** of all known plant and animal species are found in India. Reasons for decline: exploitation of forests; land cleared for farms, settlement, roads, mining and reservoirs; lopping for fodder and fuel; grazing; hunting and poaching; and forest fire.

**Tools:**
- The **Wildlife Act, 1972** (comprehensively amended in 1991 as in NCERT; later amendments exist, verify): protects scheduled endangered species and gives legal support to national parks, sanctuaries and closed areas. The book gives **107 national parks and 573 sanctuaries**; these counts are dated, verify (Day 18 lesson GS-131).
- **Project Tiger** (1973): started with nine reserves covering 16,339 sq. km; the book now gives 58 tiger reserves over 84,487 sq. km of core habitat in 18 states, and a tiger count rising from 1,411 (2006) to 3,682 (2023) (as printed; Day 18 lesson GS-131 explains that 3,682 is an estimate, so verify and compare like with like). **Project Elephant** (1992) runs in 18 states.
- **Biosphere reserves**: representative ecosystems recognised under UNESCO's Man and Biosphere Programme. NCERT lists **18** in Table 5.1 (as in the 2024-25 report), of which **12** were in UNESCO's World Network as printed; a later addition (Cold Desert, 2025) is reported, so verify the current count. Those relevant to rain-forest regions include Nilgiri (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka), Agasthyamalai (Tamil Nadu, Kerala), Great Nicobar and the north-eastern reserves of Manas, Dibru-Saikhowa, Dehang Debang and Nokrek. The table gives states only, so the association with rain-forest belts is geographic knowledge to verify on a map.

## Why rain-forest sanctuaries matter

Evergreen forests hold dense layered habitat, many species and the sources of Peninsular rivers (the Western Ghats). A sanctuary gives legal protection against clearing and hunting, protects watersheds, and supports research and ecotourism (the second half of the 2023 Mains question).

### 10. Exam: the seven official questions and an elimination method

*Objective:* Sort the seven verified questions into types, apply a method for each, and mark every fact outside the extracts for verification.

## Three question types

1. **Mains frameworks** (2020 forest resources and climate change; 2023 vegetation diversity and rain-forest sanctuaries).
2. **Species items** that need plant facts outside the textbooks (2021 moringa and tamarind; 2023 jackfruit, mahua and teak; 2024 cashew, papaya and red sanders; 2026 foxtail orchid).
3. **Statistics** that need a forest survey report (2019 order of forest cover in four states).

## The method for statement and species items

1. **Split** the statement set into single claims.
2. **Decide each claim** before you look at the options.
3. **Sort each claim**: in the extract, standard knowledge, or unknown.
4. **Use the tree-type logic** you learned: deciduous or evergreen by rainfall and season; native or introduced is separate knowledge.
5. **Never guess blind.** A wrong answer in Prelims GS costs one-third of the question's marks (2 marks lose about 0.67). A blind guess among four options has expected value zero; removing even one option makes a guess worth taking.

**2020 (15 marks): status of forest resources and the impact on climate change.** Define; give the policy target (33 per cent); the five forest groups; dated figures with verify (mangroves 4,992 sq km as printed, without the world share); pressures (module 8); the climate chain (clearing makes microclimate drier, runoff up, infiltration down, erosion; forests also store carbon and regulate water, standard); policy response (social forestry, wildlife protection); the latest official report as the place for current figures.

**2023 (15 marks): factors of diversity, then sanctuaries in rain-forest regions.** Part 1: rainfall, altitude, soil and relief, latitude, human action, each with an Indian example. ## Traps

- Treating a famous tree as proof of a forest type.
- Giving statistics from memory.
- Mixing up 'native', 'endemic' and 'introduced'.
- Forgetting that 'forest cover' and 'forest area' are different measures.
- Counting a state's forest cover in sq. km instead of as a percentage of its area.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Natural vegetation (NCERT): a plant community left undisturbed over a long time so its species adjust to climate and soil as fully as possible.
- Semi-natural vegetation (Leong): vegetation influenced to varying degrees by human activity; most vegetation today.
- Deciduous: sheds leaves in a season, usually dry; evergreen: no fixed leaf-fall, green all year.
- Xerophyte: dry-adapted plant; hydrophyte: water plant; mesophyte: needs moderate water.
- Climax community: the stable community at the end of succession, set mainly by regional climate (Leong, PDF p. 230).
- Biome: the most extensive ecosystem unit, with a characteristic vegetation under a particular climate (Leong, PDF p. 239).
- Sholas: temperate forests of the Nilgiri, Anaimalai and Palani hills.
- Transhumance: seasonal movement of herds between low and high pastures (the Gujjars, Bakarwals, Bhotiyas and Gaddis in NCERT).
- Biosphere reserve: a representative ecosystem recognised within UNESCO's Man and Biosphere Programme.

### Notes: Cause and effect chains

- South-west monsoon strikes the Western Ghats, heavy rain over 200 cm on the west slope, evergreen forest; the east slope is drier with moist deciduous forest.
- Rain falls from east to west across the north, so forest thins from moist deciduous to dry deciduous to thorn scrub.
- Temperature falls with height, so the Himalaya shows deciduous, wet temperate, pine, fir and alpine bands, then lichens.
- Clearing removes cover, so the microclimate dries, the soil is exposed, runoff rises, infiltration falls, silt chokes rivers (Leong, PDF p. 239).
- Railways and plantations during British rule replaced oak with chir pine and clearing for tea, rubber and coffee (kegy105 p. 3).

### Notes: Conservation and wildlife facts (as printed in kegy105, 2026-27 reprint)

- National forest policy 1952, modified 1988; target 33 per cent of the geographical area under forest cover.
- Social forestry (National Commission on Agriculture, 1976): urban, rural (agro-forestry, community forestry) and farm forestry.
- Wildlife Act 1972, comprehensively amended 1991 (as in NCERT; later amendments exist, verify); 107 national parks and 573 sanctuaries as printed (verify).
- Project Tiger 1973 with 9 reserves of 16,339 sq. km, now 58 reserves of 84,487 sq. km in 18 states as printed; Project Elephant 1992 in 18 states.

### Notes: Source notes and slips (checked)

- Species counts in Leong: chapter 16 says a rain forest may have up to 40 species in a quarter of a hectare, chapter 17 says as many as 490 species per hectare in a Malaysian forest. Different samples and measures; quote neither as a rule (check).
- NCERT's rainfall bands for forests (evergreen over 200, moist deciduous 100-200, dry deciduous 70-100, thorn below 50) leave 50-70 cm unlabelled and differ from the four rainfall regions in the climate chapter (over 200, 100-200, 50-100, below 50). Treat bands as soft.
- Species lists overlap: teak is in the moist deciduous list and the dry deciduous parkland; khair and palas are in both the dry deciduous and thorn lists.
- Brahmaputra valley and adjoining hills (check): the climate chapter gives under 200 cm there, while the vegetation chapter puts evergreen forest in the north-eastern hills, which largely lie in the over-200 cm belt. The two sentences alone do not show evergreen forest below 200 cm (verify); bands are soft, and the semi-evergreen belt is the safer example.
- NCERT writes 'Uttaranchal' (now Uttarakhand), 'mahogony' (mahogany) and 'Palconda/Palkonda' spellings; use the standard spellings in answers.
- Dated figures in the extract: wetland area 3.9 million hectares, about 70 per cent paddy, two Ramsar sites, mangrove area, national park and sanctuary counts, tiger numbers. Verify against current official reports.
- Definitions of 'natural vegetation' differ: NCERT (undisturbed over a long time) and Leong (wholly unaffected by humans).
- The 1988 policy's one-third overall and two-thirds hills split is standard knowledge, not in the extract (verify).
- Mangrove share: NCERT prints 4,992 sq. km with 7 per cent of the world's mangroves; the share appears carried over from an older edition's larger area (verify). Do not quote the share.

### Notes: Standard knowledge added (verify)

- Jackfruit is an evergreen tree; teak and mahua are deciduous (the last two are named in kegy105).
- Cashew and papaya are introduced from tropical America; red sanders (Pterocarpus santalinus) is endemic to a limited region of the Eastern Ghats in Andhra Pradesh.
- Moringa belongs to the family Moringaceae, not the legume family; tamarind is a legume native to tropical Africa and naturalised in South Asia; tamarind is a minor forest produce.
- Foxtail orchid (Rhynchostylis retusa) is an epiphytic orchid with a wide range in South and South-East Asia; it is the state flower of Assam and Arunachal Pradesh.
- Forest area (legally recorded forest land) differs from forest cover (land under tree canopy).
- Order of four states by forest cover percentage in the 2019 item: depends on the forest survey report of the time.
- Later changes to dated NCERT counts (verify): UNESCO's World Network of Biosphere Reserves reportedly gained the Cold Desert reserve (Himachal Pradesh) in 2025; the Wildlife (Protection) Act has been amended again after 1991.
- Evergreen patches in the Brahmaputra valley and adjoining hills below 200 cm of rain: not shown by the extracts; verify before using as an example.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Evergreen (no fixed leaf-fall) and semi-evergreen (evergreen plus moist deciduous mix).
- Moist deciduous (100-200 cm) and dry deciduous (70-100 cm).
- Native, endemic and introduced species.
- Forest area and forest cover.
- Natural (undisturbed) and semi-natural vegetation.
- Social forestry, agro-forestry, community forestry and farm forestry.

### Core concepts

- Climate decides the world belts; soil, relief and humans modify them.
- India's forests: evergreen, deciduous (moist, dry), thorn, montane, littoral and swamp.
- Rain bands: over 200, 100-200, 70-100, below 50 cm map to the first three groups.
- The Himalaya shows the tropical-to-tundra sequence with altitude.
- Human action (British era clearing, grazing) reshaped Indian forests.

### Practice

**Q.** Name the five forest groups of India in NCERT's classification.

**A.** Tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen, tropical deciduous, tropical thorn, montane, and littoral and swamp forests.

**Q.** Give the rainfall band for each of moist deciduous, dry deciduous and thorn forests.

**A.** Moist deciduous 100-200 cm, dry deciduous 70-100 cm, thorn below 50 cm.

**Q.** What is the difference between a deciduous and an evergreen forest?

**A.** Deciduous forests shed leaves in a season (usually dry); evergreen forests have no fixed leaf-fall and look green all year.

**Q.** Name two trees each of moist deciduous and thorn forests.

**A.** Moist deciduous: sal, teak, mahua. Thorn: babool, khejri, ber.

**Q.** Why does the east slope of the Western Ghats carry deciduous forest rather than evergreen?

**A.** It lies on the leeward side, with less rain than the windward west slope, so the forest is moist deciduous.

**Q.** Why is a single species such as teak an unreliable clue to the forest type?

**A.** Teak appears in the moist deciduous list and in the dry deciduous parkland, so rainfall and region are also needed.

**Q.** How does altitude produce a vegetation sequence in the Himalaya?

**A.** Temperature falls with height, so tropical deciduous forest at the foot gives way to wet temperate, pine, fir and alpine zones and finally mosses and lichens.

**Q.** Explain two effects of removing natural vegetation on the local environment.

**A.** The microclimate becomes drier and the soil is exposed to erosion; runoff increases while infiltration falls.

**Q.** A forest at 85 cm of rainfall is open and park-like, with teak and grass, and sheds all leaves in the dry season. Name its type and one more tree.

**A.** Dry deciduous forest; tendu, bel or axlewood.

**Q.** Place the following on a blank India outline: Sunderbans, Western Ghats west slope, Rajasthan thorn forest, Nilgiri sholas, north-eastern hills. Name the forest type for each.
