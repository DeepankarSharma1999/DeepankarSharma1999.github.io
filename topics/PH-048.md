### Bridge: Phase, path difference and optical path

A wave repeats itself every wavelength. If a wave travels one extra wavelength it comes back to the same point of its cycle, so one wavelength of extra path is a phase change of 2 pi radians (360 degrees). A path difference of half a wavelength is a phase difference of pi radians; a quarter wavelength is pi/2. In general the phase difference delta = 2 pi x (path difference) / lambda.

Two waves **in step** (phase difference 0, 2 pi, 4 pi ...) add: this is constructive interference. Two waves **half a cycle apart** (phase difference pi, 3 pi ...) cancel if they are equal: destructive interference.

In a medium of refractive index mu light travels more slowly and its wavelength shrinks to lambda/mu, so a geometric path L contains mu L / lambda wavelengths. The product mu L is the **optical path**. Always compare optical paths when light goes through different materials (Brij Lal PDF p. 330, Eqs. 14.3 to 14.5). Reflection at a boundary into a denser medium adds a further phase jump of pi (PDF p. 331).

**Example.** Light of wavelength 500 nm travels 1.0 micrometre further along one route than another in air. The path difference is 2 wavelengths, so the phase difference is 4 pi: the waves are in step.

### Bridge: Small angles, units and the identity 1 + cos A = 2 cos^2(A/2)

Three habits make every double-slit calculation safer.

**Habit 1: convert units first.** 1 nm = 10^-9 m, 1 Angstrom = 10^-10 m, 1 micrometre = 10^-6 m, 1 mm = 10^-3 m, 1 cm = 10^-2 m. A wavelength of 5100 Angstrom is 5.1 x 10^-7 m. Mixing millimetres and metres in one fraction is the commonest source of an answer wrong by a factor of 1000.

**Habit 2: small angles.** When the angle theta (in radians) is small, sin theta, tan theta and theta nearly agree: at 1 degree they are 0.017452, 0.017455 and 0.017453; at 5 degrees 0.08716, 0.08749 and 0.08727. A point on the screen at height x and distance D has tan theta = x/D, so for small angles x/D is the angle in radians. Laboratory fringes are well inside this range.

**Habit 3: the half-angle identity.** From cos 2A = 2 cos^2 A - 1 you get 1 + cos 2A = 2 cos^2 A. Writing 2A = delta: 1 + cos delta = 2 cos^2(delta/2). This is how 2 I0 (1 + cos delta) becomes 4 I0 cos^2(delta/2).

**Example.** 1 + cos 60 degrees = 1.5, and 2 cos^2 30 degrees = 2 x 0.75 = 1.5.

### 1. Orientation: what Young's experiment shows and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Describe the double-slit experiment in words, name its symbols, and list the three results and the five verified questions you are working toward.

## The picture first

Put a lamp behind a narrow slit S0. A little further on, a card carries two narrow parallel slits S1 and S2 a fraction of a millimetre apart. A screen stands about a metre beyond the card. You might expect two bright lines, one behind each slit. Instead the screen shows many bright and dark stripes of equal spacing, with a bright stripe on the central line. Cover one slit and the stripes vanish. The stripes appear only when light from both slits arrives together, so they come from the **superposition** of two waves. This is **interference**. Thomas Young gave the first demonstration in 1801 (Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam, PDF p. 327), and it was the first experimental evidence that light is a wave.

## Words you need now

- **Fringe**: one bright or dark stripe. **Order m**: the integer numbering the bright fringes outward from the centre (m = 0 is the central one).
- **Path difference** (Δ): how much further the light from S2 travels than the light from S1 to reach the same screen point.
- **Coherent**: two waves are coherent if their phase difference stays fixed in time; incoherent if it wanders.
- **Fringe width β**: the distance between two neighbouring bright (or dark) fringes.

## Notation used throughout

λ is the wavelength; d the distance between the slit centres; D the distance from the slits to the screen; x the distance of a screen point P from the central point O (O lies on the perpendicular bisector of S1S2); δ the phase difference; I0 the intensity that one slit alone would give at the screen, so that the bright fringes of two equal slits reach 4 I0. (In the Fraunhofer double slit of Day 22 lesson PH-055 the book writes the slit separation as a + b; do not mix the two notations.)

## Three results to carry out of this lesson

1. Bright fringes at x = m λ D / d; dark fringes halfway between.
2. Fringe width β = λ D / d (equal everywhere on the screen).
3. Intensity I = 4 I0 cos²(π x d / λ D), which varies between 0 and 4 I0.

The second half of the title, **coherence**, is the reason the experiment needs the first slit S0 and a source of narrow spectral width (modules 6 and 7).

### 2. Foundation: adding two waves and the two-beam intensity formula

*Objective:* Derive I = I1 + I2 + 2 sqrt(I1 I2) cos(delta) and the equal-wave form 4 I0 cos^2(delta/2), and explain why the average equals I1 + I2.

## Intensity is amplitude squared

A light wave carries an electric field E that oscillates. The intensity I (energy arriving per unit area per unit time) is proportional to the square of the amplitude: I is proportional to E² (Brij Lal, PDF p. 335). Only the ratios of intensities matter in this lesson, so the constant of proportionality is dropped.

## Two waves of the same frequency

At the screen point P let the two waves have fields

E1 sin ωt   and   E2 sin(ωt + δ),

where δ is the phase difference. By the principle of superposition the resultant field is the sum. Expand sin(ωt + δ) and collect terms in sin ωt and cos ωt:

E = (E1 + E2 cos δ) sin ωt + (E2 sin δ) cos ωt.

Any combination of the form P sin ωt + Q cos ωt equals E_R sin(ωt + φ) with E_R cos φ = P and E_R sin φ = Q. Squaring and adding, E_R² = P² + Q², so

$$ E_R² = E1² + E2² + 2 E1 E2 cos δ $$

(Eq. 14.21, PDF p. 334). (The phasor picture gives the same result by the cosine rule.) Because intensity is amplitude squared,

$$ I = I1 + I2 + 2 √(I1 I2) cos δ $$

The last term is the **interference term**: it is what makes the result differ from I1 + I2.

## Bright and dark points

- Maximum when cos δ = +1, that is δ = 0, ±2π, ±4π ...: I_max = (√I1 + √I2)².
- Minimum when cos δ = −1, that is δ = ±π, ±3π ...: I_min = (√I1 − √I2)².

For two **equal** waves, I1 = I2 = I0 and

$$ I = 2 I0 (1 + cos δ) = 4 I0 cos²(δ/2) $$

using the identity 1 + cos δ = 2 cos²(δ/2) (bridge module B2). The maximum is 4 I0, not 2 I0: two equal waves in step give twice the amplitude, hence four times the intensity (PDF pp. 335 and 336). The minimum is zero.

## Energy is redistributed, not created

Average cos δ over many fringes and it is zero, so the average intensity is I1 + I2 = 2 I0. The pattern borrows light from the dark fringes (which get less than 2 I0) and gives it to the bright ones (which get more). *Check:* the mean of 4 I0 cos²(δ/2) over a full cycle is 4 I0 × ½ = 2 I0, which is correct.

## Unequal waves, a quick example

Let I2 = 4 I1. Then I_max = 9 I1 and I_min = I1; the mean (9 + 1)/2 = 5 I1 = I1 + I2 is conserved. The dark fringes are no longer completely dark (module 4 turns this into the visibility).

## Incoherent waves

If the phase difference δ jumps about at random, the cos δ term averages to zero faster than any detector can respond, and the detector records only I1 + I2: uniform light with no fringes (PDF pp. 336 and 337). The book quotes phase fluctuations of the order of 10^8 per second for ordinary light. This is exactly Grimaldi's failure in 1665 (module 7).

**Beginner trap:** writing the maximum as 2 I0. Add the amplitudes first, then square.

### 3. Core: path difference, bright and dark fringes, and the fringe width

*Objective:* Derive the path difference x d / D, the bright and dark positions and the fringe width beta = lambda D / d, stating every assumption.

## What we want

A rule that tells us where on the screen the fringes are. The light reaching a point P from S1 and from S2 starts in phase (S1 and S2 lie the same distance from S0, so the same wavefront reaches both at the same instant), so everything depends on the path difference Δ = S2P − S1P.

## Set-up and assumptions

- S1 and S2 are narrow slits, parallel, in phase, separated by d (centre to centre).
- The light is monochromatic, travelling in air (optical path = geometric path).
- The screen is parallel to the line S1S2 at distance D, with D much larger than d; O is the point on the perpendicular bisector of S1S2, and x is measured from O along the screen.
- We look only at points with x much smaller than D (small angles).

## Derivation 1 (Brij Lal, PDF pp. 338 and 339)

Place S1 at distance d/2 on one side of the bisector and S2 at d/2 on the other, with P at height x above O. Then, by Pythagoras,

S1P² = D² + (x − d/2)²    and    S2P² = D² + (x + d/2)².

Subtract: S2P² − S1P² = (x + d/2)² − (x − d/2)² = 2 x d.

The left side factorises: (S2P − S1P)(S2P + S1P) = 2 x d. When D is much larger than d and x, both distances are close to D, so S2P + S1P ≈ 2D and

$$ Δ = S2P − S1P ≈ x d / D $$

Equivalently, for a far screen the extra path is d sin θ, and sin θ ≈ tan θ = x/D for small θ.

*Numbers (recomputed with python).* d = 0.5 mm, D = 1 m. At x = 1 cm the exact difference is 4.99975 µm against x d/D = 5.00000 µm (0.005 per cent); at x = 10 cm, 49.752 against 50.000 µm (0.50 per cent); at x = 30 cm, 143.67 against 150.00 µm (4.4 per cent). The formula is safe for a laboratory screen.

## Bright fringes

Constructive interference needs Δ to be a whole number of wavelengths (Eq. 14.13):

x d / D = m λ, so

$$ x_m = m λ D / d ,   m = 0, ±1, ±2, ... $$

m = 0 is the central bright fringe at O, where Δ = 0.

## Dark fringes

Destructive interference needs Δ to be an odd number of half wavelengths (Eq. 14.14):

x d / D = (m + ½) λ, so x = (m + ½) λ D / d, with m = 0, ±1, ... The first dark fringe on either side of the centre is at λ D / 2d. (Brij Lal writes (2m + 1) λ/2 and calls m = 0 the first-order dark fringe.)

## Fringe width

Neighbouring bright fringes: x_{m+1} − x_m = λ D / d. The same holds for the dark ones, so

$$ β = λ D / d $$

**What this says.** β does not depend on the order m, so the fringes are equally spaced; each bright strip and each dark strip occupies half a fringe width. The angular spacing is β/D = λ/d.

*Checks:* λ D / d has the dimension of length. As d → 0, β → ∞ (one broad patch); as λ → 0, β → 0 (ray optics).

**Worked example.** λ = 600 nm, D = 1.5 m, d = 0.3 mm: β = 6.0 × 10^-7 × 1.5 / 3.0 × 10^-4 = 3.0 mm.

**Shape.** Surfaces of constant Δ are hyperbolas with S1 and S2 as foci; on a distant screen the stripes look straight. A hand-drawn answer shows S1, S2, the screen, O, the two rays to P and Δ marked.

### 4. Core: the intensity along the screen, visibility and the 2024 numerical

*Objective:* Convert path difference to phase, write I(x) = 4 I0 cos^2(pi x d / lambda D), define visibility and solve the 2024 intensity numerical.

## From path difference to phase difference

One wavelength of extra path means a phase difference of 2π (bridge module B1), so a path difference Δ in air gives

δ = 2π Δ / λ.

Put Δ = x d / D from module 3:

$$ δ = 2π x d / (λ D) = 2π x / β $$

## The intensity along the screen (derivation 2)

Take two equal slits so that I1 = I2 = I0 (the intensity each would give alone). Module 2 gave I = 4 I0 cos²(δ/2). Substituting δ/2 = π x d / (λ D):

$$ I(x) = 4 I0 cos²( π x d / (λ D) ) = 4 I0 cos²( π x / β ) $$

This is Eq. 14.25 with the phase written in terms of the screen position (PDF p. 336).

**Reading the formula.**

- At x = m β, cos² = 1, so I = 4 I0: bright fringes.
- At x = (m + ½) β, cos² = 0, so I = 0: dark fringes.
- Between them the intensity rises and falls smoothly as cos², not as sharp lines. The fringes are **sinusoidal-squared** bands.

*Sketch:* a curve oscillating about the level 2 I0, peaking at 4 I0 at x = 0, ±β, ±2β ... and touching zero halfway between; label the axis in units of β.

## Average intensity and conservation of energy

The average of cos² over a cycle is ½, so the average intensity is 4 I0 × ½ = 2 I0, equal to the sum of the two single-slit intensities I0 + I0. Interference moves light about; it does not make or destroy any.

## Visibility (contrast)

Brij Lal defines the **visibility** of the fringes as

$$ V = (I_max − I_min) / (I_max + I_min) $$

(Eq. 14.40, PDF p. 349). For equal slits V = 1. For unequal slits, with I_max = (√I1 + √I2)² and I_min = (√I1 − √I2)²:

V = [ (a + b)² − (a − b)² ] / [ (a + b)² + (a − b)² ] with a = √I1 and b = √I2, which equals 4ab / (2a² + 2b²) = 2 √(I1 I2) / (I1 + I2).

*Examples (recomputed):* I2/I1 = 1, 4, 9 give V = 1, 0.8, 0.6. V falls as the slits become more unequal even for perfectly coherent light. The book adds that V is always 1 for monochromatic light; that holds only for equal intensities, as its own Eq. 14.41 shows (PDF p. 350).

## The 2024 numerical, solved

The intensity at the central maximum is 2 × 10^-3 W/m². The path difference at a point is λ/6.

1. Assumption: two equal slits, slit width negligible, so the central maximum is 4 I0 = 2 × 10^-3 W/m² and I0 = 5 × 10^-4 W/m².
2. Phase difference δ = 2π Δ / λ = 2π/6 = π/3 (60 degrees).
3. I = 4 I0 cos²(δ/2) = 2 × 10^-3 × cos²(30 degrees) = 2 × 10^-3 × 0.75.

$$ I = 1.5 × 10^-3 W/m² $$

*Cross-check by the full formula:* I = 2 I0 (1 + cos δ) = 2 × 5 × 10^-4 × (1 + 0.5) = 1.5 × 10^-3 W/m². The result is 75 per cent of the maximum. Using cos² of the whole phase (60 degrees, giving 25 per cent) is the usual wrong answer.

**Stopping rule.** If you can write I = 4 I0 cos²(δ/2) and explain its three ingredients (equal amplitudes, phase from path difference, identity), you can stop here.

### 5. Core: what changes the fringe width, white light and the thin-sheet shift

*Objective:* Predict the change in fringe width when lambda, D, d or the medium changes, and compute the shift caused by a thin sheet over one slit.

## One formula, four levers

β = λ D / d. Each symbol is a lever in the laboratory, and the 2020 question asks you to discuss exactly this (Brij Lal, PDF p. 340).

| Change | Effect on β | Effect on the pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Longer wavelength (red for blue) | β grows in proportion to λ | red fringes wider than blue; white light gives a white centre and coloured edges |
| Screen further away | β grows in proportion to D | same fringes, spread out; the angular spacing λ/d is unchanged |
| Slits closer | β grows as 1/d | wide, easy-to-see fringes; wide slit spacing gives fringes that blur together |
| Set-up in a liquid of index μ | β falls to β/μ | fringes crowd closer |

*Why the liquid case works.* The frequency of light does not change in a medium, but its speed drops to c/μ, so the wavelength becomes λ/μ (PDF p. 329). Equivalently the optical path is μ times the geometric path (Eq. 14.3), so brightness needs μ x d / D = m λ and β = λ D / (μ d).

*Numbers (recomputed).* λ = 589.3 nm, D = 1 m, d = 0.5 mm gives β = 1.1786 mm in air and 1.1786 / 1.33 = 0.886 mm under water (μ = 1.33, standard value). Doubling D, or halving d, gives 2.357 mm.

## White light

At x = 0 the path difference is zero for every colour, so every colour is bright there and the centre is white. Away from the centre the bright fringes of different colours separate, red further out than blue, because β is larger for red (700 nm against 400 nm: ratio 1.75). A few coloured fringes then blur into general illumination (PDF pp. 345 and 350). With monochromatic light all bright fringes look alike, so the white central fringe is used to find the zero order.

## A thin sheet in one beam: the shift of the fringes

Cover S1 with a thin transparent sheet of thickness t and index μ. The light from S1 now spends a length t in glass, so its optical path is S1P + (μ − 1) t: the sheet replaces a length t of air (which would count t) by μ t, a net gain of (μ − 1) t.

1. The zero-order fringe is where the optical paths are equal: S1P + (μ − 1) t = S2P, so S2P − S1P = (μ − 1) t.
2. With S2P − S1P = x d / D the central fringe moves to

$$ x = (μ − 1) t D / d $$

(Eq. 14.37, PDF p. 346, written there for the biprism but identical here).

3. The shift is toward the covered slit: its route is optically longer, so the equal-path point lies nearer to it.
4. In fringe widths, shift / β = (μ − 1) t / λ, the number of extra wavelengths introduced.

*Numbers (recomputed).* μ = 1.5, t = 10 µm, λ = 500 nm, D = 1 m, d = 1 mm. β = 0.5 mm; (μ − 1) t / λ = 10 fringes; shift = 5.0 mm toward the covered slit. The spacing and brightness are unchanged: the whole pattern slides.

*Check:* μ = 1 or t = 0 gives no shift. This catches the common slip of writing μ t instead of (μ − 1) t.

### 6. Core: coherence in time - wave trains, coherence length and the conditions for fringes

*Objective:* Explain wave trains, coherence time and length, use l_coh = lambda^2 / delta-lambda, and list the seven conditions for a visible pattern with reasons.

## Why the experiment needs a special kind of light

The formulas of modules 3 and 4 assumed that light from S1 and S2 keeps a fixed phase relation. Real light does not do this automatically.

## Wave trains

An atom emits light as it falls from an excited state; the transition lasts about 10^-8 s (PDF p. 341; PDF p. 402), after which another burst follows with a new random phase. Each burst is a **wave train** of finite length. If one lasts Δt = 10^-8 s, its length in vacuum is l = c Δt = 3 m (Eq. 16.1) and it holds N = l / λ oscillations: 6 × 10^6 at 500 nm (the book writes "about a million", PDF p. 403). Many atoms emit independently, so the phase jumps at random about 10^8 times a second.

## Coherence time and length

- **Coherence time** t_coh: how long the phase stays predictable, about Δt.
- **Coherence length** l_coh = c t_coh: the length over which a wave train is a clean sine wave.

A train lasting Δt has a frequency spread Δν = 1 / Δt (Eq. 16.6). With ν = c / λ,

$$ l_coh = c / Δν = λ² / Δλ $$

(Eqs. 16.7 and 16.11, PDF pp. 404 and 405). A narrow line means a long train. The book's worked examples recompute correctly: Δt = 4 × 10^-12 s gives 1.2 mm; a CO2 line of width 10^-5 nm at 10.6 µm gives 11.2 km (Δt = 10^-12 s at 5893 Å: 0.3 mm).

## Condition on the path difference

The two routes can interfere only if parts of the same wave train still overlap, so Δ must be less than l_coh (Eq. 14.32, PDF p. 342). As Δ approaches l_coh the contrast fades. In Young's experiment Δ = x d / D, so fringes extend to x = l_coh D / d, that is to order m of about l_coh / λ = λ / Δλ.

*Numbers (recomputed).* White light has Δλ roughly 300 nm around 550 nm, so l_coh is about 1 µm and m reaches only about 2. For a sodium lamp Chapter 16 gives of the order of 1 mm (PDF p. 408) and Chapter 14 gives 2 to 3 cm (PDF p. 341): the chapters disagree. With 1 mm, m is about 1,700 at 589.3 nm; with d = 0.5 mm and D = 1 m the screen would have to reach x = 2 m, so on a normal screen the fringes end with the screen, not with the lamp. The 3 m figure is the natural-lifetime limit; in a real lamp Doppler motion and collisions broaden the line, so real trains are far shorter (Day 20 lesson PH-068; verify). Day 18 lesson PH-051 treats the same disagreement.

## The conditions for good fringes (PDF p. 342)

**For sustained fringes**

1. Same frequency (otherwise δ drifts and the pattern washes out).
2. Coherence: a fixed phase difference, in practice by splitting one source.
3. Path difference smaller than l_coh.
4. Same plane of polarisation; perpendicular polarisations do not interfere.

**For a distinct pattern**

5. Slits close together (otherwise β is too small to resolve).
6. Screen far enough away (β grows with D).
7. Nearly equal amplitudes, so that the minima are dark (visibility, module 4).

A narrow source slit (module 7) is added in practice.

## Why two separate lamps cannot do it

Independent sources change phase relation in about 10^-8 s, too fast for any eye or film, so only I1 + I2 is recorded (PDF pp. 336 and 337).

**Beginner trap:** treating "monochromatic" and "coherent" as the same word. A nearly monochromatic extended source can still lack spatial coherence (module 7).

### 7. Core: coherence in space - why the first slit is needed

*Objective:* Derive the condition b d / s much less than lambda on the source slit, explain Grimaldi's failure, and state the effect of moving the source slit.

## Grimaldi's failure and Young's cure

In 1665 Grimaldi let sunlight through two pinholes and saw only uniform light. Young (1801) put one pinhole before the other two. The extra aperture selects a small piece of the source, so the light reaching the two slits is correlated (PDF p. 337).

## The idea: spatial (lateral) coherence

**Temporal** coherence concerns the phase relation between two *times* at one place (module 6). **Spatial** coherence concerns the relation between two *places* across the beam. A point source gives equal phases at all points equally far from it; an extended source, made of independent emitters, gives less. The contrast of the fringes measures it (PDF pp. 407 and 408).

## Condition on the source slit

Take a source slit S0 of width b at distance s from the double slit (separation d). A source point at transverse position y sends a wave to the slits that is tilted by the angle y / s, and across the separation d this tilt adds a path y d / s in favour of one slit.

1. Each source point makes its own fringe pattern, shifted along the screen in proportion to y.
2. The two edges of S0 (y = ± b / 2) are shifted in path by b d / s relative to each other.
3. If this is much less than a wavelength, the patterns overlap and the contrast stays high:

$$ b d / s ≪ λ    that is    b ≪ λ s / d $$

The book writes b sin α ≪ λ / 2 (Eq. 16.16), where the double slit subtends 2α at S0, so sin α ≈ d / 2s (PDF p. 409): the same condition. A shift of half a fringe (b d / s = λ / 2) makes the two edge patterns cancel; a shift of one full fringe puts them bright on bright again.

## What a uniform slit actually does

Summing the shifted patterns over a uniform slit gives visibility |sin u / u| with u = π b d / (λ s), derived in Day 20 lesson PH-068 (which calls the source distance D; here it is s); its first zero is at b d = λ s, twice the two-edge cancellation value. The order of magnitude is the same: d must be small compared with λ s / b.

*Numbers (recomputed).* λ = 550 nm, s = 0.5 m. A source slit b = 0.1 mm gives a limit λ s / b = 2.75 mm, and slits 0.5 mm apart give visibility 0.95. Opening S0 to b = 1 mm cuts the limit to 0.275 mm and the same slits give 0.095 (beyond the first zero: weak, contrast-reversed fringes): the fringes have almost vanished, as observed when S0 is widened (PDF p. 409).

## Moving the source slit

If S0 moves sideways by y, the whole pattern moves the opposite way by y D / s. The source adds a path y d / s in favour of one slit, and the zero-order fringe is where the screen geometry compensates it: x = −y D / s.

## Two ways out

- A laser is coherent across its beam, so fringes appear with no S0 at all (PDF p. 410; Day 20 lesson PH-068).
- Amplitude splitting (module 8) allows a broad source.

*Limiting cases:* b → 0 (point source) gives visibility 1; large b gives uniform light.

**A trade-off:** a wider S0 brightens the screen but needs a smaller d to keep b d / s below λ.

### 8. Master: division of wavefront and division of amplitude, with the biprism, Lloyd's mirror and double mirror

*Objective:* Explain the two ways of making coherent beams with named examples and write the comparison needed for the 2017 question.

## The question (2017 Q1(d), 10 marks)

"Explain with proper example the interferences due to division of wavefront and division of amplitude." This module is the answer in teaching form.

## The logic

Interference needs two coherent beams. Two independent sources cannot be coherent (module 6), so both beams must be made from **one** source. There are two ways (Brij Lal, PDF pp. 342 and 343).

**Division of wavefront.** Different parts of one wavefront from a narrow source travel by different routes and meet again. Each part is a piece of the same wavefront, so the phase relation is fixed. Examples: Young's double slit (module 3), Fresnel's biprism, Lloyd's single mirror, Fresnel's double mirror. It needs a narrow source, because the pieces of wavefront must stay correlated (module 7).

**Division of amplitude.** A beam is split by partial reflection and transmission at a surface into a reflected and a transmitted part (the amplitude, and so the energy, is shared); the parts take different routes and recombine. Examples: thin films, the wedge, Newton's rings, the Michelson interferometer (Day 17 lesson PH-050, Day 18 lesson PH-051) and the Fabry-Perot interferometer (Day 20 lesson PH-053). An extended source is acceptable, because every source point makes the same pattern and the patterns add (the fringes are localised on a particular surface; standard knowledge, verify in PH-050).

## Comparison

| Feature | Division of wavefront | Division of amplitude |
|---|---|---|
| What is divided | Parts of one wavefront | The amplitude at a partly reflecting surface |
| Source | Narrow slit or point | Extended source acceptable |
| Devices | Young's slits, biprism, Lloyd's mirror, double mirror | Thin films, Newton's rings, Michelson, Fabry-Perot |
| Fringes | Non-localised: seen wherever the beams overlap (PDF p. 349) | Localised on a surface |
| Coherence emphasis | Spatial coherence across the wavefront | Path difference below l_coh |

## Worked devices (short)

- **Fresnel biprism** (two thin prisms base to base, apex angle about 179 degrees, PDF p. 343): refracts two halves of the wavefront so that they seem to come from two virtual images S1 and S2 of the slit. The fringe width is β = λ D / d, with D from the virtual sources to the screen and d their separation (PDF p. 344).
- **Lloyd's mirror** (a plane mirror at grazing incidence, PDF pp. 346 and 347): the direct beam from the slit and the beam reflected from the mirror interfere, the reflected one seeming to come from a virtual image. At the point O on the mirror surface the geometric paths are equal, yet the central fringe is **dark**: reflection from the denser medium adds a phase change of π, a half-wavelength of path. Only fringes on one side are seen.
- **Fresnel's double mirror** (two plane mirrors inclined at a very small angle θ): the images S1 and S2 of the slit are d = 2 a θ apart, where a is the distance from the slit to the line where the mirrors meet. With the screen a further distance b away, D = a + b and β = λ (a + b) / (2 a θ) (Eq. 14.39, PDF p. 348). *Check:* for λ = 589 nm, a = 0.5 m, b = 1.5 m, θ = 1 mrad, β = 1.18 mm.

### 9. Master: variants and limits - finite slit width, unequal slits, oblique light and fringe counting

## What this module is for

## 1. Slits of finite width

A real slit of width a diffracts the light, so the screen intensity is the two-slit result multiplied by the single-slit factor:

$$ I = 4 I0 (sin α / α)² cos² γ ,   α = π a sin θ / λ ,   γ = π d sin θ / λ $$

with I0 now the single-slit intensity at the centre (derived in Day 22 lesson PH-055; the single-slit factor is in Day 21 lesson PH-054). Here γ is a phase, not the fringe width β. With sin θ = x / D the cos² γ term is exactly the cos²(π x d / λ D) of module 4. The factor (sin α / α)² slowly reduces the brightness away from the centre. The central envelope has width 2 λ D / a against a fringe spacing λ D / d, so it holds about 2 d / a fringes. For d / a = 10 exactly (d = 0.3 mm, a = 0.03 mm) the edge orders ±10 are missing, leaving 19 (Day 22 lesson PH-055, missing orders). Brij Lal's Young analysis (PDF pp. 338 to 340) takes a as negligible.

## 2. Slits of unequal intensity

The visibility is V = 2 √(I1 I2) / (I1 + I2) (module 4). The positions of the fringes do not change; only the contrast does, and the minima are no longer dark even for perfectly coherent light.

## 3. Oblique illumination (standard result; verify)

Let a plane wave arrive at angle i from the axis, travelling upward in the sense in which θ is measured. It reaches the lower slit S2 earlier than S1 by the path d sin i, so the total path difference (S2 route minus S1 route) is

$$ Δ = d (sin θ − sin i) $$

Bright fringes lie at d (sin θ − sin i) = m λ. The central fringe (Δ = 0) is at θ = i, continuing the incident direction. This agrees with the source-shifting rule of module 7 (source moved down by y gives i = y / s and a central fringe higher by y D / s). The angular fringe spacing is unchanged, about λ / d. The same expression appears as the grating equation in Day 23 lesson PH-056.

## 4. Fringes everywhere in the overlap

With a narrow source the fringes are **non-localised**: they exist at every distance where the two beams overlap, with a spacing proportional to D (PDF p. 349). They can be caught on a screen at any distance.

## 5. When the pattern is too fine to see

The eye sees the fringe width β divided by the viewing distance, not the angle λ / d at the slits. Fringes are lost when this falls below about one arcminute, about 0.07 mm at 25 cm (standard; verify). For d = 5 mm, D = 1 m, λ = 550 nm, β = 0.11 mm (1.5 arcminutes at 25 cm): still resolved. For d = 10 mm, D = 0.5 m, β = 27.5 µm (0.38 arcminute): uniform light, though interference still occurs. This is condition 5 of module 6.

## 6. Counting fringes

The number of bright fringes between x1 and x2 is about (x2 − x1) / β. For D = 1 m, d = 0.5 mm, λ = 589.3 nm over a 2 cm strip: 0.02 / 1.1786 × 10^-3 = 17.0, about 17 fringes.

### Notes: Definitions

- Interference: redistribution of light energy when two or more coherent waves are superposed, giving bright and dark fringes (Brij Lal PDF p. 333).
- Coherent waves: waves of the same frequency that keep a constant phase difference over the time and distance of observation.
- Optical path: refractive index times geometric path; a path difference of one wavelength is a phase difference of 2π.
- Fringe width β: distance between two successive bright (or dark) fringes; order m: integer numbering the bright fringes from the centre.
- Visibility V = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min).
- Coherence length l_coh = c t_coh = λ²/Δλ: the length of a wave train over which the phase is predictable.
- Temporal (longitudinal) coherence: phase relation along the beam, set by the spectral width. Spatial (lateral) coherence: phase relation across the beam, set by the source size.
- Division of wavefront: coherent beams made from different parts of one wavefront. Division of amplitude: made by partial reflection and transmission.

- E_R² = E1² + E2² + 2 E1 E2 cos δ;  I = I1 + I2 + 2√(I1 I2) cos δ;  equal waves: I = 4 I0 cos²(δ/2).
- δ = 2π Δ/λ;  Δ = x d/D (small angles) or d sin θ.
- Bright: x_m = m λ D/d;  dark: x = (m + ½) λ D/d;  fringe width β = λ D/d;  angular fringe width λ/d.
- I(x) = 4 I0 cos²(π x d/λ D);  mean intensity 2 I0 = I1 + I2.

- Thin sheet (μ, t) over one slit: shift = (μ − 1) t D/d toward the covered slit; number of fringes (μ − 1) t/λ.
- V = 2√(I1 I2)/(I1 + I2).
- l_coh = c Δt = λ²/Δλ = c/Δν;  N = l/λ;  fringes fade as the path difference approaches l_coh (order about λ/Δλ).

### Notes: What changes the fringe width and the pattern

- λ up: β up in proportion; red fringes wider than blue; white light gives a white centre and coloured edges.
- D up: β up in proportion; angular spacing unchanged.
- d down: β up as 1/d; fringes easier to see; d too large makes fringes unresolvable.
- Medium of index μ: β/μ; the central fringe stays at the geometric centre.
- Sheet over one slit: pattern shifts by (μ − 1) t D/d toward the covered slit, spacing unchanged.
- Source slit widened: contrast falls (spatial coherence).
- Source shifted by y: pattern shifts by y D/s the opposite way.
- Slit width a increased: envelope narrows and outer fringes fade (PH-055).
- Unequal slits: visibility falls, positions unchanged.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Monochromatic is not the same as coherent: a nearly single-colour extended source can still lack spatial coherence.
- Temporal coherence (along the beam, bandwidth) is not spatial coherence (across the beam, source size).
- Maximum intensity is 4 I0, not 2 I0; the average over the pattern is 2 I0.
- Path difference λ/6 gives phase π/3 and I = I_max cos²(π/6); do not use cos² of the whole phase.
- The sheet changes the optical path by (μ − 1) t, not μ t.
- Fringe width β is the distance between neighbouring bright fringes; the dark-to-bright distance is β/2.
- Fresnel biprism and Lloyd's mirror use wavefront division even though a mirror or prism is involved.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- The result Δ = xd/D needs D ≫ d and small angles; beyond about 5 degrees use d sin θ and the exact geometry.
- Visibility is 1 only for equal intensities and perfect coherence.
- Lloyd's mirror: the central fringe at the mirror surface is dark, not bright.
- Laser light needs no source slit.
- For slits of finite width the intensities of the fringes follow the single-slit envelope; some orders may vanish (Day 22 lesson PH-055).

- **foundationChecks:**

    - **a:** 3π (an odd multiple of π): the waves are in opposite phase.
  - - **q:** Two equal waves of intensity I0 are in step. What is the resultant intensity, and why is it not 2 I0?
    - **a:** 4 I0: the amplitudes add (2A), and intensity goes as amplitude squared (4A²).
  - - **q:** What does β = λ D / d say about fringes produced by red light compared with blue light in the same set-up?
    - **a:** Red has the larger λ, so its fringes are wider than blue ones.
  - - **q:** Why is a source slit used in front of the double slit?
    - **a:** To select a small, spatially coherent piece of the source so that S1 and S2 are lit by correlated light.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Explain why the average intensity over the pattern is 2 I0 although the bright fringes reach 4 I0.
    - **a:** The interference term averages to zero; the dark fringes get less than 2 I0 and the bright ones more, so energy is redistributed, not created.
  - - **q:** Explain with a reason why two separate sodium lamps behind S1 and S2 give no steady fringes.
    - **a:** Their phases are independent and change randomly about 10^8 times per second, so the cos δ term averages to zero in any detector and only I1 + I2 is recorded.
  - - **q:** Why does the central fringe of Lloyd's mirror appear dark?
    - **a:** The reflected beam undergoes a phase change of π on reflection from the denser surface, which is equivalent to a half-wavelength of path, so the two beams cancel at the point where the geometric paths are equal.
  - - **q:** A student says 'monochromatic light is coherent light'. Comment.
    - **a:** Not necessarily: a nearly single-colour extended source has small temporal bandwidth but may lack spatial coherence; coherence needs a narrow bandwidth and a small effective source size, or splitting of one source.

  - - **id:** PH-048-D1
    - **task:** Derive the fringe positions and the fringe width in Young's double-slit experiment (target about 12 minutes for 10 marks; practice target, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Sketch: source slit S0, slits S1 and S2 separated by d, screen at distance D, centre O, point P at height x; S1 and S2 are in phase because they are equidistant from S0.
      - Assumptions: narrow slits, monochromatic light, air, D ≫ d, x ≪ D.
      - Place S1 at +d/2 and S2 at −d/2 from the axis: S1P² = D² + (x − d/2)², S2P² = D² + (x + d/2)².
      - Subtract: S2P² − S1P² = (x + d/2)² − (x − d/2)² = 2 x d.
      - Factorise: (S2P − S1P)(S2P + S1P) = 2 x d. With S2P + S1P ≈ 2D the path difference is Δ = x d / D.
      - Bright fringes: Δ = m λ, so x_m = m λ D / d with m = 0, ±1, ±2, ...; dark fringes: Δ = (m + ½) λ, so x = (m + ½) λ D / d.
      - Fringe width: x_{m+1} − x_m = λ D / d; it is independent of m, so the fringes are equally spaced.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimension: λ D / d is length × length / length = length.
      - Limit d → 0: β → ∞ (a single broad patch); limit λ → 0: β → 0 (ray optics).
      - Numerical check: λ = 600 nm, D = 1.5 m, d = 0.3 mm gives β = 3.0 mm.
      - Approximation test: for d = 0.5 mm, D = 1 m, x = 10 cm the exact path difference differs from x d / D by 0.50 per cent.
    - **traps:**
      - Writing D for the distance to the source slit.
      - Not stating that S1 and S2 are in phase.
      - Forgetting the small-angle condition.
  - - **id:** PH-048-D2
    - **task:** Derive the intensity distribution I(x) in the double-slit experiment and find its maximum, minimum and average (target about 12 minutes for 10 marks; practice target).
    - **steps:**
      - Fields at P: E1 sin ωt and E2 sin(ωt + δ), where δ = 2π Δ / λ is the phase difference.
      - Add and expand: E = (E1 + E2 cos δ) sin ωt + (E2 sin δ) cos ωt = E_R sin(ωt + φ).
      - Square and add the coefficients: E_R² = (E1 + E2 cos δ)² + (E2 sin δ)² = E1² + E2² + 2 E1 E2 cos δ.
      - Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared: I = I1 + I2 + 2 √(I1 I2) cos δ.
      - Equal slits I1 = I2 = I0: I = 2 I0 (1 + cos δ) = 4 I0 cos²(δ/2), using 1 + cos δ = 2 cos²(δ/2).
      - Insert δ = 2π x d / (λ D): I(x) = 4 I0 cos²(π x d / (λ D)).
      - Maximum 4 I0 at x = m β, minimum 0 at x = (m + ½) β; the average over a cycle is 2 I0 = I1 + I2.
    - **checks:**
      - Limit δ = 0: I = 4 I0 (not 2 I0).
      - Energy: the mean of 4 I0 cos²(δ/2) over a cycle is 2 I0 (numerically verified).
      - Unequal slits: I_max = (√I1 + √I2)², I_min = (√I1 − √I2)².
      - Numerical check: I_max = 2 × 10^-3 W/m², Δ = λ/6 gives I = 1.5 × 10^-3 W/m².
    - **traps:**
      - Writing I_max = 2 I0.
      - Using δ instead of δ/2 inside cos².
      - Omitting the sketch of I against x.
  - - **id:** PH-048-D3
    - **task:** Show that a thin sheet of index μ and thickness t over one slit shifts the central fringe by (μ − 1) t D / d, and give the direction (target about 8 minutes; practice target).
    - **steps:**
      - Let the sheet cover S1. The optical path from S1 to P is S1P + (μ − 1) t (the sheet replaces a length t of air by a length μ t).

- I = I1 + I2 + 2√(I1I2) cos δ; equal slits 4I0 cos²(δ/2): max 4I0, min 0, mean 2I0; δ = 2πΔ/λ, halve it inside cos².
- V = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin) = 2√(I1I2)/(I1 + I2): 1 only for equal beams; I2 = 4I1 gives 0.8; positions unchanged.
- Medium: β/μ. Sheet: extra path (μ − 1)t, shift (μ − 1)tD/d toward the covered slit, spacing unchanged. Source slit: bd/s ≪ λ; source shift y moves pattern yD/s the other way.
- Wavefront division needs a narrow source (Young, biprism, Lloyd, double mirror); amplitude division accepts an extended one (films, Newton's rings, Michelson). Lloyd's central fringe is dark.

### Look-alike contrasts

- - **id:** X-WAVEFRONTDIV-AMPLITUDEDIV
  - **a:**
    - **topicId:** PH-048
    - **text:** Division of wavefront: different parts of one wavefront from a narrow source travel separate routes; Young's slits, Fresnel biprism, Lloyd's mirror, Fresnel's double mirror.
  - **b:**
    - **topicId:** PH-048
    - **text:** Division of amplitude: one beam split by partial reflection and transmission; thin films, Newton's rings, Michelson, Fabry-Perot; an extended source is acceptable.
  - **difference:** What is split decides the type, not the hardware: a mirror or prism can still divide the wavefront; only amplitude division tolerates an extended source.

    - **topicId:** PH-048
