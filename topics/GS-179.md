## GS-179: Legislative procedure and types of Bills

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Union government

### Bridge: Parliament's three parts and why there are two Houses

'Parliament' is the national legislature. Under the Constitution it consists of the President and two Houses (Article 79 is the standard reference; the NCERT simply describes the two Houses). The two Houses are the Council of States, called the Rajya Sabha, and the House of the People, called the Lok Sabha. A legislature with two Houses is 'bicameral'. States may choose one or two Houses; as of the NCERT edition used here (Reprint 2026-27) six states (Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh) are bicameral. The number can change when a State creates or abolishes its Legislative Council, so verify the current count against an official list before quoting it.

The Lok Sabha is directly elected: the country is divided into 543 territorial constituencies of roughly equal population and each elects one member by universal adult suffrage. Its maximum term is five years, and it can be dissolved earlier. The Rajya Sabha represents the states and is indirectly elected: the elected members of each State Legislative Assembly elect that state's members, with the number of seats per state fixed by the Fourth Schedule (Uttar Pradesh 31, Sikkim 1). Members serve six years, one-third retire every two years, so the House is never dissolved; it is the 'permanent House'. The President also nominates twelve members from literature, science, art and social service.

Why two Houses? The NCERT gives two reasons: a large, diverse country needs representation for all regions and sections; and a second House lets every decision be reconsidered, a 'double check' against haste. The President is part of Parliament because no Bill becomes law without the President's assent, as module 4 explains. Hold on to one fact: the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha alone, because it is the directly elected House. That single fact explains most of the differences in procedure you will meet.

### Bridge: The vocabulary of law-making: introduce, pass, assent, lapse, majorities

'Introduce' means formally bringing a Bill into a House. The member asks for 'leave' (permission) of the House and, when granted, reads the title and objectives. The Bill is then published in the Gazette of India, the government's official record. Introduction plus publication make up the 'first reading'.

'Pass' means a House approves the Bill at its 'third reading' by a vote. But a Bill is 'passed by Parliament' only when both Houses have agreed to the same text, or when a joint sitting has approved it, or, for a Money Bill, when the Lok Sabha's version stands.

'Assent' is the President's signature. On assent the Bill becomes an 'Act' and is placed on the 'Statute Book', the collection of Acts in force. A 'statute' is simply an Act of a legislature.

'Session', 'sitting', 'adjournment', 'prorogation' and 'dissolution' are different. A session is a period during which the House meets; a sitting is one day's meeting; adjournment pauses a sitting; prorogation ends a session (done by the President); dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha itself. Only dissolution makes pending Bills 'lapse' (die), and only for the Lok Sabha, since the Rajya Sabha is never dissolved.

'Quorum' is the minimum attendance needed to do business: one-tenth of the total membership. 'Majority' comes in kinds. A 'simple majority' means more than half of the members present and voting; ordinary Bills and joint sittings use it. A 'special majority' is needed for Constitution Amendment Bills under Article 368: a majority of the total membership of the House and at least two-thirds of the members present and voting (standard textbook content, covered in GS-172). Keep this list beside you while reading modules 2-7.

### 1. From idea to law: Bills, Acts, ordinances and the four kinds of Bill

*Objective:* Define the key documents of law-making and sort Bills into their four constitutional categories and two political categories.

## The documents

A **Bill** is a draft of a proposed law; Laxmikanth calls it 'a proposal for legislation'. It becomes an **Act**, or law, when 'duly enacted', meaning passed by both Houses in the required way and assented to by the President. An Act in force is a **statute** and sits on the Statute Book. An **ordinance** is a temporary law made by the President under Article 123 when Parliament is not in session; it has the force of an Act but must later be placed before Parliament (module 7). The word 'law' covers all of these plus the rules made under them.

## Law-making is political before it is legal

The NCERT stresses that a Bill does not appear from nowhere. A party may want to fulfil an election promise; interest groups, the media or citizens' forums may press for a law. The Cabinet weighs the resources a law would need, the support or opposition it would attract and its electoral effect; in a coalition, all partners must accept it. Only when the Cabinet approves the policy does the concerned ministry (with the Law Ministry) draft the Bill. So the bureaucracy drafts, the Cabinet decides substance and timing, and Parliament deliberates and approves.

## Four constitutional categories

1. **Ordinary Bills**: any matter other than financial subjects.
2. **Money Bills**: only the financial matters listed in Article 110 (taxation, borrowing, the Consolidated Fund and so on).
3. **Financial Bills**: also about financial matters but not falling wholly within Article 110 (Article 117).
4. **Constitution Amendment Bills**: change the Constitution itself (Article 368; see GS-172).

All four pass through the same readings, but the Constitution adds special rules to each of the last three. Modules 2-7 take them in turn.

## Two political categories: government and private member's Bills

A **public or government Bill** is introduced by a minister; a **private Bill or private member's Bill** is introduced by any other Member of Parliament, whether from the ruling party or the opposition, elected or nominated. The 2017 Prelims question tested this: a private member is simply a non-minister MP, not 'a nominated member'.

| Government Bill | Private member's Bill |
|---|---|
| Introduced by a minister | Introduced by an MP who is not a minister |
| Reflects government policy | Reflects the member's (often opposition) stand |
| Greater chance of passing | Lesser chance of passing |
| Rejection signals want of confidence and may force resignation | Rejection has no effect on the government's survival |
| Seven days' notice | One month's notice |
| Drafted by the department with the Law Ministry | Drafted by the member |

The NCERT adds that members other than ministers can introduce Bills but these have little chance of passing without government support. Standard accounts say very few private member's Bills have ever become Acts and none in recent decades; this detail is not in the extracts read here, so verify before using it in an answer.

### 2. An ordinary Bill in the first House: first, second and third readings

*Objective:* Describe the three readings, including the three sub-stages of the second reading, and the four options a House has after general discussion.

Laxmikanth lists five stages for an ordinary Bill: first reading, second reading, third reading, the Bill in the second House, and assent. This module covers the first three; modules 3 and 4 cover the rest. The procedure is identical in both Houses.

## First reading

An ordinary Bill may be introduced in either House, by a minister or by any other member. The mover asks for the leave of the House; when leave is granted the mover introduces the Bill by reading its title and objectives. There is no discussion at this stage. The Bill is then published in the Gazette of India. If a Bill has already been published in the Gazette before introduction, leave of the House is not required. Introduction plus Gazette publication together constitute the first reading.

## Second reading: the heart of the process

This is the most important stage because the Bill receives both general and detailed scrutiny and takes its final shape. It has three sub-stages.

**(a) General discussion.** Printed copies go to all members. The principles and provisions are discussed in general, not clause by clause. The House then chooses one of four courses: take the Bill into consideration at once or on a fixed date; refer it to a **select committee** of that House; refer it to a **joint committee** of both Houses; or circulate it to elicit public opinion. A select committee has members of the originating House only; a joint committee has members of both Houses.

**(b) Committee stage.** The usual practice is reference to a select committee. The committee examines the Bill clause by clause and can amend provisions, but may not alter the principles underlying the Bill. It then reports the Bill back to the House. The NCERT calls committees 'miniature legislatures' because much of the real discussion happens there, and notes that Parliament rarely rejects committee suggestions.

**(c) Consideration stage.** The House takes up the Bill as reported, clause by clause. Each clause is discussed and voted on separately; members may move amendments, which become part of the Bill if accepted.

## Third reading

Debate is confined to accepting or rejecting the Bill as a whole; no amendments are allowed because the principles have already been scrutinised. If a majority of members present and voting accept it, the Bill is passed by that House. The presiding officer authenticates it and transmits it to the other House.

## A mental picture

Think of the first reading as announcing the Bill, the second reading as building it (debate the idea, let a committee refine it, then vote clause by clause) and the third reading as a yes-or-no on the finished product. The NCERT's flow diagram shows the same sequence: introduction, committee or House discussion, detailed discussion, acceptance or rejection, then transmission to the other House.

### 3. The second House, deadlock and joint sitting (Article 108); when Bills lapse

*Objective:* State the second House's four alternatives, the three deadlock situations, the rules of a joint sitting and which Bills lapse on dissolution.

## Bill in the second House

The second House repeats all three readings. It has four alternatives: pass the Bill as received; pass it with amendments and return it to the first House; reject it altogether; or take no action and keep it pending. A Bill is passed by Parliament only when both Houses agree, with or without amendments. If the second House passes it unchanged, or the first House accepts its amendments, the Bill goes to the President.

## Deadlock

A deadlock is deemed to arise, after one House has passed a Bill and sent it to the other, in three situations: the other House rejects the Bill; the Houses finally disagree about amendments; or more than six months pass from the other House's receipt of the Bill without its passing it. In counting the six months, periods when the other House is prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days are excluded.

## Joint sitting under Article 108

To resolve a deadlock the President may summon both Houses to a joint sitting to deliberate and vote on the Bill. Key rules from Laxmikanth:

- It applies only to **ordinary Bills and Financial Bills**, not to Money Bills (the Lok Sabha already has overriding power) and not to Constitution Amendment Bills (each House must pass them separately).
- The **Speaker** of the Lok Sabha presides; in the Speaker's absence the Deputy Speaker; then the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha; then a person chosen by the members present. The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (the Vice-President) never presides, because the Chairman is not a member of either House.
- **Quorum** is one-tenth of the total members of both Houses. The Lok Sabha's Rules of Procedure apply.
- The Bill passes if a **majority of the total members of both Houses present and voting** approve. Because the Lok Sabha is far larger, it normally prevails; the NCERT notes that every joint sitting so far has gone the Lok Sabha's way.
- New amendments are barred except those that caused the final disagreement and those made necessary by the delay.
- Once the President notifies the intention to summon a joint sitting, neither House may proceed further with the Bill.

The provision has been used three times, for the Dowry Prohibition Bill, 1960; the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill, 1977; and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill, 2002 (which the Rajya Sabha had rejected). The sittings took place in 1961, 1978 and 2002 respectively (years from standard accounts, not in the extract).

## Lapsing of Bills

From the extract: if the disputed Bill has already lapsed because the Lok Sabha was dissolved, no joint sitting can be held; but if the President notified the intention to summon a joint sitting before dissolution, the Bill does not lapse and the sitting can still be held. The wider lapse rules are standard textbook content you should learn now: a Bill pending in the Lok Sabha lapses on dissolution; a Bill passed by the Lok Sabha and pending in the Rajya Sabha lapses; a Bill pending in the Rajya Sabha that the Lok Sabha has not passed does not lapse; a Bill passed by both Houses and awaiting assent does not lapse; and a Bill returned by the President for reconsideration does not lapse. A 2024 Prelims question tested exactly these rules together with the joint-sitting exception.

### 4. Assent of the President (Article 111) and the Bill becoming an Act

*Objective:* Explain the President's three options on an ordinary Bill, the 'suspensive veto', and how the options narrow for Money Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills.

## The last stage

Every Bill passed by both Houses, singly or at a joint sitting, is presented to the President for assent. Article 111 gives three alternatives:

1. **Give assent.** The Bill becomes an Act and is placed on the Statute Book.
2. **Withhold assent.** The Bill ends and does not become an Act.
3. **Return the Bill** (if it is not a Money Bill) for reconsideration by the Houses, with or without a message suggesting amendments.

If the Houses pass the returned Bill again, with or without amendments, and present it afresh, the President must give assent. Because the President can only delay, not block, a Bill that Parliament insists on, Laxmikanth calls this a 'suspensive veto'. The NCERT's flow diagram ends with the same two branches: the President approves, or sends the Bill back for reconsideration, and assent results in enactment.

## How the options change by type of Bill

- **Money Bill**: the President may assent or withhold assent but cannot return it. In practice assent is given, because a Money Bill is introduced only on the President's own recommendation.
- **Constitution Amendment Bill**: standard textbook content (not in the extract) holds that after the 24th Amendment Act, 1971, the President must give assent to a duly passed Constitution Amendment Bill. GS-172 covers this.
- **Ordinary and Financial Bills**: all three options are available.

Article 111 sets no time limit for the President to act. Standard textbooks call indefinite inaction a 'pocket veto'; this label is not in the extract and should be verified before use in an answer.

## Putting the vocabulary together

Follow a hypothetical 'Road Safety Bill'. A minister gives seven days' notice and asks leave; leave is granted and the Bill is introduced and published (first reading). After general discussion the Lok Sabha refers it to a select committee, which reports with amendments; the House considers it clause by clause and passes it at the third reading. The Rajya Sabha returns it with one amendment, which the Lok Sabha accepts, so Parliament has 'passed' the Bill. The President returns it once with a message; both Houses pass it again unchanged; the President now assents. The Road Safety Act is on the Statute Book. Every verb in that story - introduce, refer, report, consider, pass, return, assent - has a precise meaning from bridge module B2.

## The NCERT ordering exercise

The NCERT asks learners to arrange these steps in order: minister proposes the need; Law Ministry drafts; House admits the Bill for discussion; committee changes and returns it; clause-by-clause reading and vote; passage in the first House; passage in the other House; reference to the President. Doing this exercise once, aloud, fixes the sequence better than rereading.

### 5. Money Bills: Article 110 definition, the Speaker's certificate and Article 109 procedure

*Objective:* Define a Money Bill precisely, explain the Speaker's role and describe the special procedure and the Rajya Sabha's limited powers.

## Article 110: the definition

A Bill is a Money Bill if it contains **only** provisions dealing with all or any of these matters:

1. imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax;
2. regulation of borrowing by the Union government;
3. custody of the Consolidated Fund of India or the Contingency Fund of India, and payments into or withdrawals from them;
4. appropriation of money out of the Consolidated Fund of India;
5. declaring any expenditure to be charged on the Consolidated Fund, or increasing such expenditure;
6. receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or the Public Account, its custody or issue, or the audit of Union or state accounts;
7. any matter incidental to the above.

A Bill is **not** a Money Bill merely because it provides for fines or other pecuniary penalties; fees for licences or for services rendered; or taxes imposed by a local authority for local purposes. The word 'only' is the whole test: add any general legislation and the Bill stops being a Money Bill.

## The Speaker's certificate

If any question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill, the decision of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha is final. Article 110(3) says so in terms, and Laxmikanth adds that it cannot be questioned in any court, in either House, or by the President. The Speaker endorses the Bill as a Money Bill when it is sent to the Rajya Sabha and when it is presented to the President.

**Qualifying note (verify against the official source).** The textbook statement is the constitutional text, but the courts have not treated the certificate as wholly beyond review. In Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd (2019), the Supreme Court observed that the Speaker's certification is not immune from judicial review and referred the question whether the Aadhaar Act was validly passed as a Money Bill to a larger bench. Treat the safe, always-true statement as 'Article 110(3) declares the Speaker's decision final'; treat 'no court can ever question it' as contested. Check the present status of the larger-bench reference before relying on it in a Mains answer.

## Article 109: the special procedure

- A Money Bill can be introduced **only in the Lok Sabha**, and only on the **recommendation of the President**. It is always a government Bill, introduced by a minister.
- After the Lok Sabha passes it, it goes to the Rajya Sabha for recommendations. The Rajya Sabha **cannot reject or amend** it; it may only recommend changes, and must return the Bill within **14 days**, with or without recommendations.
- The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any recommendation. If it accepts some, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses in the modified form; if it rejects them all, in the original form.
- If the Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is deemed passed by both Houses in the form the Lok Sabha passed it.
- The President may assent or withhold assent but cannot return the Bill.

The NCERT summarises: the Rajya Sabha 'cannot initiate, reject or amend money bills', and if it takes no action within 14 days the Bill is deemed passed.

## Ordinary Bill versus Money Bill

| Point | Ordinary Bill | Money Bill |
|---|---|---|
| House of introduction | Either House | Lok Sabha only |
| Who introduces | Minister or private member | Minister only |
| President's recommendation | Not required | Required |
| Rajya Sabha's power | Can amend or reject | Only recommend; return in 14 days |
| Maximum detention by Rajya Sabha | Six months | 14 days |
| Speaker's certificate | Not needed | Needed |
| Deadlock remedy | Joint sitting possible | No joint sitting; Lok Sabha prevails |
| Defeat in Lok Sabha | May lead to resignation if a government Bill | Leads to resignation of the government |
| President's options | Assent, withhold, return | Assent or withhold only |

## Why this asymmetry?

The NCERT explains it from first principles. The Rajya Sabha is elected by MLAs, not by the people; in our democracy the people are the final authority; so the directly elected House should hold the crucial powers of controlling finance and removing the government. In all other spheres, including non-money Bills, Constitution Amendments and impeachment, the two Houses are co-equal.

### 6. Financial Bills I and II (Article 117) and how they differ from Money Bills

*Objective:* Distinguish Financial Bills I and II from Money Bills and from ordinary Bills on introduction, recommendation, Rajya Sabha power, joint sitting and assent.

## Three kinds of 'financial bill'

In everyday language any Bill about revenue or spending is a financial Bill. The Constitution uses the phrase technically. Laxmikanth lists three kinds: Money Bills (Article 110), Financial Bills (I) (Article 117(1)) and Financial Bills (II) (Article 117(3)). So **all Money Bills are Financial Bills, but not all Financial Bills are Money Bills**; only those containing exclusively Article 110 matters, and certified by the Speaker, are Money Bills.

## Financial Bill (I): Article 117(1)

This Bill contains one or more Article 110 matters **plus** other matters of general legislation. Example: a Bill with a borrowing clause that does not deal exclusively with borrowing. It resembles a Money Bill in two respects only: it can be introduced **only in the Lok Sabha**, and **only on the President's recommendation**. In every other respect it is an ordinary Bill: the Rajya Sabha can amend or reject it; a deadlock can be resolved by a joint sitting; and the President may assent, withhold assent or return it. One fine point: an amendment to such a Bill other than one reducing or abolishing a tax cannot be moved in either House without the President's recommendation.

## Financial Bill (II): Article 117(3)

This Bill involves **expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India** but contains **no** Article 110 matter. It is treated as an ordinary Bill in all respects, with one special feature: neither House can **pass** it unless the President has recommended its **consideration**. So it can be introduced in either House without recommendation; the recommendation is needed at the consideration stage, not at introduction. The Rajya Sabha can amend or reject it, a joint sitting is possible, and the President has all three options.

## The master comparison

| Feature | Ordinary | Money (110) | Financial I (117(1)) | Financial II (117(3)) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Content | Non-financial | Only 110 matters | 110 matters plus general legislation | Expenditure from CFI, no 110 matter |
| House of introduction | Either | Lok Sabha only | Lok Sabha only | Either |
| President's recommendation | No | For introduction | For introduction | For consideration |
| Rajya Sabha | Amend or reject | Recommend only, 14 days | Amend or reject | Amend or reject |
| Joint sitting | Yes | No | Yes | Yes |
| President may return | Yes | No | Yes | Yes |
| Speaker's certificate | No | Yes | No | No |

## The 'Finance Bill' trap

Two similar phrases cause confusion. 'Financial Bill' is the Article 117 category above. 'The Finance Bill' is the specific annual Bill that gives effect to the government's tax proposals in the budget. Laxmikanth says the annual Finance Bill is subject to all the conditions applicable to a Money Bill, and that the Rajya Sabha should return the Money Bill or Finance Bill within fourteen days. A 2023 Prelims question paired 'Finance Bill' with 'Money Bill' and asked whether the Rajya Sabha can amend or reject the Finance Bill and whether a joint sitting is needed for it. Read such a question twice: if 'Finance Bill' means the annual Finance Bill treated as a Money Bill, the Rajya Sabha can only recommend and no joint sitting arises; if it meant a Financial Bill (I), the answer would differ. Module 9 works through this.

### 7. Constitution Amendment Bills, ordinances (Article 123) and the committee system, in outline

*Objective:* Outline the special features of Constitution Amendment Bills, the ordinance power and the committee system well enough to answer Prelims statements and link to GS-172.

## Constitution Amendment Bills (link: GS-172)

The extract says only that these Bills follow a separate procedure and that a joint sitting is unavailable because each House must pass them separately. The NCERT adds that constitutional amendments must be approved by a special majority of both Houses and that the constituent powers of the two Houses are similar. Standard textbook content (verify in GS-172): the Bill may be introduced in either House by a minister or a private member without the President's prior recommendation; it must be passed in each House by a majority of the total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting (Article 368); some amendments affecting the federal structure also need ratification by half the state legislatures; and since the 24th Amendment the President must assent.

## Ordinances (Article 123), in outline

The President may promulgate an ordinance when Parliament is not in session (either House or both), if satisfied that circumstances require immediate action. An ordinance has the same force as an Act of Parliament. It must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble and ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly unless approved, and it may be withdrawn earlier. Because it is a 'law', it faces the same limits as Parliament's own legislation: it cannot deal with matters outside Parliament's competence and cannot take away Fundamental Rights, though it can be given retrospective effect and can amend or repeal a Central Act. A further limit comes from Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar (2017), a Constitution Bench decision which held that repeatedly re-promulgating ordinances without placing them before the legislature is constitutionally impermissible (a 'fraud on the Constitution'), and that the satisfaction behind an ordinance is open to judicial scrutiny; this case is standard knowledge, not in the extracts, so verify its exact holding before quoting it. These details are standard textbook content, not in the extracts; a 2025 Prelims question tested exactly these three points (module 9).

## The committee system, in outline

The NCERT explains why committees matter: Parliament meets only in sessions and has limited time, while law-making needs in-depth study. Since 1983 India has developed a system of parliamentary standing committees; there are over twenty departmentally related committees that supervise the work, budget, expenditure and Bills of their departments. Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs), with members from both Houses, can be set up to discuss a particular Bill or to investigate financial irregularities. The committee system has reduced Parliament's burden; many Bills go to committees and Parliament usually approves their work with few changes, though legally no Bill becomes law and no budget is sanctioned without Parliament's approval.

Laxmikanth's budget section adds that the 24 departmental standing committees examine demands for grants in the three-to-four-week gap after the general discussion, and dates the departmental standing committee system to 1993 with expansion in 2004. The two sources date different things (NCERT the growth of standing committees generally; Laxmikanth the departmental system), so quote them carefully and treat the dates as a review item.

From module 2, recall the Bill-specific committees: a **select committee** (members of one House) and a **joint committee** (both Houses), either of which a House may choose at the general-discussion stage.

## Why the outline matters

Questions rarely stay inside one Article. A single Prelims item may combine a Money Bill point, a joint-sitting point and an ordinance point. Knowing the shape of each special procedure lets you place any statement in the right box before judging it.

### 8. The budget in outline, and the big picture of why the Houses are unequal

*Objective:* Sketch the budget's constitutional basis and documents, and synthesise why procedure differs between the Houses and how Parliament's legislative role is evaluated.

## The budget in outline (GS-180 covers the details)

The Constitution never uses the word 'budget'; Article 112 speaks of the 'annual financial statement', a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for a financial year (1 April to 31 March). Until 2017 there were two budgets, Railway and General (separated in 1924 on the Acworth Committee's recommendation); they were merged in 2017 into one Union Budget, now presented on 1 February.

Key constitutional rules from the extract: no demand for a grant except on the President's recommendation; no money withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund except under an appropriation made by law; no tax levied or collected except by authority of law; Parliament can reduce or abolish a tax but cannot increase it; the Rajya Sabha cannot vote on demands for grants and must return the Money Bill or Finance Bill within fourteen days. Expenditure 'charged' on the Consolidated Fund (President's emoluments, judges' salaries, the CAG, debt charges and so on) is discussed but not voted; the rest is voted.

The budget documents include the Budget Speech, the Annual Financial Statement, Demands for Grants, the Appropriation Bill, the Finance Bill, statements under the FRBM Act, the Expenditure and Receipts Budgets and 'Budget at a Glance'. The six stages are presentation, general discussion, scrutiny by departmental standing committees, voting on demands for grants (with cut motions and the guillotine), the Appropriation Bill (authorising withdrawals) and the Finance Bill (legalising the tax side, to be enacted within 75 days under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931). A vote on account bridges the gap at the start of the year. Three funds exist: the Consolidated Fund (Article 266), the Public Account (Article 266) and the Contingency Fund (Article 267). Treat all of this as orientation only.

## Why the two Houses are unequal in some matters and equal in others

The pattern across modules 3-6 is simple once stated. Where money or the survival of the government is at stake, the directly elected Lok Sabha decides: Money Bills, demands for grants, no-confidence. Where law or the Constitution is at stake, the Houses are co-equal: ordinary Bills (with joint sitting as the tie-breaker), Constitution Amendments, impeachment. The Rajya Sabha has special powers too, such as authorising Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest, because it represents the states. The NCERT's caution: Rajya Sabha members in practice represent their parties more than their states.

## Evaluating the legislative process

The NCERT is candid about weaknesses. The Cabinet sets the agenda; Parliament often merely approves. Sessions and debate time have declined; quorum is often absent; walkouts and boycotts consume time. Committees have improved scrutiny but Parliament rarely alters their work. Yet Parliament retains teeth: a government with a Lok Sabha majority but not a Rajya Sabha majority (1977, 2000) had to make concessions; the Lokpal Bill failed enactment for years; the Rajya Sabha rejected the Prevention of Terrorism Bill in 2002, forcing a joint sitting. These are the points the 2019 and 2020 Mains questions on the individual parliamentarian and the Rajya Sabha's transformation ask you to marshal.

### 9. How UPSC tests legislative procedure: patterns, PYQ walkthroughs and elimination

*Objective:* Recognise the recurring Prelims and Mains patterns and apply an elimination method to statement questions without relying on an answer key.

## Prelims patterns among the verified questions

1. **Private member's Bill** (2017). Statement: a private member's Bill is one presented by a nominated MP. Analysis: a private member is any MP who is not a minister (module 1), so the statement conflicts with the definition. The second statement, that a private member's Bill was 'recently passed for the first time', is not addressed by the extracts; standard accounts say such Bills have become Acts before, decades ago. Reason, do not guess.

2. **Finance Bill versus Money Bill** (2023). Statement 1: the Rajya Sabha can amend or reject a Finance Bill. Statement 2: it cannot amend or reject a Money Bill, only recommend. Statement 3: no joint sitting for a Money Bill but a joint sitting becomes necessary for a Finance Bill. Analysis from module 6: statement 2 matches Article 109. Laxmikanth treats the annual Finance Bill as subject to all Money Bill conditions and returnable in fourteen days, which conflicts with statement 1 and with statement 3's claim that a joint sitting becomes 'necessary'. On that reading only one statement holds. Note the ambiguity if 'Finance Bill' were read as a Financial Bill (I); UPSC's phrasing 'Finance Bill' points to the annual Bill.

3. **Lapsing** (2024). Statements: a Bill pending in the Lok Sabha lapses on dissolution; a Bill passed by the Lok Sabha and pending in the Rajya Sabha lapses; a Bill for which the President has notified a joint sitting lapses. Analysis from module 3: the first two match the standard lapse rules; the third conflicts with Laxmikanth, who says such a Bill does not lapse. Reasoning points to '1 and 2'.

4. **Ordinances** (2025). Statements: an ordinance can amend any Central Act; can abridge a Fundamental Right; can come into effect from a back date. Analysis from module 7 (standard content, not in the extracts): an ordinance has the force of an Act and can be retrospective, but as 'law' it cannot abridge Fundamental Rights. Reasoning points to the first and third statements. Verify from a fuller source.

## Elimination heuristics

- Numbers are anchors: 14 days (Money Bill), six months (deadlock), one-tenth (quorum), seven days and one month (notice), three (joint sittings held).
- 'Only' is the Money Bill test; 'either House' is the ordinary and Financial Bill (II) marker; 'Lok Sabha only' marks Money and Financial Bill (I).
- 'Final' belongs to the Speaker's Money Bill certificate (Article 110(3)); the stronger phrase 'cannot be questioned in any court' is the textbook wording but is qualified by Rojer Mathew (2019), so an option built on it needs careful reading.
- Any statement giving the Rajya Sabha the power to reject or amend a Money Bill, or sending a Money or Constitution Amendment Bill to a joint sitting, is wrong.
- 'Must assent' applies to re-passed Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills; 'cannot return' applies to Money Bills.

### Notes: Definitions

- Bill: a proposal or draft of a law; becomes an Act when passed by Parliament in the required manner and assented to by the President.
- Statute: an Act of a legislature in force; the Statute Book is the body of such Acts.
- Ordinance: a temporary law promulgated by the President under Article 123 when Parliament is not in session, with the force of an Act.
- Government (public) Bill: introduced by a minister; private member's Bill: introduced by any MP who is not a minister.
- Money Bill: a Bill containing only the matters listed in Article 110, certified by the Speaker.
- Financial Bill (I): contains Article 110 matters plus general legislation (Article 117(1)); Financial Bill (II): involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund without any Article 110 matter (Article 117(3)).
- Deadlock: rejection by the other House, final disagreement on amendments, or more than six months without passage.
- Suspensive veto: the President's power to return a Bill once; assent is compulsory if it is re-passed.

### Notes: Stages of an ordinary Bill

- First reading: leave of the House (unless already published), introduction by reading title and objectives, Gazette publication; no discussion.
- Second reading (a) general discussion: principles only; four options - consider now or later, select committee, joint committee, circulate for public opinion.
- Second reading (b) committee stage: clause-by-clause scrutiny; amendments allowed without altering principles; report back.
- Second reading (c) consideration stage: clause-by-clause discussion and vote in the House; amendments may be moved.
- Third reading: accept or reject as a whole; no amendments; majority of members present and voting; authentication by presiding officer; transmission to the other House.
- Second House: pass as is; pass with amendments and return; reject; keep pending. Deadlock after rejection, final disagreement or six months.
- Joint sitting if deadlock (ordinary and Financial Bills only). Then presentation to the President for assent.

### Notes: Joint sitting (Article 108) facts

- Summoned by the President; not available for Money Bills or Constitution Amendment Bills.
- Six-month period excludes prorogation or adjournment of more than four consecutive days.
- Presided over by the Speaker, then the Deputy Speaker, then the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, then a person chosen by members present; never the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
- Quorum one-tenth of total members of both Houses; Lok Sabha rules apply; passed by majority of total members of both Houses present and voting.
- New amendments barred except those causing the disagreement or necessitated by delay.
- After notification of a joint sitting the Bill does not lapse on dissolution and neither House may proceed with it.
- Held thrice: Dowry Prohibition Bill 1960; Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill 1977; Prevention of Terrorism Bill 2002.

### Notes: Key Articles

- Article 107: introduction and passing of Bills (general procedure; standard reference).
- Article 108: joint sitting of both Houses.
- Article 109: special procedure for Money Bills (Lok Sabha only; Rajya Sabha 14 days; recommendations only).
- Article 110: definition of Money Bill; Article 110(3) makes the Speaker's decision final (the textbook adds 'not to be questioned in any court', but Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank, 2019, held the certificate is not immune from judicial review and referred the Aadhaar Money Bill question to a larger bench; verify current status).
- Article 111: assent to Bills; three options; suspensive veto; no return of Money Bills.
- Article 112: annual financial statement (budget).
- Article 117: Financial Bills (I) in clause (1), Financial Bills (II) in clause (3).
- Article 123: ordinance-making power of the President.
- Article 368: Constitution Amendment Bills and special majority (GS-172).
- Articles 266 and 267: Consolidated Fund and Public Account; Contingency Fund.

### Notes: Four kinds of Bill compared

- Money Bills are a species of Financial Bills; the Speaker's certificate separates them.

### Notes: Lapse of Bills on dissolution of the Lok Sabha

- Lapses: Bill pending in the Lok Sabha (whether originating there or transmitted by the Rajya Sabha).
- Lapses: Bill passed by the Lok Sabha and pending in the Rajya Sabha.
- Does not lapse: Bill pending in the Rajya Sabha not yet passed by the Lok Sabha.
- Does not lapse: Bill passed by both Houses and awaiting assent, or returned by the President for reconsideration.
- Does not lapse: Bill for which the President has notified a joint sitting before dissolution (from the extract).
- Items other than the last are standard textbook content; verify in a fuller source.

### Notes: Budget in outline

- Constitution calls it the 'annual financial statement' (Article 112); financial year 1 April to 31 March; single Union Budget since 2017, presented on 1 February.
- Six stages: presentation; general discussion; scrutiny by departmental standing committees; voting on demands for grants (cut motions, guillotine); Appropriation Bill; Finance Bill (75 days under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act 1931).
- Charged expenditure is discussed but not voted; the Rajya Sabha cannot vote demands for grants.
- Vote on account: advance grant for part of the year, usually two months, about one-sixth of estimates.
- Documents include the Budget Speech, Annual Financial Statement, Demands for Grants, Appropriation Bill, Finance Bill, FRBM statements, Expenditure and Receipts Budgets.

### Notes: Committees and ordinances

- Select committee: members of the originating House; joint committee: members of both Houses; both can be chosen at the general-discussion stage.
- Departmentally related standing committees: over twenty (NCERT), 24 (Laxmikanth); examine demands for grants and Bills; NCERT dates standing committees from 1983, Laxmikanth the departmental system from 1993 (expanded 2004).
- Joint Parliamentary Committees: for a particular Bill or for investigating irregularities; members from both Houses.
- Ordinance (Article 123): when Parliament not in session; force of an Act; laid before both Houses; ceases six weeks after reassembly unless approved; cannot abridge Fundamental Rights; can be retrospective (standard content).

### Notes: Concepts commonly confused

- Financial Bill (Article 117 category) versus the annual Finance Bill (treated as a Money Bill).
- Private member (any non-minister MP) versus nominated member.
- Six months (deadlock on ordinary Bills) versus 14 days (Money Bill return) versus six weeks (ordinance lapse).
- Recommendation at introduction (Money, Financial I) versus at consideration (Financial II).
- Speaker presides over a joint sitting; the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha never does.
- President may return ordinary and Financial Bills but not Money Bills; must assent to re-passed Bills.

### Core concepts

- Every Bill passes the same three readings in each House; the Constitution adds special rules for Money, Financial and Constitution Amendment Bills.
- The second reading (general discussion, committee, consideration) is where a Bill takes its final shape.
- A joint sitting resolves deadlock only for ordinary and Financial Bills; the Lok Sabha's size makes it the usual winner.
- A Money Bill is defined by the word 'only' in Article 110 and by the Speaker's certificate; the Rajya Sabha can only recommend within 14 days.
- The President's veto over ordinary Bills is suspensive; Money Bills cannot be returned.
- The directly elected Lok Sabha controls money and the government; otherwise the Houses are co-equal.

**Q.** What is the difference between a Bill and an Act?

**A.** A Bill is a draft proposal; it becomes an Act when passed by Parliament in the required way and assented to by the President.

**Q.** Name the two Houses of Parliament and the third component.

**A.** Rajya Sabha (Council of States), Lok Sabha (House of the People) and the President.

**Q.** What are the five stages of an ordinary Bill?

**A.** First reading, second reading, third reading, Bill in the second House, assent of the President.

**Q.** Who decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill?

**A.** The Speaker of the Lok Sabha, whose decision Article 110(3) declares final. (The textbook adds that it cannot be questioned in any court, but the Supreme Court in Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank, 2019, said the certificate is not immune from judicial review and referred the Aadhaar Money Bill question to a larger bench; verify the current status.)

**Q.** Why is the second reading called the most important stage?

**A.** Because the Bill receives general and detailed scrutiny there - general discussion, committee examination and clause-by-clause consideration - and assumes its final shape; the third reading only accepts or rejects it whole.

**Q.** Why does the Lok Sabha normally prevail in a joint sitting?

**A.** The Bill passes by a majority of total members of both Houses present and voting, and the Lok Sabha has far more members than the Rajya Sabha.

**Q.** How does the word 'only' in Article 110 separate a Money Bill from a Financial Bill (I)?

**A.** A Money Bill contains only Article 110 matters; a Financial Bill (I) contains Article 110 matters plus other general legislation, so it fails the 'only' test and loses the Money Bill privileges except the Lok Sabha-only introduction and President's recommendation.

**Q.** Why can the President not return a Money Bill?

**A.** Article 111 limits the President to assent or withholding assent for Money Bills; since such a Bill is introduced only on the President's own recommendation, returning it would be inconsistent.

**Q.** A Bill passed by the Lok Sabha has been with the Rajya Sabha for seven months, including a prorogation of two months. Can the President summon a joint sitting?

**A.** Only five months count after excluding the two-month prorogation, so the six-month deadlock condition is not yet met; no joint sitting on that ground yet.

**Q.** The Lok Sabha is dissolved while a Bill it passed is pending in the Rajya Sabha. What happens to the Bill?

**A.** It lapses, unless the President had already notified the intention to summon a joint sitting, in which case it survives and the sitting can be held.
