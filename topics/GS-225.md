## GS-225: Current account and capital account

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Basic macro and monetary

*Goal:* Explain Current account and capital account and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Inside the current account

The current account records trade in goods and services and transfer payments. It covers exports and imports of goods, trade in services — split into factor income such as earnings on labour and capital and non-factor services such as shipping, banking, tourism and software — and transfers received or paid without anything in return, such as gifts, remittances and grants. Its two building blocks are the balance of trade, which nets only goods, and net invisibles, which nets services, income and transfers. A surplus means the country is lending to the rest of the world; a deficit means it is borrowing. Exports add to domestic demand, while imports are a leakage from it.

### Capital account and the financing identity

The capital account records international transactions in assets — money, shares, bonds, government debt. When an Indian buys a foreign company, foreign exchange flows out and the entry is a debit; when a foreigner buys shares of an Indian firm, it is a credit. Its main items are FDI, portfolio (FII) investment, external commercial borrowing and external assistance. Just as a household spending beyond its income must borrow or sell assets, a country with a current account deficit must finance it through net capital inflows or by running down foreign exchange reserves. NCERT notes that under BPM6 most financial-asset trade moves to a separate financial account, though RBI still publishes the older two-way split.

### India's current account structure

The Economic Survey 2025-26 describes India's typical pattern: a large merchandise trade deficit offset by strong net invisibles, led by growing services surpluses in IT, business and professional services and by private transfers. Together these keep the CAD manageable — it narrowed to USD 15 billion, or 0.8% of GDP, in April–September 2025 from 1.3% a year earlier. Remittances are the standout stabiliser: India is the world's largest recipient, with FY25 inflows of USD 135.4 billion (provisional; about 3.5% of GDP) compared with USD 55.6 billion back in FY11, and in most years they have exceeded gross FDI inflows. Comparatively, several advanced economies ran larger deficits in Q2 FY26.

### Worked example

Goods exports 400, goods imports 500 → trade balance −100. Net invisibles +70 → current account −30. Net capital inflows (FDI, portfolio, loans) +40 → overall balance +10, so official reserves rise by 10. If capital inflows were only +20, reserves would have to fall by 10 to close the gap.

### Recall

- - **q:** Are remittances in the current or capital account?
  - **a:** Current account (transfers, part of invisibles).
- - **q:** What does BPM6 add to the two-account structure?
  - **a:** A separate financial account holding most trade in financial assets like bonds and equities.
- - **q:** What was India's CAD in H1 FY26?
  - **a:** USD 15 billion, 0.8% of GDP (Economic Survey 2025-26).

### Common trap

Placing remittances in the capital account: they are private transfers and sit in the current account under invisibles.
