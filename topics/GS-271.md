## GS-271: Wildlife corridors and conservation approaches

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Environment · Ecology fundamentals

*Goal:* Explain Wildlife corridors and conservation approaches and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### The fragmentation problem

NCERT ranks habitat loss and fragmentation as the leading cause of extinctions. When human activities break large habitats into small fragments, mammals and birds that need extensive territories, and migratory animals, suffer population declines. Tropical rainforests have shrunk from covering over 14 per cent of land to no more than 6 per cent. Isolated patches cannot hold wide-ranging species, so conservation must keep patches connected. Ex situ methods such as zoos, seed banks, cryopreservation and tissue culture act as insurance for species already critically threatened, while community action extends protection beyond official boundaries: Joint Forest Management, formalised by Odisha in 1988, lets village institutions guard degraded forest in exchange for benefits, and sacred groves and Bishnoi traditions protect habitat locally.

### Corridors in law and tiger policy

The Wild Life (Protection) Act now names connectivity explicitly. Section 36A lets a state declare government land, especially areas connecting two protected areas, as a conservation reserve after consulting local people. Section 38V requires each tiger conservation plan to keep land use in linking areas ecologically compatible, so that these areas provide dispersal habitat and corridors for animals spilling over from core tiger habitat, while addressing local livelihoods. Among NTCA's statutory functions, listed in MoEFCC's 2025-26 report, is ensuring that tiger reserves and the areas joining them are not diverted to ecologically unsustainable uses, except in public interest with National Board for Wild Life approval. The 2023-24 report records a corridor planning workshop at Pachmarhi for the Satpura–Melghat–Pench landscape.

### Elephant corridors and landscape projects

Elephants show why corridors matter. India holds more than 60 per cent of the world's wild Asian elephants, and Project Elephant, a centrally sponsored scheme since 1992, aims to protect elephants, their habitats and corridors, reduce human-elephant conflict and care for captive animals. MoEFCC's 2023-24 report says Project Elephant and state forest departments have ground-validated 150 elephant corridors in 15 range states, published with maps in a report released on World Elephant Day 2023. The 2025-26 report puts the scheme in 23 States/UTs and notes a sub-committee to map train-hit hotspots and a report on reducing train collisions across 14 states. Forest policy adds a wider cover goal: the 1988 policy targets 33 per cent forest cover and social forestry on degraded land.
