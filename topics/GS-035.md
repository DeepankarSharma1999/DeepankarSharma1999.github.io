## GS-035: Press, education and nationalist consciousness

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words you need before the details

Twelve terms appear again and again in this topic. Learn them once here.

- **Press**: newspapers, journals, pamphlets and books, and the printing presses that make them.
- **Vernacular**: the everyday language of a region (Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Urdu), as opposed to English or classical languages. The *vernacular press* means newspapers in Indian languages.
- **Pre-censorship**: officials must read and approve a paper *before* it is printed. The opposite is punishing it *after* publication.
- **Security or bond**: a sum of money a printer deposits with the government. It is forfeited if the paper offends.
- **Sedition**: speech or writing that stirs up disaffection against the government. Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code was the sedition clause.
- **Orientalist** and **Anglicist**: in the education debate, an Orientalist wanted government money to keep Indian classical learning (Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian) alive; an Anglicist wanted it spent on English and Western science.
- **Minute**: a written note in which an official records his argument for a decision. **Despatch**: an official policy letter sent from London to the Government of India.
- **Grants-in-aid**: government money given to privately run schools if they meet conditions.
- **Medium of instruction**: the language in which a subject is taught, as opposed to a language that is itself a subject.
- **Dyarchy**: the 1919 arrangement in the provinces under which some subjects, including education, were handed to Indian ministers answerable to the provincial legislature (GS-047 module 2 explains the 1919 Act).

Keep one idea in mind: in this topic the same tool (a law, a school, a newspaper) was usually designed by the rulers for one purpose and used by Indians for another.

### 1. Orientation: press and education on one page

*Objective:* Hold the topic in three questions and one central argument before any dates.

## The topic in one paragraph

Two institutions that the British built for their own convenience turned into engines of Indian nationalism. The **press** carried news and criticism to readers across regions; **modern education** taught a small class Western ideas of liberty and equality and gave them English as a common language. The rulers answered the press with a long chain of controlling laws and tried to keep education narrow, cheap and loyal. The topic asks you to know both chains of **dates and measures**, and then to argue **how** each helped build national consciousness, with a balanced judgement.

## Three questions that organise everything

1. **What did the government do?** Press: regulation from 1799 to the Second World War. Education: from the first colleges and the 1813 grant to the Sargent Plan of 1944.
2. **What did Indians do with it?** They started newspapers, libraries and schools, defended press freedom, and demanded mass education.
3. **What was the net effect?** Spectrum's own conclusion: modern ideas reached the masses through parties, press, pamphlets and platforms, even though the content and structure of education served colonial interests (module 8).

### 2. Press I: from Hickey's Gazette to the Vernacular Press Act

*Objective:* State the early press laws in order with the ruler who made each, its type and its effect, and explain why the 1878 Act is a landmark.

## The first newspaper and the first fear

**James Augustus Hickey** started *The Bengal Gazette* (also called the *Calcutta General Advertiser*) in **1780**, the first newspaper in India. It was seized in **1782** because it criticised the government so openly. Others followed (*The Bengal Journal*, *The Calcutta Chronicle*, *The Madras Courier*, *The Bombay Herald*). Company officers feared that such papers might reach London and expose their misdeeds, so they began to want curbs.

## Early regulations, one line each

| Year | Measure | Made by | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1799 | Censorship of Press Act | Wellesley | Near-wartime curbs, **pre-censorship**, feared French invasion |
| 1818 | Pre-censorship dispensed with | Lord Hastings (Governor-General 1813-23; not Warren Hastings) | Relaxation; Hastings had progressive views |
| 1823 | Licensing Regulations | John Adam, acting Governor-General (Spectrum prints John Adams; verify) | Starting or using a press without a **licence** became a penal offence |
| 1835 | Press Act (Metcalfe Act) | Metcalfe | Repealed the 1823 rule; printer and publisher declare premises |
| 1857 | Licensing Act | Canning's government, after the Revolt (verify; post-1857 setting in GS-032 module 9) | Licensing added to registration; right to stop any publication |
| 1867 | Registration Act | Government | **Regulatory, not restrictive** |

## Reading the table

- **1799** came from a security scare, not a debate about liberty.
- **1823** was aimed chiefly at Indian-language papers or those edited by Indians, and Rammohan Roy's *Mirat-ul-Akbar* had to stop. Roy had protested against a resolution restricting press freedom as early as **1824** (Spectrum), so the defence of press freedom starts with the reformers (GS-034 module 3 notes that Roy asked for a free press).
- **1835**: Metcalfe earned the title 'liberator of the Indian press'; newspapers grew rapidly.
- **1857**: an emergency law passed because of the Revolt.
- **1867**: the Registration Act replaced the 1835 Act. Every book or newspaper had to print the names of printer and publisher and the place of publication, and a copy of a book went to the local government within a month. It is the model of a *regulatory* law, unlike the *restrictive* laws around it.

Pattern: curbs tighten in times of fear (1799, 1857) and loosen in liberal moods (1818, 1835).

## The Vernacular Press Act, 1878

**Context.** After 1858 the European press rallied behind the government while the vernacular press criticised it. Public opinion was strong against Lytton's policies, made worse by the terrible **famine of 1876 to 77** and the lavish imperial **Delhi Durbar** held in the same period.

**Provisions (Spectrum).**

1. The district magistrate could call on the printer and publisher of a vernacular newspaper to sign a **bond** not to cause disaffection against the government, or antipathy between religions, castes or races. A security could be demanded and forfeited, and press equipment seized if the offence recurred.
2. The magistrate's action was **final**: no appeal in a court.
3. A paper could gain exemption by submitting proofs to a government **censor**.

The Act was nicknamed the **gagging Act**. Its worst features were the **discrimination** between English and vernacular papers and the **absence of appeal**. Proceedings began against *Som Prakash*, *Bharat Mihir*, *Dacca Prakash* and *Samachar*; the *Amrita Bazar Patrika* turned overnight into an English paper to escape it. Later the pre-censorship clause was dropped and a press commissioner appointed to supply authentic news. After strong opposition, **Ripon repealed the Act in 1882**. Spectrum's chapter 10 adds that about **169** vernacular papers existed in 1877, with a circulation near 100,000: the Act hit a growing press.

### 3. Press II: the nationalist press and the later laws, 1870 to 1945

*Objective:* Describe what the nationalist press did, how journalists dodged the law, and the main repressive laws and prosecutions from 1883 to the Second World War.

## What the press did, 1870 to 1918

Spectrum says the early nationalist phase (roughly 1870 to 1918) concentrated on **political propaganda and education** rather than mass agitation: forming nationalist ideology and training and mobilising public opinion. The press was the crucial tool. The Congress in its early days relied **solely** on newspapers to spread its resolutions and proceedings.

Papers named by Spectrum (learn four with their editors): *The Hindu* and *Swadesamitran* (G. Subramania Aiyar); *The Bengalee* (Surendranath Banerjea); *Amrita Bazar Patrika* (Sisir Kumar Ghosh and Motilal Ghosh); *Kesari* (Marathi) and *Mahratta* (English) under Tilak.

Three features:

- **Public service, not profit.** The papers were seen as national service.
- **The library movement.** A single newspaper was read and discussed in local libraries in remote villages, so the libraries served political education and also political participation.
- **An institution of opposition.** Government acts were put under critical scrutiny.

## Dodging the law

Section 124A of the Penal Code punished disaffection (transportation for life or a term, or up to three years' imprisonment, as Spectrum states). Journalists prefaced hostile writing with loyalty or quoted socialist and Irish nationalist writing from English papers. 

## Prosecutions and new laws

- **1883**: **Surendranath Banerjea**, the first Indian journalist to be imprisoned, after an angry *Bengalee* editorial criticising a judge of the Calcutta High Court.
- **1897**: **Tilak** sentenced to 18 months. Background: plague in Poona, harsh measures, and the murder of the chairman of the Plague Committee by the Chapekar brothers. He was prosecuted on a *Kesari* poem and a Shivaji festival speech, and overnight became 'Lokmanya'.
- **1898**: Section 124A was amended and a new **Section 153A** added. Spectrum says these together made it an offence to bring the Government of India into contempt or to create hatred between classes (in relation to the English in India). Standard accounts give contempt of government to 124A and enmity between classes to 153A (verify). Nation-wide protests followed.
- **1908**: **Newspaper (Incitement to Offences) Act**: magistrates could confiscate the property of a press printing material likely to incite murder or violence; aimed at the Extremists. Tilak was tried for sedition (Section 124A, over *Kesari* articles; GS-038 module 7) and transported to **Mandalay** for six years. Bombay's textile and railway workers struck for days; Spectrum says Lenin hailed it as the working class's entry on the political stage.
- **1910**: **Indian Press Act**: revived the worst features of the VPA; security at registration, forfeiture, and two free copies of every issue to the government. Spectrum quotes Reba Chaudhuri: over 500 publications proscribed (verify).

## War and civil disobedience

- **First World War**: Defence of India Rules suppressed agitation and public criticism (Spectrum). (GS-039 module 6 covers the 1915 Act.)
- **1921**: on the recommendation of a Press Committee chaired by **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, the Acts of 1908 and 1910 were repealed (Spectrum; some books give 1922 for the repeal itself, verify).
- **1931**: **Indian Press (Emergency Powers) Act** against Civil Disobedience propaganda, amplified in **1932**.
- **Second World War**: pre-censorship under the Defence of India Rules, amended Press Emergency Act and Official Secrets Act; at one time all news of Congress activity was declared illegal.

## Other voices

Gandhi used weeklies for his campaigns: Spectrum names **Harijan**; *Young India* and *Navajivan* are standard knowledge (verify). The marginalised also used print: Walangkar's *Vital Vidhvasak* and Ambedkar's *Mooknayak* (1920) and *Bahishkrit Bharat* (1927), which argued that nationalism could not grow if social divisions were ignored (detail in GS-052 modules 5 and 6).

### 4. Education I: the Company, the 1813 grant and Macaulay to Wood

*Objective:* Explain the early colleges, the 1813 grant, the Orientalist-Anglicist controversy, Macaulay's Minute, Thomason and the content of Wood's Despatch.

## The Company and early colleges

For its first 60 years of dominion the Company, a trading concern, took no interest in education, apart from a few exceptions:

- **Calcutta Madrasah**, **Warren Hastings**, 1781: Muslim law and related subjects.
- **Sanskrit College, Benares**, **Jonathan Duncan** (the Resident), 1791: Hindu law and philosophy.
- **Fort William College**, **Wellesley**, 1800: to train Company civil servants in the languages and customs of Indians. Spectrum prints that it closed in 1802; standard accounts say it was cut back in 1802 and continued afterwards (verify).

The first two were meant to supply qualified Indians for the Company's law courts. Enlightened Indians and missionaries began pressing for modern, secular, Western education as a remedy for the country's ills.

## The Charter Act of 1813

The Act wrote in the principle of encouraging learned Indians and promoting knowledge of modern sciences, and told the Company to set aside **one lakh rupees a year**. Even this petty sum was not made available until **1823**, because of the controversy over where it should go. Meanwhile Raja Rammohan Roy and others helped win a grant for **Calcutta College** (1817, the Hindu College of GS-034 module 3, set up by educated Bengalis for English education in Western humanities and sciences), and the government set up three Sanskrit colleges at Calcutta, Delhi and Agra.

## The Orientalist-Anglicist controversy

| | Orientalists | Anglicists |
|---|---|---|
| Spending | Teach Western science for jobs but stress expansion of traditional learning | Spend exclusively on modern studies |
| Medium | -- | Divided: English or the vernaculars |

The body in which it was fought was the **General Committee on Public Instruction** (set up 1823, a date standard and named in a PYQ option; verify).

## Macaulay's Minute, 1835

The Minute settled the dispute in favour of the Anglicists: the limited funds were to be spent on **Western sciences and literature through English alone**. Macaulay held Indian learning to be inferior, which Spectrum says was true of the physical and social sciences of the time. The government made English the medium in its schools and opened a few English schools and colleges instead of many elementary schools, so **mass education was neglected**. The aim was to create a small class, Indian in blood and colour but English in tastes, opinions and intellect, to interpret between rulers and masses and enrich the vernaculars: the **downward filtration theory**. After **1844** government jobs went to those who knew English (Spectrum; verify the order).

## Thomason and the vernacular alternative

**James Thomason** (Spectrum prints 'Thomson' and 'James Jonathan'; standard spelling Thomason, verify), Lieutenant-Governor of the North-Western Provinces 1843 to 53, built a village scheme through vernaculars, teaching mensuration and agriculture to supply the Revenue and Public Works departments. Dalhousie's minute of 1853 favoured vernacular education; William Adam's reports (1835 to 38) described poor vernacular schools in Bengal and Bihar.

## Wood's Despatch, 1854

**Charles Wood**'s despatch, called the 'Magna Carta of English Education in India', was the first comprehensive plan. Its six points:

1. The government must take responsibility for **mass education**, repudiating downward filtration at least on paper.
2. A hierarchy: vernacular primary schools; Anglo-Vernacular high schools; an affiliated college at district level; **affiliating universities** at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras.
3. **English** as medium for higher studies, **vernaculars** at school level.
4. Stress on **female and vocational education** and **teachers' training**.
5. Government institutions to be **secular**.
6. A system of **grants-in-aid** to encourage private enterprise.

### 5. Education II: universities, Hunter and the Act of 1904

*Objective:* State what followed Wood's Despatch, the Hunter Commission's recommendations and the five provisions of the Indian Universities Act, 1904, with the nationalist reaction.

## What followed Wood

Universities at **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** were set up in **1857**, and provincial departments of education followed. The **Bethune School** (J.E.D. Bethune, Calcutta, 1849) was the first fruit of the women's education movement of the 1840s and 1850s; Bethune's efforts brought girls' schools under grants-in-aid and inspection. Spectrum lists an Agriculture Institute at **Pusa** and an Engineering Institute at **Roorkee** here, but the dates fall outside this period: Roorkee dates from 1847 (module 7) and Pusa from Curzon's time, about 1905 (standard knowledge, verify). Spectrum says Wood's ideals dominated for five decades, with rapid westernisation and European heads of institutions.

## The Hunter Commission, 1882 to 83

**Why it was needed.** Earlier schemes had neglected primary and secondary education, and the shift of education to the provinces in **1870** made it worse because provincial funds were small. In **1882** W.W. Hunter chaired a commission to review progress since 1854. It dealt mostly with primary and secondary education and:

- stressed special state care for primary education, **through the vernacular**;
- recommended transfer of primary schools to new **district and municipal boards**;
- split secondary education into a **literary** stream leading to university and a **vocational** stream for commercial careers;
- drew attention to the poor facilities for girls' education outside the presidency towns.

The next two decades saw rapid growth in secondary and collegiate education with Indian participation, and more teaching-and-examining universities such as **Punjab (1882)** and **Allahabad (1887)**.

## Raleigh Commission and the Indian Universities Act, 1904

By the dawn of the 20th century there was political unrest. The official view was that private management had lowered quality and that colleges were 'factories producing political revolutionaries'. Nationalists agreed quality had fallen but accused the government of doing nothing about illiteracy. In **1902** the **Raleigh Commission** examined universities only, barred from reporting on primary or secondary education. Its report led to the **Act of 1904**:

1. Universities to give more attention to study and research.
2. **Fewer fellows**, with shorter terms, and most **nominated by the government**.
3. Government power to **veto** senate regulations, and to amend or make regulations itself.
4. **Stricter** conditions for affiliating private colleges.
5. **Rs 5 lakh a year for five years** to improve higher education and universities.

Curzon justified closer control in the name of quality and efficiency; Spectrum judges that he really sought to restrict education and discipline the educated towards loyalty. Nationalists saw an attempt to strengthen imperialism and sabotage nationalist feeling, and **Gokhale** called it a 'retrograde measure'. Curzon also broadened professional courses and set up the Pusa agriculture college (Spectrum); the 2020 Mains question (module 9) asks you to evaluate his policies. The next step in the same story, the Partition of Bengal and the national education movement it provoked, is GS-038 modules 3 and 5.

Contrast to hold: Hunter dealt with **primary and secondary** schooling and gave control to local boards; Raleigh and the 1904 Act dealt with **universities** and gave control to government.

### 6. Education III: Gokhale, the 1913 resolution, Sadler and Hartog

*Objective:* Trace the demand for compulsory primary education, the Sadler recommendations and the Hartog findings of wastage and stagnation.

## Gokhale and primary education

In **1906** the princely state of **Baroda** introduced compulsory primary education. National leaders urged the same for British India. **Gokhale**, in the Imperial Legislative Council, moved a resolution in **1910**: compulsory education where at least 35 per cent of boys aged 6 to 7 were already at school (Spectrum's figure; verify); costs to be met by provincial governments and local bodies; a separate education department under the central government and a secretary for education. He withdrew it after an assurance of consideration; the government created the department and secretary's post but ignored the main demand. In **March 1911** Gokhale brought a **bill** for free and compulsory primary education in phases; a select committee rejected it.

## The Resolution of 1913

The government declined to accept responsibility for compulsory education but **accepted the policy of removing illiteracy** and urged provinces to give free elementary education to poorer and backward sections. Private effort was to be encouraged and secondary schools improved. A **university in each province** was planned and teaching activity in universities encouraged. 

## Sadler (Calcutta University) Commission, 1917 to 19

Appointed in **1917** with **Michael Sadler** as chair, to study Calcutta University, but its findings applied widely. Its view: better university education needs better secondary education first. Recommendations:

1. A **12-year school course**; entry to university after an **intermediate** stage for a **three-year degree**; a separate **board of secondary and intermediate education**.
2. Less rigidity in university regulations.
3. The university as a **centralised, unitary, residential, teaching, autonomous** body rather than scattered affiliated colleges.
4. Extension of female education, applied science and technology, teachers' training, and professional and vocational colleges.

The report had great influence: from 1916 to 1921 seven new universities arose (Mysore, Patna, Benaras, Aligarh, Dacca, Lucknow, Osmania), then Delhi, Agra and Annamalai. Honours courses, post-graduate teaching and, in **1925**, the **Inter-University Board** followed. Critics said it was too advanced for the time.

## Dyarchy and education

Under the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms education went to **provincial ministries**, and the central grants given freely since 1902 stopped (GS-047 module 2 explains the 1919 Act). Money was short, yet education grew, especially through philanthropy.

## Hartog Committee, 1929

The fast growth in the number of schools and colleges had lowered standards. The **Hartog Committee**, chaired by **Philip Hartog** (Spectrum prints 'Phillip'; the standard form is Philip, verify), was set up in **1929** as an **auxiliary committee of the Simon Commission** (the Commission is GS-044, to come on Day 15, not yet written). It reported mainly on primary education. Its key terms:

- **Wastage**: a child leaving school before achieving literacy, so that money and time are lost.
- **Stagnation**: keeping children in the same class for more than a year, which pushes them out.

Causes named: poverty, caste barriers, religious conservatism, disease and crop failure; one-teacher schools, poorly qualified teachers, weak inspection. Recommendations: quality over quantity, **four-year** primary education under government control and inspection, a local curriculum, teacher refresher courses, schools as community centres, a **diversified secondary curriculum** with industrial and commercial subjects, and equal importance for girls' education. Spectrum judges that quality improved but quantitative expansion suffered.

### 7. Education IV: Wardha, Sargent, vernacular and technical education

*Objective:* Explain the Wardha Scheme, the Sargent Plan, the thread of vernacular and technical education and Spectrum's evaluation of British policy.

## The Wardha Scheme of Basic Education, 1937

The Congress held an all-India education conference at **Wardha** in **October 1937**; the **Zakir Husain committee** then drew up the detailed scheme, later called **Nai Talim**, based on Gandhi's articles in the weekly *Harijan*. Gandhi's complaint: Western education had made a gulf between the educated few and the masses, and English had put a permanent bar between them (Spectrum quotes him). Its provisions:

- free and compulsory education from age **7 to 14**, in the **mother tongue**;
- a **basic handicraft** (spinning and weaving, carpentry, agriculture, pottery, leather work) as the means of educating the child through productive activity, with its social and scientific implications studied;
- mathematics, general science, social studies, painting, music and physical education in the curriculum;
- links with the community through service; Hindi taught where it was not the mother tongue; and a contested point on religious and moral teaching (Spectrum's list says no religious and moral education. Its chapter 22 says Gandhi wanted morality in education. Standard accounts say the scheme left out religious instruction; verify how it treated moral teaching).

It saw little further development because of the Second World War and the resignation of the Congress ministries in October 1939.

## The Sargent Plan, 1944

**Sir John Sargent**, the Educational Adviser to the Government of India, wrote a post-war memorandum, submitted to the Central Advisory Board of Education in **1944**. Aim: **universal literacy within 40 years**. Main points: free **pre-primary** education for ages 3 to 6; **universal, compulsory, free** education for 6 to 14 in two stages (Junior Basic 6 to 11, Senior Basic 11 to 14) with 'learning through activity' and a craft; six-year **high school** for selected children, academic or technical, with the mother tongue as medium and English a compulsory second language; a three-year university degree after the higher secondary stage, abolishing the intermediate course; an all-India body like England's University Grants Committee; adult illiteracy ended in 20 years. Many Indian educationists thought 40 years too long and the cost exorbitant.

## Vernacular education in a line

The thread: Adam's reports (1835 to 38) to Thomason's model schools (1843 to 53) to Dalhousie's 1853 minute to Wood's Despatch (standards, supervision, normal schools) to the Hunter Commission (1882) to the 1904 policy to Hartog (1929) to encouragement by Congress ministries (1937). Between 1854 and 1871 vernacular schools rose more than five-fold.

## Technical education in a line

**Roorkee** Engineering College (1847); Calcutta College of Engineering (1856); Poona College of Engineering (1858); a medical college in Calcutta (1835); Curzon's broadening of professional courses and the Pusa agriculture college. By 1857 there were only three medical colleges (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras) and one good engineering college, at Roorkee, which Spectrum says was open only to Europeans and Eurasians.

## Spectrum's evaluation (six points, condensed to five)

1. The expansion served **other motives** than philanthropy: a cheap supply of educated subordinates for the administration and British firms; a market for British goods; and loyalty, since Western education glorified conquerors.
2. Traditional learning decayed, especially after 1844.
3. **Mass education was neglected**, leaving widespread illiteracy and a gulf between the few and the masses. Spectrum gives **illiteracy** as 84 per cent in 1911 and 92 per cent in 1921. Census literacy was only about 6 per cent in 1911 and 7 per cent in 1921 (illiteracy about 94 and 93 per cent; standard knowledge, verify), so the 1911 figure is the probable misprint. Remember the order of magnitude, not Spectrum's numbers.
4. Fees made education a monopoly of the upper, richer and urban.
5. Women's education and scientific and technical education were largely neglected.

### 8. Master: how press and education built national consciousness

*Objective:* Build a balanced argument for how the two institutions helped nationalism, together with the limits, and use it for Mains.

## The claim to test

Spectrum lists 'Western thought and education' and the 'role of press and literature' among the factors behind modern nationalism (chapter 10). The claim is not that the British wanted this. Spectrum says the English education system was conceived by the rulers in the self-interest of efficient administration, yet it gave a new direction to Indian political thinking.

## Mechanism 1: ideas

Modern education gave access to liberal and radical European thought. Spectrum names Milton, Shelley, John Stuart Mill, Rousseau, Paine, Spencer and Voltaire, who helped Indians absorb rational, secular, democratic and nationalist ideas. Spectrum adds that scholarship restored pride in India's past.

## Mechanism 2: a common language and a common class

English let leaders from different linguistic regions talk to each other. The English-educated formed the **middle-class intelligentsia**, the nucleus of the new political unrest and the leadership of political associations. NCERT Class VIII says the associations of the 1870s and 1880s (Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, Indian Association, Madras Mahajan Sabha, Bombay Presidency Association and the Congress) were mostly led by English-educated professionals, and that they spoke for 'all the people' of India, a new idea of sovereignty. The Congress is GS-036 (to come on Day 14, not yet written).

## Mechanism 3: the press as the public sphere

Newspapers criticised policy, urged unity, and spread ideas of self-government, democracy, civil rights and industrialisation. They were the exchange of political ideas among leaders in different regions. They reached villages through the library movement, and they made **civil liberties** (press freedom first) an integral part of the freedom struggle: the arrest of Tilak and other journalists in 1897 caused public outrage. The VPA itself, with the Arms Act and the Ilbert Bill controversy, is listed by Spectrum among the measures that provoked opposition.

Each law also produced a counter-move: the *Amrita Bazar Patrika* became English, journalists used stratagems, and prosecution made Tilak 'Lokmanya'.

## The limits, which an examiner wants

- **Narrow reach.** Education reached a small minority; Spectrum gives widespread illiteracy and says the educated-masses gulf was wide.
- **Filtration did not work as designed**, but ideas still travelled through parties, press, pamphlets and platforms, not through classrooms.
- **Colonial content.** The curricula served colonial interests, though they supplied basic literature on the physical and social sciences that nationalists used (Spectrum).
- **Inequality inside nationalism.** Education was costly and urban, women were neglected, and the Dalit press (Mooknayak) warned that nationalism ignoring caste would stay incomplete.

## A balanced verdict, in four sentences

British policy on both fronts was designed for control and for cheap administration. It produced an English-educated class and a vigorous press that the rulers could not fully control. Repression often **spread** the message it meant to stop. The result was an unintended, uneven gain for nationalism that reached the masses later, through Gandhian mobilisation and mass politics rather than through schools.

Links: student boycotts of 1921 belong to GS-043 (to come on Day 14, not yet written); national education of 1905 to 08 is GS-038 module 5.

### Notes: Education chronology

- 1781 Calcutta Madrasah (Hastings); 1791 Sanskrit College, Benares (Duncan); 1800 Fort William College (Wellesley).
- 1813 Charter Act: Rs 1 lakh a year principle; money released only from 1823.
- 1835 Macaulay's Minute: English medium, downward filtration.
- 1854 Wood's Despatch; 1857 universities at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras.
- 1870 education shifted to provinces; 1882-83 Hunter Commission.
- 1902 Raleigh Commission; 1904 Indian Universities Act.
- 1910 Gokhale's resolution; March 1911 bill rejected; 1913 Resolution on Education Policy.
- 1917-19 Sadler Commission; 1919 dyarchy transfers education to provinces; 1925 Inter-University Board.
- 1929 Hartog Committee; October 1937 Wardha conference and Basic Education scheme; 1944 Sargent Plan.

### Notes: Orientalist versus Anglicist and the settlement

- The debate ended in 1835 with the Minute.

### Notes: Commissions and committees

- Hunter (1882-83): primary and secondary; vernacular for primary; local boards; literary and vocational streams.
- Raleigh (1902): universities only; led to the 1904 Act.
- Sadler (1917-19): Calcutta University; 12-year school, intermediate, 3-year degree, unitary universities.
- Hartog (1929): auxiliary committee of the Simon Commission; wastage and stagnation; four-year primary.
- Sargent (1944): universal literacy in 40 years; 6-14 compulsory; UGC-like body.
- Zakir Husain committee (1937): detailed Basic Education scheme after the Wardha conference.

### Notes: How press and education built nationalism

- Western education gave liberal ideas and an English-speaking all-India educated class.
- A middle-class intelligentsia led the associations of the 1870s and 1880s.
- The press spread criticism and unity, and libraries carried it to villages.
- Repressive laws (VPA, 1908, 1910) caused outrage and made journalists heroes.
- Limits: narrow reach, colonial curricula, neglect of women and mass education.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Wood's Despatch (1854, for all levels, grants-in-aid, universities) is not Macaulay's Minute (1835, English alone).
- Hunter (primary and secondary) is not Raleigh (universities) is not Hartog (primary wastage).
- Wardha Scheme (1937, Congress, Gandhi, craft) is not the Sargent Plan (1944, government adviser, 40 years).
- Metcalfe repealed the 1823 licensing; the 1857 Act restored licensing.
- Sanskrit College Benares (Duncan) versus Calcutta Madrasah (Hastings) versus Fort William (Lord Wellesley).

### Notes: Source slips and verify list

### Practice

**Q.** What is the downward filtration theory?

**A.** The idea that educating a small upper and middle class would pass knowledge down to the masses.

**Q.** Who repealed the Vernacular Press Act and when?

**A.** Ripon, in 1882.

**Q.** What did Metcalfe's 1835 Act do?

**A.** Repealed the 1823 licensing rules and required a declaration of premises by printer or publisher.

**Q.** What is a despatch?

**A.** An official policy letter from London to the Government of India.

**Q.** Why was the 1878 Act a landmark?

**A.** It discriminated against the vernacular press, gave no appeal and provoked nationwide opposition until repeal.

**Q.** Why is Wood's Despatch called the Magna Carta of English education?

**A.** It was the first comprehensive plan for education in India.

**Q.** Why did mass education suffer under Macaulay's policy?

**A.** Funds went to a few English schools instead of many elementary ones.

**Q.** How did repression sometimes help the nationalists?

**A.** Prosecutions such as Tilak's turned journalists into heroes and spread outrage.

**Q.** A question asks for the Hunter Commission. List four recommendations.

**A.** Primary through vernacular, local boards, literary and vocational streams, more girls' education.
