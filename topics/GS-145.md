## GS-145: Family, kinship and changing households

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Family, kinship and changing households and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Forms of the family

Families can be classified by structure, headship, descent and residence. Structurally, the nuclear family contains a single parental couple and children, while an extended family brings together several couples and usually more than two generations, such as brothers with their families or elderly parents living with sons and grandsons. Residence rules distinguish matrilocal arrangements, where a couple lives with the wife's parents, from patrilocal ones. Inheritance rules distinguish matrilineal descent, from mother to daughter, from patrilineal descent, from father to son. Authority distinguishes patriarchy from matriarchy, though NCERT stresses that no society with genuine female dominance has been documented. The extended family is often treated as typically Indian, but it was confined to certain sections and regions, and I.P. Desai noted that Indian words for it are translations of the English phrase.

### How wider change reshapes households

The private sphere of the family is tied to economic, political, cultural and educational change. NCERT's examples are concrete: men migrating out of Himalayan villages leave an unusually high proportion of households headed by women, and long working hours of young parents in the software industry draw grandparents into urban homes as caregivers. Some changes are accidental, following war or migration; others are deliberate, as when young people choose their own spouses or same-sex love is openly expressed. These shifts alter not only household composition but also ideas, norms and values. The family is a space of warmth and care, yet it is also a site of conflict, property disputes and violence, including female infanticide. Change in marriage and family norms has often faced violent resistance.

### Ageing and intergenerational support

Government data show the family still carries most old-age support. MoSPI's Elderly in India 2021, drawing on the NSS 75th round of 2017–18, reports that around 70% of persons aged 60 and above relied on others for daily maintenance. Gender gaps are sharp: only about a tenth of elderly women were economically independent, compared with roughly half or more of elderly men. Dependent older men and women were financially supported mainly by their own children, then by spouses and grandchildren, a pattern little changed since 2004. The share of economically independent elderly who themselves supported one or more dependents fell between 2004 and 2017–18 in both rural and urban areas. Such data link family change to pensions, care services and women's economic security in later life.
