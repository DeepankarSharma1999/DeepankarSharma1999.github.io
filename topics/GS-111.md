### Bridge: Bridge: floating, density and why an ice cube does not overflow the glass

The **density** of a material is its mass in a given volume, for example grams in each cubic centimetre (g/cm3). Water is about 1.00 g/cm3. Sea water is a little denser, roughly 1.025 g/cm3, because of dissolved salt (a standard value; the salinity story is in Day 18 lesson GS-109). Ordinary ice is a little lighter than water, roughly 0.917 g/cm3 (standard value; verify), which is why ice floats.

**Archimedes' principle** says that a floating object pushes aside (displaces) a weight of water exactly equal to its own weight. Put 900 g of ice in water and it sinks until it has pushed aside 900 g of water, which is 900 cm3 of fresh water. The part under the surface has volume 900 cm3. The whole piece of ice has volume 900 / 0.917 = 981 cm3, so about 81 cm3 sticks out above the surface.

Now let the ice melt. It becomes 900 g of water, and 900 g of fresh water has volume 900 cm3, exactly the volume the ice had been pushing aside. The water level in the glass therefore does not change. Try it at home: fill a glass to the brim with an ice cube floating in it and watch whether it overflows as the cube melts.

Keep one sentence from this bridge: **floating ice already counts in the water level; ice resting on land does not.** Module 5 uses this to separate sea ice from land ice.

### 1. Orientation: the frozen part of the planet on one page

*Objective:* State what the cryosphere is, list its parts, and say why geography and climate science treat it as important.

## Meaning

The **cryosphere** is the part of the Earth where water is frozen. PMF IAS defines it as the areas of snow or ice that are subject to temperatures below 0 degrees Celsius for at least part of the year. It includes the continental **ice sheets** of Greenland and Antarctica, **ice caps**, **glaciers**, areas of snow (PMF names the Alps and Himalayas), **permafrost** (ground that stays frozen, for example in Siberia), and the frozen parts of the ocean, rivers and lakes (PMF IAS, PDF p. 454).

A **glacier** is a mass of ice that moves slowly downhill under its own weight; Leong's phrase is a 'river of ice' (PDF p. 85). A **sheet** is a wide, thick cover of ice. The exact difference between glacier, ice sheet, ice cap, ice shelf and sea ice is the job of module 3.

## Background: the Ice Age

Leong says the most recent big change of climate began about 700 000 years ago (a dated figure; the present ice-age series is usually dated from about 2.6 million years ago: verify), when temperature fell over the whole earth; at the height of the Ice Age temperatures were only about 5 degrees Celsius lower than today, yet that was enough for large areas of the northern hemisphere to receive snow instead of rain. The snow piled up, became ice and moved down slopes and valleys as glaciers and ice sheets. During the last 20 000 years the world has warmed, and the area covered by ice is now only about one-third of its former extent (Leong, PDF p. 84). The lesson from this: **a change of a few degrees in the average temperature is enough to switch very large areas between ice and no ice.**

## Why this topic matters

PMF gives three roles of the cryosphere (PDF p. 454).

1. **Heat budget.** Snow and ice have the highest **albedo**, meaning they reflect a large share of the sunlight that falls on them back into space instead of absorbing it.
2. **Fresh water.** Glaciers and snow-covered high mountains supply fresh water to many parts of the world.
3. **A record of the past.** Ice builds up layer over layer, so studying a vertical column of ice shows past climates. PMF calls the cryosphere the Earth's 'black box' because it is the most sensitive to climate shifts.

### 2. Foundation: from snow to ice, and the gains and losses of a glacier

*Objective:* Describe how snow becomes glacier ice, define snow line, accumulation, ablation and mass balance, and compute a simple mass balance.

## From snow to ice

Newly fallen snow is light and powdery with a crystal form. Leong says it takes up three or four times the volume of the same water; that ratio fits settled snow, and fresh snow is commonly nearer ten times (as in the book; verify). As it slowly melts and refreezes the crystals lose their shape and the snow is packed with fewer air spaces; this stage is called **firn** or **neve** (Leong, PDF p. 85). The weight of more snow above compresses firn into ice. Under gravity this ice creeps downhill, usually channelled into an existing valley, and is then a glacier.

## The snow line

The **permanent snow line** is the level at which snow lost by summer melting fails to remove the snow gathered in winter (Leong, PDF p. 84). Above it snow lasts all year; below it snow melts away. Leong gives the equatorial snow line as between 4 200 and 4 800 metres and says it becomes lower as we go towards the poles, reaching sea level in the polar regions. (Leong's Fig. 6.2 caption quotes about 5 182 m for Mt Kilimanjaro, which does not match the text: check, and treat the 4 200-4 800 m range as the textbook figure for the lesson.) Reading: *a high snow line means a warm place; a snow line at sea level means a very cold one.* Standard knowledge (verify): the snow line is actually highest in the dry subtropics, about 20 to 30 degrees from the equator, because snowfall and slope aspect matter as well as temperature; this may help explain the Kilimanjaro caption.

## Accumulation, ablation and mass balance

Think of a glacier as a bank account.

- **Accumulation** is what is added: snowfall, plus snow blown or avalanched on to the glacier, mostly in the upper part.
- **Ablation** is what is lost: melting, and loss of ice at the lower end (including calving of icebergs where a glacier meets water). Ablation happens mostly in the lower part.
- **Mass balance** = accumulation minus ablation, counted over a year (standard definitions; verify wording).

Original worked example. Glacier A gains 120 units of snow in a year and loses 150 units. Mass balance = 120 - 150 = -30 units: the glacier is losing mass. If gains equal losses, the balance is zero and the glacier is in equilibrium. Glaciologists call the line on the glacier where the year's gains and losses cancel the **equilibrium line** (standard term; verify); on a glacier whose balance is near zero it is close to the snow line.

## A glacier can shrink while still flowing

Ice always flows downhill, so a glacier does not 'roll back'. When the mass balance stays negative year after year, the **snout** (the lower end) melts faster than ice arrives, ends up higher on the slope and the glacier **retreats** and thins. A positive balance makes the snout advance.

### 3. Core: glacier, ice sheet, ice cap, ice shelf, iceberg, sea ice and permafrost

*Objective:* Distinguish the main ice types by where they sit, what they are made of and how big they are, using the extracts' figures.

## Land ice that moves: glaciers

A **valley (Alpine) glacier** is ice that has moved down and been channelled into an existing valley (Leong, PDF p. 85). Where several glaciers leave their valleys at the foot of a mountain range and spread together, they form a **piedmont glacier**; Leong's example is the Malaspina Glacier of Alaska, 104 km long and 40 km wide, covering more than 4 200 sq km (PDF p. 86).

## Land ice that covers land: ice sheets and ice caps

When glaciers leave the uplands and enter a lowland, the ice spreads out and several glaciers may join to form an **ice sheet**; Leong says ice sheets move very much more slowly than glaciers (PDF p. 85; true of the interior, while outlet glaciers can be fast: module 4). Leong names the present-day examples as Greenland and Antarctica and calls them 'ice caps': Greenland covers 1 872 000 sq km (720 000 square miles, about 1 865 000 sq km; as in the book; verify) and Antarctica more than 13 million sq km, with ice hardened to a depth of over a kilometre and measured at more than 4 200 m thick in Marie Byrd Land, Antarctica. The weight is so great that the land beneath sinks gradually (PDF p. 84).

**Usage warning.** PMF lists 'continental ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica' and 'ice caps' as separate items (PDF p. 454), and current usage reserves *ice sheet* for the two continental masses and *ice cap* for a smaller dome of ice on a highland. A common textbook size rule puts the dividing line at about 50 000 sq km (standard knowledge; verify). For the examination use PMF's wording: Greenland and Antarctica are ice sheets; Leong's 'ice caps' means the same thing. Arithmetic check: Greenland's 1 872 000 sq km is about 14 per cent of Antarctica's 13 000 000 sq km, so Antarctica's ice sheet is roughly seven times larger.

## Ice that floats

- **Ice shelf.** Where an ice sheet reaches right down to the sea it often extends outward over polar water and floats as an ice shelf, ending in steep cliffs (Leong, PDF p. 85).
- **Iceberg.** When the shelf's edge breaks into individual blocks, they are icebergs. Afloat only about one-ninth of the mass shows above the surface (Leong). The ratio is explained in bridge B1: ice is only slightly less dense than sea water, so most of it sits below the surface; with 917 and 1 025 kg per cubic metre (standard values; verify), 1 - 917/1025 = 0.105, about one-tenth (rounded by Leong to one-ninth).
- **Sea ice.** Ice formed by the freezing of ocean surface water, as opposed to glaciers, which start as snow on land. PMF counts 'frozen parts of the ocean' in the cryosphere and describes the Arctic as an ocean covered by sea ice (PDF pp. 454, 456).

## Ground ice: permafrost

**Permafrost** is ground that stays frozen through the year; the usual textbook rule is ground frozen for at least two consecutive years (standard knowledge; verify). PMF's example is Siberia, and notes that tundra permafrost is thawing (PDF pp. 453, 455).

## Quick sorting rule

Ask three questions: Is it on land or sea? Does it flow in a valley or cover everything? Does it float? Land + valley = glacier. Land + covers a continent = ice sheet. Land + smaller highland dome = ice cap (verify). Floats + attached to land ice = ice shelf. Floats + broken away from land ice = iceberg. Frozen from sea water = sea ice. Frozen ground = permafrost.

### 4. Core: how glaciers move and why some surge

*Objective:* Explain what drives glacier movement, where the ice moves fastest, quote the speed ranges the extracts give, and define surge-type glaciers.

## What makes ice move

Under the force of gravity, ice moves downslope (Leong, PDF p. 85). The thicker the ice and the steeper the slope, the stronger the push. Glaciologists add that ice deforms internally and may slide on a film of melt-water at its bed (standard knowledge; verify), which is why glaciers that are warm at the base can move faster.

## Speeds quoted in the extracts

Leong says speeds vary a great deal: some glaciers move only centimetres a year, others several hundred metres a week (PDF p. 85). In the Alps the average flow is about one metre a day; in Greenland it may be more than 15 metres (Leong gives no unit; read per day; this is a figure for fast outlet glaciers, as in the book; verify); in Antarctica, where there is little heat to melt the ice, Leong says glaciers move only a few centimetres a day (PDF p. 86; verify, because Antarctic ice streams and outlet glaciers such as Pine Island move metres a day). **Take-away:** speed is not fixed; it depends on slope, thickness, temperature and the amount of melt-water.

## Where the ice moves fastest

A glacier moves faster in the **middle** than at the sides and bottom, where friction with the valley walls and floor holds it back (Leong, PDF p. 85). If a row of stakes is planted in a straight line across a glacier, after some years they take a curved shape bowing down the valley (Leong's Fig. 6.3).

Edge case test of the rule: Leong states it for valley glaciers. An ice-sheet interior has no valley walls, so the rule does not apply in that form; its outlet glaciers and ice streams can be among the fastest ice (verify).

## Cracks

At the head of a glacier, where the ice leaves the snowfield, a deep vertical crack called a **bergschrund** may open in summer; further down, where the ice bends or crosses a steep slope, more cracks called **crevasses** form (Leong, PDF p. 88).

## Surge-type glaciers

PMF defines **surge-type glaciers** as glaciers that show advancement in volume and length over a period, going against the normal trend of reduction in volume and length of most glaciers. They do not flow at a constant speed but have cyclical flow instabilities, and when they break they can cause catastrophic **glacial lake outburst floods** (GLOFs) because of global warming (PMF IAS, PDF p. 455). A GLOF is a sudden flood released when a lake held back by ice or loose rock debris bursts (standard definition; verify). An advancing glacier is not proof of cooling: a surge is a flow instability.

Glacial landforms are in the Day 23 lesson GS-101.

### 5. Core: sea ice versus land ice, and the sea-level test

*Objective:* Explain, with numbers, why melting floating ice barely changes sea level while melting land ice raises it, and name the indirect routes by which sea ice matters.

## The claim to test

Claim: **melting sea ice does not raise sea level much, while melting land ice does.** This is often stated and often misread, so test it step by step. Use the bridge B1 idea: floating ice already pushes aside its own weight of water.

## Test 1: floating ice in fresh water

Take 900 g of floating ice. It pushes aside 900 g of water, which is 900 cm3 at 1.00 g/cm3. Melted, it becomes 900 g of water, which is 900 cm3. Net change in water level: 0.

## Test 2: floating ice in sea water

Sea water is denser, about 1.025 g/cm3 (standard value; verify). The same 900 g of floating ice now pushes aside 900 / 1.025 = 878 cm3 of sea water. Melted, it becomes fresh water of 900 cm3. The difference is 900 - 878 = 22 cm3, which is 2.5 per cent of the displaced volume. So the level rises by a very small amount, **not exactly zero but tiny**. The reason is that the melt-water is fresher and therefore less dense than the sea water it replaces. For most examination purposes: *melting sea ice does not noticeably raise sea level.*

## Test 3: land ice

Take 900 g of ice sitting on a continent. It pushes aside no ocean water. When it melts, the water runs into the sea and adds 900 cm3. In this model land ice adds 900 cm3 against about 22 cm3 for the same mass of floating ice, roughly forty times as much. Ice already grounded below sea level, as in much of West Antarctica, displaces some water, so only the part above flotation adds to sea level (verify). That is why the melt of the **Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets and of mountain glaciers** raises sea level, while melting Arctic sea ice does not do so directly.

## Edge cases that test the rule

1. **An ice shelf is floating**, so its own melt hardly changes sea level. But glaciologists note that shelves hold back the glaciers behind them (buttressing; standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify): when a shelf thins or breaks, the land ice behind may flow faster to the sea and then raise sea level.
2. **Ice sliding off the land into the sea** adds to the ocean as soon as it floats, because it was previously not pushing water aside (grounding-line idea; verify).
3. **Warm water expands.** Part of sea-level rise comes from thermal expansion of ocean water as it warms (standard knowledge; verify), whatever happens to the ice.

## Reading PMF carefully

PMF lists 'global sea-level rise' as the most obvious impact of Arctic warming (PDF p. 456). That is correct only through the Arctic's **land ice** (Greenland, Arctic glaciers), not sea ice. Sea-ice loss still matters through lower albedo (module 6), shipping lanes (module 7) and mid-latitude weather.

### 6. Core: why the cryosphere is shrinking - warming, albedo and Arctic amplification

*Objective:* Explain the causal chain behind shrinking ice, draw the albedo and permafrost feedback loops, and explain why the Arctic warms faster than Antarctica.

## The first cause: warmer air and water

A glacier shrinks when ablation exceeds accumulation (module 2). Warmer air and warmer water raise melting in the ablation zone and at the sea edge, and a warmer climate can also turn snowfall into rain. PMF says the cryosphere is the part of the Earth most sensitive to climate shifts (PDF p. 454). The root cause of the warming is the rise in greenhouse gases.

## The albedo feedback

**Albedo** is the fraction of sunlight a surface reflects. Snow and ice have the highest albedo and reflect heat from the sun; PMF explains that when ice melts it reveals darker land or ocean, which absorbs more sunlight (low albedo) and warms more (PDF p. 456). More warming then melts more ice. A feedback that strengthens the original change is a **positive feedback** (it means 'self-reinforcing', not 'good').

## Arctic (polar) amplification

PMF defines **Arctic or polar amplification** as the ratio of warming at the poles to warming in the tropics. It reports a study in *Nature* that the Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the global average since 1979 (PDF p. 456). Elsewhere in the same chapter PMF says the Arctic is warming 'twice as fast' as the global average (PDF p. 455). The two figures differ by period and study: use 'faster than the global average, about two to four times depending on the study (as in the book; verify)'. PMF names the melting of polar ice as the primary cause, and says ice loss in the Arctic is running at '13% per decade' (the wording is ambiguous: it presumably refers to the decline of sea-ice extent; as in the book; verify). PMF adds that melting sea ice also releases greenhouse gases from thawing permafrost and frozen methane under the sea floor.

## Why the Arctic more than Antarctica

PMF's reason: the Arctic is an **ocean covered by sea ice**, so when the ice melts open water is exposed; Antarctica is an **elevated continent** covered by more permanent ice and snow. PMF adds that the Antarctic continent has not warmed over the past seven decades despite the rise in greenhouse gases (PDF p. 456; as in the book; verify, because parts of Antarctica such as the Peninsula are reported to have warmed). The comparison table is in the master notes.

## Permafrost and wildfire loops

Thawing permafrost releases **carbon dioxide and methane**, which warm the Arctic further (PMF: positive feedback, PDF p. 456). Wildfires add to this: PMF reports burning well above the Arctic Circle in 2020 and more frequent 'zombie fires' (holdover fires from a previous growing season that smoulder under the ground in carbon-rich peat) in the once-frozen tundra (PDF p. 453).

Black carbon (soot) and other local drivers are not in the two extracts; treat them as 'verify' additions.

### 7. Master: consequences of shrinking ice - water, hazards, coasts and the Arctic opening

*Objective:* Sort the consequences listed by PMF into physical, human and economic groups, explain GLOFs, and weigh the Arctic opportunities against the challenges.

## PMF's list, sorted

PMF lists the consequences of shrinking glaciers (PDF pp. 454-455). A good answer sorts them rather than listing them.

1. **Water and weather.** Water scarcity and 'water wars between nations'; disastrous changes in major weather patterns; reduced hydroelectric power from abnormal glacier behaviour, which raises dependence on fossil fuels and so feeds the warming loop again.
2. **Sea level and coasts.** Loss of ecologically rich coastal wetlands; distress migration from submerged coastal cities; submergence of Small Island Developing States (the first to suffer); salinisation of coastal groundwater.
3. **Living things and land.** Loss of habitat adds species to threatened and extinct lists. Thawing frozen land can increase arable land in high latitudes while coastal arable land shrinks through inundation and saline water (PMF, PDF p. 455).
4. **Hazards.** Surge-type glaciers and **glacial lake outburst floods** (module 4). Leong notes that lakes dammed by landslides, screes or avalanches are short-lived and flood downstream when the barrier gives way (Leong, chapter 9, PDF p. 122; see Day 19 lesson GS-118, section 'Core: ox-bow lakes, lagoons, barrier lakes and human-made lakes').

## Fresh water from ice

Glaciers and high snowfields are stores of fresh water that release it in the warm season (PMF, PDF p. 454). Rivers fed by snow and ice are **perennial**, flowing all year (NCERT kegy103 via Day 18 lesson GS-117). As the stored ice shrinks, melt first rises and later may fall (the 'peak water' idea, standard knowledge; verify), so a short-term increase of river flow is not a sign of safety.

## The Arctic opening

PMF describes an Arctic warming faster than the global average, with the Northern Sea Route (NSR) slowly opening as ice melts; the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is reported to project at least one summer with near-zero sea ice before 2050 (PMF, PDF p. 455; as in the book; verify). The route from Rotterdam to Yokohama would be 40 per cent shorter than via Suez. Unexplored oil and gas is estimated at 22 per cent of the world's unexplored resources, mostly in the Arctic Ocean (Barents Sea region), and Greenland holds 25 per cent of global rare-earth reserves (PMF figures, as in the book; verify).

## Challenges and governance

Mining and deep-sea drilling bring costs and environmental risks. Unlike Antarctica, the Arctic is not a global commons: no treaty governs it, only the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) addresses it. Large parts are under the sovereignty of the five littoral (coastal) states, Russia, Canada, Norway, Denmark (Greenland) and the US, so new resource use is within their rights and may trigger territorial disputes (PMF, PDF p. 456).

### 8. Master: Himalayan glaciers, the Arctic and Antarctica compared

*Objective:* Use only the extracts' facts to describe Himalayan glaciers and the Arctic-Antarctic contrasts, and show where figures must be flagged for verification.

## What the extracts say about Himalayan glaciers

The extracts give **sources, not statistics**. NCERT (kegy103, via Day 18 lesson GS-117) names the glacier that feeds many Himalayan rivers: the Indus rises from a glacier near Bokhar Chu in the Tibetan region (PDF p. 4); the Ganga's headstream Bhagirathi rises in the **Gangotri glacier near Gaumukh** (3 900 m) in Uttarkashi district; the Alaknanda rises in the **Satopanth glacier** above Badrinath; the Yamuna in the **Yamunotri glacier**; the Ghaghara in the **glaciers of Mapchachungo**; the Sarda (Goriganga) in the **Milam glacier** (NCERT adds 'in the Nepal Himalayas', but the glacier is in Pithoragarh district, Uttarakhand: a slip; verify); and the Brahmaputra in the **Chemayungdung glacier** of the Kailash range near Mansarovar lake (PDF pp. 5-6). Himalayan rivers are perennial because they are fed by both snow melt and rain (PDF p. 3).

PMF names two Himalayan glacier cases: the **Khumbu Glacier** between Mt Everest and the Lhotse-Nuptse ridge (Sagarmatha National Park; verify), PMF's example from the 21 of 46 natural World Heritage sites with glaciers where a study by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) predicts glacier extinction by 2100 if emissions continue at the current rate; and the **Yala Glacier** in Nepal, declared 'dead' after losing 66 per cent of its area since the 1970s, the only Himalayan glacier on the Global Glacier Casualty List (GGCL) (PDF p. 454). The GGCL was started in 2024 with a launch set of 15 glaciers (other examples: Okjokull in Iceland, Sarenne in France). All figures here are 'as in the book; verify'.

## The Himalayan answer (Mains 2020 style)

River flow from ice and snow supports the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems that carry water to the northern plains (agriculture, drinking water, hydropower). Mechanism: negative mass balance -> melt first rises, later falls -> floods and glacial lake outbursts in the near term, water shortage in the dry season later. Close with adaptation: monitoring, early-warning for glacial lakes, efficient water use. For a named place see the master notes block 'Named Himalayan glacier hazards' (verify).

## India and the Arctic (Mains 2018 style)

PMF's answer elements: new shipping routes (Rotterdam-Yokohama 40 per cent shorter than via Suez), oil, gas and rare earths, and the Arctic as the 'black box' for climate research. ### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Cryosphere: areas of snow or ice subject to temperatures below 0 degrees C for at least part of the year (PMF).
- Firn or neve: compacted, granular snow between fresh snow and glacier ice (Leong).
- Permanent snow line: the level at which summer melting fails to remove the snow gathered in winter (Leong).
- Accumulation: snow and other additions to a glacier; ablation: melting and other losses; mass balance = accumulation - ablation (standard definitions; verify).
- Albedo: the share of sunlight a surface reflects; snow and ice have the highest albedo (PMF).
- Arctic or polar amplification: the ratio of polar warming to tropical warming (PMF).
- Surge-type glacier: a glacier with cyclical flow instabilities that can advance in length and volume and cause glacial lake outburst floods (PMF).
- Glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF): a sudden flood when a lake dammed by ice or debris bursts (standard definition; verify).

### Notes: Feedbacks and causal chains

- Warming -> ice melts -> darker surface exposed -> more sunlight absorbed -> more warming (albedo loop, positive feedback).
- Warming -> permafrost thaws -> carbon dioxide and methane released -> more warming (PMF PDF p. 456).

- Negative mass balance -> snout retreats and ice thins; melt first rises and later falls (peak-water idea; verify).

### Notes: Where the rules have edge cases

- Sea ice does not noticeably raise sea level; but an ice shelf's thinning may speed the flow of land ice behind it, and warm-water expansion raises sea level regardless of ice (all verify).
- Floating ice in salt water melts to slightly more volume than it displaced (about 2.5 per cent in the 900 g test), so 'unchanged' is a close approximation, not exact.
- A glacier can advance (surge) in a warming world; advance does not prove cooling.
- The centre-moves-faster rule is for valley glaciers; ice-sheet interiors are slow, but their outlet glaciers and ice streams can be among the fastest ice (verify).
- Antarctica is said not to have warmed over seven decades, yet its ice still matters for sea level in the long run; do not conflate temperature trend with ice loss (verify).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Glacier versus ice sheet: valley-scale moving ice versus a continent-sized cover.
- Ice sheet versus ice shelf: the sheet rests on land, the shelf floats off its edge.
- Iceberg versus sea ice: an iceberg is a block broken from land ice (fresh water origin); sea ice is frozen sea water.
- Albedo versus emissivity: albedo is reflection of sunlight; do not use the second word for it.
- Weather versus climate: a cold winter does not disprove warming (see Day 18 lesson GS-110).
- Cryosphere versus permafrost: permafrost is one part of the cryosphere.
