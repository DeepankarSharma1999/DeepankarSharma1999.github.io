## GS-150: Urbanisation challenges and reforms

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Urbanisation challenges and reforms and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Trends and definitions

Urbanisation is the rise in the share of a country's population living in urban areas. In India it was under 11% in 1901, about 17% in 1951 and 31.16% in 2011, which the Class 12 Geography text calls low compared with developed countries, adding that urban growth has slowed in the last two decades. The Census 1991 definition refers to places with municipal-type bodies and adds population, occupational and density thresholds: at least 5,000 people, three-quarters of male workers outside agriculture and 400 persons per square kilometre. Class 11 Sociology distinguishes a town from a city mainly by size and defines an urban agglomeration as a city plus suburbs and satellite settlements. Towns can be classified by function, such as administrative, industrial, transport, commercial, mining, educational, religious and tourist centres.

### Why people move to cities

In 2011 about 68.8% of Indians lived in rural areas, yet agriculture contributed only about one-sixth of GDP and many villagers now work outside farming. The city acts as a magnet for those unable to find enough rural work. Migration is accelerated by the loss of common property resources, as ponds dry up, forests vanish and grazing land is privatised, forcing the poor to buy fuel and fodder they once gathered free. Social reasons matter too: anonymity can partly shield Scheduled Castes and Tribes from daily caste humiliation and allows poorer members of dominant groups to take low-status jobs. Metropolises grow fastest, attracting migrants from villages and small towns. Media images of urban consumption spread to villages, while rural areas remain decisive in politics.

### Urban social order is about space

Density makes space the central urban problem: housing, mass transit, separation of residential and industrial zones, and public health, sanitation and policing are all formidable tasks. Shortage of housing for the poor produces homelessness and slums, defined as congested neighbourhoods lacking proper sanitation, water and electricity; insecure property rights let local strongmen control slum territory. At the other end, affluent gated communities wall themselves off with private services. Gentrification converts lower-class neighbourhoods into middle-class ones as land values rise. When homes are far from workplaces, commuting becomes a way of life, and reliance on private cars causes congestion and pollution, whereas affordable public transport improves quality of life. NCERT identifies the key question for India as how cities will absorb continual migration on top of natural growth.
