## GS-119: Indian monsoon mechanism and variability

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Reading wind directions, left and right, on a spinning earth

The monsoon story depends on two small rules, so learn them before module 1.

**Rule 1: a wind is named after the direction it comes FROM.** A south-west wind blows from the south-west towards the north-east. A sea breeze blows from the sea. So when you read 'the south-west monsoon', picture air travelling towards the north-east.

**Rule 2: the Coriolis force.** Because the earth spins, air that is travelling over long distances is turned sideways. In the northern hemisphere the turn is to the **right** of the direction of travel; in the southern hemisphere it is to the **left** (NCERT kegy209 p4, Day 19 lesson GS-104). The force is zero at the equator and strongest at the poles.

**A worked check.** Air in the southern hemisphere blows from the south-east (a south-east trade wind) towards the north-west. It crosses the equator and is now in the northern hemisphere. Facing north-west, your right-hand side is north-east, so the air bends towards the north-east: it is now travelling from the south-west. That is exactly how a south-east trade wind becomes the south-west monsoon (module 4). Now reverse it for winter: air moving from the north-east towards the south-west stays a north-east wind; the winter monsoon is called north-east because that is where it comes from.

**A pressure rule.** Wind moves from high pressure to low pressure. Low pressure is made where air is heated and rises; high pressure where air is cooled and sinks. The word **monsoon** itself, NCERT says, means a climate with a seasonal reversal in wind direction (kegy104 p2).

### 1. Orientation: the Indian monsoon on one page

*Objective:* Say in one minute what the monsoon is, why India has it and the order of its year: build-up, burst, rains, break, retreat.

## What the word means

NCERT (kegy104 p2) says **monsoon** is the climate that goes with a **seasonal reversal in the direction of winds**. India has a *hot monsoonal climate*, the usual climate of south and south-east Asia. The same page stresses two ideas that every answer needs: there is **unity** (the whole of India is monsoonal in rhythm and character) and there is **diversity** (Kerala and Tamil Nadu differ from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, yet all are monsoon climates).

## The year in six steps

1. **Winter (about December-February).** Pressure is higher over the northern plain, so cool dry air blows out from the land towards the sea. NCERT: these winter monsoons give no rain because they come from land to sea with little moisture.
2. **Spring heating (March-May).** The sun moves north, the northern plains heat up and a deep **low-pressure** area forms in the north-west.
3. **The pull.** The low attracts air from the cooler Indian Ocean in the south; the south-east trades cross the equator and turn into the **south-west monsoon**.
4. **The burst (about 1 June at Kerala).** Rain begins abruptly with thunder and lightning; NCERT calls this the "burst" or "break" of the monsoon, and the day temperature falls by 5-8 degrees C between mid-June and mid-July.
5. **The rains, with breaks (June-September).** Two branches, the Arabian Sea branch and the Bay of Bengal branch, spread over the country. Dry spells of a week or more are called **breaks**.
6. **Retreat (October-November).** The low-pressure trough moves south, skies clear, then the north-east monsoon brings rain to the east of the Peninsula.

## Why it is hard

No single theory explains the monsoon fully (kegy104 p4), so there are two views: *land-sea heating* (module 4) and *upper-air and ocean* (module 5).

### 2. Foundation: weather, climate and the evidence of diversity

*Objective:* Define the key words and quote the NCERT figures that show both the unity and the diversity of India's climate.

## Words to own before the mechanism

- **Weather**: the momentary state of the atmosphere. **Climate**: the average of weather over a long period; NCERT says climate changes imperceptibly and shows only after 50 years or more (kegy104 p2).
- **Elements of weather**: temperature, pressure, wind direction and speed, humidity and precipitation.
- **Pressure gradient**: a difference in pressure from place to place; air moves down it, from high to low (Day 19 lesson GS-104).
- **ITCZ** (Inter Tropical Convergence Zone): a low-pressure zone near the equator where trade winds meet and air tends to rise (kegy104 p4; kegy209 p5).
- **Thermal low**: a low-pressure area produced by strong heating of land.
- **Windward / leeward**: the side of a mountain that faces the wind, and the side away from it. **Rain shadow**: the dry leeward area (module 6).
- **Isohyet**: a line joining places with equal rainfall (the activity in kegy104 p15 asks for the 100 cm isohyet). **Isotherm**: equal temperature.
- **Burst** and **break**: NCERT uses 'break' or 'burst' for the sudden onset of the first rains (kegy104 p9) and, separately, 'break in the monsoon' for a dry spell of a week or more in mid-season (kegy104 p5). In this lesson **burst** means the onset and **break** means the mid-season dry spell.

## Unity: why one word covers a whole country

India is flanked by the Indian Ocean on three sides and walled by the Himalayas in the north; the seasonal pressure contrast between land and sea reverses the winds, so the whole country has a wet and a dry season.

## Diversity: the numbers NCERT uses

| Contrast | Place and figure (kegy104 pp2-3) |
|---|---|
| Hottest summer | Western Rajasthan, occasionally 55 degrees C; Churu 50 degrees C or more on a June day |
| Coldest winter | Around Leh, as low as minus 45 degrees C; Drass (Ladakh) minus 45 degrees C on a December night |
| Same June day | Churu 50 degrees C against Tawang (Arunachal Pradesh) hardly 19 degrees C |
| Same December night | Drass minus 45 degrees C against Thiruvananthapuram or Chennai 20-22 degrees C |
| Day-night range | Kerala and the Andaman Islands: 7-8 degrees C; Thar desert: a big night drop |
| Wettest and driest | Cherrapunji and Mawsynram (Khasi Hills, Meghalaya) over 1,080 cm a year; Jaisalmer rarely over 9 cm |
| Rain in one day | Tura (Garo Hills) can get in a day what Jaisalmer gets in 10 years |

**Reading the table.** Every contrast is explained by one of the six controls in module 3. NCERT's summary: the climate has many regional variations, but "the climate of India is monsoonal in rhythm and character".


### 3. Core: the six controls of India's climate

*Objective:* Name the six factors that control India's climate, give the NCERT example for each and use them to explain one contrast.

## The six controls (kegy104 p3)

**1. Latitude.** The Tropic of Cancer passes through the central part of India in an east-west line. The part south of it lies in the **tropical** zone, near the equator, with high temperature all year and a small daily and annual range. The part north of it lies in the **sub-tropical and temperate** zone, away from the equator, with extreme climate and a large daily and annual range.

**2. The Himalayan mountains.** The lofty Himalayas and their extensions act as an effective climatic divide. They shield the subcontinent from cold winds that originate near the Arctic circle and blow across central and eastern Asia. They also **trap the monsoon winds**, forcing them to shed their moisture inside the subcontinent.

**3. Distribution of land and water.** India has the Indian Ocean on three sides and a high continuous mountain wall in the north. Water heats and cools slowly; land quickly. This **differential heating** creates different pressure zones in different seasons around the subcontinent, and the pressure difference reverses the monsoon winds.

**4. Distance from the sea.** A long coastline gives large coastal areas an **equable** (even) climate. Interior places far from the sea's moderating influence have extremes. NCERT contrasts Mumbai and the Konkan coast (hardly any idea of temperature extremes) with Delhi, Kanpur and Amritsar (strong seasonal contrast).

**5. Altitude.** Temperature falls with height; thin air makes mountain places cooler. NCERT: Agra and Darjiling lie at about the same latitude, but January temperature is 16 degrees C at Agra and 4 degrees C at Darjiling.

**6. Relief.** Physiography affects temperature, pressure, wind direction and speed, and the amount and distribution of rainfall. The windward sides of the Western Ghats and Assam get high rainfall in June-September, whereas the southern plateau stays dry because it lies on the leeward side of the Western Ghats.

## A reasoning grid

| Contrast to explain | Control that explains it |
|---|---|
| Kerala differs from Uttar Pradesh | Latitude and distance from the sea |
| Delhi has hot summers and cold winters; Mumbai does not | Distance from the sea (continentality) |
| Agra warm, Darjiling cool in January | Altitude |
| Western coast wet, Deccan interior dry | Relief (windward and leeward) |
| North India spared the Arctic cold | The Himalayan wall |


### 4. Core: the thermal account - land-sea heating, the ITCZ and the equator crossing

*Objective:* Explain step by step how heating of the north-west makes the south-east trades cross the equator and become the south-west monsoon, with directions given as from and to.

## The older, simpler account (kegy104 p4)

NCERT says that towards the end of the nineteenth century the **differential heating of land and sea** in summer was believed to set the stage for the monsoon winds. The steps:

1. **Heating of the land.** In April and May the large landmass north of the Indian Ocean gets intensely heated. A very deep **low pressure** forms in the north-western part of the subcontinent.
2. **High pressure over the sea.** Water heats slowly, so pressure over the Indian Ocean to the south stays high.
3. **The pull.** The low attracts the **south-east trade winds** from across the equator. Because the low-pressure belt is drawn north, the **ITCZ shifts northwards**.
4. **The crossing.** These winds cross the equator **between 40 degrees E and 60 degrees E** (kegy104 pp4, 9). In the northern hemisphere the Coriolis force turns them to the right, so they blow **from the south-west towards the north-east**. They become the **south-west monsoon**.
5. **Moisture.** On the way they pass over warm equatorial currents and gather moisture "in abundance" (kegy104 p9).

NCERT's summary: the south-west monsoon is a continuation of the south-east trades, deflected towards India after crossing the equator ("displaced equatorial easterlies", kegy104 p8).

## The ITCZ in July

In July the ITCZ is at about **20 N-25 N** over the Gangetic plain, "sometimes called the monsoon trough" (the box on p4); in the season description it is centred at **25 N** and stretches from the **Thar desert in the north-west to Patna and the Chotanagpur plateau in the east-south-east** (kegy104 p8). It encourages a thermal low over north and north-west India. In winter the ITCZ moves south, the pressure pattern reverses and the winds blow from the north-east: the **north-east monsoon**.

## Correction to watch

NCERT's phrase "during April and May when the sun shines vertically over the Tropic of Cancer" is loose: the sun is overhead there on **21 June** (Leong p197, Day 17 lesson GS-095); in April-May it is moving north towards it. The point, rapid heating of the north-west, stands.

## The same idea at world scale (Leong pp197-198)

Leong calls land and sea breezes "monsoons on a smaller scale": both come from differential heating, one daily, one seasonal. In **July** a low over the Asian landmass and a high over northern Australia send south-east winds across the equator, deflected right into south-west winds; in **January** a high over Mongolia reverses the flow.

## Winter in the same framework

(The winter westerly jet over north India is in Day 20 lesson GS-105.) Weak high pressure over the north-west plain (isobars of 1019 mb in the north-west and 1013 mb in the far south, NCERT p7) sends light winds of 3-5 km an hour outwards. Their direction follows relief: westerly down the Ganga valley, northerly in the delta, north-easterly over the Bay of Bengal.


### 5. Core: the upper-air and ocean account - jet streams, El Nino and the southern oscillation

*Objective:* Describe NCERT's second explanation of the monsoon, the jet-stream shift and the Pacific signals, and say how they are used to forecast.

## Why a second account is needed

NCERT (kegy104 p4) says a real breakthrough came when the monsoon was studied at the **global rather than the regional** level. The thermal account explains the pull, not the sudden **burst** or why some years fail.

## The jet-stream link (kegy104 p5)

- The shift of the ITCZ is related to the **withdrawal of the westerly jet stream** from its position over the north Indian plain, south of the Himalayas.
- The **easterly jet stream** sets in along **15 N** latitude only after the western jet has withdrawn.
- This easterly jet is "held responsible for the burst of the monsoon in India".

A jet stream is a narrow, fast current of air high in the atmosphere. The types, heights and speeds are taught in Day 20 lesson GS-105 (Jet streams and western disturbances); here you need only the order of events: westerly jet over the plain in winter, it withdraws in early summer, the easterly jet sets in, the monsoon bursts.

## El Nino and the southern oscillation

**El Nino** (kegy104 p5 box; kegy209 p5): warm water appears off the coast of Peru and replaces the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current. NCERT's points:

- It is a complex weather system that appears once every **three to seven years**, bringing drought, floods and other extremes to different parts of the world.
- The name means "Child Christ": the current appears around Christmas, when it is summer in Peru.
- It distorts equatorial circulation and evaporation and reduces plankton and fish. NCERT gives a warming of 10 degrees C on the Peruvian coast (the book's figure; verify).
- **Southern oscillation**: the pressure change over the Pacific and Australia. The combined phenomenon is **ENSO** (kegy209 p5). The intensity of the south-west monsoon winds can be tracked by the **pressure difference between Tahiti (roughly 20 S, 140 W) and Port Darwin (12 degrees 30 minutes S, 131 E)**. NCERT's Tahiti position is rounded (usual value about 17.5 S, 149.5 W; verify); the idea is a pressure see-saw between the east and west Pacific.
- Effects when ENSO is strong: heavy rain on the arid west coast of South America, drought in Australia and sometimes in India, floods in China (kegy209 p5).
- Example: in **1990-91** a wild El Nino delayed the south-west monsoon onset over most of the country by **five to twelve days** (kegy104 p5).

## Forecasting

NCERT says El Nino is used in India to forecast long-range monsoon rainfall, and that the Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) can forecast the possible behaviour of the monsoon on the basis of **16 indicators** (kegy104 p7; the IMD's method has been revised more than once, so treat "16" as the book's figure; verify).

## A signal not in the extracts

The **Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)**, named in a 2017 Prelims question, is not in the extracts; this is standard knowledge (verify). It is the difference in sea-surface temperature between the western tropical Indian Ocean (Arabian Sea side) and the eastern tropical Indian Ocean (south of Indonesia). In a *positive* phase the west is warmer than the east, which tends to favour Indian monsoon rain; a *negative* phase does the opposite. A positive IOD can partly offset an El Nino's drying effect on the monsoon (1997 is the usual example; verify). The Ocean Mean Temperature of the 2020 question is in module 10. Day 21 lesson GS-106 will take global circulation and ENSO further.

These are *tendencies*, not laws: NCERT says drought occurs "sometimes".


### 6. Core: onset, the two branches and where the rain falls

*Objective:* Trace the south-west monsoon from Kerala to the Himalaya and say which places each branch wets, which it leaves dry and why.

## Onset and advance (kegy104 p5, p9)

The south-west monsoon sets in over the **Kerala coast by about 1 June** and moves swiftly: it reaches **Mumbai and Kolkata between 10 and 13 June**, and by **mid-July** it engulfs the whole subcontinent (Figure 4.2, the map of normal onset dates). In the first week of June the "burst" may reach coastal Kerala, Karnataka, Goa and Maharashtra, while the interior may wait until the first week of July. The IMD's official normal dates have been revised in recent years, so quote the current IMD map in an exam answer (verify).

## The Arabian Sea branch splits in three

| Branch | Path | Result |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Western Ghats | Climbs the slopes from 900-1,200 m, cools | Windward Sahyadris and Western Coastal Plain: 250-400 cm; the leeward side east of the Ghats is a **rain-shadow area** with little rain |
| (ii) North of Mumbai | Up the Narmada and Tapi valleys | Rain over wide areas of central India; the Chotanagpur plateau gets about 15 cm from this branch; it then joins the Bay of Bengal branch on the Ganga plain |
| (iii) Saurashtra and Kachchh | Over west Rajasthan and along the Aravalis | Only scanty rain; in Punjab and Haryana it joins the Bay of Bengal branch; together they bring rain to the western Himalayas |

NCERT asks you to compare Kozhikode, Mangalore, Pune and Bengaluru: the first two (windward) are far wetter than the last two (leeward); read the figures from an atlas.

## The Bay of Bengal branch

It strikes the coast of Myanmar and south-east Bangladesh. The **Arakan Hills** along the Myanmar coast deflect a large part of it towards India, so the monsoon enters **West Bengal and Bangladesh from the south and south-east**, not from the south-west. Then, under the influence of the Himalaya and the thermal low in the north-west, it splits:

- one arm moves **westwards along the Ganga plains** as far as the Punjab plains (winds are easterly or south-easterly over north Bengal and Bihar, kegy104 p8);
- the other goes **up the Brahmaputra valley** into the north-east, with a sub-branch striking the **Garo and Khasi hills** of Meghalaya. **Mawsynram**, on the crest of the Khasi hills, receives the highest average annual rainfall in the world (NCERT).

## Why the Tamil Nadu coast stays dry in June-September

1. The coast runs **parallel to the Bay of Bengal branch**, so the wind blows along it rather than onto it.
2. It lies in the **rain shadow** of the Arabian Sea branch.

It receives its rain in October-November (module 7).


### 7. Core: breaks in the monsoon, the retreat and the north-east monsoon

*Objective:* Explain what a break is and why it happens in two regions, then follow the retreat month by month and say where the north-east monsoon brings rain.

## Breaks (kegy104 p5)

During the south-west monsoon, if rain fails for **one or more weeks** after a few rainy days, it is a **break in the monsoon**. These dry spells are "quite common" in the rainy season. NCERT gives two regional causes:

1. **Northern India**: rain fails if the rain-bearing storms are **not very frequent along the monsoon trough** (the ITCZ over this region).
2. **West coast**: dry spells are linked with days when the **winds blow parallel to the coast** rather than onto it.

A break is a change in *where the trough sits* or *which way the wind blows*; compare module 6, where a wind parallel to the Tamil Nadu coast keeps it dry.

## The retreat (kegy104 p11)

As the sun moves south, the low-pressure trough of the Ganga plain starts moving south and the monsoon weakens by the end of September.

| Time | Event (as NCERT states it) |
|---|---|
| First week of September | Monsoon retreats from western Rajasthan |
| End of September | Withdrawn from Rajasthan, Gujarat, western Ganga plain and the Central Highlands |
| Beginning of October | Low pressure covers the northern Bay of Bengal |
| Early November | Low pressure moves over Karnataka and Tamil Nadu |
| Mid-December | Centre of low pressure completely removed from the Peninsula |

**Dates need care.** NCERT places the start of the retreat in the first week of September; the IMD's normal date was later revised (reported as mid-September; verify). Use the book's sequence of regions and quote the current IMD date if asked.

## The 'October heat'

The retreating season brings clear skies and rising temperature. The land is still moist, so high temperature plus humidity makes the weather oppressive: the **October heat**. In the second half of October the mercury falls quickly, especially in northern India. The weather is dry in north India but rainy in the **eastern part of the Peninsula**, where October and November are the rainiest months of the year.

## Cyclones and the north-east monsoon

- Widespread rain in this season comes with **cyclonic depressions** that form over the **Andaman Sea** and cross the eastern coast of the southern Peninsula. The thickly populated **deltas of the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri** are their preferred targets (NCERT).
- A few cyclonic storms strike West Bengal, Bangladesh and Myanmar. The bulk of the **Coromandel coast** rainfall comes from these depressions and cyclones. Cyclones are less frequent in the **Arabian Sea**.
- In **October-November** the **north-east monsoon**, crossing the Bay of Bengal, picks up moisture and causes torrential rainfall over the **Tamil Nadu coast, southern Andhra Pradesh, south-east Karnataka and south-east Kerala** (kegy104 p7). This is why NCERT's exercise answer for "rainfall on the Tamil Nadu coast in the beginning of winter" is the north-eastern monsoon.

The north-east monsoon is dry over north India (land to sea) but moist on reaching the Tamil Nadu coast, because it has crossed the Bay of Bengal.

## Cold weather: why north India is so cold

NCERT lists three reasons: (i) Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan are far from the sea and have a **continental** climate; (ii) snowfall in the nearby Himalayan ranges creates cold-wave conditions; (iii) around February, cold winds from the **Caspian Sea and Turkmenistan** bring cold wave, frost and fog to the north-west. The Peninsula has no well-defined cold season because of the sea and nearness to the equator.

**Winter rain from the west.** Shallow cyclonic depressions from the **east Mediterranean** travel east across West Asia, Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan, gaining moisture from the Caspian Sea and the Persian Gulf, and bring meagre rain to Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and west Uttar Pradesh. Delhi's average winter rain is about **53 mm**; the book's sentence for Punjab and Bihar reads 25 mm and 18 mm respectively. It is highly beneficial for **rabi** crops and the snow sustains Himalayan rivers in summer. Their steering by the westerly jet is taught in Day 20 lesson GS-105. Arunachal Pradesh and Assam get 25-50 mm.

NCERT's Thiruvananthapuram figures (21 and 29.5 degrees C) look low for a mean maximum (check); keep only the point: a small seasonal range near the equator.

### 9. Master: rainfall regions, the causes of variability and what it does to India

*Objective:* Classify India's rainfall by region, list the causes of monsoon variability in space and time, and state the economic and environmental consequences.

## Spatial variability: the four rainfall regions (kegy104 p12)

The average annual rainfall of India is about **125 cm**, with great spatial variation.

| Region | Rainfall | Where (as NCERT names it) |
|---|---|---|
| High | Over 200 cm | West coast, Western Ghats, sub-Himalayan north-east, Meghalaya hills; parts of the Khasi and Jaintia hills over 1,000 cm |
| Medium | 100-200 cm | South Gujarat, east Tamil Nadu, north-eastern Peninsula (Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, east Madhya Pradesh), northern Ganga plain along the sub-Himalayas, Cachar valley, Manipur |
| Low | 50-100 cm | West Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, east Rajasthan, Gujarat, Deccan Plateau |
| Inadequate | Below 50 cm | Parts of the Peninsula (Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra), Ladakh, most of west Rajasthan |

**Source quirks** (details in the master notes): the Brahmaputra valley sits under 'high' yet is below 200 cm; Gujarat appears twice. The extract gives no area share for the exercise question on 'below 75 cm', so do not invent one; use the rainfall map (Figure 4.4).

## Temporal variability: three time scales

1. **Within the season.** The date of onset varies (1990-91: five to twelve days late); breaks of a week or more; the date of retreat.
2. **Year to year.** "Variability of rainfall brings droughts or floods every year in some parts of the country" (kegy104 p12).
3. **Long term.** NCERT's global-warming section projects about 2 degrees C of warming and a 48 cm sea-level rise by 2100 (the book's figures; verify) and a shift of climatic boundaries, some regions wetter and others drier.

## The causes the extracts give

- The **position of the ITCZ / monsoon trough**: storms along it decide rain in north India; NCERT says only that rain-bearing storms are infrequent along the trough in a break (module 7). That the trough shifts towards the Himalayan foothills during a break is standard knowledge (verify).
- The **jet streams**: the timing of the withdrawal of the westerly jet and the setting-in of the easterly jet along 15 N controls the burst (module 5).
- **El Nino and the southern oscillation**: associated with delay and sometimes drought (module 5). Not every El Nino year is a drought year, and NCERT says only "sometimes".
- **Wind direction near the coast**: a wind parallel to the west coast stops coastal rain.
- **Relief**: the Western Ghats, Arakan Hills and Himalaya decide the wet and dry sides.
- *Not in the extracts (verify):* the Indian Ocean Dipole (module 5).

## Consequences (kegy104 p12)

1. About 64 per cent of people depend on agriculture (NCERT's figure; dated, verify), and agriculture depends on the south-west monsoon; the monsoon is the axis of the agricultural cycle.
2. Except in the Himalayas, temperature is above the crop threshold all year, and regional variation allows different crops, food, clothes and houses.
3. Variability brings drought or flood; failure hurts most where irrigation is poor.
4. A sudden monsoon burst causes **soil erosion** over large areas.
5. Winter rain from temperate cyclones helps **rabi** crops.


### 10. Exam: the three official questions, answer frames and traps

*Objective:* Answer the 2023 Mains question on 'Purvaiya', handle the two Prelims forecasting items honestly, and apply an elimination method to monsoon statements.

## 1. The 2023 Mains question (10 marks): 'Purvaiya' in the Bhojpur region

*Why is the south-west monsoon called 'Purvaiya' (easterly) in the Bhojpur region? How has this directional seasonal wind system influenced the cultural ethos of the region?*

**Part 1: mechanism (from the extract).** The Bay of Bengal branch enters West Bengal and Bangladesh from the south and south-east, then splits; one arm moves **westwards along the Ganga plains** (kegy104 p11), and the winds are **easterly or south-easterly over north Bengal and Bihar** (p8). NCERT p8 links this to the ITCZ: its location attracts a surface circulation that is easterly or south-easterly over north Bengal and Bihar, and Bhojpur lies in the Patna belt on the northern side of the trough axis (standard; verify). A place in the middle Ganga plain therefore receives the rain-bearing wind *from the east*, and 'purv' means east. Bhojpur (the Bhojpuri-speaking area of western Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh; standard, verify on a map) lies on that path. The south-west monsoon of the west coast and the 'Purvaiya' of the Ganga plain are the same system bent by relief and the thermal low (module 6).

**Part 2: cultural ethos (not in the extracts).** Commonly cited, to be verified before use: the seasonal folk songs of the rainy months (for example kajari rainy-season songs and bidesiya songs of migration and separation; verify), the importance of the first rain for paddy transplanting, and the seasonal movement of men for work, which shapes songs of separation. Write these as examples, not as statistics.

## 2. The two Prelims items

- **2017: Indian Ocean Dipole.** Statement 1 says the IOD is a difference in sea-surface temperature between the tropical western Indian Ocean and the **tropical eastern Pacific**. By the definition in module 5 (standard knowledge, verify) the difference is between the western and the **eastern tropical Indian Ocean**, so statement 1 does not match. Statement 2, that the IOD can influence an El Nino's effect on the monsoon, matches the standard account. The analysis therefore points to option (b) 2 only; this is analysis, not an official key.
- **2020: Ocean Mean Temperature (OMT).** Not in the extracts; the facts below are standard knowledge as reported, so verify them before relying on them. OMT is the average ocean temperature from the surface down to the 26 degrees C isotherm, which usually lies about 50-100 m deep; the mean depth reported for the south-western Indian Ocean in January-March is about 59 m, not 129 m. OMT measured in January-March was reported as a predictor of whether monsoon rain will be above or below its long-term mean, and as doing better than SST- or ENSO-based signals. So statement 1's 129 m conflicts with the reported figure and statement 2 is consistent with it. The analysis points to option (b) 2 only; this is analysis, not an official key.

## 3. An elimination method for monsoon statements

1. **Direction test.** Is the wind named by where it comes from? Is the Coriolis turn right in the north, left in the south?
2. **Branch test.** Which branch? Arabian Sea (three sub-branches) or Bay of Bengal (two arms)?
3. **Windward/leeward test.** Wet side or rain shadow?
4. **Season test.** Hot, south-west monsoon, retreating or cold? The Tamil Nadu coast is dry in the south-west monsoon season and wet in October-November.
5. **Absolute-word test.** Watch 'all', 'only', 'always'. Edge cases: the Brahmaputra valley (below 200 cm) is drier than Meghalaya; west Rajasthan gets scanty rain.


### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Monsoon: climate with a seasonal reversal of wind direction (NCERT kegy104 p2).
- ITCZ: low-pressure zone near the equator where trade winds converge and air rises; in July it lies about 20-25 N over the Gangetic plain (the monsoon trough), centred at 25 N in the season description.
- Thermal low: low pressure made by intense heating of land; here over the north-west in summer.
- Burst: the abrupt onset of the first heavy rains with thunder and lightning. Break: a dry spell of one or more weeks in the rainy season.
- Rain shadow: the dry leeward area, for example east of the Western Ghats.
- El Nino: appearance of warm water off Peru replacing the cold Peruvian current; southern oscillation: pressure change over the Pacific and Australia; the two together are ENSO.
- Isohyet: line of equal rainfall; isotherm: line of equal temperature.

### Notes: A calendar of the monsoon year

- Mid-November: cold weather season sets in over north India (NCERT p5).
- December-January: coldest months of the northern plain.
- 22 December: the sun is vertical over the Tropic of Capricorn; weak high pressure over the north (p7).
- March: temperatures rise as the sun moves north; Deccan about 38 degrees C.
- April-May: heat belt moves north; north-west about 48 degrees C; Loo and dust storms.
- About 1 June: onset at Kerala; 10-13 June Mumbai and Kolkata; mid-July the whole country (NCERT).
- 21 June: the sun is vertical over the Tropic of Cancer (Leong p197).
- First week of September (NCERT): retreat from western Rajasthan; later normal dates were revised by the IMD (verify).
- October-November: retreating monsoon, October heat, north-east monsoon rain in the south-east, cyclones.
- Mid-December: low-pressure centre removed from the Peninsula.
- Traditional six seasons (NCERT p12; fits north and central India, not the south): Vasanta (Chaitra-Vaisakha, March-April), Grishma (Jyaistha-Asadha, May-June), Varsha (Sravana-Bhadra, July-August), Sharada (Asvina-Kartika, September-October), Hemanta (Margashirsa-Pausa, November-December), Shishira (Magha-Phalguna, January-February).

### Notes: Causes of variability and their effects

- Fewer rain-bearing storms along the trough (NCERT), so rain is rare over north India and a break follows; the trough is commonly said to shift towards the Himalayan foothills in a break (standard; verify).
- Wind parallel to the west coast, so dry spell on the coast.
- Late withdrawal of the westerly jet or late setting-in of the easterly jet, so delayed burst.
- Strong El Nino (1990-91 example), so onset delayed by 5-12 days and sometimes drought.
- Strong monsoon burst, so soil erosion over large areas.
- Uneven rainfall on poorly irrigated land, so crop loss.
- Rising global temperature (NCERT), so possible shift of climatic boundaries and rising sea level (the book's projection of 48 cm by 2100; verify).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Burst versus break: the first rains versus a mid-season dry spell (NCERT uses 'break' loosely for both).
- South-west monsoon versus north-east monsoon: the first brings summer rain to most of India; the second is dry over north India and rainy over the Tamil Nadu coast.
- North-east monsoon (a wind) versus retreating monsoon (a season): the wind belongs to the cold weather season in the north but causes Oct-Nov rain in the south-east.
- ITCZ versus monsoon trough: the monsoon trough is the ITCZ over the Gangetic plain in July.
- Nor'wester (Kalbaisakhi) versus Loo: an evening thunderstorm of Bengal and Assam versus a hot dry wind of the northern plains.
- Mawsynram versus Cherrapunji: both are in the Khasi Hills; NCERT names Mawsynram as the highest average annual rainfall in the world.
- Mango showers versus blossom showers: Kerala and coastal Karnataka; the first help mango ripening, the second make coffee flower.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- The Tamil Nadu coast is dry in June-September because it is parallel to the Bay of Bengal branch and in the rain shadow of the Arabian Sea branch.
- The Peninsula has no well-defined cold season; the cold of north India comes from continentality, snow and cold winds from the Caspian region.
- The Brahmaputra valley is in the wet north-east yet has less than 200 cm (NCERT).
- The south-west monsoon reaches the Punjab plains by joining two branches, but west Rajasthan gets scanty rain.
- Winter rain exists in the north-west (western disturbances) and the far south-east (north-east monsoon), not in most of India.

### Core concepts

- The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind caused by the differential heating of land and sea.
- The south-east trades cross the equator, turn right in the northern hemisphere and become the south-west monsoon.
- A second, upper-air account adds the jet streams and the Pacific signals.
- The Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal branches decide where the rain falls; relief makes wet and dry sides.
- Breaks, delayed bursts and El Nino years cause variability.
- NCERT recognises four seasons: cold, hot, south-west monsoon and retreating monsoon (the IMD's operational calendar differs; verify).
