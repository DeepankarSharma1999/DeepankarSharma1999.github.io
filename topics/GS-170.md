## GS-170: Emergency: national, state, financial

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: Normal government against emergency government

In normal times the Constitution's Seventh Schedule divides subjects between the **Union** and the **States**. The **Union List** (for example defence and foreign affairs) is for Parliament; the **State List** (for example police and public order) is for the State Legislatures; the **Concurrent List** is shared. Normally Parliament cannot make a law on a State List subject, the Centre can give a State executive directions only on specified matters, and the money raised by the Centre is shared with the States according to constitutional rules (GS-201 module 3 explains the three relations). Now imagine each of these three walls, executive, legislative and financial, being lowered when the country is in a crisis: Parliament can then legislate on State subjects, the Centre can direct the State on any matter and the President can change the sharing of revenue. That lowering is what the emergency chapter does. Keep this picture in mind: an emergency changes **who decides**, not **who exists**; the State governments still exist during a National Emergency, although under complete Union control, while under President's Rule the State executive is replaced.

### Bridge: Words you will meet: proclamation, resolution, requisition, revocation

A **proclamation** is the formal public declaration signed by the President, such as a proclamation of emergency. A **resolution** is a formal decision of a House of Parliament, passed by voting; to approve a proclamation Parliament passes resolutions. **Approval** means Parliament says yes within a time limit; if it does not, the proclamation ends. **Revocation** means withdrawing a proclamation: the President can do it by a later proclamation, and for a National Emergency the Lok Sabha can force it by a resolution of **disapproval**. A **requisition** is a formal demand: one-tenth of the Lok Sabha's members can give written notice to demand a special sitting. An **ordinance** is a law made by the President when Parliament is not in session (GS-174). A **special majority** here means (a) more than half of the total membership of the House **and** (b) at least two-thirds of those present and voting. A simple majority means more than half of those present and voting. Finally, an order is **laid before** a House when it is formally placed there so members can see and discuss it; the Articles use this for Presidential Orders and financial modifications.

### Bridge: Counting months, days and majorities

The Articles use time limits such as 'one month', 'two months', 'six months', '14 days' and '30 days'. A **month** here is a calendar month, so one month from 10 March is 10 April, which is 31 days later; it is not the same as 30 days, which would end on 9 April. When the day does not exist in the later month, use the last day of that month (31 January plus one month is 28 February in an ordinary year). Practice counting on a calendar, not by multiplying 30. For votes, 'a majority of the total membership' means more than half of **all** seats, whether or not members attend; for 543 seats the figure is 272 (since half is 271.5). 'Two-thirds of those present and voting' depends on attendance: 450 present means 300, 500 present means 334 (because 333.3 must be rounded up), 400 present means 267. The result that must be obtained is the **higher** of the two numbers. Never round a vote figure down when a fraction of a person cannot vote. The rules are verified by Python in module 10; this bridge only gives the habit.

### 1. Orientation: why a federal Constitution has emergency powers

*Objective:* Name the three emergencies, the Articles that carry them and the idea of a Constitution that is federal in peace and unitary in a crisis.

## The idea in one picture

In ordinary times India works as a **federation**: the Constitution divides power between the **Union** (the Centre) and the **States**, and each side has areas where the other cannot interfere. (Bridge module B1 shows this split if the words Union List and State List are new.) Now imagine an invasion, a war or a State whose government has collapsed. A system that insists on the normal split may be too slow or too divided to act. The framers therefore wrote a chapter that lets the Union take over decision-making in a crisis. That chapter is **Part XVIII, Articles 352 to 360**. Laxmikanth gives its purpose as safeguarding the sovereignty, unity, integrity and security of the country, the democratic political system and the Constitution itself.

## Federal in peace, unitary in crisis

Laxmikanth quotes Dr B.R. Ambedkar's explanation in the Constituent Assembly. A tight federal system such as the American one cannot change its shape whatever the circumstances. The Indian Constitution, he said, is framed to work as a federal system in normal times but can work as if it were a unitary system in an emergency. Laxmikanth calls this change of form **without any formal amendment** a unique feature. Learn the phrase: **federal in peace, unitary in crisis**.

## The three emergencies

1. **National Emergency**: the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion. **Article 352**.
2. **President's Rule**: the government of a State cannot be carried on according to the Constitution. **Article 356** (with Article 365). It is also called State Emergency or Constitutional Emergency.
3. **Financial Emergency**: the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. **Article 360**.

## A vocabulary trap

The Constitution uses the expression 'proclamation of emergency' for the first type only. For Article 356 it never uses the word 'emergency'; Laxmikanth says so explicitly. 'State Emergency' and 'Constitutional Emergency' are therefore textbook labels, not the Constitution's own words. The most common loose usage is 'Emergency' meaning the 1975-77 period, which was a National Emergency (module 6).

## The duty behind the power

**Article 355** places a duty on the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure that the government of every State is carried on in accordance with the Constitution. Laxmikanth presents Article 356 as the way the Centre performs this duty when the constitutional machinery in a State fails.

## What this lesson adds and what it links

The President's powers and ministerial advice are in **GS-174**; the Prime Minister, Council of Ministers and the meaning of 'Cabinet' in Article 352 are in **GS-176** (module 9); the Governor's report is in **GS-189** (module 6); the Centre-State setting, the Sarkaria and Punchhi views and the 2023 question on Article 356 are in **GS-201** (modules 3, 6, 7); the basic structure doctrine and the Emergency-era judgments are in **GS-168** and **GS-173**. This lesson spends its words on the emergency Articles themselves: grounds, approval, duration, effects, safeguards and the arithmetic of deadlines.

### 2. National Emergency I: grounds and the declaration

*Objective:* State the grounds of Article 352 after the 44th Amendment, the Cabinet requirement, and what the courts may check.

## What the President needs to be satisfied about

Under **Article 352** the President can declare a National Emergency when the **security of India or a part of it is threatened** by **war**, **external aggression** or **armed rebellion**. The President may act even **before** the event if satisfied that there is **imminent danger** (Laxmikanth). A declaration on the ground of war or external aggression is called an **External Emergency**; one on the ground of armed rebellion is an **Internal Emergency**.

## Two features added in 1975-76

- **Different proclamations**: the 38th Amendment (1975) allowed the President to issue different proclamations on different grounds, whether or not another proclamation is already in operation.
- **Part of the country**: the 42nd Amendment (1976) allowed the President to limit a National Emergency to a specified part of India. A proclamation may therefore cover the whole country or a part.

## 'Internal disturbance' becomes 'armed rebellion'

The original text listed 'internal disturbance' as the third ground. Laxmikanth says the expression was too vague and wide, so the **44th Amendment (1978)** substituted **armed rebellion**. Consequence: an emergency can no longer be declared on 'internal disturbance', as was done in June 1975. Do not blur the two words in the examination: *armed rebellion* is the present ground, *internal disturbance* the discarded one.

## The Cabinet's written recommendation

The 44th Amendment also requires that the President proclaim a National Emergency **only after receiving a written recommendation from the Cabinet**. Laxmikanth explains the point: the decision needs the concurrence of the Cabinet, not merely the advice of the Prime Minister. In 1975 the Prime Minister advised the President to proclaim an emergency without consulting the Cabinet, which was told afterwards as a *fait accompli*. His note 3 adds that Article 352 itself defines 'Cabinet' as the council of the Prime Minister and the other ministers of Cabinet rank (the wording of the definition is reviewed in GS-176 module 9). NCERT (Class 11, chapter 4) states that the President's wide executive, legislative, judicial and emergency powers are in reality used on the advice of the Council of Ministers; the general rule is in GS-174 (module 9).

## Can a court review the declaration?

The 38th Amendment (1975) tried to make the declaration immune from judicial review; the 44th Amendment (1978) **deleted** that immunity. Laxmikanth adds that in *Minerva Mills* (1980) the Supreme Court held that a proclamation can be challenged on the ground of **malafide**, or that it rests on wholly **extraneous and irrelevant facts**, or is **absurd or perverse**. Review is therefore limited: a court does not re-decide whether the emergency was wise, only whether the declaration passes these minimum tests. The case itself is in GS-168 (module 4) and GS-173.

## Quick contrast to remember

| Before the 44th (1978) | After the 44th |
|---|---|
| Ground included 'internal disturbance' | Ground is 'armed rebellion' |
| Prime Minister's advice sufficient in practice | Written recommendation of the Cabinet required |
| Approval in two months | Approval in one month (module 3) |
| Declaration made immune from review (38th) | Immunity deleted; limited review (Minerva Mills) |

### 3. National Emergency II: approval, duration and revocation

*Objective:* State the approval time, the majority, the six-month cycle and the two ways a National Emergency can end.

## The three numbers

Every National Emergency must be approved by **both Houses** of Parliament **within one month** of its issue. The original period was two months; the 44th Amendment cut it to one. If approved, the Emergency lasts **six months** and can be extended **for six months at a time** with Parliament's approval, with no outer limit. Before the 44th Amendment, once approved, an emergency could last as long as the Cabinet wished.

The resolution approving the proclamation or its continuation needs a **special majority** in each House: (a) a majority of the **total membership** of the House **and** (b) at least **two-thirds of the members present and voting**. Before 1978 a simple majority sufficed. (Module 10 works out what this means in votes.)

## If the Lok Sabha is dissolved

Two situations are in Laxmikanth. First, the proclamation is issued while the Lok Sabha stands dissolved. Second, the Lok Sabha is dissolved during the one-month window (or later, during a six-month period) without approving. In both cases the proclamation **survives until 30 days from the first sitting** of the reconstituted Lok Sabha, **provided the Rajya Sabha has approved it in the meantime**. The Rajya Sabha approval is the condition: without it the proclamation does not survive this way.

## Ending a National Emergency

1. **By the President at any time** through a later proclamation; no parliamentary approval is needed for this.
2. **By the Lok Sabha**: the 44th Amendment requires the President to revoke if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution **disapproving** its continuation. Before 1978 the Lok Sabha had no control over revocation.

## The one-tenth requisition

If **one-tenth of the total members of the Lok Sabha** give written notice to the Speaker (or to the President if the House is not in session), a **special sitting must be held within 14 days** to consider a resolution of disapproval. In a House of 543 members the minimum is 55 members, because 543 divided by 10 is 54.3 and a fraction of a member cannot sign (the figure assumes a full House; verify the current strength).

## Disapproval against approval

| Point | Resolution of disapproval | Resolution of approval or continuation |
|---|---|---|
| Which House | Lok Sabha only | Both Houses |
| Majority | Simple majority | Special majority |

Remember the logic: it is deliberately easy to stop an emergency and deliberately hard to keep one going.

## A standard rule to verify

Laxmikanth says approval gives six months. The usual textbook rule is that the six months run from the date the second House passes the resolution, and each renewal gives a further six months (standard knowledge; verify against the text of Article 352).

### 4. National Emergency III: Centre-State relations and the life of the legislature

*Objective:* Explain the executive, legislative and financial changes during a National Emergency and the extension of the legislative term.

## What stays and what changes

While a National Emergency is in operation the State governments and legislatures **continue to exist**. They are not suspended. What changes is who can override whom. Laxmikanth groups the effects under three heads: executive, legislative and financial. This is the practical meaning of 'unitary in a crisis'.

## Executive

In normal times the Centre can give executive directions to a State only on certain specified matters. During a National Emergency the Centre can direct a State on **any** matter regarding how its executive power is to be exercised. The State governments come under the **complete control** of the Centre but are not suspended.

## Legislative

- Parliament may make laws on **any subject in the State List**. The State Legislature's power is not suspended but is subject to Parliament's overriding power.
- Laws made on State subjects during the Emergency become **inoperative six months after the emergency ceases**.
- The President may issue **ordinances** on State subjects when Parliament is not in session.
- Parliament can confer powers and impose duties on Union officers and authorities for matters outside the Union List, to carry out its extended laws.
- The **42nd Amendment (1976)** extended these executive and legislative consequences to **any other State too**, not only the State where the Emergency operates.

## Financial

The President may **modify the constitutional distribution of revenues** between the Centre and the States: reduce or cancel transfers. Such modification can continue until the end of the **financial year in which the Emergency ceases**, and every such order must be laid before both Houses. (Article 354 in Laxmikanth's Table 16.3.)

## Life of the Lok Sabha and State Assembly

Normally the Lok Sabha lasts five years. During a National Emergency Parliament may extend its life by law, **one year at a time**, but not beyond **six months after the emergency has ceased**. Laxmikanth's example: the Fifth Lok Sabha (1971-77) was extended twice, by a year each time. The same pattern applies to a State Legislative Assembly (five years, extendable by one year at a time, with the same six-month limit). The Article numbers for the term (Article 83(2) for the Lok Sabha; Article 172(1) for Assemblies) are standard knowledge; verify.

NCERT mentions that the 42nd Amendment also lengthened the normal duration of the Lok Sabha from five to six years. That is a different change (a general term, not an emergency extension) and the 44th Amendment era reversed it (standard knowledge; verify).

## A clean memory frame

| Head | Normal times | National Emergency |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Centre directs States on limited matters | Centre directs on any matter |
| Legislative | Parliament legislates on Union and Concurrent subjects | Parliament may also legislate on State List |
| Financial | Constitutional sharing of revenue | President may alter the sharing |
| Legislative term | Five years | Extendable by one year at a time |

### 5. National Emergency IV: Fundamental Rights, Articles 358 and 359

*Objective:* Distinguish Article 358 from Article 359 and state what the 44th Amendment protected.

## Two provisions, two mechanisms

**Article 358** deals with the six freedoms of **Article 19** (speech, assembly and so on). **Article 359** deals with the **enforcement** of the other Fundamental Rights. Remember the difference in one line: **Article 358 suspends a right; Article 359 suspends the remedy**.

## Article 358

When a proclamation of National Emergency is made, the Article 19 freedoms are **suspended automatically**. No separate order is needed. The State may then make any law or take any executive action abridging them, and no one can challenge the law or action as inconsistent with Article 19. When the Emergency ends, Article 19 revives and laws inconsistent with it cease to have effect, but **nothing done during the Emergency can be challenged afterwards**.

The **44th Amendment (1978)** narrowed Article 358 twice:

1. Article 19 can be suspended **only if the emergency is on the ground of war or external aggression**, not armed rebellion.
2. Only laws **related to the Emergency**, and executive action under such laws, are protected; other laws are not.

## Article 359

Article 359 lets the President suspend the **right to move any court** to enforce the Fundamental Rights named in a **Presidential Order**. The rights themselves stay alive, but the remedy is suspended. The order may last for the emergency or a shorter period, may cover the whole country or a part and must be **laid before each House**. While it operates the State may make laws or take action contrary to the named rights; after it ends inconsistent laws cease to have effect but **actions already taken cannot be challenged**.

The 44th Amendment narrowed Article 359 in the same two ways, and added a **third protection**: the President **cannot suspend the right to move the court for enforcement of Articles 20 and 21**. Article 20 protects against conviction for offences (the rules on retrospective punishment, double jeopardy and self-incrimination) and Article 21 protects life and personal liberty. These two remain enforceable even in an Emergency (Laxmikanth).

## Why this change mattered

The suspension of the right to seek remedy for Article 21 in 1975-76 produced the habeas corpus case *ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla* (1976), taught in GS-173 module 5 and flagged there for verification. The 44th Amendment's response is the shield around Articles 20 and 21.

## Quick comparison (the full seven-point table is in the master notes)

| Question | Article 358 | Article 359 |
|---|---|---|
| What is suspended? | The Article 19 freedoms | The right to move court for named rights |
| How? | Automatically | By Presidential Order |
| Which emergency? | War or external aggression only | Any National Emergency |
| Can Articles 20 and 21 be touched? | Not covered (they are not Article 19) | Enforcement of 20 and 21 cannot be suspended |

## Rule check

Test the rule 'Article 359 suspends all other Fundamental Rights' against three cases: Article 20 (excluded), Article 21 (excluded), and a right the Order does not name (not suspended). The rule must read: the Order suspends the enforcement only of the rights **named in it**, and never of Articles 20 and 21.

### 6. National Emergency V: the three proclamations and the 1975-77 Emergency

*Objective:* Give the facts of the 1962, 1971 and 1975 proclamations and trace how the 1975 experience produced the 38th-44th Amendment changes.

## The three proclamations (Laxmikanth)

| Proclamation | Ground | Ended |
|---|---|---|
| October 1962, Chinese aggression in NEFA (now Arunachal Pradesh) | External aggression | January 1968 |
| December 1971, attack by Pakistan | External aggression | March 1977 |
| June 1975 | 'Internal disturbance' | March 1977 |

Because the 1962 Emergency was still in force, **no fresh proclamation** was needed for the war with Pakistan in 1965. The 1971 proclamation was still in force when the 1975 one was made; **both were revoked in March 1977**. Laxmikanth gives the 1975 ground as certain persons inciting the police and the armed forces against the discharge of their duties. From June 1975 to March 1977 is a period of about 21 months.

## The 1975-77 Emergency

It 'proved to be the most controversial' (Laxmikanth). The key facts:

- It was declared on **internal disturbance**, a ground that no longer exists.
- The Prime Minister advised the President **without consulting the Cabinet**, which was informed afterwards.
- Laxmikanth records widespread criticism of the misuse of Emergency powers.
- In the 1977 Lok Sabha elections the Congress led by Indira Gandhi lost and the **Janata Party** came to power. That government set up the **Shah Commission**, which did not justify the declaration.
- The **44th Amendment (1978)** introduced safeguards against misuse.

## Amendments in the story

NCERT (Class 11, chapter 9) calls the **38th, 39th and 42nd** Amendments the most controversial, made in the background of the internal emergency from June 1975, and says the **43rd and 44th** Amendments, after the 1977 election, cancelled most of their changes. Link each to what you already know:

- **38th (1975)**: allowed different proclamations; barred judicial review of the declaration (later deleted). Laxmikanth also records that it made the President's satisfaction final under Articles 356 and 360 (modules 7 and 9).
- **39th (1975)**: election disputes concerning the Prime Minister and the Speaker kept outside the courts (Article 329A(4)); the amendment also moved disputes over the President's and Vice-President's elections outside the ordinary courts (standard knowledge; verify). The Prime Minister and Speaker clause was struck down in *Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain* (1975) (GS-168 module 4).
- **42nd (1976)**: a wide-ranging amendment; Fundamental Duties; attempt to override *Kesavananda* (GS-168 modules 3-4); restrictions on judicial review; emergency changes in modules 2 and 4; lengthened the Lok Sabha term (NCERT).
- **43rd and 44th (1977-78)**: restored the balance (NCERT); the emergency safeguards in this lesson are mostly the 44th's.

The doctrine side of this period, from *Kesavananda* (1973) to *Minerva Mills* (1980), is in GS-168 and GS-173; this module adds only the Emergency provisions themselves.

## The safeguards in one list

| Misuse in 1975 | Safeguard introduced in 1978 |
|---|---|
| Declared on vague 'internal disturbance' | 'Armed rebellion' |
| Cabinet bypassed | Written Cabinet recommendation |
| Unlimited duration once approved | Six-monthly approval |
| Simple majority | Special majority |
| Lok Sabha powerless | Revocation by Lok Sabha; one-tenth requisition |
| Remedy for life and liberty suspended | Articles 20 and 21 enforceable |
| Courts excluded | Immunity from review deleted |

### 7. President's Rule I: grounds, approval, duration and consequences

*Objective:* State the two routes to Article 356, the approval and duration rules, the 44th Amendment limits and the consequences for the State.

## Two grounds

**Article 356** lets the President issue a proclamation if satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of a State **cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution**. The President may act **on the Governor's report or otherwise**, that is, even without a report. **Article 365** adds a second route: if a State fails to comply with or give effect to a **direction** from the Centre, it is lawful for the President to hold that such a situation has arisen. The Governor's role is in GS-189 (module 6).

## Approval and duration

| Point | Rule (Laxmikanth) |
|---|---|
| Approval | Both Houses within **two months** (compare one month for National Emergency) |
| Initial duration | Six months |
| Extension | By Parliament, six months at a time; maximum **three years** |
| Majority | **Simple majority** in each House (compare special majority) |
| Dissolved Lok Sabha | Survives 30 days from first sitting of new Lok Sabha if the Rajya Sabha has approved (same pattern as in module 3) |
| Revocation | By the President at any time; no parliamentary approval needed |

## The brake after one year

The 44th Amendment limits extension **beyond one year**. President's Rule can then be extended, six months at a time, only if **both** conditions hold: (1) a **National Emergency** is in operation in the whole of India, or in the whole or any part of the State; **and** (2) the **Election Commission certifies** that general elections to the State Assembly cannot be held because of difficulties. Laxmikanth's notes add two items of history: the 42nd Amendment had raised the six-month period to one year, which the 44th reversed; and the President's Rule imposed in Punjab in May 1987 was allowed to run for **five years** by the 68th Amendment (1991). The check is the arithmetic in module 10.

## Consequences for the State

The President acquires extraordinary powers: to take over the functions of the State government and the Governor's powers, to declare that the powers of the State Legislature are exercisable by **Parliament**, and to take other necessary steps including **suspending constitutional provisions** about any State body or authority. In practice the President dismisses the Council of Ministers; the Governor carries on administration with the **chief secretary** or advisers appointed by the President; and the Assembly is **suspended or dissolved**. Parliament passes the State's Bills and budget. Further:

1. Parliament can delegate lawmaking for the State to the President or another authority; by practice the President makes laws after consulting the State's members of Parliament. These laws are called **President's Acts**.
2. The President can authorise **expenditure** from the State's Consolidated Fund pending Parliament's sanction when the Lok Sabha is not in session.
3. The President can issue **ordinances** for the State when Parliament is not in session.
4. Laws so made **continue after the President's Rule ends**, but the State Legislature can repeal, alter or re-enact them.

## What the President cannot do

The President **cannot assume the powers of the State High Court** or suspend constitutional provisions relating to it; its position remains the same under President's Rule.

## Rule test

'President's Rule means the Assembly is automatically dissolved.' Test: the source says the President 'either suspends or dissolves' it, and in *Bommai* the Court is reported to have held that dissolution should follow only after Parliament approves (module 8). So the rule is false: suspension is the default until approval.

### 8. President's Rule II: judicial review, Bommai, proper and improper use

*Objective:* Explain how the Supreme Court limited Article 356 and record the proper and improper uses and the misuse record.

## Judicial review

The 38th Amendment (1975) tried to make the President's satisfaction under Article 356 final and unquestionable. The 44th Amendment (1978) **deleted** that, so satisfaction is **not beyond judicial review**. NCERT's federalism chapter reports the same: the constitutional validity of the decision can be examined by the judiciary.

## The Bommai propositions (S.R. Bommai v Union of India, 1994)

GS-173 module 8 and GS-201 module 7 place the case in the federalism story and flag its details for verification. Laxmikanth's ten propositions are grouped here for study; they are in the **source read here**, so treat them as the lesson's fixed material.

- **Reviewability and proof** (1-4): the proclamation is subject to judicial review; satisfaction must rest on **relevant material**; it can be struck down if irrelevant, extraneous, malafide or perverse; the **burden lies on the Centre** to show relevant material; the court cannot examine the **correctness or adequacy** of the material, only its relevance.
- **Remedy and procedure** (5-6): the court may **restore** the dismissed government and revive the Assembly; the Assembly should be **dissolved only after Parliament approves**, until then it can only be suspended; if Parliament refuses approval the Assembly is reactivated.
- **Substantive limits** (7-9): secularism is a **basic feature**, so a State government pursuing anti-secular politics is liable to Article 356; whether a government has lost the Assembly's confidence is decided **on the floor of the House**; a new party in power at the Centre cannot dismiss other parties' State governments merely because of the change.
- **Spirit** (10): Article 356 is an **exceptional power** for special situations.

## Proper and improper use (the Court's list, drawn from the Sarkaria report, 1988)

Laxmikanth lists **proper** situations: a hung Assembly after a general election; a majority party declining to form a ministry with no coalition possible; resignation after defeat with no alternative ministry; disregard of a constitutional direction of the Centre; internal subversion; physical breakdown where the government wilfully refuses its constitutional duties. **Improper** situations include: a governor recommending Rule without probing an alternative ministry or without letting the ministry prove its majority on the floor; the ruling party's massive defeat in Lok Sabha elections (1977 and 1980); internal disturbances not amounting to subversion or breakdown; maladministration or corruption allegations or stringent finances; no prior warning to the State (except extreme urgency); and use to settle intra-party problems. Full lists are in the master notes.

## The misuse record

- First use: **Punjab, 1951**. Laxmikanth reports more than 125 impositions to the date of his edition. Adding the table's rows (a computation in module 10) gives 127 once the earlier Jammu and Kashmir impositions are included; verify the up-to-date count.
- **1977**: the Janata government (Morarji Desai) imposed Rule in nine Congress-ruled States on the ground that the Assemblies no longer represented the electorate. **1980**: the returned Congress did the same in nine States.
- **1992**: Rule in three BJP-ruled States (Madhya Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan) over the ban on religious organisations; *Bommai* upheld it on the basis of secularism, but did not uphold Nagaland (1988), Karnataka (1989) or Meghalaya (1991).
- Dr Ambedkar hoped Article 356 would be a dead letter used as a last resort; Laxmikanth says it became one of the most controversial provisions. NCERT adds Kerala 1959 and the post-1967 pattern (GS-201 module 7).

## After the source (verify)

Later impositions (reported examples include Uttarakhand and Arunachal Pradesh in 2016, Maharashtra in 2019, Puducherry in 2021 and Manipur in 2025) are **not in the extract**, and the judgments on the 2016 cases are outside it; treat each as to be verified. The 2023 Mains question on the reduced use of Article 356 is analysed in module 10.

### 9. Financial Emergency, criticism and the safeguards

*Objective:* State Article 360's grounds, approval, duration and effects, and weigh the criticism and the safeguards.

## Financial Emergency (Article 360)

The President may proclaim a **Financial Emergency** if satisfied that the **financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory** is threatened. The 38th Amendment (1975) had made satisfaction unquestionable; the 44th Amendment deleted this, so judicial review is possible.

| Point | Rule (Laxmikanth) |
|---|---|
| Approval | Both Houses within **two months** |
| Majority | **Simple majority** |
| Duration | **Indefinite** once approved: no maximum, and **no repeated approval** |
| Revocation | By the President at any time; no parliamentary approval needed |
| Dissolved Lok Sabha | Same 30-day pattern with Rajya Sabha approval |

## Effects

1. The Centre may give directions to any State to follow **canons of financial propriety**, and other directions the President thinks necessary.
2. Such directions may require **reduction of salaries and allowances** of all or any class of persons serving in the State, and may require **reservation of Money Bills or other financial Bills for the President's consideration** after the State Legislature passes them.
3. The President may direct the **reduction of salaries and allowances** of persons serving the Union, including **judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts**.

In short, the Centre gains full financial control over the States. H.N. Kunzru warned that this threatens the financial autonomy of States; Dr Ambedkar explained that the Article followed the pattern of the American National Recovery Act of 1933, passed during the Great Depression. **No Financial Emergency has been declared so far** (Laxmikanth; he notes the financial crisis of 1991, when none was declared; verify as of today). NCERT adds that a Financial Emergency could not be imposed on J&K before the 2019 change in its status; that special status no longer exists (verify).

## Criticism in the Constituent Assembly

Critics argued: the federal character would be destroyed; powers would be concentrated in the Union executive; the **President would become a dictator**; the **financial autonomy** of States would be nullified; and **Fundamental Rights would become meaningless**. H.V. Kamath, K.T. Shah, T.T. Krishnamachari and H.N. Kunzru are named. Defenders: Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar ('life-breath' of the Constitution) and Mahabir Tyagi (a 'safety-valve'). Dr Ambedkar admitted the **possibility of abuse**.

## Balance: safeguards that now exist

- Cabinet's written recommendation (Article 352); approval within a short time by Parliament (one month or two months).
- Special majority and six-monthly renewal for National Emergency.
- Lok Sabha's power to disapprove and the one-tenth requisition.
- Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended; Article 358 limited to external emergencies.
- Judicial review of all three proclamations (38th Amendment immunity deleted for Articles 352, 356, 360; *Minerva Mills* and *Bommai* define the tests).
- The three-year cap and Election Commission condition for President's Rule.
- The Sarkaria Commission (1988) and the Punchhi Commission (2007-10) views on Article 356 are in GS-201 (module 6), and GS-201 (module 7) mentions the suggestion of a 'localised emergency'.

Whether these are enough is a Mains question: the powers remain wide, but they are now checked by Parliament, courts and the Cabinet.

### Notes: Definitions in one place

- National Emergency: proclaimed under Article 352 when the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion (imminent danger suffices).
- External Emergency: National Emergency on the ground of war or external aggression. Internal Emergency: on the ground of armed rebellion.
- President's Rule (State Emergency, Constitutional Emergency): proclaimed under Article 356 when the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution; Article 365 adds the failure to comply with Union directions.
- Financial Emergency: proclaimed under Article 360 when the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened.
- Special majority (for emergencies): a majority of the total membership of the House and at least two-thirds of the members present and voting.
- Resolution of disapproval: a Lok Sabha resolution by simple majority that obliges the President to revoke a National Emergency.
- President's Acts: laws for a State made by the President after consulting that State's members of Parliament during President's Rule (Laxmikanth).

### Notes: Articles at a glance (Laxmikanth Table 16.3 plus Article 365)

- Article 352: proclamation of Emergency.
- Article 353: effect of proclamation of Emergency.
- Article 354: application of provisions relating to distribution of revenues while a proclamation of Emergency is in operation.
- Article 355: duty of the Union to protect States against external aggression and internal disturbance.
- Article 356: provisions in case of failure of constitutional machinery in States.
- Article 357: exercise of legislative powers under a proclamation issued under Article 356.
- Article 358: suspension of provisions of Article 19 during Emergencies.
- Article 359: suspension of the enforcement of the rights conferred by Part III during Emergencies.
- Article 359A: application of this Part to the State of Punjab (repealed).
- Article 360: provisions as to Financial Emergency.
- Article 365 (in the text of the chapter): failure to comply with Union directions can be held to be a failure of constitutional machinery in the State.

### Notes: Amendments touching the emergency provisions

- 38th Amendment (1975): allowed different proclamations on different grounds; made the President's satisfaction under Articles 352, 356 and 360 immune from judicial review (the immunity was deleted by the 44th Amendment).
- 39th Amendment (1975): placed election disputes involving the Prime Minister and the Speaker outside the courts (Article 329A(4)); also moved disputes over the President's and Vice-President's elections outside the ordinary courts (standard knowledge; verify); the Prime Minister and Speaker clause was struck down in Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) (GS-168 module 4).
- 42nd Amendment (1976): allowed a National Emergency to be limited to part of India; extended the executive and legislative effects to any other State; raised the President's Rule period from six months to one year (Laxmikanth note 6); lengthened the normal Lok Sabha term (NCERT).
- 43rd and 44th Amendments (1977-78): per NCERT cancelled most of the changes made by the 38th, 39th and 42nd. 43rd contents are not in the extract.
- 44th Amendment (1978): armed rebellion replaces internal disturbance; Cabinet's written recommendation; approval within one month; special majority; six-monthly renewal; Lok Sabha disapproval and one-tenth requisition; Article 358 limited to external emergencies; Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended; immunity from review deleted; President's Rule limits beyond one year; period reduced from one year to six months.
- 68th Amendment (1991): permitted the President's Rule imposed in Punjab in May 1987 to continue for five years (Laxmikanth note 7).

### Notes: Judgments

- Minerva Mills v Union of India (1980): a proclamation of National Emergency can be challenged for malafide, extraneous or irrelevant facts, or if absurd or perverse (Laxmikanth). Doctrine details: GS-168, GS-173.
- S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994): ten propositions on Article 356 (module 8), upheld the 1992 proclamations in three BJP-ruled States and did not uphold Nagaland 1988, Karnataka 1989, Meghalaya 1991 (Laxmikanth).
- ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976): habeas corpus during the Emergency; taught and flagged in GS-173 module 5 (verify).
- Rameshwar Prasad v Union of India (2006): Bihar Assembly dissolution; mentioned in GS-189 module 6 as standard knowledge (verify).

### Notes: Chronology of the emergency provisions

- 1951: first President's Rule (Punjab).
- October 1962: first National Emergency (Chinese aggression in NEFA); lasted until January 1968, so no fresh proclamation in 1965.
- December 1971: second National Emergency (attack by Pakistan).
- June 1975: third National Emergency (internal disturbance); the 38th, 39th Amendments (1975) and the 42nd Amendment (1976) follow.
- March 1977: the second and third proclamations revoked; Janata government in power; the Shah Commission appointed (year of appointment not given in the extract).
- 1978: the 44th Amendment introduces safeguards.
- 1980: Minerva Mills (National Emergency reviewable); Congress returns and imposes President's Rule in nine States.
- 1988: Sarkaria Commission report; 1991: Punjab extension by the 68th Amendment; financial crisis but no Financial Emergency.
- 1992: President's Rule in three BJP-ruled States; 1994: Bommai.

### Notes: Bommai: the ten propositions and the proper and improper lists (Laxmikanth)

- 1. The proclamation is subject to judicial review.
- 2. The satisfaction must be based on relevant material; it can be struck down if based on irrelevant or extraneous grounds, malafide or perverse.
- 3. The burden lies on the Centre to prove that relevant material exists.
- 4. The court cannot go into the correctness or adequacy of the material but can see whether it is relevant.
- 5. If the proclamation is held invalid, the court can restore the dismissed government and revive the Assembly.
- 6. The Assembly should be dissolved only after Parliament approves; until then it can only be suspended; if Parliament fails to approve, the Assembly is reactivated.
- 7. Secularism is a basic feature; an anti-secular State government is liable to action under Article 356.
- 8. Loss of the Assembly's confidence must be decided on the floor of the House.
- 9. A new party in power at the Centre has no authority to dismiss ministries formed by other parties in the States.
- 10. Article 356 is an exceptional power to be used only occasionally for special situations.
- Proper: (a) hung Assembly after general election; (b) majority party declines to form a ministry and no coalition possible; (c) ministry resigns after defeat and no alternative; (d) Union direction disregarded; (e) internal subversion; (f) physical breakdown with wilful refusal of constitutional duties.
- Improper: (a) resignation or dismissal on loss of majority and governor recommends Rule without exploring an alternative ministry; (b) governor's own assessment of support without a floor test; (c) ruling party's massive defeat in Lok Sabha elections (1977, 1980); (d) internal disturbances not amounting to subversion or breakdown; (e) maladministration, corruption allegations or financial exigencies; (f) no prior warning except in extreme urgency; (g) to settle intra-party problems or for irrelevant purposes.

### Notes: 1975 misuse and the 1978 safeguards (cause and effect)

- Vague ground 'internal disturbance' used in 1975 leads to 'armed rebellion' substituted in 1978.
- Prime Minister alone advised the President, Cabinet informed afterwards, leads to the written Cabinet recommendation.
- Approved emergency could last as long as the Cabinet wished leads to six-monthly approval and special majorities.
- Lok Sabha had no control over revocation leads to the disapproval resolution and the one-tenth requisition.
- Fundamental Rights suspended in 1975-76 leads to Articles 20 and 21 being non-suspendable and Article 358 being limited.
- Courts excluded by the 38th Amendment leads to deletion of the immunity and the Minerva Mills tests.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Armed rebellion (present ground) against internal disturbance (discarded ground, used in 1975).
- Article 358 (Article 19, automatic, external emergencies only) against Article 359 (other rights, by order, any National Emergency, never Articles 20 and 21).
- Resolution of disapproval (Lok Sabha only, simple majority) against resolution of approval (both Houses, special majority).
- One month (National Emergency approval) against two months (President's Rule and Financial Emergency approval).
- Six months renewable indefinitely (National) against six months with a three-year cap (President's Rule) against indefinite with no renewal (Financial).
- Suspension of the Assembly (default under President's Rule until Parliament approves) against dissolution (after approval).
- National Emergency: State government continues; President's Rule: State executive dismissed.

### Core concepts

- Part XVIII (Articles 352-360) lets the Union take control in a crisis: federal in peace, unitary in crisis.
- Three emergencies: National (352), President's Rule (356, 365), Financial (360).
- The 44th Amendment (1978) added most of the present safeguards after the 1975-77 experience.
- Article 358 suspends the Article 19 freedoms (external emergencies only); Article 359 suspends enforcement of other rights by order, never Articles 20 and 21.
- Bommai (1994) made President's Rule reviewable and limited its use.

### Practice

**Q.** Name the three emergencies and their Articles.

**A.** National Emergency (352), President's Rule (356, with 365), Financial Emergency (360).

**Q.** What replaced 'internal disturbance' in Article 352 and by which Amendment?

**A.** Armed rebellion; the 44th Amendment (1978).

**Q.** What is meant by a 'special majority' in the emergency Articles?

**A.** A majority of the total membership of the House and at least two-thirds of those present and voting.

**Q.** Why does Laxmikanth say the National Emergency converts the federal structure into a unitary one without a formal amendment?

**A.** Because the Constitution itself gives the Centre the power to direct States and legislate on State subjects during the emergency; no amendment is needed to change the working of the system.

**Q.** Why is it said that Article 358 suspends a right and Article 359 suspends a remedy?

**A.** Article 358 automatically suspends the Article 19 freedoms; Article 359 suspends only the right to approach a court to enforce named rights.

**Q.** Explain why the 1975 Emergency led to the 44th Amendment safeguards.

**A.** It was declared on vague grounds without the Cabinet; Parliament and courts had little control; the safeguards address each of those gaps.

**Q.** A State Assembly is hung and the Governor recommends President's Rule without exploring alternatives. Using the Bommai lists, assess.

**A.** The source lists a hung Assembly as proper but a recommendation without probing an alternative ministry as improper; the answer must say the situation depends on whether other options were explored; the Centre must show relevant material.

**Q.** An emergency is declared on 10 March 2030. The Lok Sabha is dissolved on 1 April before approval. What is the earliest consequence for the survival of the proclamation?

**A.** It survives until 30 days after the first sitting of the new Lok Sabha if the Rajya Sabha has approved it meanwhile; otherwise it lapses at the end of the month, on 10 April.

**Q.** A law restricting a newspaper is passed during an Emergency declared on armed rebellion. Can it be challenged under Article 19?

**A.** Article 358 does not suspend Article 19 in an armed rebellion emergency (44th Amendment), so the law can be challenged unless an Article 359 order applies to the right.
