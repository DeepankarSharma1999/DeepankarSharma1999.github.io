### Bridge: Two questions that decide every formula: same place or same time?

Two habits prevent most errors in this topic. Nothing here needs more than school geometry.

**1. Name the two events.** A time interval always sits between two events, such as 'the clock ticks at 9:00' and 'the clock ticks at 9:01'. A length is different: it is the separation of two ends read at the same instant. So for a time problem ask: *in which frame do both events happen at the same place?* That frame's clock gives the proper time. For a length problem ask: *in which frame is the object at rest?* That frame gives the proper length.

**2. Right triangles.** If a right triangle has legs p and q, the hypotenuse h satisfies h² = p² + q². The 3-4-5 triangle (9 + 16 = 25) is the easiest check. The light-clock derivation in module 2 is exactly this relation, with one leg the mirror separation, the other the distance the clock moves, and the hypotenuse the path of the light.

**3. Reminder from PH-028.** β = v/c, γ = 1/√(1 − β²), always γ ≥ 1; compute 1 − β² first, then the square root, then the reciprocal (PH-028, bridge B1). For β = 0.6, γ = 1.25; for β = 0.8, γ = 1.6667.

### 1. Orientation: proper time, proper length and the first question to ask

*Objective:* Define proper time and proper length, say which frame owns each, and state the two results to be derived.

## What this topic is

In PH-028 you derived the rule that converts the position and time of an event between two inertial frames (PH-028 module 4), checked its inverse (module 5) and saw that observers disagree about simultaneity (module 6). This topic draws out its two most-examined consequences. A moving clock is found to run slow (**time dilation**), and a moving rod is found to be short along its motion (**length contraction**). No new postulate is used; both results come out of the same four equations. PH-028 previewed them and labelled them 'see PH-029'; this lesson fulfils those previews.

## Words to know first

- **Event, inertial frame, β and γ**: as in PH-028 module 1. Recall γ = 1/√(1 − β²) ≥ 1.
- **Rest frame of an object**: the frame in which the object does not move.
- **Proper time (Δt₀)**: the time between two events measured by a single clock that is present at both events. Equivalently, the interval in the frame where both events happen at the same place.
- **Proper length (L₀)**: the length of an object measured in its own rest frame. Because the ends do not move, they can be read at leisure.
- **Dilation** means lengthening and **contraction** means shortening.

## The first question to ask

Before writing any formula, decide which frame owns the proper quantity. For a time problem it is the frame where the two events occur at the same place. For a length problem it is the frame where the object is at rest. Every other inertial frame measures a longer time or a shorter length, by the factor γ (lengths across the motion are unchanged; inclined rods shorten by less, module 6). The words 'stationary' and 'moving' mislead here; the test is always 'same place' or 'at rest'.

## Conventions (same as PH-028 module 1)

Frame S is the observer's frame. Frame S′ moves relative to S at constant velocity v along +x; axes are parallel and right-handed, and the origins coincide at t = t′ = 0. Primed symbols are measured in S′. In this lesson the clock or rod is usually at rest in S′ and the observer is in S, so the results read

$$ Δt = γ Δt₀    and    L = L₀/γ $$

Test every answer against them: a moving clock shows fewer seconds, a moving rod is shorter, and γ > 1.

### 2. Foundation: the light clock and dt = γ dt₀

*Objective:* Derive Δt = γΔt₀ from a light clock using only Pythagoras and the constancy of c, stating each assumption.

## Why a light clock

A clock is any regular repeating process. A light clock uses light bouncing between two mirrors, because postulate 2 (PH-028 module 3) tells us how fast light travels in every inertial frame. This route is the standard textbook argument; the two extracts derive dilation from the Lorentz transformation instead (module 3), so treat this module as a second, independent derivation.

## Set-up

Two mirrors face each other a distance d apart, the line joining them along y, perpendicular to the relative motion. One tick is the trip from the bottom mirror to the top mirror and back. In the clock's rest frame S′ the light goes straight up and down, a path 2d at speed c, so the proper tick is

$$ Δt₀ = 2d/c $$

In frame S the clock moves along +x at speed v. We want the tick length Δt in S.

## Assumptions

1. S and S′ are inertial and S′ moves at +v along x.
2. Postulate 2: in S the light also travels at c, along its slanted path, regardless of the motion of the mirrors.
3. The mirror separation d is the same in S and S′, because it is perpendicular to the motion (y′ = y; PH-028 module 4, assumption 3).

## Derivation (see the diagram)

During the first half of a tick, of length Δt/2 in S, the light travels a slanted distance cΔt/2 while the clock moves vΔt/2 along x. At the end of that half the light is at the top mirror, a height d above the start. The three distances form a right triangle with hypotenuse cΔt/2 and legs d and vΔt/2:

$$ (cΔt/2)² = d² + (vΔt/2)² $$

Expand and collect the Δt terms on one side:

$$ (c² − v²) Δt²/4 = d² $$

so Δt² = 4d²/(c² − v²), and taking the positive root,

$$ Δt = (2d/c)/√(1 − v²/c²) = γ Δt₀ $$

The return half is the mirror image, so each full tick lasts γ times the proper tick.

## Reading the result

The observer in S sees a longer light path at the same light speed, so each tick takes longer. Nor is this special to light: by postulate 1, any other clock carried with this one must stay in step with it, or the ship could detect its own motion (the standard argument; verify the wording before quoting).

Checks. Dimensions: γ is dimensionless, so Δt has the units of Δt₀. Limit v → 0: Δt = Δt₀. Limit v → c: Δt grows without bound. Sign: only v² enters, so the direction of motion does not matter.

### 3. Core: dt = γ dt₀ from the Lorentz transformation; which frame is proper

*Objective:* Derive Δt = γΔt₀ from the inverse Lorentz transformation and identify which frame measures the proper time.

## The two events

Let a clock sit at a fixed place x′ in S′ and tick twice. Event 1 is the first tick, event 2 the second. In S′ both events are at the same place, so Δx′ = 0, and the clock itself reads the gap: Δt′ = Δt₀, the proper time (one clock present at both events). We want the gap Δt that observers in S record, using clocks at the two places in S where the ticks occur.

## Derivation

The inverse transformation (PH-028 module 5, verified there by substitution) gives the time in S from S′ data:

$$ t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²) $$

Write it for each event and subtract (γ, v and c are constants):

$$ Δt = γ(Δt′ + vΔx′/c²) $$

Now use Δx′ = 0, so the second term vanishes:

$$ Δt = γ Δt′ = γ Δt₀ $$

Self-check: x = γ(x′ + vt′) gives Δx = γvΔt₀ = vΔt, so the clock has moved vΔt in S, as it must. Because the ticks occur at different places in S, observers there need two synchronised clocks; this is where simultaneity (PH-028 module 6) quietly enters.

## Which frame measures the proper time?

The frame in which the two events happen at the same place, timed by one clock. It is not 'the stationary frame' and not 'the lab'. For any other inertial frame Δt = γΔt₀ ≥ Δt₀, so proper time is the shortest time between the two events. If the two events are at the same place in S instead, swap the roles: Δt′ = γΔt and S owns the proper time.

## A clock that moves at speed u in S

If the clock moves at speed u in S, then Δx = uΔt between its ticks. The interval c²Δt² − Δx² is the same in every frame (PH-028 module 8), and in the clock's frame it equals c²Δt₀² because Δx′ = 0:

$$ c²Δt₀² = c²Δt² − u²Δt² $$

so Δt₀ = Δt√(1 − u²/c²), the same result written with the clock's speed in the observer's frame.

## Reading Mathur §3.7.2 with care

As extracted, Mathur's text places the events at one point of S′, but the algebra appears to hold the same x in S for both events and concludes Δt′ = γΔt. That is correct for events at one place in S, whereas the sentence describes events at one place in S′ (where Δt = γΔt′), so the 'proper' labels look inconsistent as printed (PH-028 flagged this too; verify against the page image). Upadhyaya §6.11(3) sets it up as above. Always check which frame holds the single clock.

### 4. Core: muon decay as evidence, recomputed

*Objective:* Explain muon survival in both the Earth frame (dilation) and the muon frame (contraction) with correctly recomputed numbers.

## The puzzle as the sources give it

A **muon** is an unstable particle made when cosmic rays strike the upper atmosphere. At rest it lives, on average, τ = 2.2 μs; this is its **mean life** (average lifetime), and the population of a large group falls as N = N₀e^(−t/τ) (both sources quote this decay law, N₀ being the starting number). Mathur takes the faster muons at 0.998c. Even so, in one proper mean life such a muon covers only 0.998 × 3.00 × 10⁸ × 2.2 × 10⁻⁶ = 658.7 m ≈ 0.66 km. Both sources say muons are nevertheless found in large numbers about 10 km below where they are made (a round figure; real muons have a spread of speeds, so this is an illustrative model).

## Viewpoint 1: Earth frame, time dilation

The creation and decay of one muon are two events at the same place in the muon's frame, so τ = 2.2 μs is a proper time. In the Earth frame:

- γ = 1/√(1 − 0.998²) = 1/√0.003996 = 15.82.
- Dilated mean life = γτ = 15.82 × 2.2 μs = 34.8 μs.
- Range = 0.998c × 34.8 μs = 10.42 km, comparable with 10 km.

Survival fraction. The flight takes T = 10 000 m/(0.998c) = 33.4 μs in the Earth frame, so the fraction is exp(−T/γτ) = exp(−33.4/34.8) = 0.38. Without dilation it would be exp(−33.4/2.2) = 2.6 × 10⁻⁷: effectively none.

## Viewpoint 2: muon frame, length contraction

In the muon's rest frame, the 10 km of atmosphere (a length at rest in the Earth frame, so a proper length there) rushes upward at 0.998c and is contracted to 10 km/γ = 632 m. Crossing it takes 632 m/(0.998c) = 2.11 μs, less than one mean life, and the survival fraction is exp(−2.11/2.2) = 0.38 again. The two accounts are not rivals: they describe one outcome from two frames and agree numerically, a useful check on the formulas. Also 632 m < 658.7 m, the muon's own-frame range, so the crossing takes less than one mean life and a sizeable fraction, about 38 per cent, arrives (without relativity about 2.6 in 10 million).

## Direct test with pions

Pions are heavier unstable particles. Both sources describe a beam in which decay products are counted along the path to find the dilated mean life, and a second measurement with pions stopped in a slab to find the proper mean life; the ratio matches γ. With proper life 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ s (Upadhyaya, Mathur Example 23) and speed 0.9c, γ = 2.294 gives 5.7 × 10⁻⁸ s, a path of about 15.5 m instead of 6.75 m. Mathur's text elsewhere uses 2.6 × 10⁻⁸ s (giving 5.96 × 10⁻⁸ s), so quote the value with its source (the commonly quoted charged-pion mean life is about 2.6 × 10⁻⁸ s; standard knowledge, verify).

## What the numbers show

They are consistent with the formula for these particles at these speeds. Arithmetic slips in the sources' muon examples are listed in the master notes; the corrected values are used above.

### 5. Core: proper length and length contraction derived

*Objective:* Derive L = L₀/γ from the Lorentz transformation, state the simultaneity condition, and show the reverse case and an alternative route.

## Measuring a moving length

To measure the length of an object that moves relative to you, record the positions of its two ends **at the same instant in your frame** and subtract (Upadhyaya §6.11(1); Mathur §3.7.1). If the two readings are taken at different times, the object has moved in between and the number means nothing. For an object at rest, no such care is needed. This simultaneity condition is why PH-028 module 6 came first.

## Derivation (rod at rest in S′, observer in S)

The rod lies along the x′ axis. Its ends are at fixed x₁′ and x₂′, so the proper length is L₀ = x₂′ − x₁′. In S the observer records the ends at the same time t, at x₁ and x₂, so L = x₂ − x₁. The transformation x′ = γ(x − vt) applies to each end at that same t:

$$ x₁′ = γ(x₁ − vt),    x₂′ = γ(x₂ − vt) $$

Subtract. Because the same t appears in both, the vt terms cancel; that is exactly the condition Δt = 0:

$$ x₂′ − x₁′ = γ(x₂ − x₁)    so    L₀ = γL,    L = L₀/γ = L₀√(1 − β²) $$

## If the readings are not simultaneous

Without Δt = 0, Δx′ = γ(Δx − vΔt) gives Δx = Δx′/γ + vΔt, which depends on the delay chosen; only Δt = 0 readings define the length.

## The reverse case (reciprocity)

Let the rod be at rest in S along x, with proper length L₀ = x₂ − x₁, and let S′ record the ends at the same t′. The inverse transformation x = γ(x′ + vt′) gives x₂ − x₁ = γ(x₂′ − x₁′), so the length in S′ is again L₀/γ. Each frame finds the other's rods contracted; module 7 explains why this is consistent.

## An alternative route through time dilation

Let the rod, at rest in S′, pass a fixed mark P in S. Front and rear pass P at one place in S, so the gap is a proper time in S, Δt = L/v. In S′ the mark moves at v past the rod, taking Δt′ = L₀/v. Since Δt′ = γΔt (module 3 with the frames swapped), L₀/v = γL/v, so L = L₀/γ again.

## Checks

- v → 0 gives L = L₀; v → c gives L → 0 (the transformation exists only for v < c).
- Transverse lengths are unchanged because y′ = y and z′ = z (PH-028 module 4).
- Numbers: L₀ = 1 m at 0.6c gives 0.80 m (Mathur Example 16); a cube moving along an edge has volume V₀/γ (Upadhyaya Example 11).
- A photograph is a different measurement from Δt = 0 readings (Mathur Box III; not derived here).

### 6. Core: transverse lengths and rods at an angle

*Objective:* Find the length and orientation of an inclined moving rod, and the percentage contraction, with limiting-case checks.

## Only the component along the motion contracts

Take a rod at rest in S′ whose rest length is l₀ and which makes an angle θ₀ with the x′ axis. The velocity v of S′ is along +x, and the angle θ is measured from the x axis, positive towards +y. In its rest frame the rod has components

$$ l₀ₓ = l₀ cos θ₀,    l₀ᵧ = l₀ sin θ₀ $$

The observer in S records both ends at the same instant (module 5). The x-component transforms like a length along the motion and the y-component, perpendicular to it, does not change (Δy = Δy′):

$$ lₓ = l₀ cos θ₀/γ,    lᵧ = l₀ sin θ₀ $$

## Length and orientation

The observed length is the hypotenuse:

$$ l = √(lₓ² + lᵧ²) = l₀ √(cos²θ₀ (1 − β²) + sin²θ₀) $$

Since cos²θ₀ + sin²θ₀ = 1, this simplifies to

$$ l = l₀ √(1 − β² cos²θ₀) $$

and the observed angle satisfies

$$ tan θ = lᵧ/lₓ = γ tan θ₀ $$

Because γ > 1, θ > θ₀: the contracted rod looks steeper, so its orientation changes unless θ₀ = 0° or 90°. The fractional contraction is (l₀ − l)/l₀.

## Limiting-case checks

- θ₀ = 0° (rod along the motion): l = l₀√(1 − β²), the full contraction of module 5.
- θ₀ = 90° (rod across the motion): l = l₀, with θ = 90°.
- β → 0: l → l₀ and θ → θ₀.
- β → 1: tan θ → ∞, so every inclined rod tends to look perpendicular to the motion.
- Dimensions: the factor under the root is dimensionless, so l carries the units of l₀.

## Numbers (diagram)

For the 2020 case, β = 0.8 and θ₀ = 60°: lₓ = 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.300 l₀, lᵧ = 0.866 l₀, l = 0.9165 l₀, so the contraction is 8.35 per cent (Mathur Example 13 prints 91.6 cm for l₀ = 100 cm; Upadhyaya Example 9 prints 8.4 per cent, both consistent). State the assumption that 60° is the angle in the rod's rest frame, as both worked examples take it. If 60° is the angle seen in the laboratory, tan θ₀ = tan 60°/γ = 1.039, θ₀ = 46.1°, and l = l₀√(1 − β²cos²θ₀) = 0.832 l₀, a contraction of about 16.8 per cent (compare Upadhyaya exercise 13, where 0.8 m seen at 60° in the laboratory at 0.6c gives l₀ = 0.854 m). Say which reading you use.

In the rest-frame reading the new angle is θ = tan⁻¹(1.667 × 1.732) = 70.9°.

For a rod of 10 m at 30° and β = 0.6 (Upadhyaya Example 10): lₓ = 10 cos 30° × 0.8 = 6.93 m, lᵧ = 5.00 m, l = 8.54 m, and tan θ = 0.7217, θ = 35.8°.
For the 2018 case (symbols only), the algebra above is the whole answer; check it at θ₀ = 0° and 90°. The earlier preview for β = 0.8 and θ₀ = 30° (PH-028 pyqNotes) gives l = 0.721 l₀ and θ = 43.9°, which this formula confirms.

### 7. Core: reciprocity and the apparent paradoxes

*Objective:* Explain why each frame can find the other's clocks slow and rods short, using the train-and-platform and clock examples, and state what the sources do and do not settle about the twin paradox.

## Reciprocity

Both sources note that the effects work both ways: S and S′ each find the other's rods short and clocks slow (Mathur §3.7.1, §3.7.2). This is consistent because the two observers do not measure the same pair of events; each formula compares a proper quantity in one frame with a measured quantity in the other.

## Train and platform (Upadhyaya, end-of-chapter exercise 10)

Platform frame S: the platform is 200 m long. A train passes at 0.6c (γ = 1.25) and, at one instant in S, its front is at the far end of the platform and its rear at the near end, so it exactly fits. The train is shortened to 200 m in S, so its rest length is 200 × 1.25 = 250 m. In the train frame S′ the platform is shortened to 200/1.25 = 160 m, shorter than the 250 m train.

Both statements hold. 'Front at far end' and 'rear at near end' are two events, simultaneous in S but not in S′: their gap in S′ is γvΔx/c² = 1.25 × 0.6 × 200 m/c = 0.5 μs, front first. In that time the platform moves 0.6c × 0.5 μs = 90 m, matching 250 − 160 = 90 m.

## Clocks, with numbers

Take β = 0.6. A clock C′ at rest in S′ reads 10 s between passing two S clocks A and B, 7.5 light-seconds apart in S; S measures 12.5 s, so S says C′ is slow. In S′, each single S clock runs slow: 8 s elapse on it while C′ reads 10 s. Yet B reads 12.5 s when C′ passes it. The difference is that in S′ the two S clocks are not synchronised: B is ahead of A by vL/c² = 0.6 × 7.5 = 4.5 s, and 8 + 4.5 = 12.5. So 'moving clocks run slow' compares one clock against two clocks synchronised in the other frame, and synchronisation differs between frames.

## The twin paradox, as far as the sources go

Upadhyaya §6.11 sends twin B on a round trip to a star 10 light-years away at 0.99c. Twin A, at the origin, measures 20/0.99 = 20.2 years and is 40.2 years old. B's clock reads 20.2/γ = 2.85 years (the source prints 2.8 because it multiplies 20 years, not 20.2, by √(1 − 0.99²), giving 2.82), so B is about 22.85. The extract notes that the two statements differ and asks whether one twin looks younger, without resolving it. Mathur §3.7.2 says the traveller returns younger and does not discuss the symmetry.

The standard explanation, not in the extracts and to be verified, is that the situation is asymmetric: A stays in one inertial frame, whereas B must turn round, that is, change inertial frames, so Δt = γΔt₀ cannot be applied from B's side across the whole journey. In an answer, do not call this a contradiction, and do not claim a full resolution without that reasoning.

### 8. Master: invariance checks, limiting cases and graphs

*Objective:* Use the invariant interval to see why L₀ and L (and Δt₀ and Δt) do not contradict each other, and apply limiting-case and dimensional checks.

## What is invariant and what is not

Δt, Δx and a moving length L are not invariant; they change with the frame. The interval Δs² = c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz² is invariant (PH-028 module 8, same sign convention). Time dilation is the statement of this for a timelike pair of events: for events at the same place in some frame, Δs² = c²Δt₀², so the proper time equals Δs/c in every frame (module 3).

Length contraction is subtler because L₀ and L belong to **different pairs of events**. For the rod at rest in S′, the two ends read at the same t′ are separated by Δs² = −L₀² (Δt′ = 0, Δx′ = L₀), so L₀ is the invariant proper distance of that spacelike pair. The ends read at the same t in S form another pair, with Δs² = −L². Two different intervals, two different lengths, no contradiction.

## Limiting cases

- **v ≪ c.** Use γ ≈ 1 + β²/2 (PH-028 bridge B1; valid to within 1 per cent up to β ≈ 0.4). Then Δt − Δt₀ ≈ ½β²Δt₀ and L₀ − L ≈ ½β²L₀. For Earth's orbital speed, β = 10⁻⁴ and the fractional change is 5 × 10⁻⁹. For an aircraft at 500 mph (223.5 m s⁻¹, β = 7.45 × 10⁻⁷, ½β² = 2.78 × 10⁻¹³), a gap of 0.01 s needs about 3.6 × 10¹⁰ s, roughly 1,140 years of flight (Mathur exercise 24 asks for 'more than a thousand years').
- **v → c.** Δt → ∞ and L → 0. Mathur says a rod at v = c would have zero length and that ageing would stop. Read these as limits only: for v ≥ c, γ is not real and the transformation does not exist (PH-028 module 5). Mathur's remark that this 'shows' nothing material can exceed c is a statement about where the formulas stop, not a derivation.

## Dimensional and sign checks

β and γ are dimensionless, so Δt carries the units of Δt₀ and L those of L₀. Only v² enters, so reversing the direction of motion changes neither effect; where a sign matters (the simultaneity gap of module 7), take it from the transformation.

## The graph

The diagram plots γ and 1/γ against β. Both are flat near β = 0, which is why everyday speeds look Galilean, and they change sharply only above β ≈ 0.8.

## Which formula? A quick decision rule

List the two events (or ends); find the frame with them at one place (time) or with the object at rest (length); that frame gives Δt₀ or L₀. Multiply by γ for a time seen from elsewhere, divide by γ for a length, and check the direction: times lengthen, lengths shorten.

## Guided solution B: the 2021 explosions, why the coincidence frame owns the proper time

### Notes: Symbols, variables and units

- Δt₀ (s): proper time between two events, read on one clock present at both. Δt (s): the interval between the same events in a frame where they are at different places.
- L₀ (m): proper length, measured in the rod's rest frame. L (m): length recorded with both ends read at the same instant in a frame where the rod moves.
- τ (s): mean life of an unstable particle in its rest frame (a proper time); N = N₀e^(−t/τ) is the decay law in the particle's frame, so in the lab the same law holds with τ replaced by γτ and t the lab time. Half-life and mean life both dilate by γ (T½ = τ ln 2 exactly for exponential decay).
- β = v/c (dimensionless, 0 ≤ β < 1); γ = 1/√(1 − β²) (dimensionless, ≥ 1). c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ is used in every numerical here; 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ is the more exact value. State which you use.
- Sign and axes: S′ moves at +v along x of S; the object is at rest in S′ unless stated; only the magnitude of v enters dilation and contraction.

### Notes: Key equations and where each applies

- Time dilation: Δt = γΔt₀ (events at the same place in the clock's frame; the clock moves at v in the observer's frame).
- Same result with the clock's speed u in the observer's frame: Δt₀ = Δt√(1 − u²/c²).
- Length contraction: L = L₀/γ = L₀√(1 − β²) (both ends read at the same instant in the observer's frame).
- Inclined rod: l = l₀√(1 − β²cos²θ₀), tan θ = γ tan θ₀; transverse components unchanged.
- Cube moving along an edge: V = V₀/γ. Moving circle: semi-axes R (across the motion) and R/γ (along the motion).
- Muon or pion survival in the lab: N/N₀ = exp(−T/γτ) for a flight time T in the lab; equivalently exp(−T₀/τ) with T₀ = T/γ the particle's own elapsed time.
- Small speed: γ ≈ 1 + β²/2, so Δt − Δt₀ ≈ ½β²Δt₀ and L₀ − L ≈ ½β²L₀ (use only when β is small).

### Notes: Time dilation compared with length contraction

- Same factor γ, opposite direction, different condition on the events.

### Notes: γ, its reciprocal and the percentage contraction (recomputed)

- Values to four figures; use the table to check the size and direction of an answer.

### Notes: Cause-and-effect chain

- Postulate 2 makes light travel at c in every inertial frame, so the slanted path of a moving light clock takes longer to cross, and ticks are lengthened by γ.
- The Lorentz transformation puts x′ into t, so two events at one place in S′ are at different places in S, and S must use two synchronised clocks; this yields Δt = γΔt₀.
- A moving length needs a simultaneous reading of both ends, and simultaneity is relative, so the contracted value L₀/γ is tied to the observer's frame.
- y′ = y and z′ = z leave transverse lengths unchanged, so an inclined rod changes both length and orientation.
- Dilation in one frame and contraction in the other describe the same muon journey: both give the same survival fraction.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Proper time versus 'time in the moving frame': the proper frame may be called 'moving' by another observer; the test is 'same place', not 'moving'.
- Δt = γΔt₀ versus Δt₀ = γΔt: the second is correct only if the two events are at the same place in the other frame.
- Length contraction versus how an object looks: a photograph combines light emitted at different times and need not show L₀/γ (Mathur Box III; verify).
- Reciprocity versus the twin journey: mutual dilation is between inertial frames; the traveller's turn-round breaks the symmetry (standard explanation, verify).
- Mean life versus half-life: both dilate by the same γ.
- Rest length versus length in the frame of the rod's ends read at different times: only the Δt = 0 reading defines the moving length.
- Less than one mean life does not mean most survive: more than half survive only if the elapsed proper time is below τ ln 2 ≈ 0.69τ (1.52 μs for τ = 2.2 μs); the muon crossing of 2.11 μs gives about 38 per cent.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- Lengths perpendicular to the motion are unchanged; an inclined rod contracts only in its component along the motion.
- If the two events are at different places in both frames, neither interval is proper: use the full transformation or the invariant interval (PH-028 module 8).
- The formulas assume uniform relative velocity. For a clock whose speed changes, the elapsed proper time is built up from the instantaneous factor; that extension is beyond the extracts and is not used here.
- The transformation exists only for v < c (PH-028 module 5).
- Small-speed approximations fail at high β: γ ≈ 1 + β²/2 is 1.32 at β = 0.8 against the exact 1.667.

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - Proper time Δt₀ is read on one clock present at both events (events at the same place in that frame); every other inertial frame measures Δt = γΔt₀ ≥ Δt₀.
  - Proper length L₀ is the length in the object's rest frame; another frame, reading both ends at the same instant, finds L = L₀/γ.
  - Dilation comes from the light-clock triangle (postulate 2 plus a perpendicular distance unchanged) or from Δt = γ(Δt′ + vΔx′/c²) with Δx′ = 0.
  - Contraction comes from Δx′ = γ(Δx − vΔt) with Δt = 0; it needs simultaneous readings.
  - Transverse lengths are unchanged, so an inclined rod changes length and angle: l = l₀√(1 − β²cos²θ₀), tan θ = γ tan θ₀.
  - Each frame finds the other's clocks slow and rods short; there is no contradiction because different event pairs and different synchronisation are involved.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - γ(0.6) = 1.25; γ(0.8) = 1.667; γ(0.9) = 2.294; γ(0.99) = 7.089; γ(0.998) = 15.82.
  - Muon proper mean life 2.2 μs; at 0.998c the Earth-frame mean life is 34.8 μs and the range 10.42 km (the sources print 34.38 μs and 10.29 km).
  - In the muon frame the 10 km of atmosphere is 632 m long and is crossed in 2.11 μs; the survival fraction is 0.38 in both frames.
  - Pion proper mean life 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ s (Upadhyaya, Mathur Example 23); at 0.9c the lab value is 5.7 × 10⁻⁸ s.
  - Rod at 0.8c along its length: 60 per cent of L₀. At 0.8c and 60° to its length: 91.65 per cent, 8.35 per cent contraction.
  - Twin numbers (Upadhyaya): 0.99c, 10 light-years each way: 40.2 years for A and 22.85 years for B.
- **criticalDistinctions:**
  - Proper frame = same place (time) or at rest (length), not 'the stationary frame'.
  - Multiply by γ for a time seen from another frame; divide by γ for a length seen from another frame.
  - Length contraction (a measurement with Δt = 0) versus the apparent look of a photographed object.
  - Mutual time dilation (one clock against two) versus the twin journey (a turn-round changes the frame).
  - Dilation by the light clock (geometric) versus by the transformation (algebraic): same result, different assumptions.
- **formulae:**
  - Δt = γΔt₀
  - L = L₀/γ = L₀√(1 − v²/c²)
  - Δt₀ = Δt√(1 − u²/c²)
  - l = l₀√(1 − β²cos²θ₀); tan θ = γ tan θ₀
  - N/N₀ = exp(−T/γτ)
  - γ ≈ 1 + β²/2 for β ≪ 1
- **traps:**
  - Using Δt = γΔt₀ when the events are not at the same place in either frame.
  - Dividing by γ for a time or multiplying by γ for a length.
  - Contracting the full length of an inclined rod instead of only its component along the motion.
  - Calling the 'lab' frame proper by default.
  - Mixing c = 3.00 × 10⁸ and 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in one solution.
  - Treating the twin paradox as a contradiction of reciprocity without the turn-round argument.

  - - **q:** Why does time dilation appear in the light clock even though only Pythagoras is used?

    - **a:** Each observer compares one clock of the other frame against two synchronised clocks of their own frame, and the two frames disagree about synchronisation (module 7).

    - **a:** In the Earth frame its life is dilated; in the muon frame the atmosphere is contracted; both give the same survival fraction (module 4, D5).
  - - **q:** Why does an inclined rod change its angle but a rod along y does not change its length?
    - **a:** Only the component along the motion contracts; a rod purely along y has none, so its length is unchanged, while an inclined rod loses part of its x-component and so its direction changes.

#### Light clock: why a moving clock ticks slower (draft)

Light has the same speed c in both frames. In the frame where the clock moves the light travels a longer slanted path, so the time between ticks is longer than the proper time Δt₀ = 2d/c.

Left: a light clock at rest, two mirrors a distance d apart with a vertical double arrow. Right: the same clock moving to the right at speed v; the light goes up diagonally and then down diagonally, forming a triangle with vertical height d, horizontal half-base v delta t over 2 and slanted side c delta t over 2.

1. Left: two mirrors a distance d apart with the vertical light path; tick time Δt₀ = 2d/c.
2. Right: draw the same two mirrors and the zigzag path up and down while the clock moves right at v.

### Last-week summary

- Proper time: one clock at both events (same place); Δt = γΔt₀ ≥ Δt₀. Proper length: rest frame; L = L₀/γ.

- Inclined rod: l = l₀√(1 − β²cos²θ₀), tan θ = γ tan θ₀; only the component along the motion contracts.
- Reciprocity is no contradiction: different event pairs, one clock against two synchronised clocks.
