## GS-031: Tribal and peasant movements

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Dates and the words 'revolt', 'rebellion', 'movement', 'satyagraha'

## Dates first

This lesson moves from the 1760s to 1947. Use the century rule: a century ends on a round year, so the **18th century is 1701 to 1800**, the **19th century 1801 to 1900** and the **20th century 1901 to 2000**. A quick method is to take the first two digits of a year and add one: 1855 gives 18 + 1 = 19, the 19th century; 1921 gives 19 + 1 = 20, the 20th. 1900 itself belongs to the 19th century. By the strict rule, both 1899 and 1900 (Birsa Munda's rising) fall in the 19th century, even though many people feel that 1900 begins the new century. CE (Common Era, the same as AD) is assumed for every year in this lesson; BCE years do not appear.

## Words that are not interchangeable

Examiners and historians use these words with slightly different shades.

- **Revolt, rebellion, uprising, rising**: an armed challenge to authority, usually local. Spectrum prefers 'uprising' and 'revolt' for the civil and tribal cases.
- **Mutiny**: a revolt by soldiers against their own officers or government. The Kol 'mutiny' of 1831 uses the word loosely; the real mutinies in the sources are those of sepoys (Vellore 1806).
- **Movement**: a sustained campaign with an organisation, programme or ideas, often over years (the Wahabi movement, the Kisan Sabha movement).
- **Satyagraha**: Gandhi's method of non-violent resistance; Bardoli (1928) is the peasant example. Spectrum notes the Tana Bhagats conducted a satyagraha before Gandhi's.
- **Agrarian league or combination**: peasants organising to refuse rent and fight court cases (Pabna).
- **Civil, tribal and peasant**: Spectrum's three forms, plus military revolts. The next bridge defines the main group words.

## Why distinctions matter

If a question says 'peasant movements', do not answer with the Santhal rebellion without saying it was a tribal peasant rising, and do not treat a mutiny as a peasant movement. State the form first and the details next.

### Bridge: Rural society words: ryot, zamindar, moneylender, tribe, diku, jhum, revenue settlement

## People of the countryside

- **Peasant or ryot (raiyat)**: a cultivator. The British records spelt it ryot.
- **Zamindar**: under the Permanent Settlement, a revenue collector of the state who collected rent from villages and paid fixed revenue. NCERT stresses that in this definition the zamindar was not the owner of the village land but a revenue collector.
- **Taluqdar**: literally one who holds a taluq; in Awadh a big landlord (the Kisan Sabha module).
- **Jotedar**: a rich peasant in rural Bengal, powerful in the villages and often controlling local trade and moneylending, using sharecroppers (adhiyars or bargadars).
- **Moneylender (sahukar, mahajan)**: a lender who also often traded in grain or cloth. Tribals and peasants called outside moneylenders **dikus** (outsiders).
- **Tenant-at-will**: a tenant who can be evicted at any time. **Occupancy rights**: a tenant's secure claim on land he has cultivated.
- **Begar and vethi**: forced unpaid labour.

## Tribes and forests

A **tribe** (adivasi) is a community that shares kinship and customs and, in NCERT's description, did not have the sharp social divisions of caste societies, though it was not free of inequality. **Jhum (shifting) cultivation** means cutting and burning forest patches, farming them for a while, then leaving them fallow. **Reserved forests** were forests the colonial state declared its property, restricting movement, grazing, hunting and shifting cultivation. **Diku** is used in NCERT and Spectrum for outsiders such as landlords, moneylenders, contractors, officials and, in Birsa's movement, missionaries.

## Land revenue systems in two lines

The **Permanent Settlement (1793)** fixed revenue permanently with zamindars in Bengal, and non-payment meant auction of the zamindari (NCERT); **mahalwari** and **ryotwari** settled revenue with villages or cultivators (in the Bombay Deccan NCERT says the revenue was fixed for 30 years and then revised). The systems are taught fully in GS-029; here they only supply the background to peasant anger.

## Colonial 'law and order' words

- **Indigo planters**: nearly all European owners of indigo factories. **Nij** and **ryoti**: two systems of indigo growing (module 6).

### Bridge: Reading sources: official reports, bias and why historians disagree

## Why a lesson on movements needs a lesson on evidence

We know about most of these movements through records written by the people who suppressed them. NCERT's Class XII chapter makes this a central skill, and the same idea underlies the historiography at the end of this lesson. Learn four habits.

## Habit 1: ask who wrote it and why

The Fifth Report on the Company's Bengal administration shaped our picture of late-18th-century rural Bengal for over a century. NCERT says recent research shows it exaggerated the collapse of zamindari power because its authors wanted to criticise the Company's maladministration. A report written to make a point cannot be read as a neutral photograph.

## Habit 2: official explanations protect officials

The Deccan Riots Commission was asked whether the government's revenue demand was the cause of the 1875 revolt. NCERT notes that it concluded the demand was not the cause and that moneylenders were to blame, and adds that colonial records show a persistent reluctance to admit that discontent could arise from government action. Read official causes critically.

## Habit 3: images are also sources

NCERT's Santhal pictures show British officials riding an elephant and burning villages, which were shown to the public in England to demonstrate British power. Pictures can carry a political message.

## Habit 4: compare with other evidence

NCERT advises juxtaposing official reports with newspapers, unofficial accounts, legal records and oral sources. When a question asks for 'historiography' or 'different interpretations', use this method: say who held the view, what evidence they used and what the weakness is.

## Interpretive uncertainty

Several facts in the Spectrum extracts are given as 'said to be' or 'is considered': Tilka Manjhi as the first adivasi leader to take up arms, Titu Mir's rising as the first armed peasant uprising against the British, Jagabandhu's death in 1825 or 1829. In an answer, keep those hedges.

## Check yourself

Say why the Deccan Riots Commission's conclusion about moneylenders should not be accepted without checking other evidence.

### 1. Orientation: three forms of resistance before and after 1857

*Objective:* Name Spectrum's three forms of people's resistance (plus military revolts), place them on a timeline from the 1760s to 1947, and say what the lesson will ask you to compare.

## The idea

Most people think of the Revolt of 1857 as the first big show of anger against Company rule. Spectrum points out that many incidents before 1857 showed the same resentment, among different groups in different regions. Bipan Chandra, as cited there, sorted people's resistance into **three broad forms**: **civil rebellions**, **tribal uprisings** and **peasant movements**; Spectrum adds **military revolts** (sepoy mutinies) to be comprehensive.

## Who counts as 'the people'

Spectrum uses 'people' broadly: peasants, artisans, tribals, active or dispossessed ruling classes, soldiers (serving or disbanded), and Hindu and Muslim religious leaders. Urban protests also belong: the 1810 Benares agitation against a house tax, the 1814 Surat riots over the salt duty (a different episode from the 1844 Surat salt agitation in module 3), and the 1816 rising in Bareilly against police and municipal taxes. Their grievances differed, but, says Spectrum, they converged on ending British rule. That claim is a generalisation to test later.

## A timeline skeleton

- **1760s to 1800**: Sanyasi revolt (1763-1800), Pahariya and Tilka Manjhi (1770s-85), Chuar rising (1798), poligar revolts (1795-1805).
- **1800 to 1857**: Paika rebellion (1817), Kol rising (1831), Santhal rising (1855-56), Moplah risings in Malabar (1836-54).
- **1857 to 1900**: indigo revolt (1859-60), Pabna leagues (1870s-80s), Deccan riots (1875), Birsa Munda (1899-1900).
- **1900 to 1947**: Champaran (1917), Kisan Sabhas (1918 onwards), Eka and Mappila (1921), Bardoli (1928), All India Kisan Sabha (1936), Tebhaga (1946), Telangana (1946-48).

## The lesson's four questions

1. **Why** did they rise (causes)?
2. **Who** led them and who joined (leadership and character)?
3. **What** did they achieve (results, including laws passed)?
4. **Why** did they fail to end colonial rule, and how have historians judged them (limitations and historiography)?

## Two warnings

- **Do not merge categories**. A tribal rising is not just a peasant rising; the sources show different aims (autonomy, a Munda Raj) from those of Bengal's peasants (rent and contracts).
- **Do not assume nationalism**. Many uprisings were local; the nationalist link appears in the 20th-century movements and is partial (module 9).


### 2. Foundation: why the countryside rose, land, revenue, moneylenders and forests

*Objective:* Explain five causes of rural resentment (revenue, intermediaries and debt, forests, ruin of crafts, colonial law) and say how each pushed different groups to resist.

## Spectrum's starting point

Protests against rulers existed before the British, for example against heavy land revenue, corruption or harsh officials. Spectrum argues that colonial rule had a far more damaging effect, because the Company was interested only in extracting revenue, and colonial law and courts protected the government and its collaborators: landlords, merchants and moneylenders. People left without options took up arms. The tribals' conditions, it says, were not very different from those of the mainland, but intrusion into their independent polity made them more aggrieved and violent.

## Cause 1: revenue and intermediaries

After the Diwani of 1765 (NCERT), the Company wanted large revenue and was unwilling to set up a regular system of assessment. Bengal's crisis deepened, and a terrible famine in 1770 killed, in NCERT's wording, ten million people and about one-third of the population. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed the revenue permanently with zamindars; the demand was high, and zamindaris were auctioned for non-payment (NCERT says over 75 per cent changed hands). Later settlements (mahalwari 1822, ryotwari in the south and the Bombay Deccan) often fixed demands too high, so that peasants fled villages.

## Cause 2: debt

Where the state demanded revenue in cash, peasants borrowed. Debt bonds became the symbol of the new regime: they were legally enforceable, while earlier informal understanding was not (the Deccan mechanism is in module 6). NCERT adds that the British pushed cash crops for Europe (opium, indigo, jute, tea, wheat, cotton), tying villagers to markets, planters and lenders.

## Cause 3: forests and tribal land

The British treated shifting cultivation as primitive and wanted tribals to settle. They declared forests state property and created reserved forests for timber needed for shipping and railways, restricting movement, shifting cultivation, grazing and hunting (NCERT, Spectrum). Forest Department villages gave jhum cultivators small plots in return for labour. Tribal chiefs lost administrative power and paid tribute to the British. Traders and moneylenders came into forests with loans and cash. Roads and railways opened tribal lands to non-tribal settlers.

## Cause 4: ruin of crafts and old patrons

British manufactured goods and heavy duties devastated handloom and handicraft industries (Spectrum); workers moved to agriculture, adding pressure on land, and artisans lost their patrons, the princes, chieftains and zamindars.

## Cause 5: displaced elites and alien rule

Spectrum adds the hurt pride of sidelined zamindars and poligars, the lost livelihood of priestly and scholarly classes who depended on the old elite, and resentment of the 'foreign' rulers. These explain why **civil uprisings** had leaders from the old ruling classes.

## How causes map to forms

Dispossessed elites led civil uprisings; forest and land alienation drove tribal revolts; rent, planters and debt drove peasant movements. The patterns overlap, but this three-way mapping helps in answers.

### 3. Core: civil uprisings, 1763 to 1850s

*Objective:* Define a civil uprising, give leaders and causes for six major cases, and state the common characteristics and weaknesses Spectrum assigns to them.

## Definition

Spectrum uses 'civil' for everything not defence-related and applies it to uprisings **led by deposed rulers or their descendants, former zamindars, landlords and poligars, retainers or officials of conquered kingdoms, or religious leaders**. Mass support came from rack-rented peasants, unemployed artisans and disbanded soldiers, but ex-powerful classes were at the centre. A **poligar** (palayakkarar) was a South Indian holder of a palayam, a territory of a few villages granted for military service and tribute.

## Six cases to know

**1. Sanyasi revolt (1763-1800), Bengal and Bihar.** The famine of 1770 and the harsh economic order drove sanyasis (some originally peasants, some evicted) to fight; dispossessed small zamindars, disbanded soldiers and the rural poor joined. They raided Company factories and treasuries; Warren Hastings subdued them after prolonged action. Hindus and Muslims took part equally; because Muslim fakirs were prominent it is also called the Fakir rebellion. Leaders: Majnum Shah, Chirag Ali, Musa Shah, Bhawani Pathak and Debi Chaudhurani. Bankim Chandra's *Anandamath* is a semi-historical novel based on it.

**2. Poligars' revolt (1795-1805), Tamil country.** Poligars saw themselves as sovereign in their territories and resented the Company taking over management of Tinnevelly and the Carnatic in 1781. **Kattabomman Nayakan** of Panjalankurichi led the first phase (1795-99), was betrayed by the Raja of Pudukottai and hanged; in 1801 a second, more violent phase involved the Marudus; the fort of Panjalankurichi was razed.

**3. Kerala Varma Pazhassi Raja (1793-1805).** The Third Anglo-Mysore War extended British paramountcy over Kottayam; the British nominee Vira Varma levied exorbitant taxes. Pazhassi Raja led peasants, Nairs, Mappilas and Pathans (Tipu's disbanded soldiers) in guerrilla warfare and died in a gunfight in 1805.

**4. Velu Thampi (1808-09), Travancore.** After a subsidiary alliance in 1805 the state fell into arrears and the Resident meddled; the Dalawa Velu Thampi's Kundara Proclamation called for arms. The Maharaja defected; Velu Thampi killed himself.

**5. Paika rebellion (1817), Odisha.** Paiks were a hereditary landed militia with rent-free land for service. After the Company's conquest of Odisha (1803) and the dethronement of the Raja of Khurda, their status fell; high land revenue and salt prices added grievance. Bakshi Jagabandhu Bidyadhar led a Paika army; suppression was brutal by 1818, though the rebellion obtained remissions of arrears, reduced assessments and fixed tenures.

**6. Ahom revolt (1828), Assam.** After the First Burma War (1824-26) the British did not withdraw as pledged and tried to include Ahom territory; rebels made Gomdhar Konwar king near Jorhat; the Company followed a conciliatory policy and restored Upper Assam in part.

Other cases and the Wahabi and Kuka movements are in the master notes.

## Characteristics

Spectrum says these uprisings were **local in cause and effect**, led by **semi-feudal, backward-looking leaders** whose aim was to restore earlier forms of rule and social relations.

## Weaknesses

Localised; no alternative social vision; old in form and ideology; similarity came from common conditions, not a national impulse; obsolete weapons and strategy. Several still won concessions (Paika; Kutch).


### 4. Core: tribal movements, causes, character and the main cases

*Objective:* State why tribal revolts were the most frequent and militant, list the common characteristics, contrast mainland and north-east frontier revolts, and recall the main mainland cases.

## Why tribal movements are distinct

Spectrum says tribal movements under British rule were the **most frequent, militant and violent** of all. Tribals lived as jhum cultivators, hunters and gatherers, pastoralists and settled cultivators (the Mundas held land by clan); colonial rule changed all this.

## What colonial rule did

1. **Land settlements** measured land, defined rights and fixed revenue, upsetting joint ownership and forcing settled farming.
2. **Forest laws** made forests state property and created reserved forests; jhum cultivation was restricted. (NCERT: jhum had to be allowed in parts of the north-east after protests; forest villages supplied cheap labour).
3. **Outsiders** (police, traders, moneylenders, contractors) exploited tribals, who came to see the market as debt and poverty. Tribal chiefs lost authority.
4. **Missionaries** interfered with customs and were seen as agents of alien rule.
5. **Labour recruitment**: tribals were taken to Assam tea gardens and Jharkhand coal mines through contractors who paid low wages (NCERT).

## Common characteristics (Spectrum)

- **Tribal identity and kin ties** gave solidarity; violence was aimed at moneylenders and traders seen as extensions of the colonial government, not at all outsiders.
- Resentment of **laws** imposed by a foreign government that seemed to destroy the traditional socio-economic order.
- **Loss of land and forest rights**, worsened by roads and railways.
- **Messiah-like leaders** who promised an end to suffering.
- **Doomed** by outdated weapons against modern ones.

## Mainland and frontier

Spectrum separates **mainland** revolts (central India, the west-central region and the south) from **north-east frontier** revolts. Frontier tribes had cultural links across borders and often wanted autonomy or independence (a framing that runs into the post-1947 period), were less concerned with the nationalist struggle, generally controlled land and forest (so their revolts were not forest-based or agrarian), met the British later and fought longer; de-Sanskritisation movements spread.

## Mainland cases to know

- **Pahariyas (1778) and Tilka Manjhi**: hill folk of the Rajmahal hills, shifting cultivators; expanding settled agriculture reduced forests; the British followed a brutal attack in the 1770s and then pacification with allowances in the 1780s. Tilka Manjhi, described by Spectrum as a Santhal whose real name is said to have been Jabra Paharia (identity varies across accounts), fought with guerrilla warfare; said to have attacked Bhagalpur in 1784 and shot Augustus Cleveland; hanged in 1785; considered the first adivasi leader to take up arms.
- **Chuars or Jungle Mahal (1768 to 1799)**: Bhumij tribals; the 1798 rising under Durjan Singh arose when the Permanent Settlement and new police regulations made hired paiks redundant and the Raipur estate was auctioned; suppressed in 1799.
- **Kols (1831)**: led by Buddho Bhagat in Chotanagpur after land passed to outsiders; about a thousand outsiders were killed or burnt; large military operations restored order.
- **Hos and Mundas (1820-37)**: against occupation of Singhbhum and a new revenue policy.
- **Khonds (1837-56)**: led by Chakra Bisoi against suppression of human sacrifice, new taxes and the entry of zamindars. **Koyas**, **Bhils**, **Kolis** and **Ramosi** also rose (dates in the master notes).

## Frontier cases

Khasi uprising under Tirath Singh (to 1833) against road-building and outsiders; Singphos (1830s); Ahoms; small Lushai, Mishmi and Khampti risings. Later NCERT cases (Songram Sangma 1906, Bastar 1910, Warli 1940, forest satyagrahas of the 1930s) are in the master notes.


### 5. Core: the Santhal hul (1855-56) and Birsa Munda's Ulgulan (1899-1900)

*Objective:* Trace the Santhal rebellion from settlement to suppression and the Birsa movement from vision to legislation, with dates, leaders, causes and results.

## The Santhals and the Rajmahal hills

The Santhals began arriving in Bengal around the 1780s; zamindars hired them to clear land and officials invited them to settle in the Jangal Mahals, since the shifting-cultivating Paharias had been difficult. NCERT frames it as the battle of the **hoe** (Paharia) and the **plough** (Santhal). By **1832** a large area was demarcated as **Damin-i-Koh**, 'skirts of the hills', declared Santhal land, on condition that a tenth be cleared within ten years. Santhal villages rose from **40 in 1838 to 1,473 by 1851**, and the population from about **3,000 to over 82,000**.

## Why they rebelled

The land they cleared slipped away. The state levied heavy taxes, moneylenders (the dikus) charged high interest and took land when debts were unpaid, and zamindars asserted control over the Damin area. By the 1850s, say NCERT and Spectrum, the Santhals decided to rebel against zamindars, moneylenders and the colonial state.

## The hul

The Santhals called the rebellion **hul** (liberation). Under the brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu** they proclaimed an end to Company rule and declared the area between Bhagalpur and Rajmahal autonomous. Messengers carried **sal branches** as a secret sign. The British suppressed the rising with great force by 1856, burning villages and killing thousands; Sidhu and Kanhu were killed.

## Result

After the revolt, the **Santhal Pargana** was created, carved out of Bhagalpur and Birbhum (NCERT: 5,500 square miles), with special laws to conciliate the Santhals. For the 2018 Prelims question the extracts establish the Santhal Pargana. On land transfer, the Santhal Parganas Settlement Regulation (III of 1872) is generally cited as barring transfer of Santhal land to non-Santhals, and the Santhal Parganas Tenancy (Supplementary Provisions) Act 1949 came later (standard knowledge, verify). Check each statement against these facts; this is analysis, not an official key.

## Birsa Munda

Birsa was born in the mid-1870s in a poor Munda family. He heard tales of past Munda uprisings and of a golden age and a fight for land (**mulk ki larai**). He attended a missionary school and spent time with a Vaishnav preacher, so his movement mixed reform with revolt: he asked the Mundas to give up liquor, clean their villages and stop believing in witchcraft, and he turned against missionaries and landlords.

In 1895 Birsa spoke of a **satyug**, an age of truth when Mundas lived well. British officials were most worried by the **political aim**: to drive out missionaries, moneylenders, landlords and the government and set up a **Munda Raj** under Birsa. He was arrested in 1895 and jailed for two years. Released in 1897, he toured villages urging his people to destroy 'Ravana' (dikus and Europeans). Followers attacked police stations and churches, raided moneylenders' and zamindars' property, and raised a white flag for Birsa Raj. Spectrum says the revolt broke out in **December 1899**; the movement is called **Ulgulan** ('Great Tumult'). Birsa was arrested in 1900 and died of illness (NCERT: cholera) in 1900.

## Results of the Birsa movement

NCERT says it forced the government to introduce laws so that tribal land could not be easily taken by dikus, and showed that tribals could protest in their own way. Spectrum lists the abolition of compulsory begar, the **Tenancy Act of 1903** (recognising the Mundas' Khuntkatti system) and the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908**.

The Santhal-Birsa comparison table and the later movements (Sardari Larai, Tana Bhagat, Devi, Rampa) are in the master notes.


### 6. Core: peasant movements to 1885, Indigo, Pabna and the Deccan riots

*Objective:* Describe the indigo revolt, Pabna leagues and Deccan riots with dates, causes, forms of struggle and results, and state how peasant movements changed after 1857.

## Indigo revolt, 1859-60 (Bengal)

*Background.* NCERT explains why Britain needed Indian indigo: British cotton production boomed and supplies from other countries collapsed (figures in the master notes). There were two systems: **nij** (the planter grew indigo on land he controlled, using hired labour) and **ryoti** (the planter forced peasants to sign a contract, **satta**, took advances and committed at least 25 per cent of their holding to indigo). Spectrum describes fraud, kidnappings, floggings and seizure of cattle.

*Revolt.* In March 1859 thousands of ryots in Bengal refused to grow indigo; Spectrum names **Digambar Biswas and Bishnu Biswas of Nadia district**. They resisted lathiyals (planters' strongmen), went on a **rent strike**, and used legal action funded by collections; women fought with household tools; those who served planters were socially boycotted. Local zamindars and village headmen supported the ryots (NCERT; the Ashley Eden notice story is in the master notes). The Bengali intelligentsia helped through newspapers, meetings and memoranda.

*Result.* The government sent troops and appointed the **Indigo Commission**, which held the planters guilty of coercion and allowed ryots to refuse contracts in future. A **November 1860** notification said ryots could not be compelled to grow indigo. Spectrum says indigo cultivation was virtually wiped out from Bengal by the end of 1860; NCERT says planters shifted to Bihar and continued there (Gandhi's 1917 visit began the **Champaran** movement), and that with the discovery of synthetic dyes in the late nineteenth century the business was 'severely affected' (German synthetic indigo, c.1897: standard knowledge, verify). Two causes are therefore taught: the 1859-60 resistance in Bengal and, later, synthetic dyes.

## Pabna agrarian leagues (1870s-80s, East Bengal)

Zamindars raised rents beyond legal limits and blocked tenants from gaining occupancy rights under Act X of 1859, using evictions, seizure of cattle and costly litigation. Peasants of Yusufshahi Pargana in Pabna formed an **agrarian league**, organised a **rent strike** and raised funds for court cases. The struggle was largely **legal**, with little violence; most cases were settled by 1885, and the **Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885** followed. Supporters included Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, R.C. Dutt and the Indian Association under Surendranath Banerjea.

## Deccan riots, 1875 (Bombay Deccan)

*Background (NCERT, Spectrum).* The ryotwari revenue demand was high; moneylenders, mostly outsiders (Marwaris or Gujaratis, says Spectrum), dominated credit. The American Civil War (from 1861) created a **cotton boom**: Indian cotton acreage in the Bombay Deccan rose sharply (figures in the master notes), credit flowed easily, and many peasants went deeper into debt. After the war ended, cotton prices fell, creditors refused loans, and a new revenue settlement raised the demand sharply (figures in the master notes). Peasants saw moneylenders as breaking customary rules: for example, the norm that interest should not exceed the principal. The **Limitation Law of 1859** made bonds valid for three years; moneylenders forced new bonds every three years, capitalising the unpaid interest (NCERT).

*Revolt.* A social boycott began in 1874 (Spectrum); on 12 May 1875 ryots gathered at **Supa** in Poona district and demanded account books and debt bonds, then burnt them and attacked shops and some houses. The rising spread to Ahmednagar and over 6,500 square kilometres and more than thirty villages. Troops were called, 951 people arrested, and several months passed before control returned (NCERT).

*Result.* The **Deccan Riots Commission** reported in 1878 that moneylenders were to blame (read this critically; see bridge B3); the **Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act (1879)** was passed.

## Changed nature after 1857 (Spectrum)

- Peasants became the **main force**, with **economic** demands, **immediate** enemies (planters, zamindars, moneylenders) and **specific, limited** objectives.
- **Colonialism was not the target**; limited territorial reach; no long-term organisation.
- A strong awareness of **legal rights** and use of courts.
- *Weaknesses*: inadequate understanding of colonialism; no new ideology or programme; struggles within the old order.


### 7. Core: peasants and nationalism, 1917 to 1928

*Objective:* Describe Champaran, the Kisan Sabha, Eka, the Mappila revolt and Bardoli, and state how each related to the Congress and the national movement.

## The shift in setting

Spectrum says the 20th-century peasant movements influenced, and were influenced by, the national struggle: earlier movements fought nearby enemies, these joined the nationalist agenda partly and unevenly.

## Champaran (1917)

NCERT's account is brief: planters shifted to Bihar after 1859-60, and a Bihar peasant persuaded Gandhi to visit Champaran, where his 1917 visit began the movement against them. Further detail is outside the extracts (verify).

## United Provinces and Awadh Kisan Sabhas

After 1857 the Awadh **taluqdars** had recovered their lands; most cultivators faced high rents, **bedakhali** (summary eviction), illegal levies and **nazrana** (renewal fees), and the First World War raised prices. The **United Provinces Kisan Sabha** was set up in **February 1918** (Gauri Shankar Mishra, Indra Narayan Dwivedi) and had **450 branches by June 1919**. The **Awadh Kisan Sabha** came into being in **October 1920** because of differences among nationalists. It asked kisans to refuse bedakhali land and begar and to settle disputes through panchayats. In January 1921 activity turned into looting and clashes with police in Rae Bareli, Faizabad and Sultanpur; repression and the Awadh Rent (Amendment) Act ended it.

## Eka movement (late 1921 to March 1922)

In Hardoi, Bahraich and Sitapur, peasants protested rents 50 per cent above recorded rates, the oppression of thikadars who collected revenue, and share-rents. Meetings used a **religious ritual** vow to pay only the recorded rent, on time, not to leave when evicted, to refuse forced labour and to obey panchayat decisions. Leaders included **Madari Pasi** and other low-caste leaders and many small zamindars. Repression ended it by March 1922.

## Mappila revolt (1921)

The Mappilas were Muslim tenants in Malabar, where most landlords (**jenmies**) were Hindu; they lacked security of tenure and paid high rents and renewal fees. The movement merged with the **Khilafat** agitation. In **August 1921** the arrest of a respected priest, Ali Musaliar, sparked riots; at first the targets were British symbols and unpopular landlords. After martial law and repression the rebellion took **communal overtones**, isolating the Mappilas from the Khilafat-Non-Cooperation movement. By December 1921 all resistance had stopped. (Spectrum treats it as the second Moplah uprising, after the 1836-54 series.)

## Bardoli Satyagraha (1928)

Bardoli taluka is in Surat district. **Dating warning**: Spectrum (ch31) prints that the enhancement began in January 1926 (30 per cent) and that Patel was called in February 1926; this looks like a source slip. Standard accounts give a proposed rise of about 30 per cent, cut to about 22 per cent in 1927, with **Vallabhbhai Patel taking charge in February 1928** and the satyagraha running **February to August 1928** (verify). Teach 1928. A Congress-side Bardoli Inquiry Committee found the rise unjustified; the women of Bardoli gave Patel the title **Sardar**. Peasants refused to pay until an independent tribunal was appointed or the old amount accepted. Patel set up **chhavanis** (camps), a Patrika and a **social boycott**, with special emphasis on women; By **August 1928** tension was high, Gandhi came to Bardoli. The government looked for a graceful exit; after the 1928 settlement an official enquiry (Maxwell-Broomfield, 1929; verify) recommended a rise of only **6.03 per cent**.

## Reading these cases

Eka and the Awadh Kisan Sabha were rent movements in a taluqdari region; Bardoli was a revenue movement of occupants in a ryotwari taluka; Mappila was a tenant movement turned religious-political. Spectrum says Congress leaders were quick to protest about Bardoli, set up the Inquiry Committee and called in Patel; for Eka, Spectrum names low-caste leaders and small zamindars, and standard accounts say its initial thrust came from Congress and Khilafat leaders before it passed outside Congress control (verify).


### 8. Core: Kisan Sabhas, Congress ministries, Tebhaga and Telangana, 1930s to 1948

*Objective:* Narrate the growth of the All India Kisan Sabha, the 1937-39 peak, provincial patterns and the two post-war risings, Tebhaga and Telangana.

## The 1930s turn

Spectrum says the 1930s peasant awakening was influenced by the **Great Depression** and the **Civil Disobedience Movement**, which took the form of no-rent, no-revenue campaigns. After the active phase ended (1932), new entrants to politics turned to organising peasants.

## All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)

The **All India Kisan Congress or Sabha** was founded in **Lucknow in April 1936** with **Swami Sahajanand Saraswati** as president and **N.G. Ranga** as general secretary, and issued a kisan manifesto. AIKS and the Congress held sessions at Faizpur in 1936, and the Congress agrarian policy in the 1937 provincial elections was strongly influenced by the AIKS agenda.

## Under Congress ministries (1937-39) and in the war

Spectrum calls 1937-39 the **high watermark** of peasant activity under Congress provincial rule; the chief form was kisan conferences and meetings. The provincial pattern (Kerala, Andhra, Bihar, Punjab) is tabulated in the master notes. A pro-war line taken by the communists split the AIKS on communist and non-communist lines, and Sahajanand, Indulal Yagnik and N.G. Ranga left; the Kisan Sabha still worked in the famine of 1943.

## Tebhaga movement (from September 1946, Bengal)

The Bengal Provincial Kisan Sabha called for **tebhaga**, two-thirds of the produce to the sharecroppers (**bargadars**, also called bagchasi or adhyar) instead of one-half; they worked land rented from **jotedars**. Communist cadres, including urban student militias, organised them under the slogan **'nij khamare dhan tolo'**: take the paddy to your own threshing floor. The storm centre was north Bengal, especially the **Rajbanshis**, with many Muslims participating. It dissipated because of the Muslim League ministry's Bargadari Bill, intensified repression, the Hindu Mahasabha's agitation for a separate Bengal, and riots in Calcutta.

## Telangana movement (1946-48)

Spectrum calls it the biggest peasant guerrilla war of modern Indian history, affecting about 3,000 villages and 3 million people, in the princely state of Hyderabad, where a small Urdu-speaking Muslim elite ruled a mainly Hindu population with no civil liberties, forced labour (**vethi**) and illegal exactions by deshmukhs, jagirdars and doras. The communist-led **Andhra Mahasabha** had built a base in the war years. The rising began in **July 1946** after a deshmukh's thug killed a village militant in Jangaon taluka of Nalgonda, then spread to Warangal and Khammam. Peasants organised **village sanghams** and routed the **Razakars**, the Nizam's stormtroopers. It was most intense between **August 1947 and September 1948**, and fizzled out once Indian security forces took over Hyderabad.

*Achievements (Spectrum)*: vethi ended in guerrilla-controlled villages; wages rose; seized lands were restored; the feudal regime was shaken, clearing the way for Andhra Pradesh on linguistic lines.

The overall balance-sheet of these movements, and Spectrum's 'nationalism' claim, are weighed in module 9.


### 9. Master: character, leadership, results, limitations and the historians' debate

*Objective:* Compare civil, tribal and peasant movements on cause, leadership, form, result and limitation, and explain the main interpretive debates with the tension flagged in Spectrum.

## One-table comparison

The six-dimension comparison table (leaders, cause, aim, form, result, limitation) is in the master notes. In brief: civil uprisings were led by old elites who wanted to restore old rule and were usually crushed; tribal revolts were driven by land and forest loss and sought autonomy or a tribal raj; peasant movements sought specific economic relief and, before the 20th century, did not target colonialism.

## Early peasant risings with religious overtones

**Titu Mir (Nithar Ali, 1782-1831)** inspired Muslim tenants in West Bengal against landlords (mostly Hindu) who imposed a beard tax and against indigo planters; Spectrum calls it often considered the first armed peasant uprising against the British, and it merged into the Wahabi movement. The Pagal Panthis, Faraizis and Moplahs (22 rebellions in 1836-54) are in the master notes. None succeeded.

## Leadership patterns

Leaders came from dispossessed elites (civil); messiah-like figures and chiefs (tribal); village leaders, then lawyers and Congress or communist organisers (peasant). Birsa's golden-age vision shows that **ideas and symbols** mattered, not only grievances.

## Results

- **Concessions**: Paika remissions; Santhal Pargana; Tenancy Acts of 1903 and 1908; Indigo Commission notification (1860); Bengal Tenancy Act (1885); Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act (1879); Bardoli's 6.03 per cent.
- **Long-term**: Spectrum credits peasant movements with creating an atmosphere for agrarian reforms and eroding landed power; NCERT says Birsa's movement showed tribals' capacity to protest in their own way.

## The 'culmination' question

The 2019 Mains question asks whether 1857 was the culmination of earlier local rebellions. For continuity: shared grievances (revenue, loss of status, religion) and peasants joining in 1857, especially in western Uttar Pradesh, often with feudal leaders. Against a simple chain: Spectrum says the earlier uprisings were localised, with local causes and no common national impulse. So 1857 can be called a **culmination of grievances** rather than of a single organised tradition.

## Interpretive tension in Spectrum

Spectrum's 'weaknesses' list says the uprisings were not national, while its chapter introduction says they converged on ending British rule; its peasant 'balance-sheet' says the movements rested on the ideology of nationalism, while its 'changed nature' section says colonialism was not the target. Treat both as claims and say which period you mean (19th century or 1917-47).

## Wider reading, flagged

The Subaltern Studies group (for example Ranajit Guha) argues that peasants acted with their own consciousness, not merely as followers of elites; general knowledge, not in the extracts; verify before citing.

### Notes: Definitions

- Civil uprising: a rising led by deposed rulers, zamindars, poligars, ex-officials or religious leaders, supported by peasants, artisans and disbanded soldiers (Spectrum's usage).
- Poligar (palayakkarar): a South Indian holder of a palayam, a small territory granted for military service and tribute.
- Paik: a hereditary landed militia in Odisha with rent-free land for service; also village guards in the Jungle Mahal.
- Diku: an outsider (landlord, moneylender, trader, contractor, official, missionary) blamed by tribals for their misery.
- Jhum (shifting) cultivation: cutting and burning forest patches, farming and leaving them fallow.
- Reserved forest: state forest where movement, grazing, hunting and shifting cultivation were restricted.
- Ulgulan: 'Great Tumult', the Birsa Munda movement; hul: the Santhal term for liberation or rebellion.
- Nij and ryoti: two indigo systems, planter's own land against forced contracts (satta) with ryots.
- Bedakhali: summary eviction of tenants in Awadh; nazrana: renewal fee; hari and begar: unpaid labour.
- Tebhaga: two-thirds of the produce for sharecroppers (bargadars); vethi: forced labour in Telangana.
- Occupancy right: a tenant's secure claim over the land he cultivates.
