## GS-125: Major dams, irrigation and river projects

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### 1. Orientation: dams, canals and river projects on one page

*Objective:* Say in your own words what a dam, a reservoir, a canal and a multipurpose project are, and why India built them.

## What this topic is about

India gets most of its rain in a short monsoon season and unevenly across places (NCERT Class XII, chapter 4). Rivers carry that water to the sea. A **dam** is a barrier built across a river to hold water back. The lake that forms behind it is a **reservoir**. A **canal** is a man-made channel that carries water from a river or reservoir to fields. A **river-valley project** is a planned scheme of dams, canals and power stations in a river's valley. When it is meant to do several jobs at once (irrigation, electricity, flood control, drinking water, navigation) it is called **multipurpose**.

NCERT says India has traditionally been an agrarian economy, with about two-thirds of its people dependent on agriculture. So irrigation was given very high priority in the Five Year Plans, and multipurpose river-valley projects such as Bhakra-Nangal, Hirakud, Damodar Valley, Nagarjuna Sagar and the Indira Gandhi Canal Project were taken up. The same passage says that India's water demand today is dominated by irrigation.

## The one-page picture

- **Problem:** rain is seasonal and patchy. NCERT (Class XI, drainage chapter) says much river water is wasted in floods and runs to the sea in the rainy season, while another area suffers drought.
- **Idea:** store the extra wet-season water and release it when it is needed.
- **Tools:** dams and reservoirs, canals, wells and tubewells, tanks, and small structures such as percolation tanks and check dams.
- **Benefits:** irrigation, electricity, flood moderation, drinking and industrial water and navigation (Leong lists the river benefits in general terms).
- **Costs:** silt, loss of land and homes, damage to river life, disputes between States, and heavy pumping of groundwater where canals do not reach.
- **Answer rule:** most Mains questions on this topic are benefit-and-cost questions. Give both sides and then a way forward.

## Where this lesson sits

River names, basins and tributaries were taught in Day 18 lesson GS-117 (Major river basins and tributaries); here you use rivers as the addresses of dams. How a crop is watered (irrigated, rainfed, dryland, wetland) was introduced in Day 17 lesson GS-123 (the module on how the crop is watered). Hydel power as an energy source belongs to Day 18 lesson GS-124 (the module on hydel and thermal power); this lesson treats power only as one purpose of a dam.

### 2. Foundation: the words and the dam cross-section

*Objective:* Define the water words used in the NCERT chapter and label the parts of a dam on a cross-section.

## Words to own before the numbers

- **Precipitation:** all water falling from the sky as rain, snow or hail.
- **Surface water:** water in rivers, lakes, ponds and tanks; NCERT names these four as the major sources.
- **Groundwater:** water below the land surface, reached by wells. **Replenishable** means nature refills it each year, for example when rain soaks in.
- **Utilisable (beneficial use):** the part that can actually be put to use after topography and other limits are accounted for.
- **Cubic km:** the volume of a cube one kilometre on each side. One cubic km equals 1,000 million cubic metres (standard conversion, check by multiplying 1,000 m three times).
- **Catchment area and river basin:** the area a river and its tributaries drain (NCERT, Class XI). A **watershed** is the boundary line between two basins; NCERT also says small drainage areas are called watersheds while large ones are basins.
- **Perennial and non-perennial:** perennial rivers carry water all year; non-perennial ones have very little in the dry season. NCERT says Himalayan rivers are perennial because they are fed by both snow melt and rain, and Peninsular rivers are described as non-perennial (it adds that the Kaveri carries water through the year with less fluctuation).
- **Irrigation:** supplying water to crops by human effort. Protective irrigation guards crops against a moisture shortage; productive irrigation supplies enough water for high yields (Day 17 lesson GS-123).
- **Command area:** the land that a canal or project can water. NCERT uses the word, for example in the Jal Kranti Abhiyan (model command area of about 1,000 hectares in different regions), without defining it; the definition here is standard (verify).
- **Tank:** in Indian usage a storage pond or small reservoir; NCERT lists irrigation tanks among the traditional rainwater storage bodies.
- **Tubewell:** a deep narrow well fitted with a pump (standard; Leong in GS-123 describes electric-pump tubewells).
- **Spillway:** the channel through which surplus water is let past the dam safely (standard).

## The dam cross-section

Draw a side view with the water on the left. Label in this order: reservoir, dam wall, silt (the mud and sand that settles in the reservoir), intake, turbine and power house, spillway, canal head (on the reservoir side, above the river bed) and the river continuing downstream. The point to remember: the wall holds water upstream, the intake feeds the turbines and the canal head sends water to fields. Everything a student must say about benefits and costs hangs on this one picture.

## Basin and watershed: why they matter

NCERT says river basins and watersheds are marked by unity: what happens in one part directly affects the others. That is why they are accepted as the most appropriate micro, meso or macro planning regions. A dam changes the whole basin below it, and a village-scale watershed project (module 8) changes a small one.

### 3. Core: India's water budget and who uses it

*Objective:* State the NCERT water figures, check their arithmetic and name the sector that uses most water.

## The funnel of numbers (NCERT Class XII, chapter 4)

India has about 2.45 per cent of the world's surface area, about 4 per cent of its water resources and more than 17 per cent of its people. The ratio tells the story: many people, comparatively little water.

Read the numbers as a funnel, narrowing at each step:

- Precipitation in a year: about **4,000 cubic km**.
- Availability from surface water and replenishable groundwater: **1,869 cubic km**.
- Of this only **60 per cent** can be put to beneficial use, so the utilisable total is **1,122 cubic km**.

Check: 0.60 x 1,869 = 1,121.4, about 1,121, which is close to NCERT's 1,122. The exact match is 690 + 432 = 1,122.

## Surface water

There are four major sources: rivers, lakes, ponds and tanks. NCERT counts about **10,360 rivers and tributaries longer than 1.6 km**. It gives the mean annual flow of all river basins as 1,869 cubic km and says only about **690 cubic km** of it can be used because of topographical, hydrological and other constraints. The Ganga, the Brahmaputra and the Barak have high rainfall in their catchments: although they cover about one-third of the country's area, they hold **60 per cent** of the surface water. Much of the flow of the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri has already been harnessed; this is yet to be done in the Brahmaputra and Ganga basins.

## Groundwater

Replenishable groundwater is about **432 cubic km**. Utilisation is relatively high in the north-western river basins and parts of south India: very high in Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu; moderate in Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Tripura and Maharashtra; small in Chhattisgarh, Odisha and Kerala.

## Two slips to check, not to memorise

First, NCERT says 690 cubic km is 32 per cent of the available surface water. Recompute: 690 / 1,869 = 36.9 per cent; 32 per cent would need a base near 2,156. Treat 32 per cent as a slip and use 690 as the number. Second, 690 + 432 = 1,122 exactly, so the utilisable total can also be read as usable surface water plus replenishable groundwater. The 60 per cent route and the sum route both reach 1,122, which suggests the book mixes two descriptions of the 1,869 figure. Standard reading (CWC; verify): 1,869 cubic km is the mean annual flow of the river basins (surface water); the utilisable total is 690 of surface water plus 432 of groundwater, which makes 1,122. NCERT's wording that 1,869 is "surface water plus replenishable groundwater" is the loose one. If an exam statement uses these numbers, quote NCERT's figure and do not argue about the route.

## Who uses the water

Agriculture takes **89 per cent of surface water and 92 per cent of groundwater**. Industry uses 2 per cent of surface water and 5 per cent of groundwater. The domestic sector uses 9 per cent of surface water (so by subtraction about 3 per cent of groundwater; the extract does not print this, check). NCERT expects the industrial and domestic shares to rise with development.

## Lagoons and backwaters

Kerala, Odisha and West Bengal have vast surface water in lagoons and lakes along the indented coast. The water is generally brackish and is used for fishing and for irrigating certain paddy varieties and coconut. A hand-drawn figure of the funnel (4,000 to 1,869 to 1,122) with the 690 + 432 split is worth drawing once.

### 4. Core: why irrigation, and canal, well and tank

*Objective:* Give NCERT's reasons for irrigation, describe canal, well and tank irrigation, and say what the extract states about wells in Punjab and Haryana.

## Why India needs irrigation (NCERT, Class XII chapter 4)

NCERT gives four reasons:

1. **Variable rain.** Rainfall varies in space and time, and large tracts, notably north-western India and the Deccan plateau, are deficient in rainfall and drought-prone. Winter and summer are dry in most parts. Even West Bengal and Bihar, with ample rain, suffer dry spells when the monsoon breaks or fails.
2. **Thirsty crops.** Rice, sugarcane and jute need very high water, which can be met only through irrigation.
3. **Multiple cropping and yields.** Irrigation makes multiple cropping possible and irrigated land is more productive than unirrigated land.
4. **The Green Revolution.** High-yielding varieties need a regular moisture supply, which is why the Green Revolution strategy has largely succeeded in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh. In these three, more than 85 per cent of the net sown area is irrigated (Day 17 lesson GS-123 gives net sown area).

## The three forms

- **Canal irrigation:** a dam or a weir across a river sends water down channels. The extracts mention the Bhakra Nangal canal system fed by the Satluj, the Indira Gandhi Canal Project, and the Yamuna's water feeding the western and eastern Yamuna canals and the Agra canal (NCERT, Class XI). Leong, as quoted in Day 17 lesson GS-123, distinguishes perennial canals from flood-fed inundation canals.
- **Well and tubewell irrigation:** NCERT gives only the shares: of the total net irrigated area, **76.1 per cent in Punjab and 51.3 per cent in Haryana** are irrigated through wells and tubewells. Such States use a large part of their groundwater potential, which has caused falling groundwater tables.
- **Tank irrigation:** NCERT lists irrigation tanks among the traditional surface storage bodies in rural areas (with lakes and ponds) and names the Neeru-Meeru programme (Andhra Pradesh) and the Arvary Pani Sansad (Alwar, Rajasthan), which built structures such as percolation tanks and johads. It does not give a State-wise tank share.

## Where each dominates: what is and is not in the extract

Only the Punjab and Haryana well shares are stated. The textbook picture (verify) is that wells and tubewells are the largest source overall and dominate the alluvial plains of the north-west and the Ganga valley; canals matter in parts of the northern plains and in the east-coast deltas; and tanks are the traditional source on the Peninsular plateau (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Telangana). That picture is **not in the extracts** and State-wise shares change over time; do not use it as a certain fact in an exam. Treat it as a map memory aid only.

## The price of intensive irrigation

Over-use of groundwater has lowered the water table in Punjab and Haryana. Over-withdrawal in Rajasthan and Maharashtra raised fluoride, and in parts of West Bengal and Bihar arsenic concentration. NCERT's own question asks about increasing soil salinity and groundwater depletion in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh: salinity (soil becoming salty), falling yields and rising pumping costs are the likely effects (standard reasoning).

### 6. Core: benefits and costs of river-valley projects

*Objective:* Argue both sides of a large dam in five dimensions and separate what the extracts say from standard additions.

## Benefits

- **Irrigation.** Module 4. Irrigated land gives higher productivity and multiple crops.
- **Flood moderation.** Leong says dams hold back flood water that would otherwise cause disastrous floods lower down, for example in the Indus and Ganges plains. NCERT says the Damodar was once the 'sorrow of Bengal' and has been tamed by the Damodar Valley Corporation; the Kosi, the 'sorrow of Bihar', changes course because it dumps huge sediment loads in the plains.
- **Power.** Leong says steep gorges and waterfalls give natural sites for hydroelectric power, which attracts industries such as metal smelting and engineering that use cheap power. Hydel power is taught in Day 18 lesson GS-124.
- **Navigation and water supply.** Leong lists rivers as waterways and sources of domestic and industrial water. NCERT notes some navigation on the lower Mahanadi and on the deltaic stretch of the Godavari.
- **Fertile floodplains.** Leong says floods may add a layer of fertile silt, but excessive flooding may discourage cultivation altogether.

## Costs

- **Silt.** NCERT lists the silt load of rivers as a problem. Leong says rivers silt up ports and deposit sediment in the lower course. That silt also settles in a reservoir and can reduce its working life (standard; verify).
- **Disputes between States.** NCERT lists river-water disputes between States among the problems of using river water, and says tension over sharing a scarce resource is becoming more contested. Shared basins show why: NCERT gives the Krishna basin as 27 per cent in Maharashtra, 44 per cent in Karnataka and 29 per cent in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana; the Kaveri as 3, 41 and 56 per cent in Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. How such disputes are settled is standard (see module 7).
- **Salinity and groundwater.** NCERT's own question links intensive irrigation in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh with rising soil salinity and falling groundwater.
- **Displacement and ecology (standard; verify).** Filling a reservoir submerges land, villages and forests, so people have to be rehabilitated; a dam also changes the flow, sediment and fish movement below it. The extracts do not discuss this, but every Mains answer on dams is expected to.
- **Dam failure (standard; verify).** A failure sends a flood downstream (2023 question). Usual causes: overtopping in an extreme flood, seepage or weakness in the foundation, poor design or construction, an earthquake or landslide, and weak maintenance. Examples often cited include the Machchu dam in Gujarat (1979) and the Panshet dam in Maharashtra (1961); check before writing.

## Weighing the two sides

| Purpose | Gain | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Irrigation | More area under assured water; multiple cropping | Salinity, waterlogging (standard), groundwater neglect |
| Power | Cheap electricity, industry | Land under water, change in river flow |
| Flood control | Protects the lower plains | Failure risk; silt trapped |
| Navigation and supply | Waterways and drinking water | Needs dredging and upkeep |

## The five dimensions for a Mains answer

Physical (river, silt), human (displacement), economic (power, crops), environmental (ecology, salinity), India case (Damodar, Bhakra Nangal, Narmada). End with a balancing sentence: plan dams with rehabilitation, safety checks and small structures as well.

### 7. Core: interlinking of rivers and inter-State disputes

*Objective:* State what NCERT says about moving water between basins and what Mains answers must add.

## The idea

NCERT (Class XI, drainage chapter) asks: if water is wasted in floods in one part while another suffers drought, can surplus be moved from one basin to a water-deficit basin? NCERT (Class XII) names transfer of water from water-surplus to water-deficit areas through **inter-linking of rivers** as one of the remedies for India's water problem, along with desalination in coastal areas and in brackish arid zones. It also says the most important issue for users and communities is the **pricing of water**.

## What the extracts say about difficulties

- NCERT asks whether digging a canal is enough to move water from the Ganga basin to a Peninsular river and says the major problem is the unevenness of terrain: water has to be lifted from the plain to the plateau.
- It asks whether the north Indian rivers really have surplus water that can be transferred regularly.
- It asks the learner to rank problems of using river water: insufficient quantity, pollution, silt load, uneven seasonal flow, disputes between States and shrinking channels. The list is therefore a ready checklist for answers.

## Inter-basin schemes the book lists for teachers to explain

Periyar Diversion Scheme, Indira Gandhi Canal Project, Kurnool-Cuddapah Canal, Beas-Satluj Link Canal and Ganga-Kaveri Link Canal (no details are given). Four are built canals (Periyar Diversion, Indira Gandhi Canal, Kurnool-Cuddapah and Beas-Satluj Link); the Ganga-Kaveri link was a proposal and has not been built (standard; verify). The book lists all five together, so a question may ask which one is only a proposal.

## National Water Policy 2012 (as summarised by NCERT)

The Policy calls for a **national water framework law** and comprehensive legislation for optimum development of **inter-State rivers and river valleys**. After pre-emptive needs (safe drinking water and sanitation, food security, livelihoods of poor farmers, minimum ecosystem needs), water should be treated as an **economic good** to promote conservation and efficient use. It stresses adaptation to climate change, benchmarks such as water footprints and water auditing, removing the gap between urban and rural supply norms and **community participation** in projects.

## Additions Mains expects (standard; verify)

- Disputes over inter-State rivers are handled under Article 262 of the Constitution, which lets Parliament provide by law for adjudication; tribunals are set up under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956. Check the current text and amendments.
- The Ken and Betwa, which NCERT names as right-bank tributaries of the Yamuna from the Peninsula, are the pair linked in a project frequently reported in the news. Quote current status only after checking.
- Environmental concerns usually raised: changed river flow and sediment, forest and wildlife submergence, and displacement. Advantages usually cited: drought and flood moderation, irrigation and navigation (compare the 2017 and 2020 questions).

## A balanced frame for a 150-word answer

Advantage one line, difficulty one line (terrain, lifting, surplus doubtful), environmental cost one line, a cheaper alternative (watershed management and rainwater harvesting, module 8) and a closing line saying that linking must rest on feasibility studies and the consent of the States concerned.

### 8. Master: watershed, rainwater harvesting, groundwater and the schemes NCERT names

*Objective:* Explain watershed management and rainwater harvesting as the small-scale answer to water stress and list the schemes NCERT names.

## Why conserve

NCERT (Class XII, chapter 4) says fresh water is declining per person while demand rises, and that desalination is too costly to count as a major source. So India must conserve: prevent pollution, develop watersheds, harvest rainwater, recycle and reuse water and make **conjunctive use** of surface and groundwater (using both together to keep supply steady).

## Watershed management

NCERT: it basically means efficient management and conservation of surface and groundwater, including preventing runoff and storing and recharging groundwater through percolation tanks and recharge wells. In the broad sense it covers conservation, regeneration and wise use of all resources (land, water, plants, animals and people) within a watershed, balancing nature and society. Success depends largely on community participation. NCERT adds that only a few projects are success stories; most are still at an early stage.

- **Haryali:** a Central Government watershed project to help rural people conserve water for drinking, irrigation, fisheries and afforestation, run by Gram Panchayats.
- **Neeru-Meeru (Water and You), Andhra Pradesh** and **Arvary Pani Sansad, Alwar, Rajasthan:** water-harvesting structures such as percolation tanks, johads (dug-out ponds) and check dams through people's participation. Tamil Nadu has made house water-harvesting structures compulsory.
- **Ralegan Siddhi, Ahmadnagar, Maharashtra** (NCERT case study): work began with a percolation tank; people gave voluntary labour, banned open grazing and the water-intensive crop sugarcane, and encouraged low-water crops such as pulses and oilseeds. NCERT notes water is now adequate but fertiliser and pesticide use is very high.
- **Atal Bhujal Yojana (Atal Jal):** in 8,220 water-stressed Gram Panchayats of 229 blocks in 80 districts of seven States (Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh), as printed in the book; it aims at behaviour change from consumption to conservation.

## Rainwater harvesting

Catching and storing rain, also used to recharge aquifers. NCERT says it is low cost and eco-friendly; it increases availability, checks the falling water table, dilutes fluoride and nitrates, reduces erosion and flooding and, in coastal aquifers, arrests salt-water intrusion. Rajasthan's **kund or tanka** is a covered underground tank near the house or village. Traditional storage includes lakes, ponds and irrigation tanks.

## Schemes and policy in the chapter

- **PMKSY** (2015-16): protective irrigation for all farms, 'Har khet ko pani' and 'Per drop more crop'.
- **Jal Kranti Abhiyan** (2015-16): one water-stressed village in each of 672 districts developed as a Jal Gram (count as printed in the book; verify), model command areas and mass awareness.
- **National Water Policy 2012** (module 7).
- **Laws on pollution:** Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974, Water Cess Act 1977 and Environment Protection Act 1986. NCERT says they were not implemented effectively; the CPCB monitors water quality at 507 stations and names the Yamuna between Delhi and Etawah as the most polluted stretch.

## Groundwater: the PYQ link

Groundwater supplies are depleted by pumping for wheat and rice in the north-west (module 4), lack of recharge and pollution. Likely government steps for an answer: the schemes above plus watershed development, crop diversification and pricing (standard additions to verify).

## How to answer a benefit-cost question in 150 words

1. One-line definition or context. 2. Benefits (two or three, with a named project from module 5). 3. Costs (two or three, including one environmental and one social). 4. A way forward (watershed and rainwater harvesting, better management, safety audit). 5. A balancing closing line.

## Traps

Quoting a dam fact from memory with no source; confusing the Damodar (Bengal) with the Kosi (Bihar) as the sorrow rivers; treating 32 per cent as a correct NCERT figure; forgetting displacement in a benefit-cost answer; giving only government schemes without constraints.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Dam: a barrier across a river that holds water back; the lake behind it is the reservoir.
- Multipurpose river-valley project: a planned scheme of dams, canals and power stations meant to serve irrigation, electricity, flood control, water supply and navigation together.
- Catchment area: the area drained by a river and its tributaries; the dividing line between two basins is the watershed (NCERT also calls small drainage areas watersheds).
- Utilisable water: the part of the available water that can actually be put to beneficial use; NCERT gives 1,122 cubic km for India.
- Conjunctive use: using surface water and groundwater together to sustain supply (NCERT, Class XII).
- Watershed management: efficient management and conservation of surface and groundwater, in the broad sense of all natural and human resources in a watershed (NCERT).
- Command area: the land a canal or project can water (the term is used by NCERT in Jal Kranti Abhiyan; definition standard, verify).

### Notes: Benefit and cost chains

- Dam stores monsoon water -> canals carry it to fields in the dry season -> more irrigated area and multiple cropping -> higher yields (NCERT, Class XII).
- Dam holds back flood water -> lower river floods reduce (Leong) -> the Damodar valley was tamed by the corporation (NCERT).
- Reservoir fills -> land and villages submerge -> people need rehabilitation (standard; verify).
- Sediment settles in the reservoir -> storage space shrinks over time (standard; verify) and the river below carries less silt.
- Canal water without drainage or heavy irrigation -> soil salinity and waterlogging; NCERT's own question links intensive irrigation with salinity and groundwater depletion.
- Cheap well irrigation in Punjab and Haryana -> groundwater table falls (NCERT).
- Over-withdrawal -> fluoride rises (Rajasthan, Maharashtra) and arsenic rises (parts of West Bengal and Bihar).

### Notes: Source notes and slips (checked)

- legy204 pp. 1-2: 690 cubic km is called 32 per cent of 1,869; the correct ratio is 36.9 per cent (690 / 1,869).
- legy204 p. 1: the 1,869 figure is used both for 'surface water plus replenishable groundwater' and for the 'mean annual flow of river basins'. Because 690 + 432 = 1,122, the utilisable total can be built as surface plus groundwater; do not add 432 to 1,869. Standard reading (CWC; verify): 1,869 is the mean annual flow of the river basins (surface water), so the "plus groundwater" wording is the loose one; 0.60 x 1,869 = 1,121.4 is only close to 1,122, while 690 + 432 = 1,122 is exact.
- kegy103 p. 6: the Gandhisagar dam is described after 'upwards of Kota in Rajasthan'; standard geography places it in Madhya Pradesh, upstream of Kota (verify).
- legy204 p. 11 asks for the selection of one water-stressed village in each of 672 districts to create a Jal Gram (not a Jal Gram in every village); the district count may differ today (verify); this is a check, not a slip.
- kegy103 p. 8 and legy204 p. 10 give no figures for any inter-basin transfer; treat the schemes list as names only.
- Leong is a Nigerian-published world text; its dam examples (Akosombo, Shiroro) are African, so only its general reasoning is used.

### Core concepts

- A dam stores wet-season water; a multipurpose project uses it for irrigation, power, flood control, water supply and navigation.
- Agriculture uses most of India's water: 89 per cent of surface water and 92 per cent of groundwater.
- Wells and tubewells irrigate 76.1 per cent of the net irrigated area of Punjab and 51.3 per cent of Haryana, with falling water tables as the cost.
- Every large-dam answer needs benefits and costs, and costs include displacement, silt, ecology and disputes.
- Watershed management and rainwater harvesting are the small-scale partners of big dams.

### Practice

**Q.** Define a reservoir and a canal.

**A.** A reservoir is the lake behind a dam; a canal is a man-made channel that carries water to fields.

**Q.** Which sector uses most water in India?

**A.** Agriculture: 89 per cent of surface water and 92 per cent of groundwater.

**Q.** Name the river associated with Bhakra Nangal.

**A.** The Satluj.

**Q.** Name the sorrow river of Bengal.

**A.** The Damodar.

**Q.** Why can a dam both reduce floods and create disputes?

**A.** It stores flood water, protecting the plains below, but a shared river means upstream and downstream States compete for the stored water.

**Q.** Why does intensive well irrigation lower the water table?

**A.** Water is pumped out faster than rain and rivers recharge it, as in Punjab and Haryana.

**Q.** Why is watershed management called an integrated approach?

**A.** It manages land, water, plants, animals and people together within a drainage unit.

**Q.** A district gets canal water in the rainy season but its wells are falling. Suggest three measures from the lesson.

**A.** Percolation tanks and check dams, rainwater harvesting on rooftops and open spaces, and shifting from water-intensive crops, with community participation.

**Q.** Place on a map the four rivers with named projects and say which of them flows directly into the Arabian Sea through India.

**A.** Satluj, Damodar, Narmada and Chambal; the Narmada reaches the Arabian Sea directly (the Satluj reaches it only via the Indus system, through Pakistan).
