## GS-163: Fundamental Rights articles 12–35

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: What a 'right' is: moral, legal, constitutional and fundamental; negative and positive

A **right** is a claim that a person can make on others (especially on the State) and that those others are bound to respect. Four layers are easy to mix up.

- A **moral (or natural) right** is a claim said to belong to every human being simply by being human, whether or not any law records it. It has moral force, not legal force.
- A **legal right** is created and enforced by an ordinary law (an Act of Parliament). Parliament can change it by passing another ordinary law.
- A **constitutional right outside Part III** is written in the Constitution but is not a Fundamental Right. Laxmikanth's examples are Article 265 (no tax without law), Article 300A (no deprivation of property without law) and Article 301 (freedom of trade).
- A **Fundamental Right** is listed in Part III. NCERT stresses the two differences: it is protected by the Constitution itself, and it can be changed only by amending the Constitution.

Rights are also described as **negative** or **positive**. A negative right restricts the State: it says what the State must not do (Laxmikanth, feature 4). A positive right gives the person a privilege or benefit. NCERT's political-theory chapter on freedom uses the same pair for liberty: **negative liberty** is the area in which nobody may interfere with you (freedom from), while **positive liberty** is the set of conditions that let you develop your abilities (freedom to).

Rights and duties are linked but not tied together: NCERT notes that the Constitution does not make the enjoyment of rights conditional on performing duties (GS-166 has the details).

### Bridge: Against whom does a right work? The State, private persons, and vertical versus horizontal enforcement

The word **State** in Part III does not mean one of the 28 States of the Union. It means the whole apparatus of public power: governments, legislatures, local bodies and other public authorities (Article 12, module 2). A person (called the **petitioner** when going to court) can say, 'the State has violated my right'.

NCERT explains why this matters. A person's rights can be threatened by **another person or a private organisation**, and then the person needs the protection of the government. The organs of the government themselves (legislature, executive, bureaucracy, even the judiciary) can also violate rights in the course of their functioning. A constitution that lists rights therefore does two jobs: it stops the State from acting against the person, and it obliges the State to protect the person.

Two terms help in reading examination questions. A claim against the State is a **vertical** enforcement (individual versus State, one above the other). A claim against another private person or company is **horizontal** (side by side). Laxmikanth says most Fundamental Rights are available only against arbitrary action of the State, but some are also available against private individuals: he specifically names Article 15(2) (access to shops, wells and so on), Article 17 (untouchability) and Article 23 (traffic in human beings and forced labour). Article 24 (child labour) is also widely taught as binding private employers (standard knowledge, not in the Laxmikanth extract; verify, e.g. PUDR 1982). Where a question asks 'against whom', the safe default is the State, and the exceptions must be remembered individually.

A **legal person** is an entity such as a company or a registered society that the law treats as if it were a person. It can hold some rights (for example Article 14) but not others (Article 19, module 5).

### Bridge: Reading Article numbers: Part III, Articles 12-35, clauses, sub-clauses and the 'A' articles

The Constitution is organised into **Parts** (Part III is the Fundamental Rights), each containing **Articles**. Part III runs from Article 12 to Article 35. Articles carry their own sub-divisions: **clauses** are numbered in brackets, (1), (2), and **sub-clauses** are lettered, (a), (b). So **Article 19(1)(a)** means Article 19, clause (1), sub-clause (a): the freedom of speech and expression. In a question, '19(1)(g)' is therefore a precise address, not a different Article.

New Articles inserted later receive the number of the Article before them plus a capital letter, so that existing numbers do not change. **Article 21A** (education) was inserted after Article 21 by the 86th Amendment; **31A, 31B and 31C** were inserted after Article 31 by amendments. Removing an Article does not renumber: Article 31 was repealed in 1978 but the number is simply empty; Laxmikanth's table marks 31, 31D and 32A as repealed. Sub-clauses can also be repealed: Article 19(1)(f) (the right to acquire property) was deleted in 1978, which is why Article 19 now lists six freedoms but the letters run (a) to (e) and (g).

When you meet a list-matching question, learn the Article numbers as a number line: 12-13 general; 14-18 equality; 19-22 freedom (with 21A); 23-24 exploitation; 25-28 religion; 29-30 cultural and educational; 31 repealed (31A-C saving clauses); 32 remedies; 33-35 supplementary.

### 1. Orientation: what Part III is, why it exists, and its six groups

*Objective:* State what Part III contains, why a Constitution lists rights, what 'fundamental' means, and name the six groups with their Article ranges.

## What Part III is

The Constitution is divided into **Parts**; **Part III** (Articles 12 to 35) lists the **Fundamental Rights (FRs)**: basic claims of every person against the State that the Constitution itself guarantees and that courts can enforce. Laxmikanth calls it the Magna Carta of India (the Magna Carta was a charter of rights that English barons forced King John to issue in 1215). The Indian framers took inspiration from the American Bill of Rights, but the Indian list is more elaborate than that of any other country, Laxmikanth says. NCERT calls such a list a **bill of rights**; the Motilal Nehru Committee had demanded one as early as 1928, and the Constituent Assembly had no two opinions on including it.

## Why a Constitution lists rights at all

NCERT gives two true stories. Workers building the 1982 Asian Games stadiums were paid less than the minimum wage; social scientists petitioned the Supreme Court, which treated this as forced labour (the right against exploitation) and ordered the wages paid. Machal Lalung, found unfit to stand trial, remained in custody for 54 years without a trial and was released at 77. His right to life and liberty (and so to a fair, speedy trial) existed on paper only. The lesson is that a right needs both a **guarantee** (the list) and a **remedy** (Article 32, module 8).

Laxmikanth adds the purpose: FRs promote political democracy, protect liberty against the invasion of the State, and aim at 'a government of laws and not of men'. They limit the tyranny of the executive and the arbitrary laws of the legislature.

## Why 'fundamental'

Ordinary legal rights are made and changed by ordinary law. A fundamental right is guaranteed by the Constitution, so only a constitutional amendment can change it, and no organ of government may violate it (NCERT). It is also fundamental because it is essential for all-round development (material, intellectual, moral, spiritual) of the individual (Laxmikanth).

## The six groups (originally seven)

| Group | Articles |
|---|---|
| Right to equality | 14-18 |
| Right to freedom | 19-22 (including 21A) |
| Right against exploitation | 23-24 |
| Right to freedom of religion | 25-28 |
| Cultural and educational rights | 29-30 |
| Right to constitutional remedies | 32 |

The original seventh was the **right to property** (Article 31). The 44th Amendment (1978) removed it from the list and made it a legal right under **Article 300A**, so there are six FRs today. Around the six groups sit Articles 12-13 (definitions and the law-testing clause), 31A-31C (saving clauses) and 33-35 (supplementary).

## First-pass features

- Some FRs are for citizens only, others for all persons (module 9 table).
- They are not absolute: the State may impose **reasonable restrictions**, and the courts decide whether a restriction is reasonable.
- They work mainly against the State; a few also bind private persons (bridge B2).
- They are justiciable and the aggrieved person may go straight to the Supreme Court (Article 32).
- Parliament can amend them, but not so as to destroy the basic structure (GS-167, GS-168).
- They can be suspended during a National Emergency, except Articles 20 and 21 (module 9).

The full list of twelve features is in Master notes ('Part III at a glance: features').

### 2. Article 12 ('State') and Article 13 (laws inconsistent with rights)

*Objective:* Explain who counts as 'State', what 'law' means in Article 13, and how void, severability, eclipse and waiver work.

## Article 12: who is 'the State'

Most FRs are claims against the **State** (bridge B2). Article 12 gives a working definition for Part III. Laxmikanth lists four groups:

- the Government and Parliament of India (Union executive and legislature);
- the Government and legislature of each State;
- all **local authorities** (municipalities, panchayats, district boards, improvement trusts);
- all **other authorities**, statutory or non-statutory, such as LIC, ONGC and SAIL.

Because the definition is wide, the acts of any of these bodies can be challenged as violating a right. The Supreme Court has also held that even a private body working as an **instrument of the State** falls inside Article 12. [Standard knowledge, verify: the Court's tests for an 'instrumentality' (deep State control, monopoly status, public function, financial support), for example *Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib* (1981); whether courts acting judicially are 'State' is debated.]

## Article 13: the law-testing clause

Article 13 declares that every law **inconsistent with or in derogation of** a Fundamental Right is **void**. This is the text from which Indian courts test laws: the Supreme Court (Article 32) and the High Courts (Article 226) can declare a law unconstitutional (Laxmikanth; GS-186 module 7 for judicial review). By clause (standard knowledge; verify the exact words in the official text):

1. **13(1)** pre-constitutional laws (in force before 26 January 1950) are void **to the extent** of inconsistency with Part III;
2. **13(2)** the State shall not make a law that takes away or abridges the rights; a law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contravention;
3. **13(3)** defines 'law' and 'laws in force';
4. **13(4)**, inserted by the 24th Amendment (1971), says Article 13 does not apply to an amendment under Article 368 [verify clause number; GS-167 module 6].

**'Law' is wide** (Laxmikanth): (a) permanent laws of Parliament and State legislatures; (b) temporary laws such as **ordinances** of the President or Governors; (c) delegated legislation: order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification; (d) non-legislative sources: **custom or usage having the force of law**. Any of these can be challenged. (PYQ 2026 Q54 tests exactly this list.)

## Four working doctrines (standard knowledge; verify)

- **Severability**: because a law is void only 'to the extent' of the conflict, a court strikes down the offending part and keeps the rest if the rest can stand alone.
- **Eclipse**: a pre-constitutional law that conflicts with a right is not erased; the right 'eclipses' (covers) it. If the conflict later disappears (the right is narrowed by amendment), the law revives (*Bhikaji Narain Dhakras*, 1955). A post-constitutional law that violates a right is generally treated as void from the start.
- **Prospective effect of 13(1)**: pre-1950 laws become void from 26 January 1950, not retrospectively; past acts remain valid.
- **No waiver**: a person cannot sign away a Fundamental Right (*Basheshar Nath*, 1959), because the rights protect public policy and not private convenience.

## Article 13 and amendments

Article 13 says a constitutional amendment is not a 'law'. Yet in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) the Court held that an amendment can be struck down if it violates a right that forms part of the **basic structure** (Laxmikanth; NCERT). The whole storyline, from Shankari Prasad to Coelho, is in GS-167 module 6, GS-168 and GS-173 modules 2-4: do not re-learn it here.

### 3. Right to equality, part 1: Article 14

*Objective:* Distinguish equality before law from equal protection of laws, apply the reasonable-classification test, and list the exceptions to equality.

## The text and who holds it

**Article 14**: the State shall not deny to **any person** equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India. It is a right of all persons, citizens and foreigners alike, and 'person' includes legal persons such as statutory corporations, companies and registered societies (Laxmikanth). Enemy aliens are outside it in Laxmikanth's Table 7.2.

## Two phrases, two origins

| Phrase | Origin | Meaning | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equality before law | British | No special privileges; all subject to the ordinary law and ordinary courts; nobody is above the law | Negative |
| Equal protection of laws | American | Equal treatment in equal circumstances; like cases treated alike | Positive |

Both aim at equality of legal status, opportunity and justice.

## Reasonable classification

Equality does not mean identical treatment of everyone. NCERT's political-theory chapter makes the point from ordinary life: separate queues for the old and the disabled are accepted as just, while jumping the queue is not; equality means equal opportunity, with special treatment justified in some situations. The Supreme Court, as Laxmikanth reports, says Article 14 **forbids class legislation but permits reasonable classification** of persons, objects and transactions, provided the classification is not arbitrary, artificial or evasive and rests on an **intelligible differential and substantial distinction**. Standard knowledge (verify): the test is usually stated in two parts: (1) an intelligible differentia separating those grouped from those left out; (2) a rational nexus between that differentia and the object of the law.

**Guided example.** A tax law charges higher rates to higher incomes. Differentia: income level. Object: raise revenue fairly. Nexus: yes. Valid. Now a law says the Collector may cancel any licence 'if he thinks fit', with no guidance. There is no intelligible differentia and no standard: it gives unguided, uncontrolled discretion, and is open to challenge under Article 14 (this is PYQ 2021 Q85's concept). The standard knowledge (verify) link: since *E.P. Royappa* (1974) the Court treats **arbitrariness** as the opposite of equality, and *Maneka Gandhi* applies it (GS-173 module 5).

## Rule of law

Equality before law is an element of the **Rule of Law**, Dicey's idea. Its three elements: (i) absence of arbitrary power (nobody punished except for a breach of law); (ii) equality before the law; (iii) the primacy of individual rights as defined by courts rather than the Constitution being their source. The first two apply to India; the third does not, because here the **Constitution is the source** of individual rights. The Supreme Court has held that the Rule of Law in Article 14 is a **basic feature**, so even an amendment cannot destroy it (Laxmikanth; GS-168).

## Exceptions to equality (Laxmikanth)

1. President and Governors: immunities under Article 361 (not answerable to any court for official acts; no criminal proceedings, no arrest during the term; civil proceedings only after two months' notice).
2. Article 361-A: no liability for a substantially true newspaper, radio or television report of proceedings of either House of Parliament or State Legislature.
3. Members of Parliament and State legislatures: no liability for anything said or any vote given in the House or committee (Articles 105 and 194).
4. **Article 31C** (surviving part): laws implementing Article 39(b) or (c) cannot be challenged under Article 14; 'where Article 31-C comes in, Article 14 goes out' (module 9).
5. Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors and diplomats: immunity from criminal and civil proceedings.
6. The United Nations and its agencies: diplomatic immunity.

PYQ 2018 Q45 (features of Rule of Law) and 2021 Q85 (unguided discretion) both draw on this module; PYQ 2021 Mains Q13 (equality in the US and India) is discussed in module 10.

### 4. Right to equality, part 2: Articles 15, 16, 17 and 18

*Objective:* State each of Articles 15-18, the exceptions to Articles 15 and 16 with the amendment that added each, and the outline of the reservation debate.

## Article 15: no discrimination (citizens)

Article 15(1): the State shall not discriminate against any **citizen** on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Two words matter: 'discrimination' (an adverse distinction) and 'only' (other grounds are not barred). Article 15(2): no citizen shall face a disability on those grounds in access to **shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment**, or in the use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats and roads maintained by State funds or dedicated to the public. Clause (2) binds **private persons as well as the State**; clause (1) binds only the State.

**Exceptions** (Laxmikanth counts four): (a) 15(3) special provision for **women and children**; (b) 15(4) advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs (added by the **1st Amendment, 1951**); (c) 15(5) the same groups' admission to educational institutions, including private aided and unaided ones but not minority institutions (**93rd Amendment, 2005**; the 27% OBC quota Act of 2006 followed, upheld by the Supreme Court in 2008 with the 'creamy layer' excluded); (d) 15(6) up to **10% for economically weaker sections (EWS)** in addition to existing reservations (**103rd Amendment, 2019**). [Dated flags: Laxmikanth's creamy-layer ceiling (₹8 lakh, 2017) and EWS tests (2019) need a current check; a 2022 Supreme Court verdict on the EWS quota is standard knowledge: verify.]

## Article 16: equality in public employment (citizens)

Article 16 guarantees **equality of opportunity** in employment or appointment to any office under the State; no citizen can be ineligible on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, **descent**, place of birth or **residence**. Exceptions (clause numbers standard knowledge; verify): (a) **Parliament** may prescribe residence as a requirement (16(3)); the 1957 Act lapsed in 1974, so only Andhra Pradesh and Telangana have such a rule (Article 371D); (b) reservation for backward classes not adequately represented (16(4)); (c) office-holders of a religious institution may be required to belong to that religion (16(5)); (d) up to 10% EWS posts (16(6), 103rd Amendment). Two later additions answer parts of *Indra Sawhney*: **16(4A)** reservation in promotions for SC/ST (77th Amendment, 1995; 85th Amendment, 2001, gave 'consequential seniority') and **16(4B)** carry-forward of unfilled vacancies outside the 50% ceiling (81st Amendment, 2000).

The reservation debate in outline (chronology in Master notes; the case itself in GS-173 module 9): the Mandal Commission (1979-80) recommended 27% for OBCs, implemented in 1990; *Indra Sawhney* (1992) upheld it with conditions: exclude the creamy layer, no reservation in promotions (later nullified by the 77th Amendment), total normally within 50%. Article 16(4) is read with **Article 335** (efficiency of administration), which does not define 'efficiency' (PYQ 2023 Q40).

## Article 17: untouchability

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form; enforcing a disability arising from it is a punishable offence. The **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** (the 1955 Untouchability (Offences) Act, renamed and strengthened in 1976) implements it. 'Untouchability' is not defined; a High Court held that it means the historical practice of social disabilities by reason of birth in certain castes, so a social boycott of a few individuals is not covered. The Supreme Court has held the right is available **against private individuals** and the State must act to protect it. (PYQ 2020 Q4 asks in which category this right sits: equality.)

## Article 18: titles

Article 18 abolishes titles in four rules: (1) the State shall not confer any title except a military or academic distinction; (2) a citizen shall not accept a title from a foreign State; (3) a non-citizen holding an office of profit or trust needs the President's consent for a foreign title; (4) no person, citizen or foreigner, holding an office of profit or trust under the State may accept any present, emolument or office from or under a foreign State without the President's consent. Hereditary titles such as Maharaja and Rai Bahadur were banned. In **1996** (*Balaji Raghavan*) the Supreme Court upheld the National Awards (Bharat Ratna and the three Padma awards): they are not hereditary 'titles' and must not be used as prefix or suffix. Instituted in 1954, they were discontinued in 1977 and revived in 1980.

### 5. Right to freedom, part 1: Articles 19 and 20

*Objective:* List the six freedoms of Article 19 with what each includes, the limits on movement and residence, and the three protections of Article 20.

## What freedom means

NCERT's political-theory chapter defines freedom as both the absence of external constraints and the presence of conditions that let a person develop; it adds that no society has total absence of constraint, and that justifiable constraints must be reasonable: capable of being defended by reason and not out of proportion. That is the idea behind 'reasonable restrictions' in the Constitution. Behind it lies the **harm principle** (J.S. Mill): the State may restrict a person's liberty mainly to prevent serious harm to others.

## Article 19: six freedoms (citizens, against the State)

1. **Speech and expression**: (own views by word, writing, printing, picture). Laxmikanth lists what the Court has read in: press freedom, commercial advertisements, right against telephone tapping, right to telecast, right against a bundh called by a party, right to know about government activities, freedom of silence, right against pre-censorship, right to demonstration or picketing but **not a right to strike**.
2. **Assembly**: peaceably and without arms; only on public land; riotous or armed assemblies are not protected; again, not a right to strike. [Laxmikanth cites Section 144 CrPC and Section 141 IPC; those codes were replaced in 2024 by the BNSS and BNS: check current section numbers.]
3. **Association**: associations, unions, co-operative societies (the last added by the **97th Amendment, 2011**); includes the negative right not to join; the right to **recognition** is not a Fundamental Right; trade unions have no guaranteed right to strike or lock-out.
4. **Movement** throughout India.
5. **Residence and settlement** anywhere in India.
6. **Profession, occupation, trade or business**: the State may prescribe qualifications and may itself run a trade or industry, even as a monopoly; immoral or dangerous trades (trafficking, harmful drugs, explosives) may be banned or licensed.

Originally there were seven freedoms; the right to acquire, hold and dispose of property (19(1)(f)) was deleted by the **44th Amendment (1978)**. Article 19 is protected **only against State action**, and belongs to **citizens** (and shareholders, but not to foreigners or companies as such).

**Limits.** The State may impose only **reasonable restrictions**, **only on the grounds listed in Article 19 itself**; courts decide reasonableness. The grounds differ freedom by freedom and are studied in GS-164 (Day 7; not yet written): do not guess them. Two are needed here for PYQ 2022 Q2: movement and residence may be restricted in the **interests of the general public** and for the **protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe** (to protect tribal culture, language and property from outsiders). Courts have upheld bans on the movement of some persons on health or morality grounds. Movement has an **internal** dimension (inside India: Article 19) and an **external** dimension (leaving and returning: Article 21, as Laxmikanth records).

## Article 20: three protections for the accused (all persons)

Available to citizens, foreigners and legal persons, and **not suspended** in an Emergency.

1. **No ex-post-facto criminal law**: no conviction for an act that was not an offence when done, and no penalty greater than the law then in force prescribed. It bars retroactive **criminal** laws only (civil liability or tax can be retrospective); it bars conviction or sentence, not trial.
2. **No double jeopardy**: no one is prosecuted and punished twice for the same offence, but only in proceedings before a court or judicial tribunal (not departmental inquiries).
3. **No self-incrimination**: no accused is compelled to be a witness against himself. It covers oral and documentary evidence but not compulsory production of material objects, thumb impression, specimen signature or blood sample, and covers criminal proceedings only. [Later Supreme Court rulings on scientific tests: standard knowledge, verify.]

### 6. Right to freedom, part 2: Articles 21, 21A and 22

*Objective:* State Article 21 and how its meaning widened, Article 21A with its history, and the two parts of Article 22 including preventive detention.

## Article 21: life and personal liberty

Article 21: **no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law**. It is available to citizens and non-citizens (all persons). The move from a narrow reading (*Gopalan*, 1950: any procedure made by a valid law sufficed) to *Maneka Gandhi* (1978: the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, so protection also operates against arbitrary legislation) is told in **GS-173 module 5**. This module adds the **list and the pattern**.

Pattern (Laxmikanth, NCERT): after *Maneka*, 'life' means more than animal existence: it includes the right to live with human dignity, and 'personal liberty' is of the widest amplitude. Laxmikanth lists 35 rights declared part of Article 21, among them dignity, decent environment, livelihood, privacy, shelter, health, free legal aid, speedy trial, fair trial, travel abroad and information (full list in Master notes; case names in GS-173 module 6). NCERT stresses a speedy trial (Machal Lalung) and shelter and livelihood (no one can live without the means of living).

- **Privacy** (PYQ 2018 Q92, 2021 Q79, 2024 Q76; Mains 2017, 2024, 2026): *Puttaswamy* (2017) held it intrinsic to Article 21 and to the freedoms in Part III: see GS-173 module 6. There is no separate Article for privacy.
- **Choosing a spouse** (PYQ 2019 Q56): the Supreme Court has read the right to marry a person of one's choice into Article 21 (standard knowledge; verify, for example *Shafin Jahan v Asokan K.M.*, 2018). It is not in Laxmikanth's list of 35.

## Article 21A: education (children 6-14)

Article 21A: the State shall provide **free and compulsory education to all children of six to fourteen years**, in a manner the State decides. It makes **elementary education** (not higher or professional) a Fundamental Right. It was added by the **86th Amendment (2002)**, which also reworded **Article 45** (early childhood care until age six) and added a parental duty in **Article 51A** (see GS-165 and GS-166). The Supreme Court had already, in 1993, recognised free education until age 14 inside Article 21 (partly overruling Mohini Jain, 1992, which had covered education at any level; standard knowledge, verify). Parliament passed the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009**, in force from 1 April 2010.

## Article 22: arrest and detention

**Detention** is of two types. **Punitive** detention punishes after trial and conviction. **Preventive** detention is without trial and conviction, only to prevent a future offence; it is a precaution based on suspicion. Article 22 has two parts.

**Part 1: ordinary arrest** (citizens and aliens, but **not enemy aliens or persons under preventive detention**): (i) be told the grounds of arrest; (ii) consult and be defended by a lawyer; (iii) be produced before a magistrate within **24 hours, excluding the time needed for the journey** from the place of arrest to the magistrate's court (Article 22(2); the Laxmikanth extract prints 'including the journey time', which is a source slip: learn 'excluding'); (iv) release after 24 hours unless the magistrate authorises more. They do not cover arrest under a court order, civil arrest or deportation.

**Part 2: preventive detention** (citizens and aliens): detention beyond **three months** needs the report of an **advisory board** of High Court judges; the grounds must be communicated (facts against public interest need not be disclosed); the detenu may make a representation. Parliament may prescribe cases, maximum period and board procedure. The **44th Amendment (1978)** would reduce three months to two, but Laxmikanth says it has not been brought into force, so three months continues [verify current status].

**Who may legislate**: Parliament alone for reasons of defence, foreign affairs and security of India; Parliament and State legislatures both for security of a State, public order and essential supplies and services. Preventive detention laws still listed by Laxmikanth include COFEPOSA 1974, the National Security Act 1980 (misprinted 'NASA') and UAPA (amended up to 2019); the full list is in Master notes. No other democracy places preventive detention in its Constitution (Laxmikanth); NCERT notes its tension with Article 21.

PYQ 2023 Prelims Q77 uses this module (legal counsel in preventive detention; Article 355; POTA).

### 7. Rights against exploitation, of religion, and cultural and educational: Articles 23-30

*Objective:* State what Articles 23 to 30 guarantee, to whom, and the limits and tests attached to each.

## Right against exploitation: Articles 23-24

**Article 23** prohibits **traffic in human beings, begar (compulsory work without pay) and other similar forced labour**; contravention is a punishable offence. It is available to citizens and non-citizens and protects against the State **and private persons**. 'Traffic' includes buying and selling of men, women and children, immoral trafficking, devadasis and slavery (Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956). 'Force' includes economic compulsion such as working for less than the minimum wage (the Asiad workers' case in NCERT; statutes include the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 and the Minimum Wages Act, 1948). **Exception**: the State may impose compulsory service for public purposes (military or social service) without pay, but not on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class.

**Article 24** prohibits employment of children **below 14 years** in any factory, mine or other hazardous work (construction, railway); harmless work is not covered. Laxmikanth lists the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, renamed in 2016 as the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act; a 2006 ban covered children as domestic workers and in shops, hotels and dhabas. (PYQ 2017 Q97 groups Articles 23-24; the Act's present scope: verify.) Laxmikanth names only Articles 15(2), 17 and 23 as binding private persons; Article 24 is also widely taught as binding private employers (standard knowledge; verify, e.g. PUDR 1982).

## Right to freedom of religion: Articles 25-28

All four are for **persons**. **Article 25**: freedom of conscience and the right to **profess** (declare), **practise** (worship and rites) and **propagate** (spread) religion; propagation does not include a right to convert another person, and forcible conversion impinges on the freedom of conscience. It is subject to public order, morality, health and the other FR provisions, and the State may regulate secular activity associated with religion, provide for social welfare and reform, and open Hindu institutions of a public character to all Hindus. Explanations: wearing the **kirpan** is part of the Sikh profession; 'Hindus' includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists.

**Article 26** gives **religious denominations** (not individuals) four rights: set up and maintain institutions; manage their own affairs in religion; own and acquire property; administer property according to law. Subject to public order, morality and health, but **not** to the other FR provisions. A denomination needs (a) a system of beliefs, (b) a common organisation and (c) a distinctive name: Ramakrishna Mission and Ananda Marga qualify; Aurobindo Society does not.

**Article 27**: no person can be compelled to pay a **tax** whose proceeds promote or maintain a particular religion; a **fee** (for a service such as pilgrim safety) is allowed. NCERT's chart words this as 'freedom to pay taxes', a slip: it is freedom **from** compulsion.

**Article 28** distinguishes four kinds of institution: (i) wholly State-maintained (no religious instruction); (ii) State-administered but established under an endowment or trust (instruction allowed); (iii) State-recognised (instruction only voluntarily); (iv) State-aided (instruction only voluntarily). In (iii) and (iv) a minor needs the guardian's consent.

## Cultural and educational rights: Articles 29-30

**Article 29(1)**: any section of **citizens** with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it. **29(2)**: no citizen may be denied admission to a State or State-aided institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language. The first protects a group, the second an individual. The Supreme Court held that Article 29 is not confined to minorities, since 'section of citizens' includes the majority.

**Article 30(1)**: all **minorities, religious or linguistic**, may establish and administer educational institutions of their choice; **30(1A)** (44th Amendment) says compensation for compulsory acquisition of such an institution's property must not restrict the right; the State must not discriminate in aid. 'Minority' is not defined in the Constitution. Laxmikanth's table places Article 30 with citizens, but the Article speaks of 'minorities' [verify]. Under *Malankara Syrian Catholic College* (2007): the right includes choosing the governing body, appointing staff, admitting students and setting a reasonable fee; it is for equality with the majority, not reverse discrimination; regulation for educational standards is allowed; general laws apply; aid does not alter the minority character.

### 8. Right to constitutional remedies: Article 32, the five writs, and Articles 33-35

*Objective:* Explain Article 32 and its four clauses, define each of the five writs, and state what Articles 33, 34 and 35 do.

## Article 32: the remedy that makes the rights real

A list of rights is meaningless without enforcement (module 1). **Article 32** makes the **right to move the Supreme Court** for enforcement of FRs itself a Fundamental Right. NCERT records Dr Ambedkar's judgment that it is the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution; Laxmikanth adds that the Court has held Article 32 a **basic feature**, so no amendment can remove it. Four clauses:

1. the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings is guaranteed;
2. the Court may issue directions, orders or **writs** for enforcement of any FR: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto;
3. Parliament may empower other courts to issue them (without prejudice to the Supreme Court); this does not mean High Courts, which have Article 226;
4. the right to move the Supreme Court shall not be suspended except as the Constitution provides, so the President can suspend it during a National Emergency (Article 359; module 9).

The jurisdiction is **original** (directly, not by appeal) and **wide** (writs of all kinds), but **not exclusive**: High Courts have concurrent power under **Article 226** (compared in GS-187 module 4 and GS-186 module 5: the High Court issues writs for FRs and any other legal purpose, within its territory or where the cause of action arises; Article 226 is discretionary while Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right). **A violation of a Fundamental Right is the precondition**: Article 32 cannot be used merely to test an executive order or a law that does not infringe an FR. Alternative remedy is no bar, but the Court says the High Court should be approached first where relief is available there.

## The five writs

A 'writ' is a formal written court order; these are **prerogative writs** from English law, issued in the King's name.

- **Habeas corpus** ('have the body'): orders the detainer to produce the detained person and show the cause; frees the person if detention is illegal. Issued against public authorities **and** private persons; not issued where detention is lawful, by a competent court, or for contempt.
- **Mandamus** ('we command'): orders a public official, public body, corporation, inferior court or tribunal to perform a public duty. Laxmikanth: not against a private body, non-statutory departmental instructions, discretionary duties, contractual obligations, or the President or Governors. [Standard knowledge, verify: courts have issued it to a private body entrusted with a public duty; PYQ 2022 Q18 depends on that nuance.]
- **Prohibition** ('to forbid'): a higher court orders a lower court or tribunal to stop exceeding its jurisdiction; **preventive**, directing inactivity. Only against judicial and quasi-judicial authorities.
- **Certiorari** ('to be certified'): quashes an order already passed (or transfers a pending case) for excess or lack of jurisdiction or error of law; **preventive and curative**. Since 1991 it can issue against administrative authorities affecting rights.
- **Quo warranto** ('by what authority'): tests a person's claim to a **substantive public office** of a permanent nature created by statute or the Constitution; it can be sought by **any interested person**, not only the aggrieved.

(Comparison table: Master notes, 'The five writs'.)

## Articles 33-35: supplementary provisions

**Article 33**: **Parliament alone** may restrict or abrogate FRs for members of the armed forces, para-military forces, police, intelligence agencies and analogous forces, for discipline; such a law cannot be challenged on FR grounds (Army Act, Navy Act, Air Force Act, Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act 1966). It covers non-combatant staff such as cooks and barbers and may exclude courts martial from writ jurisdiction. **Article 34**: Parliament may restrict FRs and indemnify any person for acts done to maintain or restore order in an area under **martial law** (military rule; not defined in the Constitution; implicit in Article 34). **Article 35**: only Parliament may make the laws that give effect to certain FRs (residence rules under Article 16, powers of other courts under Article 32, Articles 33-34) and prescribe punishment for untouchability (Article 17) and forced labour and trafficking (Article 23), ensuring national uniformity.

### 9. Property history, saving clauses, whom the rights protect, Emergency, and criticism

*Objective:* Narrate the right-to-property history with Articles 31A, 31B and 31C, state who holds each right and how Emergency affects them, and summarise the criticism of Part III.

## The right to property: history

Originally two Articles protected property: **Article 19(1)(f)** (citizens' right to acquire, hold and dispose of property) and **Article 31** (any person protected against deprivation except by authority of law, acquisition only for a public purpose with compensation). It became the most controversial FR, causing conflict between Parliament and the Supreme Court and prompting the 1st, 4th, 7th, 25th, 39th, 40th and 42nd Amendments (Laxmikanth). NCERT explains why: land-reform laws abolishing the zamindari system were challenged as violating this right.

The **44th Amendment (1978)** repealed Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31 and inserted **Article 300A** in Part XII: no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law. It is now a **constitutional or legal right**, not an FR. Laxmikanth's consequences: (a) Parliament can regulate it by ordinary law; (b) it protects against executive action but not legislative action; (c) a violation cannot be taken directly to the Supreme Court under Article 32 (the High Court under Article 226 is available); (d) no guaranteed compensation. Two **compensation guarantees** remain inside Part III: minority institutions (Article 30(1A)) and land under personal cultivation within the ceiling (Article 31A, market value).

## Saving clauses

- **Article 31A** (1st Amendment, later changed by the 4th, 17th and 44th): saves five types of law from challenge under **Articles 14 and 19**: acquisition of estates; taking over management of property; amalgamation of corporations; changing rights of directors or shareholders; changing mining leases. A State law gets this protection only if reserved for and assented to by the President.
- **Article 31B** and the **Ninth Schedule** (1st Amendment, 1951): laws placed in the Schedule are immune from challenge under **any** FR, wider than 31A. In *I.R. Coelho* (2007) the Court held there is no blanket immunity: laws placed after **24 April 1973** can be challenged for violating Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 or the basic structure. The Schedule began with 13 enactments and had 282 in 2016 (Laxmikanth's count; check current).
- **Article 31C** (25th Amendment, 1971): laws implementing the Directives in **Article 39(b) or (c)** cannot be challenged under Articles 14 or 19. The second part (a declaration shields the law from court) was struck down in *Kesavananda* (1973); the 42nd Amendment's extension to all Directives was struck down in *Minerva Mills* (1980). Details: GS-165 module 6 (including a later ruling on Article 31C, to verify).

## Who holds each right, and against whom

The table 'Part III at a glance: who holds each right' in Master notes gives the full article-by-article picture. In short: **citizens only**: Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and (per Laxmikanth) 30; **all persons**: Articles 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25-28; **against private persons too**: Articles 15(2), 17, 23. [Standard knowledge, verify: *Kaushal Kishor* (2023), a five-judge bench, is reported to hold that Articles 19 and 21 can in some cases be enforced against non-State actors.]

## Emergency, martial law and the armed forces

All FRs can be suspended in a **National Emergency** except **Articles 20 and 21**; Article 19 can be suspended only on the ground of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion; the President may also suspend the right to move courts (Article 359). Details are in **GS-170** (module 5 for Articles 358-359; Article 355 in module 1). Martial law (Article 34) differs from a National Emergency: Master notes.

## Rights outside Part III and criticism

Article 265 (no tax without law), Article 300A and Article 301 (free trade) are justiciable constitutional rights, but the remedy is an ordinary suit in the civil courts or a writ petition under Article 226 in the High Court, not Article 32. The eight lines of criticism (Laxmikanth) are in Master notes: excessive limitations, no social or economic rights, vagueness, no permanency, suspension in Emergency, expensive remedy, preventive detention, no consistent philosophy. Answer a Mains 'critically examine' with both lists (criticism and significance).

### 10. Examination application: Prelims traps, a worked question, and the Mains frame

## How Part III is asked in Prelims

The verified questions fall into six patterns (full notes in the PYQ analysis):

1. **Definition questions**: what is a right (2017 Q8, Q47, Q86); right versus duty; fundamental versus constitutional versus legal right.
2. **'Which Article?'**: privacy (Articles 21: 2018, 2021, 2024), marriage of choice (2019), unguided discretion (Article 14: 2021), titles (Article 18: 2021).

5. **Amendments**: which amendment overcame judicial interpretations of FRs (2023 Q34).
6. **Rule of Law and equality** (2018 Q45).

**Eight traps to expect**:
- A citizens-only right dressed as available to all persons (Articles 15, 16, 19, 29).
- The word 'only' in Article 15, and 'descent' and 'residence' in Article 16 but not Article 15.
- Swapping Article 25 (individual) with Article 26 (denomination), or a **tax** with a **fee** in Article 27.
- Writs: prohibition and certiorari are issued to lower bodies; mandamus is not issued to a private person who owes no public duty; quo warranto may be sought by any interested person.
- The right to property: not an FR since 1978 (Article 300A), but Articles 31A-31C still exist.
- 'Article 21A is for all children up to 14': it is for ages six to fourteen.
- Statement 'all FRs are suspended in an Emergency' (Articles 20 and 21 are not).
- A claim that a private person cannot be bound by any FR (Articles 15(2), 17, 23 do bind).

## Mains frame: equality in the United States and India (PYQ 2021 Q13)

Analyse the 'distinguishing features' (not just the text). A skeleton: (1) India combines British 'equality before law' and American 'equal protection' (module 3). (2) India's text names specific grounds and special provisions (Articles 15(3)-(6), 16(4)-(6)), so affirmative action is written into the Constitution; in the United States equal protection is a general clause developed by courts (standard knowledge; verify recent developments). (3) India abolishes untouchability (Article 17) and titles (Article 18), and binds private persons in Articles 15(2) and 17. ### Notes: Core definitions

- Fundamental Right (FR): a right listed in Part III (Articles 12-35), guaranteed and protected by the Constitution, justiciable, and changeable only by a constitutional amendment (and never so as to damage the basic structure).
- Justiciable: capable of being enforced by a court. FRs are justiciable; the Directive Principles are not (GS-165).
- State (Article 12): the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and legislature of each State, all local authorities, and all other authorities (statutory or non-statutory, for example LIC, ONGC, SAIL); the Supreme Court has also brought in private bodies acting as instruments of the State.
- Law (Article 13): permanent laws of Parliament and legislatures, ordinances, delegated legislation (order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification) and custom or usage having the force of law.
- Legal person: a company, corporation or registered society treated by law as a person; it can claim Article 14 but not Article 19.
- Reasonable restriction: a limit on a right that can be defended by reason and is not excessive; whether it is reasonable is decided by the courts.
- Rule of Law (Dicey): absence of arbitrary power, equality before law, and the primacy of individual rights; in India the first two apply and the Constitution is the source of rights.
- Ex-post-facto law: a law that imposes penalties retrospectively on acts already done or increases penalties for them.
- Double jeopardy: being prosecuted and punished more than once for the same offence.
- Self-incrimination: being compelled to be a witness against oneself as an accused.
- Begar: compulsory work without remuneration; forced labour: compelling a person to work against will, including by economic compulsion.
- Preventive detention: detention without trial and conviction, to prevent a future offence; punitive detention follows conviction.
- Detenu: a person held under a preventive detention law.
- Writ: a formal written order of a court; the five writs are habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.
- Martial law: military rule over an area in extraordinary circumstances; not defined in the Constitution, implicit in Article 34.
- Religious denomination (Article 26): a group with a system of beliefs, a common organisation and a distinctive name.
- Minority: not defined in the Constitution; Articles 29 and 30 protect religious and linguistic minorities (Article 29 not exclusively).

### Notes: Part III at a glance: features, significance and criticism (Laxmikanth)

- Feature 1: some FRs are for citizens only, others for all persons (citizens, foreigners and legal persons).
- Feature 2: not absolute but qualified; the State can impose reasonable restrictions and courts decide reasonableness.
- Feature 3: all are available against arbitrary State action; some also against private individuals.
- Feature 4: some are negative (limits on the State), others positive (privileges conferred).
- Feature 5: justiciable; a person can move the courts when a right is violated.
- Feature 6: guaranteed by the Supreme Court; a person can go there directly, not only on appeal.
- Feature 7: not permanent; Parliament can curtail or repeal them only by constitutional amendment, without touching the basic structure.
- Feature 8: suspended during National Emergency except Articles 20 and 21; Article 19 only in an external emergency (war or external aggression).
- Feature 9: scope limited by Articles 31A, 31B and 31C.
- Feature 10: application to armed forces, para-military forces, police and intelligence agencies can be restricted by Parliament (Article 33).
- Feature 11: application can be restricted during martial law (Article 34).
- Feature 12: most are directly enforceable; a few depend on a law made to give them effect, and only Parliament can make that law (Article 35).
- Significance: bedrock of democracy; material and moral protection of the person; bulwark of liberty; basis of rule of law; protect minorities and weaker sections; strengthen the secular fabric; check the government's absolute authority; foundation of social equality and justice; ensure dignity; facilitate participation in political and administrative processes.
- Criticism 1: excessive limitations (exceptions, restrictions, explanations).
- Criticism 2: no social and economic rights (work, social security, rest and leisure).
- Criticism 3: no clarity (vague words such as public order, minorities, reasonable restriction).
- Criticism 4: no permanency (Parliament can amend or abolish, as with the right to property in 1978); basic structure is the only limit.
- Criticism 5: suspension during Emergency.
- Criticism 6: expensive remedy; benefits mainly the rich.
- Criticism 7: preventive detention (Article 22) takes away the spirit of the chapter.
- Criticism 8: no consistent philosophy (Ivor Jennings, quoted by Laxmikanth).

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Fundamental right versus constitutional right versus legal right: Part III with Article 32 remedy; elsewhere in the Constitution (Articles 265, 300A, 301) without Article 32; created by ordinary law.
- Equality before law (British, negative) versus equal protection of laws (American, positive).

- Tax versus fee in Article 27.
- Article 29 (any section of citizens; admission) versus Article 30 (minorities' institutions).
- Prohibition (preventive, before the decision) versus certiorari (preventive and curative, quashes the order); mandamus commands action and prohibition forbids it.
- Punitive versus preventive detention; martial law versus National Emergency versus military law (the law for the armed forces).
- Article 32 (Supreme Court, FRs only, itself an FR) versus Article 226 (High Courts, FRs and other purposes, discretionary).
- Article 31A (five categories, shields from Articles 14 and 19) versus 31B (Ninth Schedule, shields from all FRs, but reviewable after 24 April 1973) versus 31C (Directive-implementing laws, shields from Articles 14 and 19).
- Article 21 (life and liberty, all persons) versus Article 21A (elementary education, ages 6-14).
- Citizens versus persons: Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 for citizens; Articles 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22 (Part 1 not for enemy aliens), 23, 24, 25-28 for persons (GS-162).

### Core concepts

- Fundamental Rights are the justiciable rights in Part III (Articles 12-35), enforceable by the Supreme Court and High Courts and changeable only by constitutional amendment within the basic structure.
- Most FRs bind the State (Article 12); a few (Articles 15(2), 17, 23) also bind private persons.
- Equality does not forbid reasonable classification; it forbids arbitrary, class and unguided power.
- No FR is absolute: reasonable restrictions apply, and the courts judge reasonableness.
- Article 32 makes the remedy itself a Fundamental Right; the five writs are the main tools.
- Property is no longer an FR (Article 300A since 1978) but Articles 31A-31C still shield laws from FR challenges.

**Q.** Write the six present groups of Fundamental Rights and the Article range of each.

**A.** Equality 14-18; freedom 19-22; exploitation 23-24; religion 25-28; cultural and educational 29-30; constitutional remedies 32.

**Q.** What is the difference between a moral right and a Fundamental Right?

**A.** A moral right rests on morality and may not be legally enforceable; a Fundamental Right is listed in Part III, protected by the Constitution and enforceable in court, and can be changed only by constitutional amendment.

**A.** Article 32; Dr B.R. Ambedkar (NCERT).

**Q.** Why does Article 14 allow classification but forbid class legislation?

**A.** Because equality means like treatment of persons who are alike; a rational grouping (intelligible differentia with a nexus to the object) treats people in the same circumstances alike, whereas class legislation arbitrarily favours or burdens a group.

**Q.** Why can Article 19 not be claimed by a company, but Article 14 can?

**A.** Article 19 belongs to citizens (and shareholders) and a company is not a citizen; Article 14 speaks of 'any person', which includes legal persons.

**Q.** Explain how Article 25 and Article 26 protect religious freedom differently.

**A.** Article 25 protects individuals (conscience, profession, practice, propagation) and is subject to public order, morality, health and other FR provisions. Article 26 protects collective rights of religious denominations and is subject to public order, morality and health only.

**Q.** List the five writs and say which one any interested person can seek.

**A.** Habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto; quo warranto can be sought by any interested person.

**Q.** Write the history of the right to property in five dated steps, from Article 31 to Article 300A.

**A.** 1950 Articles 19(1)(f) and 31; 1951 First Amendment adds 31A and 31B; 1971 25th Amendment adds 31C; 1973 Kesavananda; 1978 44th Amendment repeals Article 31 and inserts Article 300A.

**Q.** Which rights bind private persons and which Articles are for citizens only?

**A.** Articles 15(2), 17, 23 bind private persons; citizens only: Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29.
