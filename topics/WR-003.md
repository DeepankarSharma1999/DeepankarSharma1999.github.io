## WR-003: Introduction styles (scenario / question / idea)

Essay (Mains 250) · Writing/Language · Essay craft

### Bridge: Words used for the parts of an opening

An **introduction** is the first paragraph of an essay. This lesson splits it into three parts. The **hook** is the first sentence or two, written to make the reader attend: a small scene, a question or a sharp idea. The **bridge** is the sentence that joins the hook to the topic by using the topic's own key words (WR-001). The **thesis** is the sentence (occasionally two) that states your position, with a claim, a main reason and a boundary (WR-002 module 4). A **scenario** is a very short, concrete scene with one person, one place and one moment. A **genuine question** is one the essay will answer; a **rhetorical question** has an obvious answer and is asked only for effect, which usually wastes words. An **idea opening** begins with a short claim or distinction in plain words. A **roadmap** is a clause naming the dimensions the essay will take; it is optional and short. A **cliche** is an expression so worn that it says nothing, such as 'since the dawn of civilisation'. The word *essay* in this lesson always means the Mains Essay paper.

### 1. Why the opening 80 to 120 words matter

*Objective:* State three jobs of an introduction in a 1,000 to 1,200 word essay, its share of the length and time, and what it must not do.

## The paper in a few facts

Per the project registry and WR-001 (verify on the printed paper): the Essay is a Mains paper of 250 marks in which two essays of about 1,000 to 1,200 words each are written in three hours; each verified prompt record carries 125 marks. The record instructions say to write two essays, choosing one topic from each of Sections A and B (verify on the printed paper). The syllabus clause asks you to keep closely to the subject, arrange ideas in orderly fashion, write concisely, and use effective and exact expression.

## Size and time

An opening of 80 to 120 words is 80 / 1200 = 6.7 per cent to 120 / 1000 = 12 per cent of the essay. At 15 to 20 words a minute (a practice figure; test your own speed), 80 words take 80 / 20 = 4 minutes and 120 words take 120 / 15 = 8 minutes. Small in length, the paragraph does three jobs.

1. **Fix the subject.** The reader sees at once that you have read the topic's own words (WR-001, 'keep closely to the subject').
2. **Take a position.** The thesis of WR-002 appears in the open, so every later paragraph has something to defend.
3. **Set the order.** One clause can tell the reader which dimensions follow, which serves 'arrange ideas in orderly fashion'.

## What an opening is not

It is not a summary of the whole essay, a dictionary definition, a quotation parade or an announcement that you will discuss the topic. Module 7 takes these errors one by one.

## A note on honesty

UPSC publishes no marking scheme or model essay that this project has found. So nothing here claims how an examiner reacts; the claim is narrower: an opening that shows the subject and the position within 120 words makes the rest of the essay easier for you to write, because the plan is visible on the first page.

## Link to the earlier lessons

WR-001 gives the decoding; WR-002 gives the grid, the central question and the thesis. This lesson starts where WR-002 ends: with a thesis already chosen. Write the opening *after* the plan, never before it, because an opening written without a thesis tends to wander.

### 2. Anatomy of an opening: hook, bridge, thesis

*Objective:* Build an opening from a hook, a bridge and a thesis within 80 to 120 words and write the three parts in the right order of drafting.

## Three parts

Every sample in this lesson has the same skeleton.

1. **Hook** (first sentences): the scene, the question or the idea. It makes the reader attend.
2. **Bridge** (one or two sentences): joins the hook to the topic using the topic's own key words. It answers: what has this to do with the topic?
3. **Thesis** (last sentence or two): claim, because, boundary (WR-002 module 4), in plain words. WR-002 asks for one sentence; where claim, reason and boundary will not fit one sentence, a second short sentence for the boundary is the exception, as in some samples.

## Measured word budgets

The samples are original and the counts below were made by computer. They show what a balanced opening looks like.

| Sample | Hook | Bridge | Thesis | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Muddy water, scenario | 69 (five sentences of scene) | 11 | 22 | 102 |
| Cost of being wrong, question | 37 (two questions) | 29 (two context sentences) | 18 | 84 |
| Muddy water, idea | 35 (idea plus grounding) | 11 | 34 | 80 |

The scene is longest because it needs detail; the idea is shortest because it is already a claim. The thesis is never below about 18 words, since claim, reason and boundary need room.

## Order of drafting

Do not write in reading order. First copy the thesis from your plan. Then write the bridge, which only needs the topic's key words. Last, write the hook, because you now know what it must lead to. Drafting the hook first causes most long or off-topic openings.

## Three quick tests

- **Key-word test.** Do the topic's own words (or exact equivalents) appear by the end of the bridge, before the thesis?
- **Cut test.** Remove the hook. Does the reader still know your position? If not, the thesis is weak.
- **Thesis test.** Cover everything except the last sentence. Does it still say what you hold, why, and when it holds? If not, rewrite it before polishing the hook.

## Length control

Count words by sentences (use a tally in the margin). If you pass 120, cut the hook first: the hook is the part least needed to understand the argument.

### 3. Scenario openings

*Objective:* Write a scenario opening: one person, one place, one moment, a turn, a bridge and a thesis; and name its strengths and risks.

## What it is

A **scenario** opening begins with a very short scene that stands for the topic. It shows the tension of the topic in action instead of describing it.

## How to build one

1. Choose **one person, one place, one moment**: a farmer with a bucket, a health worker at two in the morning, a student in a hostel.
2. Put the two forces of the topic into the scene. For 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone', the forces are effort and restraint; the scene lets restraint win.
3. Add a **turn**: the sentence that says what the scene showed ('She had left the water still and let it settle').
4. Write the **bridge**: 'Public life has muddy water too: tempers, rumours, markets in panic.' It widens the scene to the topic.
5. Close with the **thesis**: 'Restraint clears it only when the cause of the mud has stopped; where someone is still stirring, leaving it alone is neglect.'

In the muddy-water sample the scene is 69 words, the bridge 11 and the thesis 22: 102 in all.

## Strengths

- It is concrete, so the reader remembers it and you can return to its image in the conclusion.
- It suits abstract or figurative topics ('Muddy water ...', 'The time to repair the roof ...'), because the scene gives them a body.
- The scene's tension supplies the thesis's *because* and *boundary* almost automatically.

## Risks

- **Length.** Past about 70 words, the scene eats the thesis.
- **Off-topic anecdote.** A moving scene about another subject (module 7).
- **Unverified fact.** If the scene claims a real event, every detail must be certain; otherwise label it a type of case ('A health worker, somewhere ...') or mark it *verify* when practising. The samples here are invented scenes and are labelled as examples.
- **Sentiment.** A scene that tries to move the reader instead of showing the tension reads as decoration.
- **Mismatch.** The scene points one way and the thesis another.

## When to avoid it

On a topic with several moving parts ('Management of Indian border disputes') a single scene trivialises the problem; use a question or an idea.

## Practice rule

Use a scenario when you can name the person, place and moment in about 20 seconds. If you cannot, switch to another style rather than invent one slowly.

### 4. Question openings

*Objective:* Write a question opening whose question the thesis answers, and avoid rhetorical, dictionary and stacked questions.

## What it is

A **question** opening asks the reader the question the essay will answer. It is the closest style to the central question (CQ) of WR-002 module 3; often the opening question *is* the CQ in everyday words.

## Three kinds of genuine question

1. **The real question the topic raises.** 'Is it better to act and be wrong, or to wait and be safe?' for 'The cost of being wrong is less than the cost of doing nothing.'
2. **The test question.** 'Does the screen make the young lonelier, or does it show a loneliness already there?' (the cause-and-effect topic of 2024).
3. **The condition question.** 'The answer turns on one question: has the stirring stopped?'

## How to build one

1. Ask the question in the topic's own words, keeping its qualifier.
2. Add one or two sentences of context (the bridge) that show why the question is live: who faces it, what is at stake.
3. If you ask a second question, make it narrower than the first.
4. Answer it at once in the thesis, with claim, because and boundary.

In the cost-of-being-wrong sample the two questions take 37 words, two context sentences 29 and the thesis 18: 84 words.

## Strengths

- It shows at once that the essay is about a question, not a theme.
- The reader's attention goes to the thing you will settle.
- It is the quickest style for question-form and two-sided topics ('Has ...?', 'the threat of ... or ...').

## Risks

- **Rhetorical question.** If everyone answers the same way, the question fails the two-answer test (WR-002 module 3).
- **Question stack.** Three or four questions in a row; use at most two hook questions. The single condition-bridge question ('has the stirring stopped?', module 6) is exempt, because it sets up the thesis instead of asking again.
- **Dictionary question.** 'What is education?' leads to a definition, not a position.
- **Unanswered question.** The thesis answers a different question.
- **Padding.** A long run-up before the question.

## The matched-pair rule

Read the first and last sentences together. The thesis should read as an answer to the question: same key words, same qualifier. If it does not, rewrite one of them until they match.

### 5. Idea openings

*Objective:* Write an idea opening: a plain claim or distinction, one grounding sentence, a bridge and a thesis; and name its strengths and risks.

## What it is

An **idea** opening begins with a short claim or distinction in plain words and then grounds it. It is the most direct style: the thinking is visible in the first sentence.

## Four kinds of opening idea

1. **A distinction.** 'Education is not the stock of facts a mind holds but the habits it keeps when the facts have gone.'
2. **A principle.** 'Preparation is cheapest when it feels least needed.'
3. **A reversal.** 'Doing nothing is not neutral; it is a choice whose cost is hidden.'
4. **A mechanism.** 'Muddy water clears because its particles settle under their own weight once the stirring stops.'

## How to build one

1. State the idea in one sentence of plain words, using a key word of the topic.
2. **Ground it** with one sentence: a mechanism, an image or a small case. An idea with no grounding sounds like a slogan.
3. Write the bridge: say what the idea means for the topic.
4. State the thesis with claim, because and boundary.

In the muddy-water idea sample the idea sentence is 10 words, the grounding 25, the bridge 11 and the thesis 34: 80 words, the shortest of the three styles.

## Strengths

- Fastest route to the thesis: the first sentence already carries a claim.
- Works well for abstract topics ('Wisdom finds truth') and for aphorisms where a paraphrase is itself a good opening.
- Easy to keep inside the word budget.

## Risks

- **Abstract fog.** 'Life is a journey' and 'Time is a great teacher' are cliches with no content.
- **Unsupported grand claim.** 'All progress comes from doubt' without a mechanism or case.
- **Dictionary slide.** Starting 'X is defined as ...' (module 7).
- **Monotone.** If every paragraph of the essay is also pure idea, the essay has no examples; keep one concrete image in the opening.
- **Overreach.** The idea claims more than the thesis can defend.

## The grounding test

After the idea sentence, can a reader point to a case or mechanism in your next sentence? If the next sentence is another abstract claim, replace it with an image.

### 6. Choosing a style and linking it to the thesis

*Objective:* Choose a style by topic type and by a 20-second test, and write the bridge sentence that carries each style into the thesis.

## Match style to topic type

WR-001 module 2 sorted topics into types. The table gives a *teaching suggestion* for each, not an official rule.

| Topic type (WR-001 module 2) | Often works well | Why | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quotation with an image ('Muddy water ...') | Scenario or idea | The image becomes a scene or a principle | A scene about water only, with no public parallel |
| Abstract ('Wisdom finds truth') | Idea | The topic is already a claim | Slogans |
| Issue or policy ('Management of Indian border disputes') | Question or idea | Many moving parts | A single anecdote trivialises it |
| Two-sided or dilemma ('threat ... or better job opportunities') | Question | The two sides are the question | Stacked questions |
| Contrast pair ('Culture is what we are ...') | Idea (state the pivot) | The pivot is the claim | Repeating the topic |
| Question form ('Has ... lost its relevance ...?') | Question, answered at once | The topic is already a question | Asking it back without answering |
| Cause and effect ('Social media is triggering ...') | Scenario or question | A scene shows the mechanism | Over-claiming the cause |

## The 20-second test

1. Can I name a person, place and moment? If yes, **scenario**.
2. Does the central question read well aloud? If yes, **question**.
3. Is the thesis already crisp as a plain claim? If yes, **idea**.

Take the first yes. If you have no yes, write an idea opening: it is the safest because it is the shortest and closest to the thesis. Never spend more than a minute choosing.

## Linking the hook to the thesis

Each style needs a different bridge sentence.

- **Scenario, pattern bridge.** 'What happened at the stream happens wherever ...' ('Public life has muddy water too: tempers, rumours, markets in panic.')
- **Question, condition bridge.** 'The answer turns on one question: ...' ('has the stirring stopped?')
- **Idea, grounding bridge.** 'This is clearest in ...' or 'For the topic, this means ...' ('Restraint is therefore a judgement and not an absence of action.')

Then state the thesis by the WR-002 formula: claim, because, boundary.

## Mixing styles

A short question may follow a scene, or an idea may lead into a question. Mix only inside the 120-word limit and only if the thesis is still the last sentence. The samples in the master notes keep one style each, so that the contrast is clear.

## Same topic, three styles

For 'The time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining', the scenario shows the schoolmaster in May and July; the question asks why we mend the roof only after it leaks; the idea says preparation is cheapest when it feels least needed. The three differ in the first sentence but end in the same kind of thesis: preparation belongs in good times, and routine makes it happen.

### 7. Common errors and how to repair them

*Objective:* Recognise eight common faults in an opening and repair each in the same word count.

## Eight faults

**1. Cliche quotation.** *Before:* 'Since the dawn of civilisation, great thinkers have said that patience is a virtue.' It says nothing about the topic and attributes words to nobody. *Repair:* use a quotation only if it is short, remembered exactly, and then explained and used as your idea. Never invent a quotation or a source. If unsure, paraphrase and leave out the name.

**2. Off-topic anecdote.** *Before:* 'Last summer I watched a peacock dance in my grandmother's village.' *Repair:* a scene must carry the topic's two forces (module 3). Test: if the scene could open any other essay unchanged, it is off-topic.

**3. Definition-only opening.** *Before:* 'Education is defined as the process of receiving or giving systematic instruction.' It gives no position. *Repair:* define a term only when the topic turns on it, and put the definition into a claim ('Education is not the stock of facts ... but the habits ...').

**4. Announcement-only opening.** *Before:* 'In this essay I will discuss the cost of being wrong and the cost of doing nothing.' It names the topic twice and holds no view. *Repair:* state the thesis: 'Being wrong costs less than doing nothing where errors can be corrected.' A short 'This essay argues that ...' is acceptable only before a real claim.

**5. Sweeping generalisation.** *Before:* 'In today's fast-changing world, decisions are very important.' *Repair:* begin with the specific tension of the topic.

**6. Over-long hook.** A scene or run-up of 90 words leaves 30 for bridge and thesis. *Repair:* cut the hook to 70 words or fewer; the hook is the part least needed for the argument.

**7. Hook and thesis point different ways.** A scene about hurry and a thesis about patience. *Repair:* rewrite the scene or the thesis so that both carry the same claim; use the matched-pair rule of module 4.

**8. Dropped qualifier.** For 'The cost of being wrong is less than the cost of doing nothing', a thesis 'Doing nothing is bad' drops 'less than' and the comparison. *Repair:* keep the topic's operative words (WR-001, WR-002 checklist item 1).

## Facts inside an opening

Never invent a statistic, date, quotation or name to make an opening vivid. If you are not sure, say 'a health worker, somewhere' or leave the figure out; while practising, mark *verify*. The sample openings in this lesson use invented, ordinary scenes and no numbers, and are labelled as examples.

## Check against the errors

After drafting, read the opening once, looking only for faults 1 to 8. Fix the fault, not the wording: if the fault is a dropped qualifier, adding adjectives will not help.

### 8. Self-check rubric and timed practice routine

*Objective:* Score an opening on an eight-item rubric, find the weakest item, and run an eight-minute timed routine over several days.

## The eight-item rubric (a self-assessment tool made for this lesson, not an official scale)

Score each item 0, 1 or 2; the total is out of 16.

| # | Item | 2 points | 0 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Key words | Topic's own words appear by the end of the bridge, before the thesis | Absent |
| 2 | Hook | One idea, concrete, 70 words or fewer | Cliche or long |
| 3 | Bridge | Joins hook to topic in one or two sentences | Missing |
| 4 | Thesis | Claim, because and boundary (WR-002 module 4 formula) | Vague |
| 5 | Position | Thesis in the last one or two sentences | Buried or absent |
| 6 | Length | 80 to 120 words | More than 20 words outside |
| 7 | Errors | None of faults 1 to 8 | Two or more |
| 8 | Fit | Style suits the topic; thesis answers the hook | Mismatch |

Score 1 for partial credit. Record the *lowest item*; it is the next drill.

## The eight-minute routine (practice target, not an official allotment)

| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 | Copy the thesis from your WR-002 plan |
| 1 | Choose a style by the 20-second test |
| 4 | Write the opening in thesis, bridge, hook order |
| 1 | Count words by sentence; cut the hook if over 120 |
| 1 | Score on the rubric |

With a twelve-minute plan from WR-002 module 8, plan plus opening is 12 + 8 = 20 minutes. The 4 minutes of writing assume about 20 words a minute for 80 words; if you write 15 words a minute a 120-word opening takes 8, so allow longer while learning and time yourself honestly.

## A three-day cycle with a retest

- **Day 1:** two prompts, scenario style.
- **Day 2:** the same two prompts, question style.
- **Day 3:** the same two prompts, idea style.
- **After seven days or more:** rewrite one of the six openings from the plan alone, without looking, and score it.

Comparing the same thesis in three styles shows which style you write best and which you avoid. Keep an error log of faults 1 to 8 by number.

## Warnings

Completing the routine is not mastery; scoring honestly, repairing the lowest item and retesting later are the evidence. Do not memorise an opening for use in the exam: the topic will not match, and a pre-written opening is the surest way to drift from the subject.

### Notes: Definitions

- Introduction: the first paragraph of the essay, here 80 to 120 words.
- Hook: the first sentences (scene, question or idea) that make the reader attend.
- Bridge: one or two sentences joining the hook to the topic's own key words.
- Thesis: one sentence with claim, because and boundary (WR-002 module 4).
- Scenario opening: a very short concrete scene. Question opening: a genuine question the essay answers. Idea opening: a plain claim or distinction with one grounding sentence.
- Matched-pair rule: the thesis reads as the answer to the opening question, with the same key words and qualifier.
- Roadmap: an optional clause naming the dimensions the essay will take.

### Notes: Sample openings: scenario style (six, original, labelled as examples)

- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone' (official Essay 2025 Q5, verified record upsc-2025-essay-q5). After the first rain, a farmer carries a bucket of brown water up from the stream. He strains it through a cloth, then through a second cloth, and it stays brown. His neighbour sets her bucket on the doorstep and goes in to cook. By evening the silt lies at the bottom and the water above it is clear. She had left the water still and let it settle. Public life has muddy water too: tempers, rumours, markets in panic. Restraint clears it only when the cause of the mud has stopped; where someone is still stirring, leaving it alone is neglect. [102 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'The cost of being wrong is less than the cost of doing nothing' (official Essay 2024 Q8, verified record upsc-2024-essay-q8). At two in the morning a village health worker watches a child's fever climb. The doctor is two hours away and the signs may be nothing. If she sends for the ambulance and the fever breaks, she has lost a night's sleep and some fuel. If she waits and is wrong, nothing can be repaired. She sends for it. She did not know she was right; she knew the cost of being wrong was one she could live with. Acting under uncertainty costs less than doing nothing whenever the error can be seen and corrected quickly, and the balance reverses only when a wrong action cannot be undone. [108 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'A society that has more justice is a society that needs less charity' (official Essay 2023 Q7, verified record upsc-2023-essay-q7). Every evening a volunteer serves free meals outside a railway station, and the queue grows longer. One night a man in the line says, without anger, that he would rather have been paid for the two weeks he worked. The volunteer has no answer. The meal is real; the unpaid wages are also real. Charity answers tonight's hunger, while justice would have prevented it. A society with more justice needs less of the charity that rescues people from preventable need, though never less of the charity that comforts losses no system can prevent. [93 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'The time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining' (official Essay 2022 Q5, verified record upsc-2022-essay-q5). In May the schoolmaster of a hill village notices cracks in the tin sheets above his classroom. Mending them would take a day and a little money, and the sky is clear. He puts it off. In July the first heavy rain floods the room and soaks the register, and the repair now costs more and cannot even be started in the downpour. Everyone had known in May that the time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining. Preparation belongs in good times because it needs calm, money and time, which crisis removes, and institutions must build it into routine instead of trusting foresight alone. [108 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Social media is triggering ‘Fear of Missing Out’ amongst the youth, precipitating depression and loneliness' (official Essay 2024 Q5, verified record upsc-2024-essay-q5). It is eleven at night in a hostel, and a student has chosen to stay in and study. Her friends have gone to a film, and their photographs appear on her screen one by one. The choice she made an hour ago now feels like a loss. She shuts the phone, then opens it again. That pang is the fear of missing out, and social media keeps it supplied. It can trigger depression and loneliness among the young by sharpening comparison, but it acts on vulnerabilities that already exist; its harm depends on how it is used and whether it replaces real contact. [103 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Education is what remains after one has forgotten what one has learned in school' (official Essay 2023 Q8, verified record upsc-2023-essay-q8). Years after leaving school, a woman cannot recall the date of a single battle or the formula for a chord. Yet when a rumour spreads in her lane, she asks who said it before repeating it, and when a bill arrives she checks the sum. No teacher set these habits as lessons. They stayed behind when the lessons were forgotten, and that is what the saying calls education. Education is best measured by such habits of mind, which outlast facts, though facts are the material from which the habits are built. [91 words]

### Notes: Sample openings: question style (six, original, labelled as examples)

- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone' (official Essay 2025 Q5, verified record upsc-2025-essay-q5). When a dispute is heated, a market is in panic or a debate is all noise, should we add effort or step back? The saying that muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone says step back. Is that wisdom, or only a polite name for neglect? The answer turns on one question: has the stirring stopped? Patience clears muddy water when the disturbance has ended and the system can settle by itself, as silt does; it becomes neglect when someone is still stirring the water. [87 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'The cost of being wrong is less than the cost of doing nothing' (official Essay 2024 Q8, verified record upsc-2024-essay-q8). Is it better to act and be wrong, or to wait and be safe? Hospitals, governments and families face this daily, and each treats waiting as if it were free. Yet delay is also a decision, and its price arrives later and unseen. When a flood warning, a failing school or a spreading disease demands a choice before the facts are complete, which mistake is dearer? Being wrong costs less than doing nothing where errors can be corrected, and more where they are irreversible. [84 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'A society that has more justice is a society that needs less charity' (official Essay 2023 Q7, verified record upsc-2023-essay-q7). If wages were paid in full, courts were open to all, and public schools and clinics worked, would anyone need to queue for a free meal? The question suggests that charity fills the gaps injustice leaves, and that fewer gaps mean less need. Is that wholly true? A worker whose wages were withheld needs a remedy; a family whose home was lost to a flood needs a neighbour. More justice reduces the charity of rescue, which answers preventable deprivation, but no justice removes the charity of compassion, which answers grief, illness and chance. [93 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'The time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining' (official Essay 2022 Q5, verified record upsc-2022-essay-q5). Why do we mend the roof only after it leaks? Households postpone repairs, governments postpone reforms and individuals postpone health checks, though all know the rain will come. The delay is rarely ignorance; it is the pull of the present. Is the cause forgetfulness, cost or hope? The saying that the time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining points to the answer: only in good times is repair cheap. Preparation belongs in good times, and it happens when habits and institutions, not good intentions, make it routine. [91 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Social media is triggering ‘Fear of Missing Out’ amongst the youth, precipitating depression and loneliness' (official Essay 2024 Q5, verified record upsc-2024-essay-q5). Does the screen make the young lonelier, or does it show a loneliness that was already there? The claim that social media triggers the fear of missing out, and through it depression and loneliness, is widely repeated. Parents, teachers and the young themselves disagree about it. Is social media a cause, a trigger or only a mirror? It can be a trigger, because it keeps comparison constant, but its power depends on existing vulnerabilities and on whether it replaces or extends real contact. [83 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Education is what remains after one has forgotten what one has learned in school' (official Essay 2023 Q8, verified record upsc-2023-essay-q8). What do we keep when we have forgotten what we were taught? Every adult has tried to recall a lesson and failed. Dates, formulae and definitions fade within years, yet something remains: a way of asking, doubting and judging. Is that what education is? If it is, an examination mark measures only what is about to be lost. Education lies in the habits of mind that outlast facts, but those habits are formed by learning the facts, so forgetting is no argument for teaching less. [85 words]

### Notes: Sample openings: idea style (six, original, labelled as examples)

- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone' (official Essay 2025 Q5, verified record upsc-2025-essay-q5). Clarity is often a by-product of patience, not of effort. Muddy water clears because its particles settle under their own weight once the stirring stops; extra effort at the wrong moment only keeps them afloat. Restraint is therefore a judgement and not an absence of action. Leaving a troubled situation alone is the best course when its cause has ended and the system can restore itself, and it fails wherever the cause is still at work behind a calm surface. [80 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'The cost of being wrong is less than the cost of doing nothing' (official Essay 2024 Q8, verified record upsc-2024-essay-q8). Every decision compares two possible errors: acting wrongly and failing to act. The second is easy to overlook because it leaves no visible event, only a missed chance. Doing nothing is therefore not neutral; it is a choice whose cost is hidden. Delay feels safe only because its price is paid later. Action under uncertainty is the lesser risk when mistakes can be noticed and corrected quickly, and the greater risk when they cannot be undone. The skill lies in telling which situation we face. [85 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'A society that has more justice is a society that needs less charity' (official Essay 2023 Q7, verified record upsc-2023-essay-q7). Charity and justice answer the same suffering at different moments: charity after the harm, justice before it. Where fair wages, fair trial and basic services are protected, fewer people fall to the point where a gift is their only help. A meal given to a man whose wages were withheld treats the symptom; a court that orders the wages paid treats the cause. A more just society therefore needs less charity of the rescuing kind. It does not need less kindness, since some losses are beyond the reach of any law. [91 words]
- Example (not an official answer). Prompt: 'The time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining' (official Essay 2022 Q5, verified record upsc-2022-essay-q5). ### Notes: Confused concepts

- Hook versus bridge: the hook attracts, the bridge connects to the topic's words.
- Question opening versus central question: the opening question is the CQ in everyday words; the CQ is a planning tool, the opening is for the reader.
- Scenario versus anecdote: a scenario carries the topic's two forces; an anecdote may not.
- Idea opening versus definition: an idea is a claim; a definition only names.
- Thesis versus announcement: the thesis states a position; an announcement names a topic.

### Core concepts

- An opening has three parts: hook, bridge, thesis, in 80 to 120 words.
- Draft the thesis first, then the bridge, then the hook.
- Three styles: scenario, question, idea, each with its own bridge.
- Eight faults: cliche quotation, off-topic anecdote, definition-only, announcement-only, sweeping, over-long, mismatch, dropped qualifier.

### Practice

**Q.** Name the three parts of an opening and the order in which to draft them.

**A.** Hook, bridge, thesis; draft thesis first, then bridge, then hook.

**Q.** An opening has 150 words and the essay has 1,100. What share is that, and is it inside the lesson's target?

**A.** 150 / 1100 = 13.6 per cent; it is above the 80 to 120 word target (6.7 to 12 per cent), so the hook should be cut by at least 30 words.

**Q.** Which style begins with one person, one place and one moment?

**A.** The scenario opening.

**Q.** Is 'Education is defined as systematic instruction' a good opening? Why?

**A.** No: it is a definition-only opening with no position; put the definition inside a claim.

**Q.** Why should the opening be written after the plan?

**A.** The thesis from the plan fixes where the hook must lead; an opening written first has no destination and tends to drift from the subject.

**Q.** Why must a question opening and its thesis be a matched pair?

**A.** If the thesis answers a different question, the reader feels a gap between hook and claim; matched key words and qualifier remove it.

**Q.** Why must a scenario carry the two forces of the topic?

**A.** The tension in the scene becomes the thesis's reason and boundary; a scene without the tension is an off-topic anecdote.

**Q.** Why does an idea opening need a grounding sentence?

**A.** Without a mechanism, image or case, a plain claim sounds like a slogan.

**Q.** When is a rhetorical question a poor hook?

**A.** When everyone answers it the same way; it fails the two-answer test of WR-002 and wastes words.

**Q.** Diagnose: 'In today's world, justice is very important. Justice means fairness. In this essay I will discuss justice and charity.' Name the faults and say how to repair.

**A.** Faults 5 (sweeping generalisation), 3 (definition-only) and 4 (announcement-only), and no thesis. Repair: start with the topic's tension (justice and charity answer suffering at different moments) and end with a thesis with claim, reason and boundary.

**Q.** Identify the style: 'Is it better to act and be wrong, or to wait and be safe?'

**A.** Question opening; the thesis must answer it with the same key words.

**Q.** A scenario hook is 95 words, the bridge 11 and the thesis 20. Total 126. What do you cut and why?

**A.** Cut the hook, the part least needed to follow the argument, to 70 words or fewer (fault 6 and rubric item 2): that removes at least 25 words and brings the total to about 101. Keep the person, place and moment; the bridge and thesis stay.

**Q.** Timed task (8 minutes after a 12-minute plan, practice target). Prompt: 'Poverty anywhere is a threat to prosperity everywhere' (official Essay 2018 Q3, verified record upsc-2018-essay-q3). Write an opening of 80 to 120 words in the idea style: hook, bridge, thesis. Then score it on the eight-item rubric (module 8) and record the lowest item.

**A.** Indicative points (one reasonable reading, not an official expectation): the topic's own words are 'poverty anywhere', 'threat' and 'prosperity everywhere'; the claim links distant deprivation to shared well-being through channels such as health, migration, markets and conflict; a boundary could say the link is stronger where places are closely connected. Avoid statistics. Ground the idea in one mechanism or image.

**Q.** Timed task (8 minutes after a 12-minute plan, practice target). Prompt: 'Ships do not sink because of water around them, ships sink because of water that gets into them' (official Essay 2020 Q3, verified record upsc-2020-essay-q3). Write an opening of 80 to 120 words in the scenario style: hook, bridge, thesis. Then score it on the eight-item rubric (module 8) and record the lowest item.

**A.** Indicative points: contrast pair with a pivot (water around versus water inside). A scene might show a crew at a small leak that is ignored while the sea outside looks calm. The turn names the lesson: outside pressure harms through an inner weakness. A boundary: some storms overwhelm any hull. Keep the scene to about 60 words and invent no named incident.

**Q.** Timed task (8 minutes after a 12-minute plan, practice target). Prompt: 'What is research, but a blind date with knowledge!' (official Essay 2021 Q6, verified record upsc-2021-essay-q6). Write an opening of 80 to 120 words in the question style: hook, bridge, thesis. Then score it on the eight-item rubric (module 8) and record the lowest item.

**A.** Indicative points: the tone is playful; read the figure literally and figuratively first (WR-001-B1). A question such as 'Does the researcher meet knowledge without knowing what it will be like?' sets up a thesis that research resembles a blind date in uncertainty and openness, but that, unlike a date, it needs preparation and method. Thesis must answer the question in the same key words.

**Q.** Timed task (8 minutes after a 12-minute plan, practice target). Prompt: 'A ship in harbour is safe, but that is not what ship is for' (official Essay 2022 Q4, verified record upsc-2022-essay-q4). Write an opening of 80 to 120 words in the idea style: hook, bridge, thesis. Then score it on the eight-item rubric (module 8) and record the lowest item.

**A.** Indicative points: safety versus purpose. An idea opening could state that a thing's purpose can justify risk, ground it in the ship, and bound it: only calculated risk, and only where the purpose is worth it. The thesis keeps the topic's contrast; avoid a slogan such as 'no risk, no reward' with no boundary.

**Q.** Timed task (8 minutes after a 12-minute plan, practice target). Prompt: 'When two elephants fight, it is the grass that gets trampled' (official Essay 2026 Q4, verified record upsc-2026-essay-q4). Write an opening of 80 to 120 words in the scenario style: hook, bridge, thesis. Then score it on the eight-item rubric (module 8) and record the lowest item.

**A.** Indicative points: the weaker party suffers in a contest between the powerful. A scene can be a small one (a field edge, a footpath) without any claim about a real conflict. Bridge to public life, then a thesis with a boundary, for example that the grass can be protected where rules or shared interests restrain the elephants. Do not cite real wars or numbers unless certain.

**Q.** Timed task (8 minutes after a 12-minute plan, practice target). Prompt: ''Social media' is inherently a selfish medium' (official Essay 2017 B4, verified record upsc-2017-essay-b4). Write an opening of 80 to 120 words in the question style: hook, bridge, thesis. Then score it on the eight-item rubric (module 8) and record the lowest item.

**A.** Indicative points: the operative word is 'inherently' (by its nature, not by use). A question can ask whether the selfishness lies in the medium or in how people use it. A thesis could hold that the medium rewards self-display but does not force it, so outcomes depend on use and design. Keep the qualifier; avoid slipping to 'social media is bad'.

**Q.** Three-style comparison (30 minutes over one sitting, practice target). Choose one prompt from the sample prompts in the master notes that you have not yet opened in a style. Write the opening in all three styles, 80 to 120 words each, from the same thesis. Score all three on the rubric and say which you would use in an exam, with a one-line reason.

**A.** Indicative self-check (not an official expectation): the three openings share one thesis; the key words appear by the end of the bridge, before the thesis, in each; word counts are 80 to 120; the chosen style is the one that finished fastest and scored highest on items 2, 4 and 8. A repeated lowest item across the three styles points to a thesis problem (WR-002), not a style problem.

**Q.** State the three parts of an opening, the drafting order, and the word range.

**A.** Hook, bridge, thesis; draft thesis, bridge, hook; 80 to 120 words.

**Q.** Name the bridge belonging to each of the three styles.

**A.** Scenario: pattern bridge; question: condition bridge; idea: grounding bridge.

**Q.** List five of the eight faults and one repair.

**A.** Cliche quotation, off-topic anecdote, definition-only, announcement-only, sweeping generalisation, over-long hook, mismatch, dropped qualifier; for example, put a definition inside a claim.

**Q.** Rewrite from your plan alone, without looking, one earlier opening for a prompt of your choice and score it on the rubric.

**A.** Self-scored out of 16 with the lowest item recorded; the drill is the lowest item.

### Memory skeleton

- Opening = hook + bridge + thesis, 80 to 120 words, thesis in the last one or two sentences
- Draft backwards: thesis from the WR-002 plan, then bridge, then hook
- Scenario: one person, one place, one moment; pattern bridge
- Question: one genuine question; condition bridge; thesis answers it (matched pair)
- Idea: plain claim plus one grounding sentence; grounding bridge
- Choose by the 20-second test; default to idea
- Repair the eight faults; score on the rubric
