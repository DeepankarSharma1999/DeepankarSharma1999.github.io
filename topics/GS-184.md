## GS-184: Attorney General and CAG

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Union government

### Bridge: Audit vocabulary: accounts, audit, regularity, propriety and 'comptroller'

An **account** is a written record of money received and money spent. An **audit** is an independent examination of those records, done by someone who does not work for the department being checked, to see whether the money was handled properly. Auditing happens after the money has moved; it is a check, not a payment.

Auditors ask two families of questions. **Regularity (or compliance) questions** ask: did the department have legal authority to spend this money, on this purpose, and were the rules followed? Laxmikanth calls this the *legal and regulatory audit*. **Propriety questions** ask whether the spending was wise, faithful to the purpose and economical, even if it was legal; Laxmikanth's phrase is 'wisdom, faithfulness and economy'. A purchase can pass the first test and fail the second.

Two more words appear in the lesson. **Appropriation** means setting aside a sum, by an Act of Parliament, for a stated purpose; the *Appropriation Act* gives the government legal authority to take money out of the Consolidated Fund (see GS-180 module 7). A **comptroller** is a person who *controls* the issue of money: nothing is paid out until the comptroller agrees. An **auditor** only examines afterwards. The Constitution's title 'Comptroller and Auditor General' contains both words, and a famous Mains debate (module 8) is about which one the Indian officer really is.

### Bridge: Government accounts: the Consolidated Fund and 'charged on' in one minute

GS-180 module 2 explains that the Union government keeps three accounts: the **Consolidated Fund of India** (Article 266: all revenues, loans raised and repayments; money leaves it only through a law of Parliament), the **Public Account of India** (other public money held by the government, such as provident-fund deposits; spent by executive action) and the **Contingency Fund of India** (Article 267: an emergency advance for unforeseen needs). Each State has the same three accounts under the same scheme.

Two phrases from GS-180 module 5 matter here. Expenditure that Parliament *votes* every year is **voted**. Expenditure that the Constitution fixes directly on the Consolidated Fund is **charged**: Parliament may discuss it but does not vote on it. Putting an office's salary and expenses in the 'charged' category protects the office from yearly bargaining. Laxmikanth uses exactly this device for the CAG's office, which is why the CAG lesson keeps pointing back to the Consolidated Fund.

When the CAG audits 'the Consolidated Fund', he is checking the main account of the nation: every rupee drawn from it must match an Appropriation Act and an authorising rule.

### 1. Two guardians: a law officer for the government and an auditor for Parliament

*Objective:* State in one sentence what the Attorney General and the CAG each do, and name the Article of each office.

## What this lesson covers

Governments make decisions and spend public money. Two offices created by the Constitution help to keep that power lawful. The **Attorney General of India** (AG) is the government's chief legal adviser and lawyer. The **Comptroller and Auditor General of India** (CAG) is the independent auditor who checks, after the money is spent, whether it was spent lawfully and sensibly. Neither office makes policy. Both are tested by UPSC because both sit at the border between the executive (the government that governs) and the institutions that hold it to account.

## Words to define first

- **Constitutional office**: a post created directly by the Constitution. The AG (Article 76) and the CAG (Article 148) are constitutional offices.
- **Law officer**: a lawyer who advises or represents the government. The AG is the highest law officer in the country, in Laxmikanth's phrase.
- **Audit**: an independent after-the-event examination of accounts (bridge module B1).
- **Pleasure of the President**: the holder may be removed whenever the President chooses (module 2).

## The map

| Office | Main Articles | Appointed by | Tenure | Removal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attorney General of India | 76 (rights in Parliament 88; privileges 105) | President | Not fixed by the Constitution | At any time; during the pleasure of the President |
| Comptroller and Auditor General | 148 (duties 149; accounts form 150; reports 151; net proceeds 279) | President, by warrant under hand and seal | Six years or age 65, whichever is earlier | Like a Supreme Court judge: address of both Houses by special majority |
| Advocate General of a State | 165 (standard knowledge; not in the extracts) | Governor | During the pleasure of the Governor | At any time (module 4) |

## The one idea that explains both chapters

Look at the 'Removal' column. The AG is the government's *own* lawyer, so he serves while the government trusts him. The CAG *checks* the government, so the Constitution protects him as it protects a Supreme Court judge. Almost every rule you will learn in the next modules follows from that contrast: tenure, salary protection, ban on later jobs, and who the officer reports to.

## How to use the lesson

Core for the first pass: modules 1 to 3, 5 and 6. The Advocate General (module 4) and the audit types (module 7) come next. Modules 8 to 10 (debates, exam patterns and practice) are a deeper layer; go back to them after one full pass. The Articles above are from Laxmikanth's chapters 51 and 52; the full Constitution text was not read for this lesson, so check Article wording at legislative.gov.in before quoting it in the exam.

### 2. The Attorney General (Article 76): appointment, qualifications and term

*Objective:* State who the AG is, how he is appointed, who can be appointed, how long he serves and how he can leave office.

## Who he is

Article 76 provides for the office of the **Attorney General for India**. Laxmikanth stresses that this is the only Article that deals with the office; and he calls the AG the highest law officer in the country (chapter 52, page 979).

## Appointment and qualifications

The **President** appoints the AG. The person must be qualified to be appointed a **judge of the Supreme Court**. Spelled out in the textbook, he must be a citizen of India and must have been a judge of a High Court for five years, or an advocate of a High Court for ten years, or an eminent jurist in the opinion of the President. (The Supreme Court qualifications are the same; see GS-186 module 3. No minimum age is mentioned.)

A quick test of your understanding. A citizen who has practised in a High Court for eight years is *not* qualified on the advocate route, because ten years are required. A citizen who served as a High Court judge for five years is qualified. A well-known legal scholar who has never been a judge or advocate may be appointed only if the President considers him an eminent jurist. A foreign national fails the first condition.

## Term and removal

The Constitution does **not fix a term** for the AG and does **not lay down any procedure or grounds** for removal. He **holds office during the pleasure of the President**, which means the President may remove him at any time. He may resign by writing to the President. By **convention** (an unwritten practice) he resigns when the government (Council of Ministers) resigns or is replaced, because it was on its advice that he was appointed. A convention is not a rule of the Constitution: the written text says only 'pleasure of the President'. This difference is exactly what a UPSC statement tests (module 9).

## Remuneration

His remuneration is not fixed by the Constitution either; he receives what the President determines.

## What the AG is *not*

- He is **not a member of the Union Cabinet**. A separate law minister sits in the Cabinet for legal matters at the government level. Laxmikanth records (chapter 52, note 3) that during Nehru's time the Central government proposed merging the office of the AG with the law minister's office and that this did not materialise.
- He is **not a full-time counsel** of the government and **does not count as a government servant**. He may continue private legal practice, with the limits you will see in module 3.

## Why 'pleasure' tenure?

The AG advises and appears for the government of the day. If the government loses confidence in its lawyer, a fixed term would be awkward. Compare the CAG in module 5, whose job is to check the government and who therefore cannot be removed at its pleasure.

### 3. The Attorney General's duties, rights, limitations; Solicitor General

*Objective:* List the AG's duties, his rights in Parliament and courts, the five limitations, and say what the Solicitor General is.

## Duties and functions

As chief law officer of the Government of India the AG's duties (chapter 52, page 981) include: (1) giving advice to the Government of India on legal matters referred to him by the President; (2) performing other duties of a legal character assigned to him by the President; (3) discharging functions conferred on him by the Constitution or any other law.

In practice the President has assigned him three duties (by a 1950 notification cited by Laxmikanth): appear for the Government of India in all Supreme Court cases in which it is concerned; represent the Government of India in any **reference** made by the President to the Supreme Court under Article 143 (the advisory jurisdiction; GS-186 module 6); and appear, when required, in any High Court in a case concerning the Government of India.

## Rights

- **Right of audience in all courts** in the territory of India.
- **Right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses** of Parliament, or their joint sitting, and of any committee of which he is named a member, **but without a right to vote** (Article 88).
- All the **privileges and immunities of a member of Parliament** (Article 105).

Notice the balance: he may *speak* in Parliament even though he is not a member, but he cannot *vote*. It is this right that is meant when UPSC asks whether he may 'participate in the meetings of Parliament'.

## Limitations (to avoid conflict of duty)

1. He should not advise or hold a brief **against** the Government of India.
2. He should not advise or hold a brief in cases in which he is called upon to advise or appear **for** the Government of India (a conflict of loyalty).
3. He should not **defend accused persons in criminal prosecutions** without the Government's permission.
4. He should not accept a **directorship** in any company or corporation without the Government's permission.
5. He should not advise any ministry, department, statutory organisation or public sector undertaking unless the reference comes through the **Ministry of Law and Justice, Department of Legal Affairs**.

Because he is not full time and not a government servant, he is **not barred from private practice** (module 2).

## Solicitor General and Additional Solicitors General

Two more **law officers** of the Government of India assist the AG: the **Solicitor General of India** and the **Additional Solicitors General**. The important exam point is that **Article 76 does not mention them**: only the AG's office is created by the Constitution. Laxmikanth does not give their appointment details or their rights in Parliament. Standard knowledge (verify at legislative.gov.in): Article 88 gives every Minister and the Attorney General the right to speak in and take part in the proceedings of either House, a joint sitting and any committee of which he is named a member, without a vote. The Solicitor General is not named in Article 88 and has no such constitutional right. Their appointment and service terms are governed by the Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules, 1987 (verify).

### 4. The Advocate General of a State (Article 165) compared with the AG

*Objective:* State the main rules for the Advocate General of a State and compare them, row by row, with the AG of India.

## A flag before you start

Laxmikanth's chapter on the Advocate General (chapter 53) was not among the extracts read for this lesson. What follows is **standard knowledge** that you must verify at legislative.gov.in (Articles 165, 177, 194 and 217) before using it in an answer. GS-189 mentions the Advocate General as one of the officers whom the Governor appoints.

## The State's counterpart

Each State has an **Advocate General** (Article 165), the State's highest law officer. The Governor appoints him; he must be qualified to be a **High Court judge** (a citizen, with ten years' judicial office or ten years as a High Court advocate; Article 217(2), see GS-187 module 2). He holds office during the pleasure of the **Governor**, and his remuneration is what the Governor determines. His duties are to advise the State government on legal matters and perform legal duties assigned by the Governor. He has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of the State legislature (both Houses, if there are two) and its committees of which he is named a member, but without a vote (Article 177), with the privileges of a member (Article 194).

## Comparison

| Feature | Attorney General of India | Advocate General of a State |
|---|---|---|
| Article | 76 | 165 |
| Appointed by | President | Governor |
| Qualification | Qualified for appointment as a Supreme Court judge (High Court judge 5 years, or High Court advocate 10 years, or eminent jurist) | Qualified for appointment as a High Court judge (standard knowledge: 10 years judicial office or High Court advocate) |
| Tenure | Not fixed; pleasure of the President | Not fixed; pleasure of the Governor |
| Remuneration | Determined by the President | Determined by the Governor |
| Appears for | Government of India, in the Supreme Court and High Courts | State government, in the High Court and other courts |
| Right in legislature | Speak in both Houses of Parliament and committees; no vote (Article 88) | Speak in the State legislature and committees; no vote (Article 177) |
| Not a member of | Union Cabinet | State Council of Ministers |

## Two traps

First, the **qualification differs**: Supreme Court standard for the AG, High Court standard for the Advocate General. Second, Laxmikanth chapter 52 gives no removal procedure for the Attorney General, and for the Advocate General the standard position is the same pleasure rule (Article 165(3); verify): the Constitution leaves both on the 'pleasure' footing. The word 'pleasure' does not mean they can act unlawfully; it is only the rule on *how they leave office*.

### 5. The CAG (Article 148): appointment, term, removal and independence

*Objective:* Describe how the CAG is appointed, how long he serves, how he is removed and the six safeguards of his independence.

## Why the office exists

Article 148 provides for an independent **Comptroller and Auditor General of India**. He is head of the Indian Audit and Accounts Department, the 'guardian of the public purse', and controls (in Laxmikanth's words) the financial system at both Centre and State levels. Laxmikanth reports Dr B. R. Ambedkar's view that the CAG would be the most important officer under the Constitution; he lists the CAG with the Supreme Court, the Election Commission and the UPSC as the bulwarks of democratic government (GS-202 module 6).

## Appointment, oath, term

The **President** appoints the CAG by a **warrant under his hand and seal**. Before taking office he swears or affirms, before the President: (1) true faith and allegiance to the Constitution; (2) to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India; (3) to perform duties without fear or favour, affection or ill-will; (4) to uphold the Constitution and the laws (the form of oath is in the Third Schedule; standard knowledge, verify).

He holds office for **six years or up to the age of 65, whichever is earlier**. Laxmikanth does not name the legal source of this term; standard knowledge is that it comes from the 1971 Act rather than Article 148 itself (verify). He can resign by writing to the President. Clause map of Article 148 (standard knowledge; verify at legislative.gov.in): 148(1) appointment and removal; 148(2) oath; 148(3) salary by law, or as in the Second Schedule until fixed; 148(4) no further office under the Union or a State after retirement; 148(5) conditions of service of the IA&AD and administrative powers; 148(6) administrative expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India. This serves the 2018 Mains question on the method and terms of appointment.

## Removal

The President can remove him **on the same grounds and in the same manner as a Supreme Court judge**: a resolution of both Houses by special majority on proved misbehaviour or incapacity (the exact steps, including the Judges (Inquiry) Act, are in GS-186 module 3). The textbook's phrasing is useful: he is *appointed* by the President but does **not** hold office at the pleasure of the President.

## The six safeguards of independence (Laxmikanth)

1. **Security of tenure**: removal only by the constitutional procedure above.
2. **No later government job**: not eligible for any further office under the Government of India or a State after he leaves.
3. **Salary and conditions fixed by Parliament**; his salary equals that of a Supreme Court judge.
4. **No disadvantage**: his salary, leave, pension and age of retirement cannot be altered to his disadvantage after appointment.
5. **Staff and administration**: the conditions of service of the Indian Audit and Accounts Department and the CAG's administrative powers are prescribed by the President after consulting the CAG.
6. **Charged expenses**: the administrative expenses of the office, including salaries, allowances and pensions of its staff, are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** and are not subject to the vote of Parliament (bridge B2).

Further, **no minister can represent the CAG in Parliament**, and no minister is answerable for his actions. He answers to Parliament alone.

## What the extract does *not* tell you

Laxmikanth does not list qualifications for appointment as CAG or any consultation process. Standard knowledge (verify): the Constitution lays down no qualifications for the CAG, and the choice rests with the executive; critics often ask for a wider consultation process. Treat that as a reform question, not a rule from the text.

### 6. CAG duties and powers: Articles 149 to 151, the 1971 Act and the audit reports

*Objective:* Summarise what the CAG audits, the Articles involved, the three audit reports and the path of a report to Parliament and the Public Accounts Committee.

## The source of his powers

**Article 149** authorises Parliament to prescribe the CAG's duties and powers regarding the accounts of the Union, the States and any other authority or body. Parliament did so in the **CAG's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971**, which was **amended in 1976 to separate accounts from audit** in the Central government (departmentalisation of accounts): from then the CAG was relieved of compiling and maintaining the accounts of the Central government, though he still keeps those of the States (as the textbook states; verify later changes). The Union List also contains the subject 'audit of the accounts of the Union and of the States' (Entry 76) and 'emoluments and service conditions of ... the Comptroller and Auditor General' (Entry 75); see Appendix II.

## What he audits (group the list)

1. **Funds**: all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India, of each State and of each Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly; also the Contingency Fund and the Public Account of the Union and of the States.
2. **Government departments' commercial accounts**: trading, manufacturing, profit-and-loss accounts, balance sheets and subsidiary accounts of any department of the Centre or States.
3. **Receipts**: he audits receipts and expenditure of the Centre and each State to satisfy himself that the rules are designed to secure an effective check on the assessment, collection and proper allocation of revenue.
4. **Bodies**: those substantially financed from Central or State revenues, government companies, and other corporations or bodies when laws require.
5. **Debt and balances**: debt, sinking funds, deposits, advances, suspense and remittance business, and stores and stock accounts, with the President's approval or when the President requires.
6. **Others on request**: the accounts of any other authority when asked by the President or Governor, for example local bodies.

## Other Articles

- **Article 150**: the CAG advises the President on the **form in which accounts** of the Centre and States shall be kept.
- **Article 151**: his audit reports on the Union accounts go to the **President**, who lays them before **both Houses of Parliament**; reports on a State's accounts go to the **Governor**, who lays them before the **State legislature**.
- **Article 279**: he **ascertains and certifies the net proceeds** of a tax or duty (proceeds minus the cost of collection); his certificate is final (GS-193 module 2).
- He is **'guide, friend and philosopher'** of the Parliament's **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**.

## Three reports to the President

The CAG submits **three reports**: on **appropriation accounts**, on **finance accounts** and on **public undertakings**. Appropriation accounts compare *actual expenditure with the sums sanctioned by Parliament* through the Appropriation Act. Finance accounts show the *annual receipts and disbursements* of the Union government. After the President lays the reports, the **PAC examines them and reports to Parliament** (GS-182 module 3; GS-180 module 8 on checking spending after the money is gone).

## A chain, not a single step

CAG audits, then the report goes to the President or Governor, then the legislature, then the PAC (or State equivalent), then Parliament decides what to demand of the executive. Each link is a different institution: that division is what makes the system a check.

### 7. Kinds of audit: regularity, propriety, secret service and corporations

*Objective:* Distinguish regularity (legal and regulatory) audit from propriety audit, state the limits on the CAG's audit, and sort corporations into three audit categories.

## Regularity audit and propriety audit

The textbook (chapter 51, page 974) says the CAG has **to ascertain whether money shown as disbursed was legally available for and applicable to the service or purpose to which it was applied, and whether the expenditure conforms to the authority that governs it**. That is the **legal and regulatory audit**, often called regularity or compliance audit in modern writing (see bridge module B1). It is **obligatory**: the CAG must do it.

In addition he **can** conduct **propriety audit**: he can look into the **wisdom, faithfulness and economy** of government expenditure and comment on **wastefulness and extravagance**. Unlike regularity audit, propriety audit is **discretionary**.

An illustration (not from the textbook). A Ministry spends Rs 5 crore on a scheme Parliament funded but pays a supplier through a route the rules forbid: regularity fails. Another Ministry follows every rule but buys equipment that stays unused: regularity passes, propriety is in doubt. Standard knowledge adds that modern CAG reports also carry **performance audit** (efficiency, economy and effectiveness of programmes); this word is not in the extract, so verify before using it.

## Freedom varies by subject

The CAG has **more freedom over expenditure audit than over receipts, stores and stock**. For expenditure he decides the scope of audit and frames his own audit codes and manuals; for other audits he must proceed with the approval of the executive government as regards rules.

## A limit: secret service expenditure

For **secret service expenditure** the CAG cannot call for details from the executive agencies; he must accept a **certificate from the competent administrative authority** that the money was spent under his authority.

## Corporations: three categories (Laxmikanth)

| Category | Who audits | Examples in the textbook |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Audited totally and directly by the CAG | Damodar Valley Corporation, Oil and Natural Gas Commission, Air India, Indian Airlines Corporation |
| (ii) | Private professional auditors appointed by the Central government in consultation with the CAG; CAG may do a supplementary audit | Central Warehousing Corporation, Industrial Finance Corporation |
| (iii) | Wholly private audit; reports go directly to Parliament | Life Insurance Corporation, Reserve Bank of India, State Bank of India, Food Corporation of India |

These examples are the textbook's. Several have since changed status or name (for example, the textbook's Indian Airlines and Air India examples predate later restructuring and privatisation), so learn the *categories*, not the example list, and verify any example you want to use.

## Government companies

Laxmikanth says government companies are audited by **private auditors appointed by the Government on the advice of the CAG**, with the CAG able to undertake supplementary or test audit. Standard knowledge (verify): under the Companies Act 2013 the CAG appoints the statutory auditors of government companies. Where the two statements differ, quote the law, not the older textbook wording.

## Audit Board

In **1968** an **Audit Board** was set up within the CAG's office (on the Administrative Reforms Commission's recommendation) to bring in outside experts for specialised audits such as engineering, iron and steel and chemicals: a chairman and two members appointed by the CAG.

### 8. Comptroller or Auditor? The UK comparison, Appleby and the accountability role

*Objective:* Explain why the Indian CAG is called an Auditor General only, compare with the UK, summarise Appleby's critique and place the CAG in the accountability chain.

## Comptroller *and* Auditor General

The Constitution, in Laxmikanth's reading, visualises the CAG as **Comptroller as well as Auditor General**. **In practice he acts as Auditor General only.** He has **no control over the issue of money from the Consolidated Fund**; many departments are authorised to draw money by issuing cheques without specific authority from the CAG, and he enters only at the audit stage, when the expenditure has already taken place. In **Britain** the Comptroller and Auditor General has both powers: the executive can draw money from the public exchequer only with his approval. So the Indian CAG **differs entirely** from the British one. In Mains, 'post-mortem' is a fair description of Indian audit; the point is that the CAG detects, but cannot prevent, irregular payment.

| Point | India (as stated by Laxmikanth) | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|
| Issue of money | Departments draw money without prior CAG approval | Money drawn only with the CAG's approval |
| CAG's role in practice | Auditor only, after spending | Comptroller and auditor |

## Appleby's criticism

**Paul H. Appleby**, in two reports on Indian administration (1953 and 1956), criticised the CAG and even recommended abolishing the office. Among his points (Laxmikanth page 977): the office is an **inheritance from colonial rule**; auditing has a **repressive and negative influence** and is a cause of a widespread unwillingness to decide and act; Parliament has an exaggerated view of the importance of auditing; auditors cannot be expected to know much about good administration. Remember these as **a critique, not a ruling**: Parliament did not abolish the office, and the Constitution treats the CAG as a bulwark.

## How to use the criticism in an answer

State Appleby's argument fairly (audit can breed caution, 'file-pushing' and fear of decisions). Then answer it: the CAG's reports are the base on which Parliament's financial control rests; the safeguards in module 5 exist so the CAG can speak plainly; and propriety audit is discretionary, which allows the CAG to avoid trespassing on policy. A balanced Mains answer says that accountability and administrative initiative both matter and that audit should be sensible and timely.

## CAG reports and accountability (examples to use with care)

Executive accountability to Parliament in financial matters is secured through the CAG's reports (the textbook describes the CAG as an agent of Parliament, responsible only to it). The mechanism: report, then PAC, then Parliament. Widely discussed examples include the CAG's audit reports around 2010 to 2011 on telecom spectrum allocation (the '2G' case) and on preparations for the Commonwealth Games. These are **flagged examples only**: the loss estimates in public debate were disputed, and later legal outcomes differed from the early debate. If you use them, say 'reports of the CAG led to public and parliamentary scrutiny' and avoid quoting figures unless you have verified them.

## Strengthening the role (ideas for a Mains conclusion)

Timely tabling of reports; stronger follow-up on PAC recommendations; the CAG's access to information from all bodies that receive public money; outcome-focused audit; and respecting the CAG's constitutional independence (module 5). Present these as reform suggestions, not rules from the text.

## An MCQ, thought aloud

### Notes: Core definitions

- Attorney General of India: the highest law officer of the country, appointed by the President under Article 76; qualified to be a Supreme Court judge; holds office during the pleasure of the President.
- Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG): the independent head of the Indian Audit and Accounts Department under Article 148; the guardian of the public purse; removable only like a Supreme Court judge.
- Regularity (legal and regulatory) audit: checks that money was legally available for the purpose and that spending conforms to the governing authority; obligatory for the CAG.
- Propriety audit: examines the wisdom, faithfulness and economy of spending and comments on wastefulness and extravagance; discretionary.
- Audit versus comptroller control: an auditor examines after spending; a comptroller controls the issue of money in advance.
- Net proceeds: the proceeds of a tax or duty minus the cost of collection; the CAG's certificate is final (Article 279).

### Notes: CAG audit powers and types

- Obligatory: regularity (legal and regulatory) audit of expenditure from the Consolidated Funds, Contingency Funds and Public Accounts of the Union, States and Union Territories with a Legislative Assembly.
- Obligatory: audit of trading, manufacturing, profit and loss accounts and balance sheets of departments; audit of receipts to check assessment, collection and allocation of revenue.
- Discretionary: propriety audit of the wisdom, faithfulness and economy of expenditure.
- By request: accounts of any other authority when the President or Governor asks (for example local bodies).
- Three reports: appropriation accounts, finance accounts, public undertakings. The President lays them before both Houses; the PAC examines them.
- Limits: secret service expenditure (certificate accepted); more freedom over expenditure than over receipts, stores and stock; limited role in corporations and government companies.

### Notes: The reporting chain

- Union accounts: CAG, then the President (Article 151), then both Houses of Parliament, then the Public Accounts Committee, then the House.
- State accounts: CAG, then the Governor (Article 151), then the State legislature (GS-189 notes that the Governor lays the CAG's State report).
- Appropriation accounts compare actual spending with Parliament's sanction; finance accounts show annual receipts and disbursements of the Union.
- The CAG assists the PAC as guide, friend and philosopher (GS-182 module 3).

### Notes: Exceptions and confusions to avoid

- The CAG is appointed by the President but does not hold office at his pleasure; the AG does.
- Article 76 does not mention the Solicitor General or Additional Solicitors General; only the AG's office is constitutional.
- 'Conventionally resigns with the government' is a convention; the Constitution says 'pleasure of the President'.
- The AG may not defend accused persons in criminal cases or take a directorship without the Government's permission, but he is not barred from private practice.
- The CAG's reports go to the President (Union) or the Governor (State), not directly to Parliament; the PAC then examines.
- Regularity audit is obligatory; propriety audit is discretionary.
- The textbook's corporation examples (for example Air India, Indian Airlines) predate later changes; learn the three categories.

### Notes: Flags: points to verify against current sources

- Dates differ by milestone: 1753 (the Indian Audit and Accounts Department created under British rule, Laxmikanth's note) and 1860 (the office of Auditor General; verify). Low priority; not treated as a source slip.
- The six-year term of the CAG is stated by Laxmikanth without a legal source; standard knowledge links it to the 1971 Act (verify).
- Companies Act 2013: CAG appoints auditors of government companies (standard knowledge; verify); the textbook describes the older arrangement.
- Salary: Laxmikanth's note puts a Supreme Court judge's salary at Rs 2.50 lakh per month in 2018; salaries change, so do not quote the figure without checking.
- Advocate General details (Articles 165, 177, 194, 217) are standard knowledge, not from the extracts.

### Core concepts

- The AG is the government's legal adviser and lawyer; the CAG is the independent auditor of public money.
- Tenure contrast: AG at pleasure; CAG protected like a Supreme Court judge.
- CAG's accountability chain: audit, report to President or Governor, legislature, PAC.
- Regularity audit is obligatory; propriety audit is discretionary.
- The Indian CAG is an auditor, not a comptroller.

### Practice

**Q.** Which Article creates the office of the Attorney General of India?

**A.** Article 76.

**Q.** For how long does the CAG hold office?

**A.** Six years or up to age 65, whichever is earlier.

**Q.** Who submits the CAG's audit reports on the Union's accounts to Parliament, and which Article?

**A.** The President lays them before both Houses (Article 151).

**Q.** What are the three audit reports the CAG submits?

**A.** Appropriation accounts, finance accounts and public undertakings.

**Q.** Explain why the AG holds office at the pleasure of the President while the CAG does not.

**A.** The AG is the government's own legal adviser and lawyer, so he serves while the government trusts him; the CAG checks the government, so his tenure is protected like a Supreme Court judge's.

**Q.** Why is the Indian CAG called an Auditor General only?

**A.** He has no control over the issue of money from the Consolidated Fund; departments draw money without his prior approval and he audits afterwards.

**Q.** Distinguish regularity audit from propriety audit.

**A.** Regularity checks legal authority and conformity with rules and is obligatory; propriety examines wisdom, faithfulness and economy and is discretionary.

**Q.** Why does the CAG's salary equal that of a Supreme Court judge and fall under 'charged' expenditure?

**A.** To secure independence from executive pressure and from yearly parliamentary bargaining.

**Q.** A new Ministry wants the AG to advise a public sector undertaking directly. What limitation applies, and what route is needed?

**A.** He should not advise any ministry, department, statutory body or public sector undertaking unless the reference is received through the Ministry of Law and Justice, Department of Legal Affairs.

**Q.** A State government departments' accounts show spending above what the Appropriation Act sanctioned. Which CAG report and which parliamentary body are most relevant?

**A.** The appropriation accounts audit report, which compares actual spending with sanction; the Public Accounts Committee examines it (State equivalent for State accounts).

**Q.** A politician proposes that the CAG should approve every payment before money is issued. What is the constitutional vision and what is current practice?

**A.** The Constitution visualises a Comptroller as well as an Auditor General; in practice the CAG audits only after the expenditure, unlike the British C&AG.
