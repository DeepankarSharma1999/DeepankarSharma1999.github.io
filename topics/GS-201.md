### Bridge: The Seventh Schedule: Union, State and Concurrent Lists in one table

The Constitution divides subjects of law-making in the **Seventh Schedule** (a Schedule is an appendix to the Constitution; an Article is a numbered rule in the main text; a Part is a group of Articles). It contains three **Lists**. Under Article 246 each List decides who may legislate.

| List | Who makes laws | Examples (as listed by NCERT) | Entries today (Laxmikanth 2019) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Union List** | Parliament alone | Defence, atomic energy, foreign affairs, war and peace, banking, railways, post and telegraph, airways, ports, foreign trade, currency and coinage | 98 (last numbered 97) |
| **State List** | Normally the State legislature alone | Agriculture, police, prison, local government, public health, land, liquor, livestock and animal husbandry, State public services | 59 (last numbered 66) |
| **Concurrent List** | Both Parliament and State legislatures | Education, transfer of property other than agricultural land, forests, trade unions, adulteration, adoption and succession | 52 (last numbered 47) |
| **Residuary** | Parliament alone | Every subject in no List (NCERT's example: cyber laws) | not a List |

Two rules matter. First, on a **Concurrent List** subject both can legislate, and if their laws conflict the **Union law prevails** (standard rule of Article 254; verify the wording before quoting it). Exception, Article 254(2) (standard knowledge; verify): a State law on a Concurrent subject that has received the President's assent prevails in that State, unless Parliament later legislates on the same matter. Second, **residuary power belongs to Parliament**, unlike the USA, where it belongs to the States; that is one reason scholars call the Indian system strongly centred.

Note also that the Concurrent List contains **no tax entries**: taxing power sits in the Union and State Lists, and concurrent power to levy the goods and services tax was conferred by Article 246A, inserted by the 101st Amendment (2016), outside the Seventh Schedule Lists (a simplification: Concurrent List Entry 44 covers stamp duties other than judicial stamps; verify). The exact entry counts above come from Laxmikanth's notes (2019); the 'last numbered' figures differ from the totals because some entries were added or omitted.

### Bridge: Unitary, federal and quasi-federal: three words and why they matter

In a **unitary** state one central government holds all legal power; any lower governments are created and controlled by it. In a **federation** there are at least two levels of government, each autonomous in its own sphere under a written constitution that neither can alter at will, with an independent court to settle disputes between them. **Autonomy** means freedom to take decisions in one's own sphere. **Secession** means a unit leaving the country; NCERT stresses that demands for autonomy are different from demands for secession.

NCERT adds that federalism is not a fixed set of principles: American, German and Indian federalism differ, and the **actual practice** (trust, restraint, party politics) decides how a constitution works. Dual citizenship exists in some federations; India has **single citizenship**.

India's Constitution calls the country a **'Union of States'** (Article 1) and avoids the word 'federation'. Many commentators therefore call the system **'quasi-federal'**, meaning it has federal features (two levels of government, division of powers, independent judiciary) combined with unitary or centralising features (powerful Centre, emergency powers, financial dominance, appointment of Governors by the Centre). The term is usually credited to K. C. Wheare (standard knowledge; verify before attributing). Sarkaria's own description is that federalism is better understood as a **functional arrangement for co-operative action** than as a static institutional label.

Why this matters for UPSC: Prelims asks which listed items are or are not federal features (2017 and 2021 PYQs); Mains asks whether the unitary tilt is necessary for unity and how far the system is really cooperative. Keep the two-column list of features in module 2 ready.

### 1. What federalism is, and why India chose a 'Union of States'

*Objective:* State what a federation is, what NCERT says makes federalism succeed or fail, and how India's Constitution describes itself.

## The idea

NCERT defines federalism as an **institutional mechanism to accommodate two sets of polities**: one at the regional level and one at the national level. Each government is autonomous in its own sphere; citizens carry **two loyalties** (a Gujarati or a Jharkhandi, and an Indian); the details of the division are in a **written constitution** which is supreme and is the source of power of both governments; and an **independent judiciary** settles disputes between the Centre and the States. Subjects of national concern, such as defence and currency, belong to the Union; regional and local matters to the States.

## Why the form is not enough

NCERT's examples make a sharp point: the USSR, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and Pakistan were federations or federal-like, and still broke up; Canada came close; the **West Indies federation** (1958) dissolved in 1962 because of a weak centre and rivalry between units; Nigeria's federation was strained by ethnic, religious and oil-revenue conflicts. So the **practice** of federalism (trust, cooperation, mutual respect and restraint, and the behaviour of political parties) matters as much as the constitutional text. If one unit, language or ideology dominates, resentment and secession demands follow.

## India's choice

Before Independence, national leaders accepted that a country as large and diverse as India needed a division of powers between provinces and the Centre. After Partition, the Constituent Assembly framed a government 'based on the principles of **unity and cooperation** between the Centre and the States and separate powers to the States'. NCERT calls cooperation **the most important feature** of the federal system adopted. The Constitution does not use the word 'federation'; **Article 1(1)** says: 'India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.'

## Strong centre, by design

NCERT explains why the framers created a strong Centre: India's size and diversity, more than 500 princely States to be integrated, and socio-economic problems such as poverty, illiteracy and inequality which needed planning and coordination. T. T. Krishnamachari's remark, quoted by NCERT, was that the drift in all constitutions has been towards the Centre because States have become welfare States and the ultimate responsibility for the country's economic well-being rests with the Centre.

## Where this lesson fits

This lesson maps the **tensions** in that design and the **institutions that try to heal them**. Day 1's **GS-193 (Finance Commission and grants)** and **GS-200 (special provisions for States and Union Territories)** are cross-referenced rather than repeated. **GS-194 (Inter-State Council and dispute resolution)** and **GS-173 (Landmark constitutional judgments)** come later on Day 2, and **GS-189 (Governor and Chief Minister)** on Day 4.

## Three words to carry forward

- **Cooperative federalism**: Centre and States act as partners.
- **Competitive federalism**: States compete with each other for investment or ranking (standard usage; module 4).
- **Coercive federalism**: the Centre uses constitutional or financial leverage to impose its choice (a Mains label, not a constitutional term; module 4).

### 2. Federal features, unitary tilts and the label 'quasi-federal'

*Objective:* Sort Indian constitutional features into federal and unitary columns, using the NCERT list, and judge a statement about 'features of Indian federalism'.

## Federal features

- **Two sets of governments with constitutional status** and clearly identified areas of activity: the Union and the States.
- **Division of powers** through three Lists (bridge module B1). A dispute over which list a subject belongs to is settled by the judiciary.
- **Written, supreme Constitution** and an **independent judiciary** that umpires federal disputes (NCERT; the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction under Article 131 is in GS-186).
- **Representation of the States in the Union legislature**: NCERT notes that Rajya Sabha representation is **asymmetrical** (unequal by population, with a minimum for small States), unlike a federation that gives equal seats.
- Single Constitution for both levels; State governments are not delegates of the Centre.

## Unitary or centralising features (NCERT list)

| Area | What the Constitution does |
|---|---|
| Territory | Parliament can form a new State, unite States, alter boundaries or names, with the State legislature's view sought as a safeguard |
| Emergencies | In an emergency Parliament can make laws on State subjects and power is lawfully centralised |
| Money | Centre controls the main revenue sources; States depend on grants and loans; planning added centralisation |
| Governor | Appointed by the Centre; can recommend dismissal of the State government and dissolution of the Assembly; can reserve a State bill for the President |
| Law-making | Parliament may legislate on State List subjects if the Rajya Sabha supports (the 'national interest' provision, standard Article 249; verify) |
| Executive power | Article 257(1): the State's executive power must not impede the Union's, and the Union may give directions to the State |
| Services | **All-India Services** (IAS, IPS and others) are common to the whole country; States can neither discipline nor remove such officers |
| Special laws | Articles 33 and 34 allow protection of persons acting during martial law; NCERT notes the AFSPA is based on these provisions |

## The label 'quasi-federal'

Because both columns are populated, commentators call the system **quasi-federal** (bridge module B2). Its defenders, including the Constituent Assembly voices that NCERT quotes, say a strong Centre was needed for unity and development; its critics say it leaves States as dependants. The Sarkaria Commission (module 6) rejected the demand to cut Centre's powers but warned against over-centralisation; the Punchhi Commission (module 6) chose **cooperative federalism** as the key.

## Two points from standard knowledge (verify)

Dr Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly argument, commonly cited, was that India is a 'Union' because it is **not the result of an agreement among the States** and no State has the right to **secede**. This is relevant to a 2017 Prelims PYQ that asks which item is **not** a feature of Indian federalism (module 10). Separately, the Supreme Court is commonly said to have treated federalism as part of the basic structure (S. R. Bommai, 1994; see GS-173); check that reference before quoting it.

## Why the tilt is persistently debated

Unity argument: after Partition and the integration of 500-plus princely States, centralised powers kept the country together. Autonomy argument: diversity of language and culture, and responsibility for welfare delivery at the State level, need matching powers and money. NCERT's closing line: no formula can guarantee smooth federalism; trust and a political culture of cooperation are decisive.

### 3. Legislative, administrative and financial relations in outline

*Objective:* Recall the key Articles of each branch of Centre-State relations and the three financial ideas that matter for this lesson: tax allocation, grants and protection of States.

## Legislative relations (Articles 245-255: standard outline, verify)

The extracts you have read do not include this part of Laxmikanth, so treat the list as an outline to be checked. **Article 245** gives territorial reach; **Article 246** allocates subjects among the three Lists (bridge module B1); **Article 248** gives residuary power to Parliament; **Article 249** lets Parliament legislate on a State List subject in the national interest if the Rajya Sabha supports a resolution (NCERT: the move must be 'ratified by the Rajya Sabha'); **Article 250** does the same during a national emergency; **Article 252** lets Parliament legislate for States with their consent; **Article 253** allows legislation to implement treaties (Punchhi calls for a law on Entry 14 of the Union List to streamline this); **Article 254** resolves conflict between Union and State laws on the Concurrent List in the Union's favour, with the exception in Article 254(2): a State law on a Concurrent subject that has received the President's assent prevails in that State unless Parliament later legislates on the matter (standard knowledge; verify).

## Administrative relations (Articles 256-263 and 355)

Source table lists: **256** (obligation of States and the Union), **257** (control of the Union over States in certain cases), **258** (Union conferring powers on States), **258A** (States entrusting functions to the Union, added by the 7th Amendment 1956), **261** (public acts, records and proceedings), **262** (inter-State river disputes), **263** (Inter-State Council). NCERT quotes Article 257(1): the executive power of the State must not impede the Union's, and the Union may issue directions. **Article 355** (not in the table but cited by the Punchhi Commission) makes it the Union's duty to protect States; it is the basis for debates on deploying central forces. **All-India Services** give the Centre a continuous presence in the State administration (module 7).

## Financial relations (Articles 268-293)

- **Taxing power.** Parliament has exclusive power over Union List taxes (13 entries); the State legislature over State List taxes (18 entries); the **Concurrent List contains no tax entries**; concurrent power to levy **goods and services tax (GST)** was conferred on Parliament and the States by **Article 246A**, inserted by the **101st Amendment (2016)**, outside the Seventh Schedule Lists (Laxmikanth's 'no tax entries' is a simplification; Concurrent List Entry 44 covers stamp duties other than judicial stamps; verify). **Residuary taxation** (taxes not in any List) belongs to Parliament; gift tax, wealth tax and expenditure tax were imposed under it.
- **Levy versus proceeds.** The Constitution distinguishes the power to **levy and collect** a tax from the power to **appropriate** its proceeds. Categories: duties levied by the Centre but collected and kept by States (Article 268, e.g. certain stamp duties); taxes levied and collected by the Centre but assigned to States (Article 269); GST on inter-State supply levied and collected by the Centre and divided as Parliament provides on the GST Council's recommendation (Article 269A); taxes levied and collected by the Centre and distributed between Centre and States on the Finance Commission's recommendation (Article 270); **surcharge** for the Centre alone (Article 271).
- **Grants.** Statutory grants-in-aid on the Finance Commission's recommendation (Article 275) and **discretionary grants** which either government can give for any public purpose (Article 282). Laxmikanth quotes that discretionary grants help States fulfil plan targets and give the Centre some leverage over States.
- **Finance Commission** (Article 280) is called the 'balancing wheel of fiscal federalism'. Its composition and functions are in **GS-193**.
- **Protection of States.** A bill that imposes or varies a tax in which States are interested, or affects the principles on which taxes are distributed, or imposes a Central surcharge, can be introduced in Parliament only on the **President's recommendation**.
- **Borrowing.** The Centre can borrow within or outside India; a State can borrow only within India, and cannot borrow without the Centre's consent while any Central loan or guarantee is outstanding.
- **Emergencies.** A national emergency lets the President modify the distribution of revenues until the end of the financial year in which the emergency ceases to operate; a financial emergency lets the Centre direct States on financial propriety and salary cuts.
- **Immunities.** Union property is exempt from State taxation, State property and income from Central taxation, with limits (customs and excise on State goods can still be levied, per a 1963 advisory opinion).

### 4. Cooperative, competitive and coercive federalism, and the forums of cooperation

*Objective:* Define the three styles of federalism and explain, with Articles and numbers, how the Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, NITI Aayog and the GST Council work.

## Cooperative federalism

Laxmikanth, in the NITI Aayog chapter, says the NITI Aayog was constituted to actualise **cooperative federalism**: it is impossible for any federal government to achieve national objectives without active cooperation from the States, so the Centre and States must work **together as equals**. Two key aspects: (i) **joint focus on the national development agenda** by Centre and States; (ii) **advocacy of State perspectives** with Central ministries. The Punchhi Commission (2010) concluded that cooperative federalism will be the key to India's unity, integrity and development.

## Competitive and coercive (standard usage; verify)

**Competitive federalism**: States compete with one another for investment, rankings and performance; it is usually discussed with NITI Aayog indices. **Coercive federalism**: a Mains label for situations where the Centre uses constitutional tools (Governor, Article 356, directions under Article 257) or financial leverage (conditional grants, cess and surcharge, central schemes) to impose its preference. Neither term is defined in the sources you read, so define them in your own words in an answer. The 2020 Mains question asks how cooperation, competition and **confrontation** have shaped the federation: bring one example for each from this lesson.

## The forums

| Forum | Basis | Members and working |
|---|---|---|
| **Inter-State Council** | Article 263; set up in **1990** after the Sarkaria Commission | PM chairs; chief ministers of all States and of Union Territories with legislatures; administrators of other Union Territories; Governors of States under President's rule; six Union Cabinet ministers; recommendatory; meets at least three times a year; decisions by consensus; Standing Committee since 1996; Secretariat since 1991 (also secretariat of the Zonal Councils since 2011) |
| **Zonal Councils** | Statutory (States Reorganisation Act, 1956), five zones | Union Home Minister is the common chairman; chief ministers as vice-chairman by rotation; deliberative and advisory; North-Eastern Council by a 1971 Act |
| **NITI Aayog** | Replaced the Planning Commission in 2015 (standard knowledge; the extract pages do not give the date; verify) | Meetings of the Governing Council; sub-groups of Chief Ministers; task forces; NITI Forum for North East; Sustainable Development in the Indian Himalayan Region; **Development Support Services to States**; **SATH** programme to help States improve social-sector indicators |
| **GST Council** | **Article 279A**, 101st Amendment 2016; constituted by the President in September 2016 | Union Finance Minister as chairperson; Union Minister of State for Revenue or Finance; one minister of each State; States choose a vice-chairperson |

## The GST Council's voting arithmetic

The Council decides by a majority of **not less than three-fourths of the weighted votes** of members present and voting. The **Centre has one-third of the weight** and **all States together two-thirds**. Assume everyone votes.

- States alone: at most two-thirds, that is 66.7 per cent, which is **below 75 per cent**, so States cannot decide without the Centre.
- The Centre alone: one-third, 33.3 per cent, far below 75 per cent.
- Therefore every decision needs the Centre plus States holding at least 75 minus 33.3, that is 41.7 percentage points of the total, which is **62.5 per cent of the States' combined weight** (41.7 divided by 66.7).
- A bloc holding **more than 25 per cent** can block: the Centre alone can; States alone would need more than 25 divided by 66.7, that is **37.5 per cent of their combined weight**.

This makes the Council a body where neither side can dominate, which is why the 2023 Mains question asks how far the 101st Amendment reflects the 'accommodative spirit of federalism'. The Council also recommends compensation to States for revenue loss for **five years**, with Parliament enacting the law in 2017, and must set up a mechanism to adjudicate disputes between the Centre and States or among States. In Mohit Minerals (2022) the Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding on Parliament or State legislatures, a point central to the 2023 Mains question on the Council and federalism (verify).

### 5. Tension areas and the early demands: ARC, Rajamannar, Anandpur Sahib, West Bengal

*Objective:* State the twelve tension areas and trace four early attempts to reframe Centre-State relations with correct years and bodies.

## How tension began

Laxmikanth records that until 1967 Centre-State relations were by and large smooth because the same party ruled at the Centre and in most States. In the 1967 elections the Congress lost in **nine States** and weakened at the Centre; non-Congress State governments raised the issue of **State autonomy**, demanding more powers and financial resources. NCERT describes three phases: Congress dominance in the 1950s and early 1960s; opposition governments in States from the mid-1960s; and, since the 1990s, **coalition politics** at the Centre, giving States a greater say and 'a more mature federalism'.

## Twelve tension areas (Laxmikanth)

1. Mode of appointment and dismissal of the Governor. 2. Partisan role of Governors. 3. President's Rule imposed for partisan interests. 4. Deployment of Central forces in States. 5. Reservation of State bills for the President. 6. Discrimination in financial allocations. 7. The Planning Commission's role in approving State projects. 8. Management of All-India Services. 9. Use of electronic media for political purposes. 10. Enquiry commissions against Chief Ministers. 11. Sharing of finances. 12. Encroachment by the Centre on the State List.

NCERT groups the demands for autonomy into four types: changes in the **division of powers**, **financial autonomy**, **administrative powers**, and **cultural and linguistic issues** (for example, resistance to Hindi imposition in Tamil Nadu). It also lists demands for new States, border disputes (Belgaum between Maharashtra and Karnataka; Manipur and Nagaland), the Chandigarh question between Punjab and Haryana, and river-water disputes such as the Cauvery and Narmada.

## Four early attempts

| Year | Body | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **1966-69** | **Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC)**, six members, under Morarji Desai and later K. Hanumanthayya; M. C. Setalvad study team | 22 recommendations: Inter-State Council under Article 263; non-partisan, experienced Governors; maximum delegation to States; more financial resources to States; armed forces deployment. **No action taken.** |
| **1969-71** | **Rajamannar Committee** appointed by the **Tamil Nadu (DMK) Government** | Reasons for unitary trends (special powers of the Centre, one-party rule, States' financial inadequacy, central planning); recommendations: Inter-State Council, permanent Finance Commission, disband the Planning Commission, omit Articles 356, 357 and 365, shift subjects to the State List, residuary powers to States, abolish All-India Services. **Ignored by the Centre.** |
| **1973** | **Anandpur Sahib Resolution** (Akali Dal) | Centre's jurisdiction only for defence, foreign affairs, communications and currency; residuary powers to States; 'federal in real sense' |
| **1977** | **West Bengal Memorandum** (Left Front Government) | Replace 'union' by 'federal'; Centre confined to defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications and economic coordination; omit Articles 356, 357, 360; 75 per cent of revenue to States; equal Rajya Sabha powers; abolish All-India Services. **Not accepted.** |

## Reading the pattern

Notice who made the demand: opposition-ruled States (Tamil Nadu, Punjab, West Bengal). That is the point behind the 2022 Mains statement that national parties favour centralisation while regional parties favour autonomy: examine it with a **balanced** view. National parties in power have tended to centralise, but the same parties in opposition at the State level demand autonomy; and regional parties, once in alliances at the Centre, have also used central power. NCERT's phases support this reading.

### 6. The Sarkaria Commission (1983-88) and the Punchhi Commission (2007-10)

*Objective:* State each Commission's approach and recall at least five of its recommendations with the correct subject.

## Sarkaria Commission (appointed 1983, reported 1988)

A three-member commission under **R. S. Sarkaria**, a retired Supreme Court judge, was asked to review working of Centre-State arrangements in all spheres. Its term was extended four times. Its approach: it **did not favour structural changes**, regarded the constitutional arrangements as basically sound and called for changes in functioning; federalism is more a **functional arrangement for co-operative action** than a static institutional concept; it rejected curtailment of the Centre's powers because a strong Centre is essential for unity, but said that over-centralisation causes 'blood pressure at the centre and anaemia at the periphery'. It made **247 recommendations**; Laxmikanth records that 180 were implemented, the most important being the Inter-State Council in 1990.

Selected recommendations:

- A permanent **Inter-State Council** (to be called the Inter-Governmental Council) under Article 263.
- **Article 356 to be used very sparingly**, in extreme cases as a last resort.
- Strengthen and add **All-India Services**.
- Residuary taxation stays with Parliament; **other residuary powers into the Concurrent List**.
- Reasons to be communicated when the President withholds assent to State bills.
- The Centre should **consult States** before legislating on a Concurrent List subject; may deploy its armed forces without State consent, but should consult.
- Consult the Chief Minister in appointing the Governor; the Governor **cannot dismiss** a council of ministers that has majority; a five-year term should not be disturbed except for compelling reasons.
- Corporation tax may be shareable; surcharge on income tax only for a specific purpose and limited period.

## Punchhi Commission (appointed April 2007, reported April 2010)

A commission under **Madan Mohan Punchhi**, a former Chief Justice of India, was set up to re-examine Centre-State relations after the sea-changes in polity and economy since Sarkaria. Its report ran to 1,456 pages in seven volumes; it relied on the Sarkaria report, the NCRWC report and the Second ARC report, differing in several areas. It made **over 310 recommendations** and concluded that **cooperative federalism** would be the key.

| Theme | Selected Punchhi recommendations |
|---|---|
| Legislation | Broad Union-State agreement before legislating on a Concurrent List subject; the Union should be 'extremely restrained' in asserting parliamentary supremacy; take only those concurrent subjects needed for uniformity |
| Governor | Selection guidelines (eminent person, from outside the State, detached from local politics, not recently active in politics); fixed five-year tenure; impeachment procedure like the President; Article 163 gives no general discretionary power; decide on assent to Bills within six months; clear guidelines in a hung Assembly (Punchhi's order of preference for calling a Chief Minister: (a) the group of parties with a pre-poll alliance commanding the largest number; (b) the largest single party staking a claim with the support of others; (c) a post-electoral coalition with all partners joining the government; (d) a post-electoral alliance with some parties joining and the rest, including independents, supporting from outside); floor test before dismissing a Chief Minister; do not make Governors university chancellors |
| Emergency | Exhaust Article 355 remedies before Article 356; incorporate the *S. R. Bommai* (1994) guidelines; consider a 'localised emergency' |
| Institutions | Strengthen Article 263; Zonal Councils to meet at least twice a year; forum of Chief Ministers; new All-India Services in health, education, engineering and judiciary; equal Rajya Sabha representation of States |
| Finance | Cost-sharing for central laws implemented by States (as in the RTE Act); revise mineral royalties every three years; remove profession tax ceiling; review cesses and surcharges; involve States in setting the Finance Commission's terms of reference; Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission (Article 307) |

The report was circulated, and the comments received are with the Inter-State Council (as Laxmikanth records); do not claim that the report has been implemented.

### 7. Governor, President's Rule and All-India Services

*Objective:* Explain why these three are recurring flash-points, what the sources say about Article 356 and judicial review, and give legal and political reasons for the decline of its use.

## The Governor

NCERT's account: the Governor is **not elected**; many have been retired military officers, civil servants or politicians; the Governor is **appointed by the central government**, so actions are often viewed as central interference, especially when different parties rule at the Centre and the State. The Governor can recommend dismissal of the State government and dissolution of the Assembly, and can reserve a State bill for the President, which gives the Centre a chance to delay or veto State legislation. Sarkaria recommended non-partisan appointments; Punchhi added the selection guidelines, five-year tenure and a six-month limit on assent (module 6). The full legal position is in **GS-189 (Governor and Chief Minister, Day 4)**.

## Article 356 (President's Rule)

Article 356 applies when 'a situation has arisen in which the Government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution'. It results in the takeover of the State government by the Union. The proclamation has to be **ratified by Parliament**, and President's Rule can be extended up to **three years** (NCERT). NCERT records its misuse: governments were dismissed even when they had a majority (Kerala, 1959) or without testing their majority (several States after 1967); Article 356 was used sparingly until 1967, and after 1967 many States had non-Congress governments while Congress ruled the Centre; elected governments in Andhra Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir were removed in the 1980s. Some cases went to the Supreme Court and **the Court ruled that the constitutional validity of the decision to impose President's Rule can be examined by the judiciary**.

**S. R. Bommai v Union of India (1994):** the sources read here support only this: Punchhi calls it a 'landmark judgement' that set strict parameters for Articles 352 and 356 to be used as a 'last resort', and wants its guidelines incorporated by constitutional amendment. The usual textbook details (the floor of the House as the test of majority, Parliament's approval before the Assembly is dissolved, secularism as basic structure) are standard knowledge to verify and are covered in **GS-173**.

## Why Article 356 is used less often since the mid-1990s (2023 Mains frame)

- **Legal factors**: judicial review of the proclamation; the *Bommai* guidelines; the insistence that majority be tested on the floor; the Sarkaria and Punchhi pressure to use Article 356 as a last resort after Article 355 alternatives; the constitutional safeguards that Parliament has added over time (standard knowledge; verify).
- **Political factors**: NCERT notes that since the 1990s one-party dominance has ended and **coalition politics** at the Centre gives States and regional parties a greater say; a Centre dependent on regional allies cannot easily dismiss State governments.

## All-India Services

NCERT: we have an integrated administrative system; the IAS and IPS officers who serve as collector or police chief are under **central control**; States can neither take disciplinary action nor remove them. The Constitution allows such services to be created (Article 312, standard knowledge; verify). Positions: Sarkaria (strengthen and add more), Punchhi (create new ones in health, education, engineering and judiciary), Rajamannar and West Bengal (abolish). The 'tension area' is the dual loyalty of the officer: serving the State but disciplined by the Centre.

## Link to the Centre's executive power

Article 257(1) and Article 355 together underline that the Union may direct and protect States, which is why the Punchhi suggestion of a 'localised emergency' (a tool short of Article 356) was proposed: to let the Centre respond to a local crisis without dismissing the elected government.

### 8. Fiscal federalism tensions: cesses, surcharges, schemes, GST and devolution

*Objective:* Explain the vertical fiscal imbalance and apply the sourced points on surcharges, cesses, discretionary grants, the Planning Commission and GST compensation to a Mains question.

## The basic imbalance

NCERT states that economic and financial powers are **centralised**: the States have 'immense responsibilities but very meagre revenue sources', depending on grants and financial assistance from the Centre. This gap between responsibilities and revenues is called **vertical fiscal imbalance**. The Finance Commission (GS-193) and the GST Council (module 4) are the two constitutional correctives.

## Sourced pressure points

| Instrument | Why it causes tension | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Surcharge** (Article 271) | Proceeds go to the Centre alone; States have no share; Sarkaria wanted it only for a specific purpose and limited period | Laxmikanth p348, p361 |
| **Cess** (levied for a specific purpose) | Article 270 excludes cesses from the divisible pool; Punchhi asked the Centre to review all cesses and surcharges and reduce their share in gross tax revenue | Laxmikanth p348, p368 |
| **Discretionary grants** (Article 282) | The Centre may give them for any public purpose; Laxmikanth quotes that they help meet plan targets and give the Centre leverage over State action; NCERT notes charges of discrimination against opposition-ruled States | Laxmikanth p350; NCERT p12 |
| **Planning Commission** | A non-constitutional, non-statutory body of Centre alone that controlled State plans; critics called it a 'Super Cabinet' and an 'Economic Cabinet'; Santhanam: planning 'has superseded the federation'; Rajamannar: overlap with the Finance Commission | Laxmikanth p1010-1012 |
| **Central laws implemented by States** | Punchhi: future Central laws needing States' involvement should provide cost sharing, as in the RTE Act; current laws should be amended likewise | Laxmikanth p368 |
| **Finance Commission terms of reference** | Punchhi: terms should be even-handed and States should be involved in finalising them | Laxmikanth p368 |

*Centrally sponsored schemes* (standard usage; verify the present scheme structure): programmes designed by Union ministries but funded jointly and implemented by the States. They are a modern form of the Centre's leverage; States complain of rigid conditions and matching-share obligations. The extracts do not give figures, so give no numbers in an answer unless you have a dated source.

## GST: pooled sovereignty, shared risk

The 101st Amendment merged many Union and State indirect taxes (excise, service tax, State VAT, entry tax, central surcharges and cesses on goods and services and others) into one GST. States lost independent power to fix rates on those items; rates are now largely settled through the **Council** where States have two-thirds weight but the Centre can block; its recommendations are persuasive rather than binding on legislatures (Mohit Minerals, 2022; verify). In return, the Council must recommend compensation for States' revenue loss for **five years**, and Parliament enacted the compensation law in 2017. Disputes on compensation after the shortfall years, and on borrowing arrangements to cover the gap, are recent developments **not in the sources**: verify them before writing, and do not state figures.

## Devolution disputes

The Finance Commission recommends the sharing of the divisible pool and grants; the recommendations are advisory (GS-193). The contested points are the share, the conditions attached to grants, and whether a growing share of Central revenue comes through cesses and surcharges that are outside the pool. Cite the Punchhi recommendations (review cesses; involve States in terms of reference) and flag any figure as 'verify'.

## A Mains skeleton

1. The imbalance (NCERT). 2. Constitutional correctives (Finance Commission, Article 275, GST Council). 3. Pressure points (surcharge, cess, discretionary grants, schemes). 4. GST's accommodative design and limits. 5. Way forward (Punchhi: cost-sharing, transparent terms, review of cesses; stable compensation or revenue guarantee).

### 9. Contemporary challenges and the way forward: a Mains framework

*Objective:* Build a four-part Mains structure (design, tensions, institutions, reform) and place the recent PYQs (CBI consent, special laws, asymmetric federalism) in it with correct verification flags.

## The four-part frame

1. **Design**: Union of States; strong Centre; division of powers; independent judiciary (modules 1-3).
2. **Tension domains**: legislative (Concurrent List, special laws), administrative (Governor, All-India Services, central agencies), financial (module 8), and territorial or regional (new States, borders, rivers).
3. **Institutions of cooperation**: Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, NITI Aayog, GST Council, Finance Commission, courts and tribunals (module 4).
4. **Reform**: Punchhi and Sarkaria; changes in practice (module 6).

## Legislative centralisation (2020 Mains Q11)

The question links centralising tendencies to the **Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897**, the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** and the **farm Acts**. The sources here do not cover these laws. Use the structural points you do have: Parliament's power over State subjects in the national interest (Article 249), in an emergency (Article 250), and the Union's directions (Article 257), plus the fact that agriculture sits in the State List (NCERT). Verify: how these Acts allow the Centre to act in health and agriculture and what happened to the farm laws before using them.

## Central agencies and State consent (2021 Mains Q11)

The CBI question tests the principle that the police is a **State List** subject (NCERT's list), so a central agency operating inside a State normally needs the State's consent, whereas a court can order an inquiry; a State's refusal is not absolute. The Delhi Special Police Establishment Act and recent court rulings are standard knowledge, not in the sources: verify and cite carefully.

## Asymmetric federalism (2026 Mains Q13)

NCERT notes the 'differential treatment' of States: special provisions for the North-East because of indigenous tribal populations; for hilly States such as Himachal Pradesh; for several others (Andhra Pradesh, Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Sikkim, Telangana); and the **former special status of Jammu and Kashmir under Article 370**, which no longer exists after the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, the State becoming two Union Territories from 31 October 2019 (see GS-200). Treat later developments as 'verify'. For accommodation of **regional and cultural diversity**, also cite linguistic reorganisation of States (the States Reorganisation Commission, 1953; reorganisation from 1956; Gujarat and Maharashtra 1960; Punjab and Haryana 1966; the North-East; Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand 2000; Telangana 2014) and the resistance to Hindi imposition.

## Dispute resolution that works better

Legal disputes between the Centre and States go to the Supreme Court; political demands for autonomy are resolved by negotiation (NCERT). Inter-State rivalries (borders, river water) are listed in module 5; Article 262 lets Parliament set up river tribunals, and the Inter-State Council advises on disputes (Laxmikanth: advisory unlike a court). The GST Council has to establish its own dispute mechanism. Punchhi's suggestions (Article 263 amendment, regular Zonal Council meetings, a Chief Ministers' forum, an Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission) are workable improvements.

## Recent changes (2024 Mains Q13)

The question asks about changes 'recently introduced'. The sources cannot answer that; build from structural reforms you can date: GST (2016), Planning Commission to NITI Aayog (2015, verify), the Jammu and Kashmir reorganisation (2019), and anything the Finance Commission or Inter-State Council has newly done. Mark every item 'verify' and prefer a few well-dated points to a long list.

## Way forward (usable closing)

Respect for the cooperative principle; consult States before legislating on Concurrent List subjects; transparent finance (even-handed terms of reference, review of cesses and surcharges, cost sharing); a more active Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils; non-partisan Governors with fixed tenure; and Article 356 as a last resort. Conclude with NCERT's idea: federalism celebrates unity as well as diversity.

## Pattern 1: 'which is (not) a feature of Indian federalism?' (Prelims)

**2017 Q100** asks which one is **not** a feature of Indian federalism: independent judiciary, clear division of powers between Centre and States, unequal representation of units in the Rajya Sabha, or a federation that is the result of an agreement among the units. NCERT lists an independent judiciary as the umpire of federal disputes. Test each option for 'could this exist in a non-federal system?' This is analysis, not an official key.

## Worked Prelims reasoning (think aloud)

*Statement:* 'The Centre alone can decide any change in GST rates.' Step 1: who decides? The **GST Council**, where the Centre has one-third weight and States two-thirds. Step 2: threshold? Three-fourths of weighted votes. Step 3: can the Centre decide alone? 33.3 per cent is far below 75, so no. The statement is wrong because it ignores the voting rule. Write the reason before you read options.

## Worked Mains outline

*Question:* 'Cooperative federalism is more a political culture than a constitutional design. Comment.' Introduction: NCERT's point that trust and restraint decide how federalism works. Body: constitutional design (Lists, Article 257, Articles 263 and 279A); political practice (phases of Congress dominance, opposition-ruled States, coalition era); institutions (Inter-State Council, NITI, GST Council); tension zones (Governor, Article 356, cesses). Judgement: design provides the forums but practice supplies the trust. Conclusion: reform through Punchhi-style consultation and fiscal transparency.

## Negative marking, with explicit arithmetic

A wrong answer in a 2-mark Prelims question costs one-third of 2, that is about 0.67. If you guess blindly among four options, the expected marks are 1/4 x 2 minus 3/4 x 0.67 = 0.50 minus 0.50, which is 0. If one option can be eliminated with certainty, the expected value becomes positive (1/3 x 2 minus 2/3 x 0.67 is about 0.22). The reward for each elimination is modest but real; the habit to build is to state one reason for each statement rather than guess.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Federalism (NCERT): an institutional mechanism to accommodate a regional and a national polity, each autonomous in its sphere, under a written constitution with an independent judiciary.
- Quasi-federal: federal features combined with unitary tilts; India is a Union of States (Article 1).
- Cooperative federalism (Laxmikanth, NITI Aayog chapter): Centre and States work together as equals; joint focus on the national development agenda and advocacy of State perspectives with Central ministries.
- Competitive federalism: States compete with one another (standard usage; verify).
- Coercive federalism: Centre uses constitutional or financial leverage to impose its choice (Mains label; not in the sources).
- Vertical fiscal imbalance: States' responsibilities exceed their revenues.
- Divisible pool: central taxes shareable with States on the Finance Commission's recommendation; cess and surcharge are outside it.
- Asymmetric federalism: differential constitutional treatment of States (special provisions).

### Notes: Union, State and Concurrent Lists

- Entry counts as printed in Laxmikanth (2019); examples as printed by NCERT.
- Residuary power (including taxation) is with Parliament; the Concurrent List contains no tax entries, and concurrent power to levy GST comes from Article 246A inserted by the 101st Amendment.

### Notes: Federal versus unitary features

- Source: NCERT chapter 7; unitary column includes the Governor, All-India Services and emergency powers.
- A 'quasi-federal' answer should show both columns before concluding.

### Notes: Commissions and committees

- ARC (1966-69): Morarji Desai, then K. Hanumanthayya; Setalvad study team; 22 recommendations; no action.
- Rajamannar Committee (appointed 1969 by Tamil Nadu, report 1971): autonomy to States; ignored by the Centre.
- Sarkaria Commission (1983-88): R. S. Sarkaria; 247 recommendations; 180 implemented; Inter-State Council 1990.
- Punchhi Commission (2007-10): Madan Mohan Punchhi; over 310 recommendations; 1,456 pages in seven volumes; cooperative federalism as key.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) and West Bengal Memorandum (1977): party and State government demands for autonomy.

### Notes: Cooperative forums compared

- Inter-State Council is recommendatory (advisory); Zonal Councils are deliberative and advisory; GST Council makes recommendations on GST, which the Supreme Court held persuasive, not binding, in Mohit Minerals (2022; verify); NITI Aayog is a think-tank-style platform.
- Use GS-194 (Day 2) for Inter-State Council depth.

### Notes: GST Council voting rule (worked)

- Weights: Centre one-third (33.3 per cent), all States two-thirds (66.7 per cent).
- Pass: at least 75 per cent of weighted votes of members present and voting.
- States alone can reach at most 66.7 per cent, so they cannot pass without the Centre.
- Centre alone has 33.3 per cent, so it cannot pass alone.
- Centre plus States with at least 41.7 points of the total (62.5 per cent of the States' weight) can pass.
- Blocking threshold: more than 25 per cent; Centre alone can block; States need more than 37.5 per cent of their combined weight.

### Notes: Tension areas in Centre-State relations (12)

- Appointment and dismissal of Governor; partisan role of Governors; President's Rule for partisan interests.

### Notes: Financial relations: who gets which tax

- Article 268: duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by States (stamp duties).

- Articles 275 and 282: statutory and discretionary grants. Article 279A: GST Council. Article 280: Finance Commission.
