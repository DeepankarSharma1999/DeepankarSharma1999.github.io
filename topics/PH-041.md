### Bridge: Slope of ω(k), first-order Taylor expansion and the partial derivative

A wave of a single wave number k has one angular frequency ω fixed by the medium. Writing ω as a function of k, ω(k), gives a curve. The **derivative dω/dk is the slope of that curve** at a chosen k: the change in ω per unit change in k, in units (rad/s)/(rad/m) = m/s, a speed.

**First-order Taylor expansion.** Near a chosen k₀ a smooth curve looks like its tangent line. Write q = k − k₀ (small). Then ω(k) ≈ ω₀ + ω′ q, with ω₀ = ω(k₀) and ω′ = dω/dk at k₀. The next term is ½ ω″ q², where ω″ = d²ω/dk² measures how much the curve bends. *Example:* ω = √(gk) with g = 9.8 m/s² at k₀ = 0.30 rad/m: ω₀ = 1.7146 rad/s, ω′ = ½√(g/k₀) = 2.8577 m/s, ω″ = −¼ √g k₀^(−3/2) = −4.763 m²/s. At k = 0.32 the first-order estimate is 1.7146 + 2.8577 × 0.02 = 1.7718, the second-order estimate adds ½(−4.763)(0.02)² = −0.00095 to give 1.7708, and the exact value is 1.7709 rad/s (recomputed).

**Partial derivative, minimally.** A wave depends on two variables, x and t. ∂y/∂x means: differentiate with respect to x while treating t as a constant number (and ∂y/∂t the other way). For y = cos(kx − ωt), ∂y/∂x = −k sin(kx − ωt) and ∂y/∂t = +ω sin(kx − ωt).

**Two reminders.** Always differentiate with respect to **k** to get v_g (not with respect to f or λ unless you convert). And ω = 2πf, k = 2π/λ: never put f where ω belongs.

### Bridge: Reading ω(k) as a graph: chord and tangent

Draw k on the horizontal axis and ω on the vertical axis. Mark the point P = (k, ω(k)).

- The **chord** from the origin O to P has slope ω/k. That is the **phase velocity** v_p.
- The **tangent** to the curve at P has slope dω/dk. That is the **group velocity** v_g (derived in modules 3 and 4).

If the curve is a straight line through the origin (ω = vk) the chord and the tangent are the same line: v_p = v_g = v, the non-dispersive case. For a curve through the origin: if it bends downwards (like √k), the tangent is flatter than the chord: v_g < v_p; if it bends upwards (like k² or k^(3/2)), the tangent is steeper: v_g > v_p. A curve that does not pass through the origin, as for a plasma (ω → ω_p > 0 when k → 0), has a chord that grows without limit as k → 0, so v_p becomes very large while the tangent flattens to v_g → 0.

**Text sketch** (ω against k):

        ω
        |            _.-'   tangent at P: slope = v_g
        |        _.-'  P
        |    _.-'   .'  chord OP: slope = v_p
        | .-'    .'
        O'----'----------- k

Use this picture as a quick sanity check on every answer: compare the tangent with the chord from the origin. The sign of ω″ alone decides only for curves through the origin; the plasma curve bends upwards (ω″ > 0) yet has v_g < v_p.

### 1. Orientation: three speeds, dispersion and what this lesson settles

*Objective:* Separate particle speed, phase velocity and group velocity, define dispersion and the dispersion relation ω(k), and place this lesson against PH-038 and PH-040.

## The promise from PH-040
PH-040 built a pulse and a Gaussian wave packet and, in module 8, stated v_g = dω/dk only as a pointer 'derived properly on Day 15 in PH-041'. This lesson keeps that promise: it derives the relation twice, shows when two natural speeds differ, and works the standard examination examples. It does **not** rebuild the packet (PH-040 module 6); the two-wave product form of PH-038 module 7 and PH-040 module 8 is restated only in the three lines that derivation 1 needs, with the new step ω₁ = ω(k₁), ω₂ = ω(k₂).

## Three different 'speeds'
Think of a ripple moving along a stretched rope.
- **Particle speed:** how fast a small piece of rope moves up and down, ∂y/∂t. It changes constantly and is not a wave speed.
- **Phase velocity v_p:** how fast a point of fixed phase of a *pure* sine wave (for example one crest) moves along the rope. Module 2.
- **Group velocity v_g:** how fast a *lump* made of several sine waves of nearby wave numbers (the envelope) moves along. Modules 3 and 4.

On a uniform string all wave numbers travel at one speed v = √(T/μ) (PH-039 module 2), the crests and the lump move together, and the shape never changes (PH-040 modules 2 and 6). The new physics appears when that is not true.

## Dispersion in one sentence
In a **dispersive** medium the speed of a sine wave depends on its wave number, so the component waves of a lump run at different speeds. The rule that tells a medium's story is the **dispersion relation**, the function ω(k) that gives the angular frequency allowed for each wave number k. A medium is non-dispersive exactly when ω is proportional to k.

## A picture to keep
Watch a small group of ripples on deep water. Individual crests appear at the back of the group, run forward through it, and fade at the front, because the crests move twice as fast as the group (module 6 derives v_g = v_p/2; the observation is standard; verify). The crest speed and the group speed are visibly different things.

## Notation
x increases to the right; cos(kx − ωt) moves to +x; k = 2π/λ, ω = 2πf; subscript 0 means 'at the central value'; c is the vacuum speed of light.

### 2. Foundation: phase velocity v_p = ω/k and the dispersion relation

*Objective:* Derive v_p = ω/k from the phase of a travelling wave, state what a dispersion relation is, and classify ω = vk as non-dispersive.

## Setting
A single sinusoidal wave moving towards +x: y(x, t) = A cos(kx − ωt), with k = 2π/λ and ω = 2πf (PH-040 bridge B1 revises both). The argument φ(x, t) = kx − ωt is the **phase**.

## Derivation 1: the phase velocity
1. Choose a feature of fixed phase, for example a crest, where φ = φ₀ (a constant).
2. The crest moves, so its position x_c depends on t, but its phase stays φ₀: k x_c(t) − ω t = φ₀.
3. Differentiate with respect to t (k and ω are constants): k dx_c/dt − ω = 0.
4. Hence dx_c/dt = ω/k, that is

$$ v_p = ω / k $$

5. With k = 2π/λ and ω = 2πf this is v_p = fλ. Mathur (PDF p. 532) and Brij Lal (PDF p. 277) reach the same result by the same step, writing the wave as sin(ωt − kx).

*Sign convention:* for cos(kx + ωt) the same steps give dx_c/dt = −ω/k, a wave moving towards −x.
*Dimension check:* ω in s⁻¹ and k in m⁻¹ give m/s ✓.
*Alternative:* y = A cos k(x − (ω/k)t) is f(x − vt) of PH-040 module 2 with v = ω/k.

## The dispersion relation
Does a wave of every k exist on a uniform string? Substitute y = cos(kx − ωt) into the wave equation y_tt = v² y_xx of PH-039 module 2: the left side is −ω² y and the right side is −v²k² y, so a solution exists only if

$$ ω² = v² k²   or   ω = v k  (taking ω > 0) $$

This rule, ω as a function of k, is the **dispersion relation** of the medium. For ω = vk, v_p = ω/k = v for every k: the speed does not depend on k and the medium is **non-dispersive**. In most real media ω(k) is not a straight line through the origin, so v_p varies with k and the medium is **dispersive** (Brij Lal PDF p. 282 says most media in nature are dispersive; NCERT PDF p. 22 includes an exercise asking why a pulse is distorted in one).

## Number (recomputed)
y = 0.01 cos(30x − 450t) in SI units. Comparing with cos(kx − ωt): k = 30 rad/m, ω = 450 rad/s, so v_p = 450/30 = 15 m/s towards +x. Then λ = 2π/30 = 0.2094 m, f = 450/2π = 71.62 Hz, and fλ = 15.0 m/s ✓.

## Trap: v_p is not a message speed
A pure sine wave fills all x and t with constant amplitude, so a crest delivers no news. A signal needs switching or amplitude variation (modulation), and a modulated wave is a group with a spread of k: modules 3 and 4.

## Limit
If ω = vk the lump of PH-040 module 6 moves at v unchanged. Otherwise ω/k alone does not describe a lump; the next two modules show what does.

### 3. Core: group velocity from two close waves (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive v_g = Δω/Δk → dω/dk from the product form of two close waves, separating the carrier speed from the envelope speed.

## Setting and assumptions
Two waves travelling towards +x with equal amplitude A and slightly different wave numbers, in a medium with dispersion relation ω(k): y₁ = A cos(k₁x − ω₁t), y₂ = A cos(k₂x − ω₂t), where ω₁ = ω(k₁) and ω₂ = ω(k₂). Define the mean and difference k̄ = (k₁ + k₂)/2, Δk = k₂ − k₁, ω̄ = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2, Δω = ω₂ − ω₁. This is the space-time version of the beats of PH-038 module 7 (and of Mathur PDF pp. 532 to 534, which uses λ and λ + δλ); that lesson showed the loud regions move at Δω/Δk and promised the dispersive case for today.

## Derivation 1: the product form
1. Use cos P + cos Q = 2 cos[(P − Q)/2] cos[(P + Q)/2] with P = k₁x − ω₁t, Q = k₂x − ω₂t.
2. (P + Q)/2 = k̄x − ω̄t and (P − Q)/2 = −(Δk x − Δω t)/2.
3. Since cosine is even,

$$ y = 2A cos[(Δk/2) x − (Δω/2) t] · cos(k̄x − ω̄t) $$

4. The second factor is a carrier wave of wave number k̄ and frequency ω̄. Its crests move at **ω̄/k̄**, the phase velocity of the middle wave.
5. The first factor varies slowly (Δk, Δω are small) and acts as the **envelope**. Its phase (Δk/2)x − (Δω/2)t stays constant when Δk dx = Δω dt, so a point of the envelope (for example the centre of a loud group) moves at

$$ v_g = Δω / Δk $$

6. Let the two waves approach each other, Δk → 0. Then Δω/Δk → dω/dk evaluated at k̄:

$$ v_g = dω/dk $$

7. The groups repeat every 2π/Δk (the beat length of PH-038 module 7). Mathur reaches dω/dk with a limiting step on PDF p. 535.

*Dimension check:* ω/k and dω/dk both have units (rad/s)/(rad/m) = m/s ✓.
*Limiting case:* ω = vk gives dω/dk = v = ω/k: carrier and envelope move together, the pattern is rigid (non-dispersive; Mathur PDF p. 533).

## Number (recomputed): deep water
Take ω = √(gk) with g = 9.8 m/s² (module 6 explains the relation), k₁ = 0.30 and k₂ = 0.32 rad/m. Then ω₁ = 1.7146 and ω₂ = 1.7709 rad/s.
- Individual phase speeds: ω₁/k₁ = 5.715 m/s and ω₂/k₂ = 5.534 m/s.
- Carrier speed ω̄/k̄ = 1.7427/0.31 = 5.622 m/s.
- Envelope speed Δω/Δk = 0.0563/0.02 = 2.812 m/s; exact dω/dk at k̄ = ½√(g/k̄) = 2.811 m/s ✓.
- Group length 2π/Δk = 314 m.

The envelope moves at only about half the carrier speed: crests overtake the group, as in the ripple picture of module 1.

## Limits of this derivation
Equal amplitudes give a periodic string of groups, not one lump; unequal amplitudes keep the same envelope speed (PH-038 module 7, set-up with a₁ and a₂). One isolated lump needs a continuous band of k: module 4.

### 4. Core: group velocity from the Taylor expansion of a narrow packet (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive v_g = ω′(k₀) for a narrow packet by expanding ω(k) to first order, read it as stationary phase, and state the energy meaning.

## Setting and assumptions
PH-040 module 6 built the packet ψ(x, t) = ∫ A(k) e^{i(kx − ωt)} dk with a narrow Gaussian weight A(k) about k₀ and ω = vk. Here the same sum is used with a general dispersion relation: **each component keeps its own ω(k)**, so ψ(x, t) = ∫ A(k) e^{i(kx − ω(k)t)} dk (physical wave = real part). Assumptions: A(k) is negligible outside a narrow band about k₀, and ω(k) is smooth there. Sign convention: x to the right, components move to +x.

## Derivation 2: first-order expansion
1. Write k = k₀ + q with q small. The phase of a component is Θ = kx − ω(k)t = (k₀x − ω₀t) + qx − [ω(k₀ + q) − ω₀]t.
2. Taylor expansion (bridge B1): ω(k₀ + q) − ω₀ = ω′q + ½ω″q² + …, with ω′ and ω″ evaluated at k₀.
3. Keep only the first-order term: Θ ≈ (k₀x − ω₀t) + q(x − ω′t).
4. Pull the common factor out of the integral:

$$ ψ(x, t) ≈ e^{i(k₀x − ω₀t)} F(x − ω′t),   F(ξ) = ∫ A(k₀ + q) e^{iqξ} dq $$

5. F(ξ) is exactly the envelope of the packet at t = 0 (for the Gaussian of PH-040 module 6, step 3: F(ξ) = 2σ_k√π e^{−σ_k²ξ²}). At time t the envelope is the t = 0 envelope **shifted by ω′t without changing shape**. So the envelope moves rigidly at

$$ v_g = ω′(k₀) = (dω/dk) at k₀ $$

6. The carrier e^{i(k₀x − ω₀t)} moves at ω₀/k₀ = v_p. For the Gaussian: ψ ≈ 2σ_k√π e^{i(k₀x − ω₀t)} exp[−σ_k²(x − v_g t)²], with peak at x = v_g t.

*Checks:* x − ω′t is a length ✓; positive ω′ means motion to +x ✓. *Limiting case:* ω = vk makes ω″ = 0 and ω′ = v exactly: the result is exact, the packet is the rigid pulse f(x − vt) (PH-040 module 2). *Agreement:* this is the Δk → 0 limit of module 3.

## Stationary-phase reading (why the peak sits at x = ω′t)
Components with different k have phases Θ(k) = kx − ω(k)t that change quickly with k, so they cancel in the integral, except where dΘ/dk = x − ω′(k)t = 0. Near k₀ the components then add in step: the peak is where x = ω′(k₀)t. This is a heuristic reading of the standard stationary-phase method (verify).

## Number (recomputed)
Deep water, g = 9.8 m/s², k₀ = 0.30 rad/m: ω′ = 2.858 m/s, v_p = 5.715 m/s. After 100 s the peak is at about 286 m and a crest that began under it is at about 572 m: crests pass through the group.

## Energy and information
PH-040 module 4 showed energy carried at v for a pulse on a string. For a narrow packet in a dispersive medium Brij Lal (PDF p. 282) says the group velocity is the velocity with which the packet's energy is transmitted, and Mathur (PDF p. 535) says experiments measure the group velocity. Caveat: with strong absorption or anomalous dispersion the packet distorts and 'the' group velocity stops describing the signal (standard; verify).

Dropping the ½ω″q² term needs 2σ_k²|ω″|t ≪ 1, i.e. |ω″|t ≪ 2σ_x0² (module 8); beyond that the envelope changes shape (module 8).

### 5. Core: Rayleigh's relation, normal and anomalous dispersion, and the refractive-index form

*Objective:* Derive v_g = v_p + k dv_p/dk = v_p − λ dv_p/dλ and v_g = c/(n + ω dn/dω), and decide when v_g is below or above v_p.

## Derivation 3: the Rayleigh relation
1. By definition ω = k v_p(k), where v_p is itself a function of k.
2. Product rule: v_g = dω/dk = v_p + k dv_p/dk. (Brij Lal eq. 12.35, PDF p. 282; Mathur PDF p. 535.)
3. To convert to wavelength, use λ = 2π/k, so dλ/dk = −2π/k² = −λ/k. Chain rule: dv_p/dk = (dv_p/dλ)(dλ/dk) = −(λ/k) dv_p/dλ.
4. Multiply by k: k dv_p/dk = −λ dv_p/dλ. Therefore

$$ v_g = v_p + k dv_p/dk = v_p − λ dv_p/dλ $$

Brij Lal gets the second form as eq. 12.36 (PDF p. 282); Mathur gets u = v − λ dv/dλ from two waves of wavelengths λ and λ + δλ (PDF pp. 533 to 534).

*Dimension check:* λ dv_p/dλ has the units of v_p ✓. *Limit:* dv_p/dλ = 0 gives v_g = v_p. *Test:* v_p = Bλ^m gives v_g = (1 − m)v_p; deep water (m = ½) gives v_p/2.

## Which is larger? A table
| dv_p/dλ | dv_p/dk | Result | Typical case |
|---|---|---|---|
| > 0 (longer waves faster) | < 0 | v_g < v_p | deep water, plasma, glass in the visible (normal dispersion) |
| = 0 | = 0 | v_g = v_p | string, light in vacuum |
| < 0 (shorter waves faster) | > 0 | v_g > v_p | capillary ripples; anomalous dispersion in light |

**Source wording to read carefully:** Brij Lal (PDF p. 283) says v_g is 'in general less than' v_p without stating a condition. Mathur (PDF p. 534) says the same but ties it to dv/dλ > 0 (normal dispersion), and on PDF p. 535 adds that in a dispersive medium the group velocity may be greater or smaller than the phase velocity. Never write 'v_g < v_p always'. Mathur's line v = dω/dk = … on PDF p. 535 should read u = dω/dk (a misprint in the extract).

## Derivation 4: the refractive-index form (light in a medium)
1. Phase velocity v_p = c/n(ω), so k = nω/c, with n depending on ω.
2. Differentiate with respect to ω: dk/dω = (n + ω dn/dω)/c.
3. v_g = dω/dk = 1/(dk/dω):

$$ v_g = c / (n + ω dn/dω) = c / n_g,   n_g = n + ω dn/dω $$

4. For vacuum wavelength λ = 2πc/ω: dω/dλ = −2πc/λ², so ω dn/dω = ω (dn/dλ)/(dω/dλ) = −λ dn/dλ, giving n_g = n − λ dn/dλ.

## Number (recomputed)
Cauchy model n = 1.50 + B/λ² with B = 0.0050 µm², at λ = 0.500 µm: n = 1.52; dn/dλ = −2B/λ³ = −0.08 per µm; n_g = 1.52 + 0.500 × 0.08 = 1.56. So v_p = c/1.52 = 1.972 × 10⁸ m/s and v_g = c/1.56 = 1.922 × 10⁸ m/s, ratio v_g/v_p = 0.974. Normal dispersion (n falls as λ rises) gives v_g < v_p ✓. (The Cauchy form is an empirical fit; the constants here are illustrative, not measured data.)

## Anomalous dispersion
Near an absorption line n falls as ω rises (dn/dω < 0), so n_g can drop below n, and in strongly absorbing regions even below 1. Then v_g > c or negative appears in the mathematics, but the packet is so distorted and absorbed that v_g is no longer the signal speed (standard; verify). Mathur (PDF p. 535) says the group velocity cannot exceed c; quote this with the caveat.

### 6. Core: dispersion relations worked — string, water, capillary and ripples with a minimum speed

*Objective:* Compute v_p, v_g and v_g/v_p from ω(k) for string, deep water, capillary and gravity–capillary waves, and use dv_p/dk = 0 to find the minimum phase speed.

## Method for any ω(k)
1. v_p = ω/k. 2. v_g = dω/dk. 3. Ratio v_g/v_p = (k/ω) dω/dk = d ln ω / d ln k. 4. For a power law ω = a kⁿ the ratio is exactly n.

## Table (all computed by differentiation and checked with sympy)
| Medium | ω(k) | v_p | v_g | v_g/v_p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| String, EM in vacuum | v k | v | v | 1 |
| Deep water, gravity waves | √(gk) | √(g/k) | ½√(g/k) | 1/2 |
| Capillary ripples | √(T k³/ρ) | √(T k/ρ) | (3/2)√(T k/ρ) | 3/2 |
| Gravity plus capillary | √(gk + T k³/ρ) | √(g/k + T k/ρ) | (g + 3Tk²/ρ)/(2ω) | (g + 3Tk²/ρ)/(2(g + Tk²/ρ)) |
| Free non-relativistic particle | ħk²/2m | ħk/2m | ħk/m | 2 |
| Plasma (module 7) | √(ω_p² + c²k²) | ω/k | c²k/ω | c²/v_p² |

The water relations (g gravity, T surface tension, ρ density) and the plasma relation are **standard results not derived in the supplied extracts** (verify); deep water needs depth much larger than λ.

## Worked steps: deep water
ω = (gk)^(1/2), so dω/dk = ½ g^(1/2) k^(−1/2) = ω/(2k): **v_g = v_p/2**. *Limits:* as k → 0 (long waves) v_p and v_g → ∞; as k → ∞ both → 0 (short gravity waves are slow). *Dimension:* g/k has units (m/s²)/(1/m) = m²/s² ✓.

## Worked steps: capillary ripples
ω = (T/ρ)^(1/2) k^(3/2) gives v_p = (T/ρ)^(1/2) k^(1/2) and dω/dk = (3/2)(T/ρ)^(1/2) k^(1/2), so v_g = (3/2)v_p: the group runs **faster** than the crests, the opposite of gravity waves. *Dimension:* (T/ρ)k has units m²/s² ✓.

## Gravity plus capillary: a minimum phase speed
Water carries both effects: ω² = gk + T k³/ρ. Then v_p² = g/k + T k/ρ. The first term falls with k and the second rises, so v_p has a minimum where d(v_p²)/dk = −g/k² + T/ρ = 0, i.e. **k_m = √(ρg/T)**. At the minimum dv_p/dk = 0, so by Rayleigh (module 5) **v_g = v_p**. Substituting, g/k_m = T k_m/ρ = √(gT/ρ), so v_p,min = (4gT/ρ)^(1/4).

*Numbers (recomputed; g = 9.8, T = 0.0728 N/m for clean water near room temperature (standard; verify), ρ = 1000 kg/m³):* k_m = 367 rad/m, λ_m = 1.71 cm, v_p,min = 0.231 m/s, and numerical differentiation gives v_g = 0.2312 m/s at k_m ✓. Longer waves are gravity-dominated (v_g < v_p), shorter ripples capillary-dominated (v_g > v_p).

### 7. Master: v_p v_g = c² for a plasma and for a relativistic particle

*Objective:* Derive v_p v_g = c² from ω² = ω_p² + c²k² and from E² = p²c² + m²c⁴ with E = ħω, p = ħk, and explain why v_p > c does not break relativity.

## Derivation 5: plasma (and waveguide) form
Assumed dispersion relation, a standard result for radio waves in an ionised gas and for a hollow metal waveguide (not in the supplied extracts; verify): **ω² = ω_p² + c²k²**, with ω_p a constant of the medium (for a plasma ω_p² = N e²/(ε₀ m_e), N electrons per m³, charge e; verify). Take ω > 0, k > 0.
1. Differentiate both sides with respect to k: 2ω dω/dk = 2c²k.
2. Hence v_g = dω/dk = c²k/ω.
3. And v_p = ω/k.
4. Multiply: v_p v_g = (ω/k)(c²k/ω) = **c²**.
5. Since ω² > c²k², v_p = ω/k > c and then v_g = c²/v_p < c.

*Dimension check:* both sides have units m²/s² ✓. *Limits:* k → ∞ gives ω → ck, so v_p, v_g → c (the plasma becomes transparent to very short waves). k → 0 gives ω → ω_p, v_p → ∞ and v_g → 0; a wave of ω < ω_p would need k² < 0, so it does not propagate (cut-off).

*Number (recomputed):* N = 10¹² m⁻³ gives ω_p = 5.64 × 10⁷ rad/s, f_p = 8.98 MHz. At 10 MHz v_g/c = √(1 − f_p²/f²) = 0.4403 and v_p/c = 2.2713; product 1.0000 ✓.

## Derivation 6: a free relativistic particle
Matter-wave identification (de Broglie; Planck–Einstein), **standard knowledge not found in the supplied relativity extracts; verify**: a free particle of energy E and momentum p is described by a wave e^{i(kx − ωt)} with E = ħω and p = ħk.
1. PH-032 module 4: E² = p²c² + m²c⁴, with E the total energy.
2. Substitute: ħ²ω² = ħ²k²c² + m²c⁴, so **ω² = c²k² + (mc²/ħ)²**, the plasma form with ω_p replaced by mc²/ħ.
3. So v_p v_g = c² again. Directly: v_g = dω/dk = dE/dp; from 2E dE = 2pc² dp, dE/dp = pc²/E, which is the particle's own speed v (PH-032 module 4: v = pc²/E).
4. v_p = E/p = c²/v > c.

*Number (recomputed):* an electron at v = 1.0 × 10⁶ m/s (γ = 1.0000056): p = 9.109 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m/s, de Broglie λ = 2πħ/p = 0.727 nm, v_g = 1.0 × 10⁶ m/s, v_p = c²/v = 8.99 × 10¹⁰ m/s ≈ 300c, v_p v_g = c² ✓.

## Trap: the non-relativistic matter wave
If E is taken as kinetic energy only, ω = ħk²/(2m): v_p = ħk/2m = v/2 and v_g = ħk/m = v. Including the rest energy mc² adds a constant mc²/ħ to ω and changes v_p to c²/v. Check: E ≈ mc² + ½mv² gives E/p ≈ c²/v + v/2 (the two terms). The **group velocity is the same either way; v_p is not a measurable speed for a particle.**

## Meaning
Energy and information travel with v_g, bounded by c here. A phase velocity above c is allowed because a pure sine wave carries no signal (module 2); no object or energy moves at v_p. The phase kx − ωt is a Lorentz invariant (the pair (ω/c, k) transforms like the four-momentum (E/c, p) of PH-033 module 4; PH-034 is the listed covariance topic; verify), which is why 'phase speed' is a statement about pattern, not about matter. Caveat: in strong anomalous dispersion v_g can exceed c with a badly distorted, absorbed pulse; it is then no signal speed (standard; verify).

### 8. Master: why packets spread — the second-order term and the width formula

*Objective:* Derive σ_x(t) = σ_x0 √(1 + (ω″t/2σ_x0²)²) for a Gaussian packet and apply it to a matter wave.

## Setting
Module 4 dropped the ½ω″q² term and found rigid motion. Keep it now for the Gaussian packet of PH-040 module 6 (A(k) = exp[−(k − k₀)²/(4σ_k²)], rms width σ_k of the intensity |A|², and σ_x0 = 1/(2σ_k) at t = 0). Width conventions are those of PH-040 module 6. The spreading formula is a **standard result not in the supplied extracts**; it is derived and recomputed here (verify).

## Derivation 7: the width at time t
1. With ξ = x − v_g t and the second-order term kept,

ψ = e^{i(k₀x − ω₀t)} ∫ exp[−q²/(4σ_k²) − i(ω″t/2)q² + iqξ] dq.

2. Write the exponent as −a q² + iqξ with a = 1/(4σ_k²) + iω″t/2 (Re a > 0).
3. The Gaussian integral of PH-040 bridge B2 (Facts 1 and 3), extended to complex a with Re a > 0 by the same completing-the-square step (standard; verify): ∫ e^{−aq² + iqξ} dq = √(π/a) e^{−ξ²/(4a)}.
4. Now 4a = 1/σ_k² + 2iω″t, so 1/(4a) = σ_k²/(1 + 2iσ_k²ω″t) = σ_k²(1 − 2iσ_k²ω″t)/(1 + 4σ_k⁴ω″²t²).
5. |ψ|² ∝ exp[−2 Re(1/(4a)) ξ²] = exp[−2σ_k²ξ²/(1 + 4σ_k⁴ω″²t²)].
6. Compare with exp(−ξ²/(2σ_x²)): σ_x(t)² = (1 + 4σ_k⁴ω″²t²)/(4σ_k²). With σ_x0 = 1/(2σ_k) and 2σ_k² = 1/(2σ_x0²):

$$ σ_x(t) = σ_x0 √(1 + (ω″ t / 2σ_x0²)²) $$

*Checks.* t = 0 returns σ_x0; ω″ = 0 gives no spreading. For large t, σ_x ≈ |ω″|t σ_k: the packet broadens at rate |ω″|σ_k, the spread of group velocities across the band (units m²/s × 1/m = m/s ✓). Numerical integration of the packet at σ_k = 2 m⁻¹, ω″ = 0.3 m²/s gave σ_x = 0.250 m at t = 0 and 0.650 m at t = 1 s, both matching the formula. The sign of ω″ does not matter for an unchirped Gaussian: ω″ > 0 and ω″ < 0 broaden it equally. (The sign of ω″ is not the normal/anomalous label: deep water has ω″ < 0 and a plasma ω″ > 0, yet both have v_g < v_p.)

## Number: an electron packet
For the free particle of module 6, ω = ħk²/2m, so ω″ = ħ/m = 1.158 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s (recomputed). Take σ_x0 = 1.0 nm. The width doubles when ω″t/(2σ_x0²) = √3, i.e. t = 2√3σ_x0²/ω″ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ s. At t = 1.0 ps the width is 57.9 nm. A narrower packet spreads faster, as the trade-off σ_x σ_k = ½ of PH-040 demands.

## Examples of ω″
- Deep water: ω″ = −¼ √g k^(−3/2).
- Light pulses in glass fibre: the broadening comes from the same second derivative of the dispersion relation (group-velocity dispersion); named only.
- Plasma: ω″ = c²ω_p²/ω³ > 0 (from ω′ = c²k/ω: ω″ = c²/ω − c⁴k²/ω³ = c²ω_p²/ω³).

## Boundary of the picture
Both pictures need a narrow band; for a wide band, strong absorption or strong anomalous dispersion 'the group velocity' may not describe the lump (standard; verify).

## Five standard question types, solved

### Notes: Definitions

- Phase of a travelling wave: φ = kx − ωt; wave number k = 2π/λ (rad/m); angular frequency ω = 2πf (rad/s).

- Group velocity: the speed of the envelope of a narrow packet, v_g = dω/dk at the central k₀.

Phase velocity is the slope of the chord from the origin to the point on the dispersion curve; group velocity is the slope of the tangent there. They differ whenever the curve is not a straight line through the origin.

Curve of omega against k bending downward (square-root shape), a point at k0, a straight chord from the origin to the point whose slope is the phase velocity and a shallower dashed tangent at the point whose slope is the group velocity.

1. Draw the k axis and omega axis and a concave curve through the origin (omega = c sqrt(k)).
2. Mark a point at k0; draw the chord from the origin to it: slope omega/k = v_p.
3. At the same point draw the tangent: slope d(omega)/dk = v_g, shallower than the chord.
4. Note that for deep water v_g = v_p/2.

### Memory skeleton

- Phase φ = kx − ωt; crest at constant phase ⇒ v_p = ω/k = fλ
- Dispersion relation ω(k); non-dispersive exactly when ω = vk
- Two close waves: envelope 2A cos[(Δk/2)x − (Δω/2)t] moves at Δω/Δk → dω/dk
- Narrow packet, first-order Taylor: ψ ≈ e^{i(k₀x − ω₀t)} F(x − ω′t)
- Rayleigh: v_g = v_p + k dv_p/dk = v_p − λ dv_p/dλ; normal versus anomalous
- Light: v_g = c/n_g, n_g = n + ω dn/dω = n − λ dn/dλ
- Second-order term ½ω″q² spreads the packet

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, derive v_p = ω/k, then v_g = dω/dk by two close waves and by the Taylor expansion, then the Rayleigh relation and n_g.

### Last-week summary

- v_p = ω/k from k x_c − ωt = φ₀; v_g = dω/dk from envelope phase constant (Δk dx = Δω dt) or first-order Taylor.
- First order: envelope moves rigidly at ω′(k₀); valid while 2σ_k²|ω″|t ≪ 1; the ω″ term spreads it.
- ω = k v_p ⇒ v_g = v_p + k dv_p/dk = v_p − λ dv_p/dλ. dv_p/dλ > 0 (normal): v_g < v_p; < 0 (anomalous): v_g > v_p.
- k = nω/c ⇒ v_g = c/(n + ω dn/dω) = c/n_g, n_g = n − λ dn/dλ. Differentiate by k, never λ or f.
