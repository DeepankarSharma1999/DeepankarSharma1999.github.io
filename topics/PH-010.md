### Bridge: Flux, solid angle and cross-section as an effective area

Imagine a stream of particles all moving the same way. The **flux** I₀ is the number of particles crossing one square metre (held perpendicular to the stream) per second, in units m⁻² s⁻¹. If the stream falls on a small patch of area σ, the number hitting it per second is I₀ σ. So any area σ that multiplies a flux to give a rate is called a **cross-section**: it is the *effective target area* a scatterer presents.

An **angle in three dimensions** is a **solid angle**. A patch of area A on a sphere of radius R, seen from the centre, subtends Ω = A/R² **steradians** (sr). The whole sphere is 4π sr. Steradians are dimensionless, like radians.

Use polar angle θ measured from the beam direction and an azimuth angle ψ around the beam. A thin ring on the sphere between θ and θ + dθ has radius R sin θ and width R dθ, so its area is 2π R sin θ · R dθ, and

$$ dΩ = 2π sin θ dθ  (ring about the beam axis) $$

Check: ∫ from 0 to π of 2π sin θ dθ = 2π [−cos θ] = 4π, the whole sphere.

Units of area in nuclear physics: 1 **barn** = 10⁻²⁸ m² (stated in the Upadhyaya text), and 1 fm² = 10⁻³⁰ m², so 1 barn = 100 fm².

Check. A detector of area 10 mm² facing the target from 0.10 m subtends ΔΩ = (10⁻⁵ m²)/(0.10 m)² = 10⁻³ sr.

### Bridge: Half-angle trigonometry and the derivative of cot(θ/2)

The Rutherford derivation uses four trigonometric facts, all proved from sin² + cos² = 1.

1. **Double angle**: sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2). (Put x = θ/2 in sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x.)
2. **Cosecant identity**: divide sin² x + cos² x = 1 by sin² x to get 1 + cot² x = csc² x, where cot x = cos x/sin x and csc x = 1/sin x.
3. **Derivative**: d/dx cot x = −csc² x. By the chain rule with x = θ/2, d/dθ cot(θ/2) = −½ csc²(θ/2).
4. **Small angles**: for small x (in radians), sin x ≈ x and tan x ≈ x.

Worked numbers. At θ = 90°: sin 45° = 0.7071, cot 45° = 1, csc² 45° = 2, so d/dθ cot(θ/2) = −½ × 2 = −1 per radian. At θ = 60°: cot 30° = 1.7321; check 1 + cot² 30° = 4 = csc² 30° (sin 30° = 0.5, 1/0.25 = 4).

Why the half angle? A scattering angle runs from 0 to π; the orbit geometry gives angles of size (π − θ)/2, so θ/2 appears naturally.

Check. Show that the ratio sin⁴(60°/2)/sin⁴(120°/2) is 1/9 and that its inverse, 9, is the ratio of Rutherford cross-sections σ(60°)/σ(120°): sin 30° = 0.5, sin 60° = 0.8660, (0.5/0.866)⁴ = 0.1111 = 1/9.

### Bridge: Centre-of-mass velocity and relative velocity (recap)

This is a short recap of PH-001 module 8 and PH-009.

For two particles with velocities v1 and v2 the **centre-of-mass (CM) velocity** is V = (m1 v1 + m2 v2)/(m1 + m2) and the **relative velocity** is v1 − v2. The **CM frame** moves with velocity V; velocities in it are u′ = u − V (Galilean transformation, valid for speeds much less than light). Total momentum in the CM frame is zero, and for an isolated pair V is constant, so the CM frame is inertial.

For a projectile of mass m1 and speed v0 hitting a target of mass m2 at rest in the laboratory:

$$ V = m1 v0/(m1 + m2) $$

In the CM frame the projectile has speed u₁′ = v0 − V = m2 v0/(m1 + m2) and the target has velocity −V. Check zero momentum: m1 u₁′ = m1 m2 v0/(m1 + m2) = m2 V. ✓.

The kinetic energy in the CM frame is the relative-motion energy ½ μ v0², with μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2):

$$ E_cm = ½ μ v0² = E_lab · m2/(m1 + m2) $$

The remainder, E_lab · m1/(m1 + m2) = ½ (m1 + m2) V², is the energy of the CM's own motion and cannot be converted into relative motion.

Check. For equal masses V = v0/2, u₁′ = v0/2, and E_cm = E_lab/2. For a very heavy target (m2 ≫ m1): V ≈ 0, u₁′ ≈ v0 and E_cm ≈ E_lab: the target does not matter.

### 1. Orientation: what 'Rutherford scattering; Centre of mass and laboratory reference frames' asks of you

*Objective:* Say in plain words what Rutherford scattering measures, what is derived in this lesson and why laboratory and centre-of-mass frames appear in the same syllabus line.

## What the syllabus words mean

The syllabus line (Paper I, section 1(a)) is 'Rutherford scattering; Centre of mass and laboratory reference frames'. **Scattering** means a beam of fast particles is fired at a target and some are deflected. In **Rutherford scattering** the particles are alpha particles (helium nuclei, charge +2q_e) and the target nuclei are heavy atomic nuclei such as gold; the deflection comes from the electric repulsion between like charges. The topic asks two things: how much a particle is deflected, depending on how close to the nucleus it was aimed, and how many particles arrive at each angle. The second half of the line asks how the same collision looks from the laboratory and from the centre of mass.

## The big idea

If you know how deflection angle θ depends on the aiming distance b (the **impact parameter**), then counting particles at each angle gives the **differential cross-section**. Rutherford's result is

$$ dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)² / sin⁴(θ/2),  k = Z₁Z₂ q_e²/(4π ε₀) $$

and its agreement with measurement told physicists that an atom's positive charge sits in a tiny nucleus.

### 2. Foundation: the scattering geometry, impact parameter, solid angle and the differential cross-section

*Objective:* Define flux, impact parameter, scattering angle, solid angle and dσ/dΩ, state the assumptions of Rutherford scattering and write the counting formula.

## The set-up and the assumptions

A parallel beam of identical particles, each of mass m and charge q₁ with kinetic energy E when far from the target, moves along the +x axis towards a nucleus of charge q₂ at the origin O. The beam is uniform with flux I₀ (module B1). Assumptions, which an examiner expects you to list:

1. The nucleus is so heavy that it stays at rest (relaxed in module 9).
2. Both are point charges and the only force is the Coulomb repulsion F = +k/r² along the line joining them, with k = q₁ q₂/(4π ε₀) > 0 (like charges). For an alpha particle (2q_e) on a nucleus Z q_e, k = 2 Z q_e²/(4π ε₀).
3. Classical, non-relativistic motion.
4. The target foil is thin, so each particle is scattered at most once.
5. The beam is wide compared with an atom, so every impact parameter occurs.

## The two key quantities

The **impact parameter** b is the perpendicular distance from O to the original line of flight (extended as if no force acted). The **scattering angle** θ is the angle between the final and the initial directions of motion, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π: θ = 0 means undeflected and θ = π means turned back. Since the force is central, the motion is in a plane through O and the beam axis, and every particle with the same b is deflected by the same θ(b), whatever its azimuth. Expectation: a smaller b means a stronger repulsion and a larger θ.

## Counting: the differential cross-section

A detector counts the particles per second entering a small solid angle dΩ in the direction θ. Define

$$ dσ/dΩ = (number scattered per second into dΩ)/(I₀ dΩ) $$

so that the number per second is I₀ (dσ/dΩ) dΩ. Its units are m² sr⁻¹: an area per steradian. The **total cross-section** σ = ∫ (dσ/dΩ) dΩ is the effective area that the target presents (nuclear physicists quote barns, 1 b = 10⁻²⁸ m²). For N_t target nuclei in the beam, with no shadowing, the detector rate is

$$ rate = I₀ N_t (dσ/dΩ) ΔΩ $$

## Check

Dimensions: I₀ (m⁻² s⁻¹) × σ (m²) = s⁻¹, a rate. Limit: a target that does not interact has σ = 0 and nothing is scattered. The figure shows a path computed numerically for a repulsive centre.

### 3. Core: from b(θ) to dσ/dΩ; the hard sphere (2019 question)

*Objective:* Derive dσ/dΩ = (b/sin θ)|db/dθ| from particle counting and use it to show that elastic scattering from a hard sphere has dσ/dΩ = R²/4.

## The counting argument

Suppose the deflection angle θ(b) falls steadily as b grows (one b for each θ). Particles with impact parameters between b and b + db pass through a ring of area 2π b |db| perpendicular to the beam. The number per second crossing it is I₀ 2π b |db|. These particles, and only these, leave in the cone between θ and θ + dθ, with solid angle 2π sin θ |dθ| (bridge B1). Equating the two counts:

$$ I₀ 2π b |db| = I₀ (dσ/dΩ) 2π sin θ |dθ| $$

$$ dσ/dΩ = (b/sin θ) |db/dθ| $$

The modulus appears because b decreases as θ increases, so db/dθ is negative (Upadhyaya writes the same relation with a minus sign). If θ(b) were not monotonic, one would add the contributions of every b that gives the same θ.

## Example: a hard sphere (2019)

A point particle bounces elastically off an immovable sphere of radius R. Take the straight incoming line at impact parameter b < R. It strikes the surface at a point whose normal makes angle α with the incoming line, so b = R sin α. The reflection law (angle of incidence equals angle of reflection) deflects the direction by θ = π − 2α, so α = (π − θ)/2 and

$$ b = R sin((π − θ)/2) = R cos(θ/2) $$

Then db/dθ = −(R/2) sin(θ/2), and with sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2):

$$ dσ/dΩ = R cos(θ/2) (R/2) sin(θ/2) / (2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2)) = R²/4 $$

**Inference.** The scattering is isotropic: it does not depend on θ. The total cross-section is σ = (R²/4) 4π = π R², the geometric cross-sectional area of the sphere: classically the sphere 'blocks' exactly the area it presents to the beam. (In quantum mechanics at low energy the total hard-sphere cross-section is 4π R², four times larger; this is a standard result quoted here without derivation, to be verified.) Compare Rutherford scattering (module 6), where the cross-section depends strongly on angle and the total diverges.

## Checks

Units: R²/4 is an area per steradian. Limit θ = π (head-on, b = 0): the particle is reflected straight back; θ → 0 as b → R (a glancing touch), consistent with b = R cos(θ/2). For R = 5 fm: dσ/dΩ = 6.25 fm² sr⁻¹ and σ = 78.5 fm² = 0.785 barn.

### 4. Core: the repulsive Coulomb orbit is a hyperbola; eccentricity and scattering angle

*Objective:* Solve the orbit equation for the repulsive inverse-square force, find the eccentricity in terms of E and b, and show that the scattering angle satisfies sin(θ/2) = 1/e.

## Conserved quantities

Far from O the speed is v0 with ½ m v0² = E, and the angular momentum about O is L = m v0 b = b √(2 m E) (the lever arm of the incoming line is b). The potential energy is U = +k/r (repulsive, zero at infinity), so

$$ E = ½ m ṙ² + L²/(2 m r²) + k/r $$

## The orbit equation

From PH-003 module 5, u'' + u = −(m/(L² u²)) F with u = 1/r. For the repulsive force F = +k/r² = +k u²:

$$ u'' + u = −m k/L² = −1/ℓ,   ℓ = L²/(m k) > 0 $$

The general solution is u = −1/ℓ + A cos(φ − φ₀). Choose the axis so that φ₀ = 0 at the closest approach and write A = e/ℓ:

$$ u = (e cos φ − 1)/ℓ,   r = ℓ/(e cos φ − 1) $$

Only angles with e cos φ > 1 have r > 0, so the particle moves between −φ_∞ and +φ_∞, where cos φ_∞ = 1/e; this needs e > 1. The curve is one branch of a **hyperbola**, bending away from the nucleus, which sits at the outer focus. (Mathur describes the path as a hyperbola PAP′ with the nucleus at its external focus.) Compare the attractive case in PH-003, where the nucleus is at the inner focus.

## The eccentricity

At φ = 0, u′ = 0 and u₀ = (e − 1)/ℓ. The energy (module 5 of PH-003, with U = +k u) gives E = (L²/2m) u₀² + k u₀. Use L²/(m ℓ) = k:

$$ E = (k/2ℓ)(e − 1)² + (k/ℓ)(e − 1) = (k/2ℓ)(e − 1)(e + 1) = (k/2ℓ)(e² − 1) $$

so e² = 1 + 2 E ℓ/k = 1 + 2 E L²/(m k²). Substituting L² = 2 m E b²:

$$ e² = 1 + (2 E b/k)² $$

Since E > 0 and b ≥ 0, e ≥ 1, and e = 1 only for b = 0.

## The asymptotes and the scattering angle

As t → −∞ the particle is far away at φ = −φ_∞ moving inwards; as t → +∞ it is far away at φ = +φ_∞ moving outwards. At large distance the velocity is along ∓r̂, so the initial direction is (−cos φ_∞, sin φ_∞) and the final direction is (cos φ_∞, sin φ_∞). Their scalar product is cos θ = −cos² φ_∞ + sin² φ_∞ = −cos 2φ_∞ = cos(π − 2φ_∞). Hence

$$ θ = π − 2 φ_∞  ⇒  φ_∞ = (π − θ)/2  ⇒  sin(θ/2) = cos φ_∞ = 1/e $$

## Checks

Units: 2Eb/k is dimensionless (energy × length / energy × length). Limit b = 0: e = 1, sin(θ/2) = 1, θ = π, a head-on bounce straight back. Limit b → ∞: e → ∞, θ → 0 (no deflection). Numerical test (units k/(2E) = 1, b = 1): e = √2, θ = 2 arcsin(1/√2) = 90°; integrating the equations of motion gives a final direction of 89.98° and a closest approach 2.413 against the predicted 2.414.

### 5. Core: b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2) and the Rutherford formula, every step

*Objective:* Derive b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2) and then dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2) with all the algebra, and read the meaning of the formula.

## The relation between b and θ

From module 4, sin(θ/2) = 1/e and e² = 1 + (2Eb/k)². Square the first: 1/sin²(θ/2) = e² = 1 + (2Eb/k)². By bridge B2, 1/sin² x = 1 + cot² x, so

$$ cot²(θ/2) = (2 E b/k)² $$

For 0 < θ < π, cot(θ/2) is positive and so is b, so we take the positive root:

$$ b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2) $$

This reproduces the textbook relation (Upadhyaya eq. 9). Direction check: θ → π gives b → 0 (head-on); θ → 0 gives b → ∞.

## Differentiate

By bridge B2, d/dθ cot(θ/2) = −½ csc²(θ/2), so

$$ db/dθ = −(k/4E) / sin²(θ/2) $$

## The cross-section

Use the counting formula of module 3. First b/sin θ with sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2):

$$ b/sin θ = (k/2E) [cos(θ/2)/sin(θ/2)] / [2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2)] = (k/4E) / sin²(θ/2) $$

Then multiply by |db/dθ| = (k/4E)/sin²(θ/2):

$$ dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)² / sin⁴(θ/2) $$

With k = Z₁Z₂ q_e²/(4π ε₀) this is [Z₁Z₂ q_e²/(16π ε₀ E)]²/sin⁴(θ/2). For an alpha particle (Z₁ = 2) on a nucleus Z: (Z q_e²/(8π ε₀ E))²/sin⁴(θ/2).

## What the formula says

- The number scattered into a fixed solid angle varies as 1/sin⁴(θ/2), the cosec⁴ law: large at small angles, small at back angles.
- It is proportional to (Z₁Z₂)²: doubling the nuclear charge multiplies the counts by 4; proton projectiles (Z₁ = 1) give one quarter of the alpha rate at the same energy.
- It is proportional to 1/E²: doubling the kinetic energy divides the counts by 4.

## Checks

Units: k/E has units of length, so (k/4E)² is an area. Small angles: sin(θ/2) ≈ θ/2, so dσ/dΩ ≈ (k/E)²/θ⁴. Numerical test of b(θ) by integrating the equations of motion (units k/(2E) = 0.5): b = 0.5, 1, 2, 5 give θ = 89.99°, 53.12°, 28.07°, 11.42°, against the formula's 90.00°, 53.13°, 28.07°, 11.42°. Angular-law check: σ(60°)/σ(120°) = sin⁴ 60°/sin⁴ 30° = 9.

### 6. Core: reading the formula - the divergent total cross-section and the distance of closest approach

*Objective:* Show why the total Rutherford cross-section is infinite, find the cut-off from screening, and derive the head-on and general distances of closest approach.

## The total cross-section diverges

Integrate dσ/dΩ over all angles larger than a small cut-off θ_m, using dΩ = 2π sin θ dθ and sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2):

$$ σ(θ > θ_m) = (k/4E)² 2π ∫ 2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2)/sin⁴(θ/2) dθ = 4π (k/4E)² ∫ cos(θ/2)/sin³(θ/2) dθ $$

Put s = sin(θ/2), ds = ½ cos(θ/2) dθ, so the integral is 8π (k/4E)² ∫ ds/s³ from s_m to 1, which equals 4π (k/4E)² (1/s_m² − 1) = 4π (k/4E)² cot²(θ_m/2). As a check, this is π b_m² with b_m = (k/2E) cot(θ_m/2): the particles that scatter beyond θ_m are exactly those with impact parameter below b_m. As θ_m → 0, σ → ∞.

The reason is that the Coulomb force has infinite range: even a particle passing very far from a nucleus is deflected a little. In a real atom the electrons screen the nucleus beyond about the atomic radius b_max ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m, so the formula holds only for θ above θ_min ≈ k/(E b_max). For a 5 MeV alpha on gold (k/2E = 22.75 fm) this is 4.6 × 10⁻⁴ rad, or 0.026°.

## Closest approach, head-on

For b = 0 the particle goes straight in, stops momentarily at the distance d and returns (θ = π). Energy conservation, all kinetic energy becoming potential energy:

$$ E = k/d  ⇒  d = k/E = 2 Z q_e²/(4π ε₀ E)  (alpha on a nucleus Z) $$

This is the head-on result in Upadhyaya (section on head-on collision).

## Closest approach, general case

At the apsis the orbit gives r_min = ℓ/(e − 1). Since L² = 2 m E b², ℓ = L²/(m k) = 2 E b²/k = (k/2E)(2Eb/k)² = (k/2E)(e² − 1). Hence

$$ r_min = (k/2E)(e + 1) = (k/2E)(1 + 1/sin(θ/2)) = (d/2)(1 + 1/sin(θ/2)) $$

Check θ = π: r_min = (d/2)(1 + 1) = d ✓. For θ = 90°: r_min = (d/2)(1 + √2) = 1.207 d.

## Numbers: 5 MeV alpha on gold (Z = 79)

Using q_e²/(4π ε₀) = 1.440 MeV fm, k = 2 × 79 × 1.440 = 227.5 MeV fm. Then d = k/E = 45.5 fm, k/2E = 22.75 fm. The impact parameter for θ = 90° is 22.75 fm, and r_min(90°) = 22.75 × 2.414 = 54.9 fm. For comparison, a proton of the same energy has k = 113.8 MeV fm and d = 22.75 fm.

## Checks

Units: k/E is a length. Limiting case: very large E gives small d, so faster particles probe deeper. Cross-section of the formula in barns: dσ/dΩ(90°) = (11.375 fm)²/sin⁴45° = 517.6 fm² sr⁻¹ = 5.18 barn sr⁻¹.

### 7. Core: counting alpha particles and what Rutherford's formula says about the nucleus

*Objective:* Compute the counting rate in a detector for alpha particles on gold foil and use the closest approach to put an upper limit on the size of the nucleus.

## Counting formula

For a thin foil of thickness t containing n atoms per m³, a beam spot of N_inc particles per second fully on the foil meets N_t = n t A_beam nuclei, and the rate into a detector of solid angle ΔΩ at angle θ is

$$ N_det = N_inc · n t · (dσ/dΩ) · ΔΩ $$

(valid for single scattering; n t is the number of nuclei per m² of foil).

## Worked numbers (alpha on gold)

A beam current of 1 nA of alpha particles (charge 2q_e = 3.204 × 10⁻¹⁹ C each) delivers N_inc = 10⁻⁹/3.204 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.12 × 10⁹ particles per second. Gold: density 19.3 g cm⁻³ and atomic mass 197 u (standard values; verify) give n = 5.90 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. For a foil of t = 0.4 μm, n t = 2.36 × 10²² m⁻². Energy 5 MeV, so dσ/dΩ(60°) = 20.7 barn sr⁻¹ = 2.07 × 10⁻²⁷ m² sr⁻¹ and dσ/dΩ(90°) = 5.18 × 10⁻²⁸ m² sr⁻¹ (module 6). A detector of area 10 mm² at 0.10 m has ΔΩ = 10⁻³ sr. Then

- at 60°: N_det = 3.12 × 10⁹ × 2.36 × 10²² × 2.07 × 10⁻²⁷ × 10⁻³ = 153 per second;
- at 90°: 38 per second, one quarter of the 60° rate, as (sin 45°/sin 30°)⁴ = 4 predicts.

Fraction of all particles scattered through more than 90°: n t σ(> 90°), with σ(> 90°) = π b² at b = 22.75 fm, i.e. π (22.75 fm)² = 1626 fm² = 1.63 × 10⁻²⁷ m². So the fraction is 2.36 × 10²² × 1.63 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.8 × 10⁻⁵: about 1 in 26,000, small but not negligible.

## What Rutherford concluded

Large deflections need a very strong, very localised electric field: the repulsion k/r² is large only if the positive charge is concentrated within a tiny region. If the measured counts follow the formula all the way to large angles for alpha particles that reach the head-on distance d, then within d of the centre there is no sign of the positive charge having a size of its own. Hence the radius R of the positive charge must be less than d. Example: a 5 MeV alpha on gold reaches 45.5 fm (4.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ m), still following the point-charge formula, while the atom has a radius of about 10⁻¹⁰ m, more than 2000 times larger. So the positive charge and nearly all the mass are in a **nucleus** of radius below 4.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ m (below 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m for the 10 MeV lead case of the 2017 question), surrounded by mostly empty space and the electrons. (Textbook nuclear radii, about 1.2 A^(1/3) fm = 7 fm for gold, are smaller still; this is a standard rule, to be verified, so the Rutherford limit is a conservative upper bound.)

## Checks

Units: N_inc in s⁻¹, n t in m⁻², dσ/dΩ in m² sr⁻¹, ΔΩ in sr: product s⁻¹ ✓. Limit: doubling the foil thickness doubles the rate while single scattering holds. The numbers rely on the density and mass of gold, which are not in the extracts.

### 8. Core: laboratory and centre-of-mass frames - angles, recoil and the maximum laboratory angle

*Objective:* Derive tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + m1/m2), the recoil angle (π − Θ)/2 and the maximum laboratory angle for an elastic collision with a target initially at rest.

## Why two frames

The formula so far assumed a target that never moves. A real target recoils. The detector is fixed in the **laboratory (lab) frame**, where the target is at rest before the collision; the physics is simplest in the **centre-of-mass (CM) frame**, where the total momentum is zero (Mathur calls it the C-frame; it is inertial for an isolated pair). We need to translate angles between them.

## Velocities (bridge B3)

Projectile m1 with speed v0 along +x hits m2 at rest. The CM velocity is V = m1 v0/(m1 + m2) along +x. In the CM frame the projectile has speed u₁′ = m2 v0/(m1 + m2) and the target has velocity −V (equal and opposite momenta, m1 u₁′ = m2 V).

## Elastic scattering in the CM frame

Momentum stays zero and energy is conserved, so each speed is unchanged; only the directions rotate. Let the projectile's CM velocity turn through the **CM scattering angle** Θ: after the collision it is u₁′ (cos Θ, sin Θ), and the target's CM velocity is −V (cos Θ, sin Θ).

## Projectile: the lab angle

Galilean addition (lab velocity = CM-frame velocity + V x̂): v₁x = V + u₁′ cos Θ and v₁y = u₁′ sin Θ. Hence, with γ = V/u₁′ = m1/m2:

$$ tan θ = u₁′ sin Θ/(V + u₁′ cos Θ) = sin Θ/(cos Θ + γ) $$

## Target: the recoil angle

The target's lab velocity is V x̂ − V (cos Θ, sin Θ) = V (1 − cos Θ, −sin Θ). Its angle below the x axis: tan φ = sin Θ/(1 − cos Θ) = cot(Θ/2), so φ = (π − Θ)/2, independent of the masses. Its speed is V √((1 − cos Θ)² + sin² Θ) = 2V sin(Θ/2), and the energy it takes is

$$ T₂ = ½ m2 (2V sin(Θ/2))² = [4 m1 m2/(m1 + m2)²] E_lab sin²(Θ/2) $$

The maximum, 4 m1 m2/(m1 + m2)² of E_lab, occurs at Θ = π (head-on).

## The three cases (γ = m1/m2)

- γ < 1 (heavy target, such as alpha on gold, γ = 0.0203): cos Θ + γ can be zero or negative, so θ can take every value up to 180°; for γ ≪ 1, as here, θ is close to Θ (Θ = 90° gives θ = 88.84°, below).
- γ = 1: tan θ = sin Θ/(1 + cos Θ) = tan(Θ/2), so θ = Θ/2 ≤ 90°, and the two particles leave at right angles.
- γ > 1 (light target, such as alpha on hydrogen, γ = 3.97): cos Θ + γ > 0, so θ < 90°. Maximum: d(tan θ)/dΘ = (1 + γ cos Θ)/(cos Θ + γ)² = 0 at cos Θ = −1/γ; then tan θ_max = 1/√(γ² − 1), i.e. **sin θ_max = 1/γ = m2/m1**. These cases agree with those in Mathur's section on the value of the scattering angle.

## Numbers and checks

Alpha (4.0026 u) on gold (196.97 u): γ = 0.02032; Θ = 90° gives θ = 88.84°; Θ = 150° gives 149.41°. Alpha on hydrogen (1.00783 u): θ_max = arcsin(1/3.9715) = 14.58°. For γ = 1, Θ = 90° gives θ = 45° and the target recoils at (π − Θ)/2 = 45° on the other side, so the paths are perpendicular. Checks: when m2 → ∞, γ → 0 and θ → Θ (the infinite-mass target of modules 3-6); Θ = 0 gives θ = 0 and no recoil.

### 9. Master: finite target mass, the cross-section in the laboratory frame and the limits of validity

*Objective:* Extend the Rutherford formula to a recoiling target through the reduced mass, transform dσ/dΩ to the lab frame, and list the limits of validity.

## Rutherford scattering with a recoiling target

In the CM frame the relative motion obeys μ r̈ = F(r) r̂ with μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2) (PH-009). Modules 4-5 go through unchanged with m replaced by μ, v0 the relative speed at infinity and E replaced by the CM energy E_cm = ½ μ v0² = E_lab m2/(m1 + m2); the angle is the CM scattering angle Θ. Therefore

$$ dσ/dΩ_C = (k/4E_cm)² / sin⁴(Θ/2) $$

## From the CM frame to the lab frame

The particles that go into dΩ_C also go into dΩ_L, so (dσ/dΩ_L) dΩ_L = (dσ/dΩ_C) dΩ_C. The azimuth is the same in both frames, and dΩ = 2π d(cos angle), so dσ/dΩ_L = (dσ/dΩ_C) |d cos Θ/d cos θ|. From module 8, cos θ = (cos Θ + γ)/s with s² = 1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ and ds/d(cos Θ) = γ/s. Differentiating:

$$ d cos θ/d cos Θ = 1/s − γ (cos Θ + γ)/s³ = (s² − γ cos Θ − γ²)/s³ = (1 + γ cos Θ)/s³ $$

$$ dσ/dΩ_L = (dσ/dΩ_C) · s³/|1 + γ cos Θ| $$

evaluated at the CM angle Θ that corresponds to the lab angle θ.

## How large are the corrections? (alpha particle, 4.0026 u)

| Target | γ = m1/m2 | E_cm/E_lab | (E_lab/E_cm)² |
|---|---|---|---|
| gold-197 | 0.0203 | 0.980 | 1.041 |
| aluminium-27 | 0.148 | 0.871 | 1.319 |
| helium-4 (identical particles; see limit 6) | 1.000 | 0.500 | 4.00 |
| hydrogen-1 | 3.97 | 0.201 | 24.7 |

The last column is the factor by which the true CM cross-section at the same angle exceeds the infinite-mass formula evaluated with E_lab. For gold at Θ = 90° the Jacobian s³/(1 + γ cos Θ) = (1 + γ²)^(3/2) = 1.0006, so the lab cross-section is the CM one to 0.06 %: the infinite-mass formula is good to a few percent for heavy targets and wrong for light ones.

**Two lab-angle branches for γ > 1.** When γ > 1 (helium-4 and hydrogen targets in the table) the lab angle never exceeds θ_max, and each θ < θ_max is reached from two CM angles, cos Θ = −γ sin²θ ± cos θ √(1 − γ² sin²θ). The lab cross-section at θ is the sum of both contributions, dσ/dΩ_L = Σ over the two Θ of (dσ/dΩ_C) s³/|1 + γ cos Θ|, and the Jacobian diverges at θ_max, where 1 + γ cos Θ = 0. The single-term formula above holds as it stands only for γ < 1.

## Limits of validity

1. **Nuclear force**: the formula assumes only the Coulomb force. When the closest approach becomes comparable to the sum of the radii, the nuclear force appears and the back-angle counts fall below the prediction. With the common rule R = 1.2 A^(1/3) fm (standard; verify) the sum of radii for alpha on lead is about 9.0 fm and the Coulomb barrier about 26 MeV.
2. **Screening** by the atomic electrons at large b: below θ_min ≈ k/(E b_max) ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁴ rad (5 MeV alpha on gold) the formula fails.
3. **Single scattering**: the foil must be thin; many small deflections otherwise blur the angular law.
4. **Non-relativistic** motion and a nucleus treated as a point.
5. **Classical versus quantum**: for the pure Coulomb potential between distinguishable particles the quantum calculation gives the same differential cross-section; this standard result is quoted without proof. Upadhyaya remarks that the problem is quantum mechanical but the classical procedure is similar.
6. **Helium-4 and other light or identical-particle cases**: the head-on distance 1.15 fm for 10 MeV alpha on helium-4 (N8(b)) is far inside the nuclear-force range (the sum of radii is about 3.8 fm), and alpha on helium-4 is identical-particle scattering, where classical Rutherford scattering does not apply (a Mott-type quantum treatment is needed; verify). The number is a pure-Coulomb kinematic exercise, not a physical prediction.

## Why a smeared-out positive charge fails (order-of-magnitude estimate by this lesson)

If the positive charge were spread over an atom of radius 10⁻¹⁰ m, the largest single deflection of a 5 MeV alpha is about k/(E R) = 4.6 × 10⁻⁴ rad. A random walk through the 1.6 × 10³ atomic layers of a 0.4 μm gold foil gives an rms angle of about 4.6 × 10⁻⁴ × √1560 ≈ 0.018 rad ≈ 1°, far below 90°. Large deflections therefore need a concentrated charge.

### 10. Exam application: writing the Rutherford derivation and the three past questions

*Objective:* Present a Rutherford-scattering derivation in an examiner-friendly form and know how the three verified past questions are answered.

## The skeleton of a good answer

(1) State the assumptions and the symbols (m, E, b, θ, k, Z, flux). (2) Draw the figure: incoming line, impact parameter, hyperbola, nucleus at the outer focus, asymptotes, θ. (3) Conservation laws: L = m v0 b and E = ½ m v0². (4) Orbit and angle: r = ℓ/(e cos φ − 1), e² = 1 + (2Eb/k)², sin(θ/2) = 1/e. (5) b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2), differentiate, form (b/sin θ)|db/dθ|. (6) Result, units and limits. Practice targets (not official allotments): about 12 minutes for 10 marks, 25 minutes for 20 marks.

## 2017 (Paper II Q5(a), 10 marks): nuclear radius of lead

Back-scattering means θ = π, a head-on approach: ½ m v² = 2Z q_e²/(4π ε₀ d), so d = 2Z q_e²/(4π ε₀ E). With Z = 82, E = 10 MeV = 10 × 10⁶ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.6 × 10⁻¹² J, q_e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and (4π ε₀)⁻¹ = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²:

$$ d = 2 × 82 × (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)² × 9 × 10⁹/(1.6 × 10⁻¹²) = 2.36 × 10⁻¹⁴ m $$

So the nuclear radius is below about 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m: the order of 10⁻¹⁴ m (about 4 × 10³ times smaller than an atom of radius 10⁻¹⁰ m). State that this is an upper limit, since the alpha particle did not touch matter, and that treating lead as infinitely heavy is a small approximation (E_cm = 9.81 MeV).

## 2019 (Paper I Q2(c), 10 marks): the R²/4 cross-section

Module 3: b = R cos(θ/2), σ = (b/sin θ)|db/dθ| = R²/4, independent of angle. Inference: isotropic scattering; the total cross-section is π R², the geometric area. The question wording speaks of 'the cross-section ... is R²/4'; read it as the differential cross-section and state the total as a bonus.

## 2023 (Paper II Q6(a), 20 marks): establish the Rutherford formula

Give the assumptions (point nucleus at rest, Coulomb force, classical, thin foil) and the symbols; the figure; the conservation laws; the orbit and the eccentricity; the angle relation; b(θ); then σ(θ). Finish with the dependence on Z, E and θ, the closest approach and a remark on the lab frame and the limits. For 20 marks the extra credit lies in the full derivation of e and of sin(θ/2) = 1/e, not in quoting them.

## Traps

Using the nuclear charge Z in place of the product 2Z in k for alpha particles; forgetting that E must be in joules when using SI constants; quoting b = (k/2E) tan(θ/2) (tan instead of cot); forgetting the modulus in σ; confusing the lab angle with the CM angle.

### Notes: Definitions

- Flux I₀: particles crossing unit area perpendicular to the beam per second (m⁻² s⁻¹).
- Impact parameter b: perpendicular distance from the scattering centre to the incident line of flight.
- Scattering angle θ: angle between the final and initial directions of motion, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π.
- Differential cross-section dσ/dΩ = (number scattered per second into dΩ)/(I₀ dΩ), units m² sr⁻¹; total cross-section σ = ∫ (dσ/dΩ) dΩ.
- Solid angle: Ω = A/R² steradians; ring about the beam axis dΩ = 2π sin θ dθ; 4π sr in all.
- Barn: 1 b = 10⁻²⁸ m²; 1 fm² = 10⁻³⁰ m², so 1 b = 100 fm².
- Laboratory frame: target at rest. Centre-of-mass (CM) frame: moves with the CM, total momentum zero, inertial for an isolated pair.
- k = Z₁Z₂ q_e²/(4π ε₀); for an alpha particle on a nucleus Z, k = 2Z q_e²/(4π ε₀); q_e²/(4π ε₀) = 1.440 MeV fm.

### Notes: Governing equations

- General: dσ/dΩ = (b/sin θ)|db/dθ| (single-valued monotonic b(θ)).
- Coulomb orbit: r = ℓ/(e cos φ − 1), ℓ = L²/(m k), e² = 1 + (2Eb/k)², sin(θ/2) = 1/e.
- b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2); db/dθ = −(k/4E)/sin²(θ/2).
- Rutherford: dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2).
- σ(θ > θ_m) = π b_m² = 4π (k/4E)² cot²(θ_m/2) → ∞ as θ_m → 0.
- Head-on closest approach d = k/E; general r_min = (k/2E)(1 + 1/sin(θ/2)).
- Rate: N_det = N_inc · n t · (dσ/dΩ) · ΔΩ.
- Lab-CM: tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + m1/m2); recoil angle (π − Θ)/2; sin θ_max = m2/m1 if m1 > m2.
- CM energy: E_cm = E_lab m2/(m1 + m2); dσ/dΩ_L = (dσ/dΩ_C) s³/|1 + γ cos Θ|, s² = 1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ.

### Notes: Derivation chain for the Rutherford formula

- Conservation: L = m v0 b = b √(2mE); E = ½ m ṙ² + L²/(2mr²) + k/r.
- Binet with F = +k u²: u'' + u = −1/ℓ → r = ℓ/(e cos φ − 1).
- Energy at the apsis: e² = 1 + 2EL²/(mk²) = 1 + (2Eb/k)².
- Asymptotes: θ = π − 2φ_∞, cos φ_∞ = 1/e → sin(θ/2) = 1/e.
- Square and use 1/sin² = 1 + cot²: cot(θ/2) = 2Eb/k → b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2).
- Differentiate and form (b/sin θ)|db/dθ| → (k/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2).

### Notes: Worked results to remember

- 5 MeV alpha on gold (Z = 79): k = 227.5 MeV fm, d = 45.5 fm, b(90°) = 22.75 fm, r_min(90°) = 54.9 fm.
- dσ/dΩ at 5 MeV on gold: 20.7 barn/sr at 60°, 5.18 barn/sr at 90° (ratio 4).
- Fraction of alpha particles scattered beyond 90° in a 0.4 μm gold foil: 3.8 × 10⁻⁵.
- 10 MeV alpha on lead head-on: d = 2.36 × 10⁻¹⁴ m (2017 estimate; upper limit on the nuclear radius).
- Hard sphere of radius R: dσ/dΩ = R²/4 (isotropic), σ = π R².
- Alpha on gold: E_cm = 0.980 E_lab; lab angle 88.84° for CM angle 90°. Alpha on hydrogen: θ_max = 14.58°.

### Notes: Rutherford versus hard-sphere scattering

- Two classical cross-sections, one with a finite-range 'wall' and one with a long-range force.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Scattering angle θ (between directions) versus the polar angle φ in the orbit (from the symmetry axis): θ = π − 2φ_∞.
- Impact parameter b (distance of the incoming line from the centre) versus closest approach r_min (actual smallest distance): r_min > b for repulsion.
- Differential cross-section dσ/dΩ (per steradian) versus the cross-section per unit angle dσ/dθ = 2π sin θ dσ/dΩ.
- CM angle Θ versus lab angle θ: equal only for an infinitely heavy target; for equal masses θ = Θ/2.
- E_lab versus E_cm in the formula: for light targets E_cm is much smaller.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- The formula fails at very small angles (screening by atomic electrons).
- It fails at large angles when the projectile comes within the range of the nuclear force.
- Multiple scattering in a thick foil invalidates the single-scattering counting formula.
- For a light target use the CM energy and transform to the lab frame.
- Classical derivation agrees with quantum mechanics for the pure Coulomb potential (standard result, stated not proved).

  - Writing e for both the eccentricity and the electronic charge without distinguishing them

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Define the impact parameter.
    - **a:** The perpendicular distance from the scattering centre to the original (undeflected) line of flight of the incident particle.
  - - **q:** What is the unit of solid angle and how large is a full sphere?
    - **a:** The steradian; 4π sr.
  - - **q:** What does the differential cross-section count?
    - **a:** The number of particles scattered per second into a unit solid angle, per unit incident flux (units m² sr⁻¹).
  - - **q:** State the Rutherford formula and name its symbols.
    - **a:** dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2), where k = Z₁Z₂ q_e²/(4π ε₀), E is the incident kinetic energy and θ the scattering angle.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why does Rutherford's formula fail at very small angles in a real atom?
    - **a:** The atomic electrons screen the nuclear charge at distances beyond about the atomic radius, so particles with larger impact parameters are not deflected as the unscreened Coulomb law predicts.
  - - **q:** Why must the target foil be thin for the counting formula?
    - **a:** So that each alpha particle is scattered at most once; multiple scattering would blur the angular distribution and break the single-scattering counting formula.
  - - **q:** Why is the Rutherford cross-section ∝ (Z₁Z₂)²?
    - **a:** Because k ∝ Z₁Z₂ and dσ/dΩ ∝ k².
  - - **q:** In which limit do the laboratory and CM scattering angles coincide?
    - **a:** When the target is infinitely heavy (γ = m1/m2 → 0); tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + γ) → tan Θ.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A 6 MeV alpha particle is scattered by a copper nucleus (Z = 29) through 120°. Find the impact parameter (q_e²/(4π ε₀) = 1.44 MeV fm).
    - **a:** k = 2 × 29 × 1.44 = 83.5 MeV fm; k/2E = 83.5/12 = 6.96 fm; b = 6.96 cot 60° = 6.96 × 0.5774 = 4.02 fm.
  - - **q:** Compare the Rutherford counts at 40° and 80° for the same beam, target and detector.
    - **a:** N(40°)/N(80°) = (sin 40°/sin 20°)⁴ = (0.6428/0.3420)⁴ = (1.879)⁴ = 12.5.
  - - **q:** An alpha particle (4 u) hits a stationary 8 u nucleus. Find the maximum lab angle and E_cm/E_lab.
    - **a:** γ = 4/8 = 0.5 < 1, so the lab angle can reach 180°; E_cm/E_lab = 8/12 = 0.667.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-HYP-IMPACT (verified):** Impact parameter: the perpendicular distance of closest approach if the projectile were undeflected; in Rutherford scattering the scattering angle depends on it, with larger deflection for smaller impact parameter.
  Source: [HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), Rutherford Scattering and Scattering Cross Section](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/Nuclear/rutsca2.html)
- **F-HYP-NUCSIZE (verified):** Rutherford scattering was the first method used to measure the size of nuclei; it showed nuclear density is approximately constant, so nuclear radii can be modelled from mass numbers.
  Source: [HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), Rutherford Scattering](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/Nuclear/rutsca2.html)

### Memory skeleton

- Geometry: flux I₀, impact parameter b, scattering angle θ, dΩ = 2π sin θ dθ
- Counting: dσ/dΩ = (b/sin θ)|db/dθ|; hard sphere R²/4
- Repulsive Coulomb orbit: hyperbola, e² = 1 + (2Eb/k)², sin(θ/2) = 1/e
- b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2) → dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2)
- Total σ infinite; closest approach d = k/E bounds the nucleus
- Lab and CM frames: tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + m1/m2); E_cm = E_lab m2/(m1 + m2)
- Limits: screening, nuclear force, multiple scattering

### Last-week summary

- Particles through ring 2πb db go into 2π sin θ dθ: dσ/dΩ = (b/sin θ)|db/dθ|.
- L = m v0 b; Binet gives r = ℓ/(e cos φ − 1); e² = 1 + (2Eb/k)²; θ = π − 2φ_∞, sin(θ/2) = 1/e.
- b = (k/2E) cot(θ/2); dσ/dΩ = (k/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2), k = Z₁Z₂q_e²/(4πε₀) (alpha: 2Z).
- ∝ (Z₁Z₂)²/E²; total σ diverges; head-on d = k/E; hard sphere R²/4, σ = πR².
- tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + m1/m2); recoil (π − Θ)/2; equal masses θ = Θ/2.
