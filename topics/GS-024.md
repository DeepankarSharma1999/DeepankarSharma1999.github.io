### Bridge: Reading dates: CE, BCE, centuries and what 'mid-18th century' means

## Why a history lesson starts with arithmetic

Every fact in this lesson carries a date, and UPSC questions use phrases such as 'the first half of the eighteenth century' or 'mid-eighteenth century India'. If you misplace the century, you misplace the whole story. This bridge fixes the reading rules once.

## CE and BCE

**CE** means **Common Era** and is the same as the older label **AD**. **BCE** means **Before Common Era** and is the same as **BC**. Years in the CE count upward from year 1; years in the BCE count downward towards year 1. NCERT, for example, writes that the dharmashastras were composed from about 500 BCE onwards, meaning five centuries before year 1. This lesson uses CE years only, so plain numbers such as 1707 or 1757 are CE.

## The century rule

A century is a block of 100 years that **ends in 00**. The 1st century is years 1 to 100, so the **18th century is 1701 to 1800**. A shortcut: take the first two digits of the year and add one. For 1707, 17 + 1 = 18, so it is in the 18th century. For 1757, again the 18th century. For 1857, 18 + 1 = 19, so it is in the 19th century. Edge cases: 1700 belongs to the 17th century and 1800 to the 18th, because a century ends on the round year.

## Halves and 'mid' in exam language

- **First half of the 18th century**: roughly 1701 to 1750. Spectrum uses this phrase for the Mughal decline after Aurangzeb died in 1707.
- **Second half of the 18th century**: roughly 1751 to 1800. NCERT places the rise of the British as a new political power here.
- **Mid-18th century**: not a fixed range, but the decades around 1750, in practice roughly 1735 to 1765. The 2017 Mains question in this lesson uses it, so think of Nadir Shah (1739), Plassey (1757) and Panipat (1761) as all 'mid-century' events.

## Regnal periods

Rulers are listed as 'ruled 1719-48'. That means the reign began in 1719 and ended in 1748, a span of 29 years. Always subtract to check a claim about how long someone ruled.

### Bridge: Words of the Mughal state: subah, nawab, jagir, zamindar, farman, diwani

## Why the vocabulary comes first

Almost every cause of Mughal decline is written with Mughal administrative words. If the words are unclear, the causes become slogans. Meet them here in plain language; the main lesson then reuses them.

## Core terms

- **Subah**: a province of the empire. The governor was the **subadar** (also spelled subahdar). NCERT notes that after 1707 'many Mughal governors (subadars) and big zamindars' began asserting their authority and building regional kingdoms.
- **Nawab**: a title for a ruler or governor in the Mughal world; later the ruler of a regional state, for instance the Nawab of Bengal or Awadh.
- **Wazir**: the emperor's chief minister. Nizam-ul-Mulk became wazir under Muhammad Shah (standard accounts: appointed 1722; Spectrum prints 1724; verify).
- **Mansab and mansabdar**: a mansab was a numerical rank in the imperial service; its holder was a mansabdar. Nobles holding mansabs were the main administrators of the empire. (The full system belongs to GS-019, the topic on mansabdari and jagirdari.)
- **Jagir and jagirdar**: a jagir was a land assignment whose revenue paid the mansabdar for his rank. A jagirdar was its holder. **Khalisa** land was land whose revenue went straight to the crown.
- **Zamindar**: Spectrum describes zamindars as hereditary owners of their lands with hereditary privileges, who helped collect revenue and run local administration and kept soldiers for that purpose. They shared power with the emperor and the nobles.
- **Izara (revenue farming)**: a system where the right to collect revenue from an area is given to a contractor for a fixed sum. Zulfikar Khan introduced it under Jahandar Shah (Spectrum).
- **Farman**: a royal order or edict (NCERT's glossary). **Diwani**: the right to collect land revenue in a province.
- **Residents and subsidiary alliance**: Company-era terms from the political-context module of this lesson. A Resident was a Company agent at an Indian court; a subsidiary alliance meant the ruler gave up an independent army and paid for Company troops.

## Three kinds of post-Mughal state

Spectrum sorts the new political units into three classes. Learn the class names now:

1. **Successor states**: former Mughal provinces whose governors made their rule hereditary and virtually independent, without openly denying the emperor. Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad.
2. **Independent kingdoms**: states that arose because Mughal control over provinces broke down. Mysore, the Rajput states.
3. **New states**: states set up by rebels against the Mughals. The Maratha, Sikh and Jat states.

## Check yourself

Say which class each of Awadh, Mysore and the Sikh misls belongs to before reading module 6.

### 1. Orientation: an empire loosens, many states appear

*Objective:* State in five sentences what 'decline of the Mughals' means, name the five anchor dates, and say what the lesson will ask you to explain.

## What this topic is about

In 1707 Aurangzeb died. He had ruled since 1658 and, as NCERT puts it, was the last of the powerful Mughal rulers, controlling a very large part of present-day India. Within about fifty years the Mughal emperor in Delhi had become a figure whom provincial governors, local chiefs and even rebels treated with respect but did not obey. Out of the gaps appeared new states: Hyderabad, Awadh, Bengal, Mysore, Travancore, the Rajput kingdoms, the Jat, Sikh and Maratha powers, and small Afghan states in Rohilkhand and Farrukhabad. By the mid-century a very different power, the English East India Company, was becoming a political actor.

The topic **'Decline of Mughals and regional states'** therefore has four layers, and the lesson follows them in order:

1. **Narrative**: who ruled in Delhi after Aurangzeb, and what shocks hit them (modules 3 and 5).
2. **Causes**: why a strong empire weakened (module 4).
3. **Outcome**: which states emerged, how they worked, and why none replaced the Mughals (modules 6 to 8).
4. **Context and debate**: how the Company entered this political landscape, and whether the decline came mainly from within or from outside (modules 9 and 10).

## Five anchor dates

Hold five dates before you hold anything else. Each is explained later.

- **1707**: death of Aurangzeb.
- **1739**: Nadir Shah defeats the Mughal army at Karnal and sacks Delhi.
- **1757**: Battle of Plassey in Bengal (also the year Abdali captured Delhi).
- **1761**: Third Battle of Panipat between the Marathas and Ahmad Shah Abdali.
- **1765**: the Company receives the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

## What 'decline' does and does not mean

- The empire did **not** vanish in 1707. Mughal emperors continued until 1857, and NCERT stresses that they stayed symbolically important: when the great rebellion of 1857 began, Bahadur Shah Zafar was seen as the natural leader. In legal terms Spectrum dates the end of the Mughal empire to 1 November 1858.
- Decline was a **shift of real power** from the centre to the regions. It was a gradual process, not one event.
- 'Regional state' does not mean 'small state'. Hyderabad, Mysore and the Marathas were large and well armed.


### 2. Foundation: how the Mughal state held together

*Objective:* Explain in your own words the three pillars (emperor, nobles, zamindars) and the jagir-revenue link that the causes of decline later attack.

## The idea of 'holding together'

An empire the size of the Mughals needs a way to turn distant villages into money and soldiers, and to keep ambitious people loyal. The Mughal answer was a partnership between the emperor and two classes, as Spectrum puts it: **the nobles and the zamindars shared the power of the state with the emperor**.

## Pillar 1: the emperor and personal rule

Spectrum describes Mughal government as a **personal despotism** (a federation of ranks and local powers, not one organisation with one army): success depended on the character and ability of the reigning ruler. That is a strength under a capable emperor and a weakness under a poor one, because there is no other institution that can correct him. Keep this in mind when module 3 lists the later emperors.

## Pillar 2: the nobility

Nobles were people given a **mansab** and often a **jagir**, or posted as governors of provinces. Many Rajput rulers, provincial governors and mansabdars belonged to this class. Spectrum notes that Mughal rule has often been called 'the rule of the nobility', because these nobles played a central role in administering the empire. Akbar had given the nobles a well-knit organisation, but the nobility was divided by religion, homeland and tribe, and groups competed for power when strong leadership was missing (Spectrum). Spectrum names some of these groups in its Muslim-society passage: Irani, Afghan, Turani and Hindustani nobles, and Sunni and Shia nobles, were kept apart by their identities.

## Pillar 3: the zamindars

Zamindars, called by various local names (Spectrum lists rais, rajas, thakurs, khuts, deshmukhs), were hereditary holders of land who helped collect revenue and keep local order, and they maintained soldiers for the purpose. The Mughals tried to curb them and deal directly with the peasants, but, Spectrum says, they did not wholly succeed. Under Aurangzeb zamindars gained influence, which encouraged regional loyalties.

## The money loop

Revenue from land supported the whole structure. Peasants, technically backward but hard-working, paid revenue to the state, zamindars, jagirdars and revenue farmers (Spectrum). A noble's rank (mansab) was paid by assigning him a jagir. If the number of nobles and ranks grows faster than the land available to assign, the loop breaks: some nobles get no jagir (**bejagiri**), others try to squeeze more from the land they have. Spectrum calls this the **jagirdari crisis** (module 4).

## What to carry forward

Three short questions drive the rest of the lesson: Did the **centre** fail (emperor and court)? Did the **middle layer** fail (nobles and jagirs)? Did the **local layer** drift away (zamindars, chiefs, regional rulers)? Spectrum itself says historians divide between an 'empire-related' (Mughal-centric) view and a 'region-related' view, and that the decline was due to both aspects.

### 3. Core: the later Mughals, 1707 to 1858

*Objective:* Narrate the sequence of emperors after Aurangzeb in three phases and name the turning points: succession wars, king-makers, Muhammad Shah, and the Company's protection.

## Reading the sequence in three phases

Do not memorise fifteen names. Learn three phases and the turning point of each. The emperor-by-emperor list is in the master notes.

## Phase 1: succession wars and king-makers (1707 to 1719)

Aurangzeb left no fixed rule of succession (Spectrum: no law of primogeniture), so princes fought. **Bahadur Shah I** (1707-12), already 63, won after a war of about two years; he followed a peaceful policy towards Marathas, Rajputs and Jats, but attacked the Sikh leader Banda Bahadur. **Jahandar Shah** (1712-13) ruled through his minister Zulfikar Khan, who introduced the izara system; Spectrum credits Jahandar Shah with abolishing the jizya. **Farrukhsiyar** (1713-19) came to the throne with the help of the **Sayyid brothers**, Abdulla Khan and Hussain Ali, known as 'king-makers'. In 1717 he gave farmans to the British. The jizya was later reimposed and abolished again under Farrukhsiyar (Spectrum credits him too; standard accounts: reimposed 1717, abolished 1719, verify). In 1719 the Sayyid brothers, with Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath, deposed him; he was blinded and killed, the first time nobles killed a Mughal emperor (Spectrum). Two short-lived emperors followed in 1719.

## Phase 2: Muhammad Shah and the lost opportunity (1719 to 1748)

**Muhammad Shah** (ruled 29 years, nicknamed 'Rangeela') was chosen by the Sayyid brothers but, with help from **Nizam-ul-Mulk**, killed them. Spectrum says a revival of imperial fortunes did not occur, because he was incompetent. His reign saw the establishment of the independent states of Hyderabad, Bengal, Awadh and Punjab (Spectrum's chapter introduction). In 1737 Baji Rao I, the Maratha Peshwa, appeared at Delhi with about 500 horsemen, and in 1739 Nadir Shah defeated the Mughals at Karnal (module 5).

## Phase 3: puppet emperors and the Company (1748 to 1858)

- **Ahmad Shah Bahadur** (1748-54) and **Alamgir II** (1754-59) were controlled by court factions; Alamgir II was assassinated. In his reign Abdali reached Delhi (January 1757) and Plassey was fought (June 1757).
- **Shah Alam II** (Spectrum prints Oct 1760 to July 1788 and Oct 1788 to Nov 1806; the break is his deposition and blinding by Ghulam Qadir in 1788, and standard accounts date his accession to Dec 1759; verify): his reign saw the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) and Buxar (1764). Under the Treaty of Allahabad of August 1765 he came under Company protection at Allahabad and issued the farman granting the Company the **Diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. The Marathas took him to Delhi in 1772; in 1803, after Scindia's defeat, he accepted English protection and became a pensioner.
- **Akbar Shah II** (1806-37): in 1835 the Company stopped issuing coins in his name. **Bahadur Shah II (Zafar)** (1837-57) was the last emperor; sent to Rangoon after 1857, he died there in 1862. The Mughal empire ended in legal terms on **1 November 1858** (Spectrum).

## What the sequence teaches

Nobles make and unmake emperors (phase 1); the emperor survives while provinces slip away (phase 2); an outside company protects and then pensions him (phase 3). No institution could correct a weak ruler.


### 4. Core: the causes of decline in five baskets

*Objective:* Group the causes of Mughal decline into five baskets, give one piece of evidence for each, and say which basket is contested.

## Why a basket method

Spectrum lists eight 'nutshell' causes plus more in the text. A beginner who memorises eight loose points forgets half. Sort them into five baskets, each with a question and an evidence line.

## Basket A: rulers and succession (the centre)

*Question: did the top fail?* Spectrum's points: the government was a personal despotism, so success depended on the ruler; later Mughals neglected administration; there was no definite law of succession, so every succession brought a war, fostering partisanship at the cost of patriotism; degenerate rulers produced degenerate nobles. **Evidence**: the sequence in module 3, where one emperor was blinded and killed by his nobles and several reigned for months.

## Basket B: nobles, jagirs and money (the middle layer)

*Question: did the system that paid the state break?* The number of amirs and ranks rose sharply and little land remained for jagirs. Aurangzeb tried to ease the shortage of jagirs by showing a higher recorded income from jagirs, which was a short-sighted fix: the amirs then pressed the peasants to recover the recorded sum, antagonising both nobles and peasantry. Wars, luxury and a reduction in khalisa land made expenditure exceed income. Spectrum adds that there was no significant scientific or technological advance to improve a stagnant economy, and that trade did not enrich the empire's treasury.

## Basket C: zamindars and regional ambitions (the local layer)

*Question: did local power drift away?* Zamindars grew in influence from Aurangzeb's reign and helped nobles carve out independent kingdoms. Jats, Sikhs and Marathas rebelled and set up kingdoms; Rajputs, once staunch supporters, were estranged after attempts to suppress them. The Marathas sought legal sanction to collect **sardeshmukhi and chauth** across India. Spectrum cautions that the Sikhs, Marathas and Rajputs did not have the capacity to overthrow the empire; they resisted to gain and keep their own territories.

## Basket D: army, size and the Deccan

*Question: could the machine be run and defended?* The army deteriorated; the empire became too vast to govern from one centre under weak rulers given the transport and communication of the time; Aurangzeb's **Deccan policy** is called a complete failure by Spectrum and kept the emperor away from the capital for a long period; the north-western frontier was neglected.

## Basket E: religion and policy (the contested basket)

*Question: did policy turn supporters into enemies?* Spectrum states that Aurangzeb's religious policy was largely responsible for revolts by Rajputs, Sikhs, Jats and Marathas, and quotes Sidney Owen, who put the reimposed jizya at the centre. Note that the same page quotes Irfan Habib, who argues that contemporary authorities stressed economic and administrative causes and knew little of religious reaction or national consciousness. So this basket is a **debate**, not a settled fact. In exam answers present it as 'one view'.

## Putting the baskets together

Spectrum's own summary: disintegration began under Aurangzeb but picked up speed only after 1707; at his death decline was not unstoppable; it had both empire-related and region-related aspects. Satish Chandra stresses deeper impersonal factors (jagirdari crisis, economic stagnation, noble factionalism, failure to accommodate the Marathas). Invasions (Nadir Shah, Abdali) are also on Spectrum's list, but they come in module 5 because their effect depended on the weakness described here.


### 5. Core: Marathas, Nadir Shah, Abdali and Panipat III

*Objective:* Narrate the north-western invasions in order with dates, explain why Panipat was the favourite battlefield, and state what the Third Battle of Panipat did and did not end.

## The weak frontier

Spectrum says later Mughals neglected the north-western borders and put little effort into protecting them. In the absence of internal strength, the empire could not meet external challenges that arrived as a series of invasions from the north-west. Two invaders matter for the exam: Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali.

## Nadir Shah, 1738-39

Nadir Shah, the Persian ruler, attacked India in 1738-39, conquered Lahore and defeated the Mughal army at **Karnal on 13 February 1739** (Spectrum; many works give 24 February 1739, New Style; verify). Muhammad Shah was captured and Delhi was looted and devastated. According to an estimate Spectrum cites, apart from the Peacock Throne and the Kohinoor diamond, seventy crore rupees were collected from the official treasury and nobles' safes. Nadir Shah took the strategically important Mughal territory west of the Indus including Kabul, which left India open to attack from the north-west again. Jon Wilson, quoted in Spectrum, argues that the consequences were wider than the plunder: authority centres broke, mounted bands ransacked the countryside, and plunder rather than negotiation created wealth, laying the ground for the Company's first territorial conquest. This is one historian's interpretation, useful in a Mains answer.

## Ahmad Shah Abdali (Durrani), 1748 to 1767

Abdali (an Afghan; Spectrum once calls him 'Iranian') succeeded Nadir Shah after Nadir's death in 1747. He invaded several times between 1748 and 1767 (the last in 1767). The Mughals tried to buy peace in 1751-52 by ceding **Punjab**. In 1757 he captured Delhi and left an Afghan caretaker; before leaving he recognised Alamgir II as emperor and the Rohilla chief **Najib-ud-Daula** as Mir Bakhshi, to act as his personal supreme agent. In 1758 **Raghunath Rao** expelled Najib and captured Punjab; in 1759 Abdali returned for revenge.

## The Marathas as claimants

The Marathas moved from regaining Maharashtra to claiming a share of the empire's revenue (chauth and sardeshmukhi) and, as Spectrum says, a bid to inherit the imperial mantle. Baji Rao I's 1737 raid on Delhi with about 500 horsemen shows how hollow Delhi's defences were.

## The Third Battle of Panipat, 1761

In 1761 Abdali defeated the Marathas at Panipat. The battle ended the Maratha ambition of ruling over India (Spectrum); NCERT says the Marathas' dream of ruling from Delhi was shattered. But the Marathas recovered quickly and offered the most formidable challenge to the Company in the struggle for supremacy (Spectrum); NCERT lists the Anglo-Maratha wars through 1817-19 (module 9). So Panipat III ended an *all-India* Maratha claim, not Maratha power.

## Why so many battles at Panipat?

Two reasons carry the point: Panipat lies on the route from the Khyber Pass to Delhi, and its flat ground suited cavalry, the main arm of war (the full list of Spectrum's reasons is in the master notes). The three battles: **1526** (Babur against Ibrahim Lodi, beginning Mughal rule), **1556** (Akbar against Hemu, continuing Mughal rule), **1761** (Marathas against Abdali).

## Cause and effect to hold

Invasions exposed and magnified the weakness: loss of Punjab and Kabul territory, a looted treasury, governors who no longer trusted Delhi, and Sikh assertion (module 7).


### 6. Core: successor states, Hyderabad, Awadh and Bengal

*Objective:* State for each of Hyderabad, Awadh and Bengal its founder, how autonomy was gained, and how it kept a formal link with the emperor.

## The pattern in one paragraph

The successor states share a pattern: a Mughal governor, strong in the field and trusted by local groups, made his office hereditary, kept the emperor's name and titles as a source of legitimacy, and treated revenue and army as his own. Spectrum says they did not challenge the sovereignty of the Mughal ruler, but their virtually independent hereditary authority marked the arrival of an autonomous polity.

## Hyderabad (the Asaf Jahi house)

The founder was **Kilich Khan, known as Nizam-ul-Mulk**. Spectrum says Zulfikar Khan had first conceived an independent state in the Deccan, but with his death in 1713 the idea was unfulfilled. Kilich Khan became wazir under Muhammad Shah (standard accounts: appointed 1722; Spectrum prints 1724; verify) and in 1724 left the court for the Deccan. Annoyed that the emperor had appointed **Mubariz Khan** as full viceroy of the Deccan, he fought and killed him at the **Battle of Shakr-Kheda (1724)** and took control of the Deccan. Spectrum gives two separate events here, not a conflict: **1724 = Shakr-Kheda, the effective founding of Hyderabad**; **1725 = he became viceroy and took the title Asaf-Jah**. He also helped Muhammad Shah to kill the Sayyid brothers (module 3).

Later links: in 1792 the Nizam joined the Marathas and the Company against Tipu Sultan (NCERT), and Hyderabad was forced to cede territory when it failed to pay for subsidiary forces (NCERT).

## Awadh

The founder was **Saadat Khan, Burhan-ul-Mulk**, a Shia. He had joined a conspiracy against the Sayyid brothers and was rewarded with a higher mansab; later, driven out of court, he decided to found a new independent state. Spectrum records that Saadat Khan committed suicide under pressure from Nadir Shah, who demanded a huge booty, and was succeeded by **Safdar Jang** as Nawab. (NCERT: Awadh and Banaras recruited peasants as professional soldiers, a model the Company copied.) NCERT also records that in 1801 the Nawab of Awadh was made to hand over more than half his territory to the Company for failing to pay for subsidiary forces, and that in 1856 the Company took over Awadh, which angered its people and helped them join the revolt of 1857.

## Bengal

The founder was **Murshid Quli Khan**, a capable ruler who, says Spectrum, made Bengal prosperous (the summary gives 1717). He was succeeded in 1727 by his son-in-law Shuja-ud-din (Spectrum prints 'son'; standard accounts give son-in-law; verify). His successor Sarfaraz Khan was killed in 1740 by **Alivardi Khan**, the deputy governor of Bihar, at Gheria; Alivardi took power and, the book says, secured his independence while paying annual tribute to the emperor. NCERT shows the Bengal nawabs (Murshid Quli Khan, Alivardi Khan, Sirajuddaulah) as strong rulers who refused concessions, denied the Company the right to mint coins and stopped its fortifications: the friction that led to Plassey (module 9).

## What binds the three

All three were rich regions in a political landscape without a strong centre; all three rulers had to balance zamindars, merchants and local nobles; and all three eventually met the Company on unequal terms. The three differ in how fast and how fully that happened, which is the subject of GS-027 and GS-028.

### 7. Core: independent and new states, Mysore to the Marathas

*Objective:* State for Mysore, Kerala, the Rajputs, Jats, Sikhs, Marathas and the Afghan states of Rohilkhand and Farrukhabad the leader, the region and one defining fact.

## The second and third classes

Module 2 introduced three classes. The successor states were done in module 6. Now: the **independent kingdoms** (Mysore, the Rajput states, and, in Spectrum's summary, Kerala) and the **new states** set up by anti-Mughal rebels (Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, and per the summary, Afghans). The grouping of Kerala and the Afghan states differs between Spectrum's text and summary; learn the summary version but note the difference.

## Mysore

Located at the junction of the Eastern and Western Ghats and ruled by the **Wodeyars**, Mysore became a constant battlefield for powers who wanted it (Spectrum). It ended up under **Haidar Ali**, who ruled amid constant conflict and fought continual wars with the British, as later did his son **Tipu Sultan**. NCERT dates Haidar's rule 1761-82 and Tipu's 1782-99, says Mysore controlled the profitable trade of the Malabar coast where the Company bought pepper and cardamom, and says Tipu modernised his army with French help. Four Anglo-Mysore wars were fought (1767-69, 1780-84, 1790-92, 1799). Spectrum notes Mysore for modernising.

## Kerala (Travancore)

**Martanda Varma** established an independent state with Travancore as capital, extended it from Kanyakumari to Cochin, organised his army on Western lines and adopted measures to develop the state (Spectrum).

## The Rajputs

Rajputs tried to regain independence in the 18th century, which forced Bahadur Shah I to march against **Ajit Singh** in 1708, allied with **Jai Singh II** and **Durgadas Rathor**; the alliance broke. At one time the Rajputs held territory from south of Delhi to the western coast. **Sawai Jai Singh** built Jaipur and five astronomical observatories (Delhi, Jaipur, Benares, Mathura, Ujjain) according to Spectrum.

## The Jats

Jat cultivators around Delhi, Mathura and Agra revolted against Aurangzeb's policies; the first major rebellion was in Mathura district in 1669 under **Gokula**, and failed. **Churaman and Badan Singh** created the Jat kingdom of **Bharatpur**; it peaked under **Suraj Mal**, who extended territory from the Ganga to the Chambal. After Suraj Mal's death in 1763 the state split among petty zamindars.

## The Sikhs

Aurangzeb executed the ninth Guru, **Guru Tegh Bahadur**, in 1675; **Guru Gobind Singh** rebelled and turned the Sikhs into a militant sect. **Banda Bahadur** led from 1708 and set up a short-lived Sikh rule (c.1710-15; verify) before he was defeated and killed. After Nadir Shah and Abdali the Sikhs reasserted themselves in **12 misls (confederacies)**. **Ranjit Singh**, son of Mahan Singh of the Sukarchakiya misl, built a strong kingdom of Punjab from the Sutlej to the Jhelum, conquered Lahore (1799) and Amritsar (1802), and by the Treaty of Amritsar accepted British rights over the Cis-Sutlej territories (the year is not in the extract; verify 1809). He died in 1839. NCERT: two prolonged wars followed his death and Punjab was annexed in 1849.

## The Marathas

Under the **Peshwas** the Marathas uprooted Mughal authority from Malwa and Gujarat; after Panipat III they were divided among Sindhia, Holkar, Gaikwad and Bhonsle chiefs held together as a confederacy under the Peshwa at Pune (NCERT) (Shivaji and the early Maratha state: GS-021).

## Rohilkhand and Farrukhabad

Afghan migration in the mid-18th century, caused by turmoil in Afghanistan, brought many Afghans to India. **Ali Muhammad Khan** used the collapse of authority after Nadir Shah's invasion to found **Rohilkhand**, the Himalayan foothills between Kumaon and the Ganga; the Rohillas suffered heavily from Jats, Awadh, Marathas and the British. **Mohammad Khan Bangash** set up a state around **Farrukhabad** east of Delhi. Note: Rohilkhand arose from the collapse after Nadir Shah, not out of land held by Abdali.

### 8. Core: nature, economy, society and limits of the regional states

*Objective:* Describe how regional states functioned, what economic and social conditions they shared, and four limitations that stopped them replacing the Mughals.

## How the regional states worked

Spectrum says the new political systems **kept ties with the Mughal authority** and treated the emperor as an umbrella of legitimacy; even rebel Maratha and Sikh chieftains recognised the emperor as supreme. Their polities were regional and functioned with the collaboration of zamindars, merchants, local nobles and chiefs, so rulers had to look after these local interests. A notable exception was Mysore, where rulers did not recognise local chieftains.

## Four limitations

1. **Weak finance, administration and military**: provincial rulers failed to build systems resting on sound finance, administration and military organisation.
2. **Technological backwardness**: Mysore tried to modernise, but on the whole the states were backward in science and technology.
3. **Constant wars with neighbours** in which none could finally dominate.
4. **No all-India alternative**: states could challenge Mughal power but none could replace it with a stable all-India polity.

The jagirdari crisis also intensified as agricultural income fell and the number of claimants multiplied (Spectrum).

## Economy: the headline picture

Agriculture was technically backward but sustained by hard peasant labour; peasants paid revenue to the state, zamindars, jagirdars and revenue farmers. Internal and foreign trade continued and even prospered while the rest of the economy stagnated. Exports exceeded imports, with the balance met by imports of gold and silver, so India was known as a **sink of precious metals**. The lists of exports, imports, textile centres and shipbuilding coasts are in the master notes ("Economy and society checklist").

**Caution on a contested claim**: Spectrum says the life of the masses was, by and large, better in the 18th century than after 100 years of British rule. This is a historian's judgement given without the full evidence in the extract; present it as 'Spectrum argues', not as a proven fact.

## Society

Spectrum describes a society of tradition and stagnation, divided by caste, religion, region, tribe and language, with a certain broad cultural unity. Muslims were divided as Sunni and Shia and by sharif and ajlaf status; women had little individuality in the patriarchal family system; purdah, sati, child marriage and polygamy persisted; slavery existed. Education was traditional with little natural science. The decline of imperial courts pushed talent to new courts at Hyderabad, Lucknow, Jaipur, Murshidabad, Patna and Kashmir (examples in the master notes).

## Reading limit

These descriptions come from a survey book that covers a long century across the whole subcontinent in a few pages. In an answer, state differences across regions rather than one picture for all of India.


### 9. Master: the political context of European entry, 1600 to 1857

*Objective:* Trace the Company's route from trading charter to territorial power in six steps, and explain how a fragmented polity made each step easier.

## Recap, not repetition

The detailed European story belongs to GS-025 (arrival of European companies), GS-026 (Carnatic and Anglo-Mysore conflict), GS-027 (Plassey, Buxar and Company rise) and GS-028 (subsidiary alliance and lapse). This module keeps only the six-step chain that links them to the political map above; dates are in the master notes.

1. **Trading rivals**: the Company's 1600 charter, and Portuguese, Dutch and French rivals. Companies made profit by excluding competition, so they fought with ships and forts and found it hard to separate trade from politics (NCERT).
2. **Bengal friction**: the Company traded under imperial grants. There were two distinct farmans: Aurangzeb's order of c.1690-91 allowed duty-free Bengal trade for an annual payment of Rs 3,000 (NCERT mentions the grant; the sum and date are standard knowledge, verify), and Farrukhsiyar's farman of 1717 (Spectrum), the one usually tested. Learn both with their dates. Bengal nawabs then asserted autonomy and refused the Company's demands (module 6).
3. **Conquest of Bengal**: Sirajuddaulah became Nawab in 1756; Clive won at **Plassey (1757)** because Mir Jafar, promised the nawabship, did not fight; Mir Qasim was defeated at **Buxar (1764)**; in **1765** the emperor appointed the Company Diwan of Bengal, so Bengal's revenue could pay for goods and troops and the outflow of gold from Britain stopped (NCERT).
4. **Indirect control**: after Buxar the Company appointed **Residents**, interfered in succession, and sometimes imposed a **subsidiary alliance**: the ruler gave up his army and paid for Company troops, and lost territory if payment failed (Awadh 1801).
5. **Wars on rivals**: four Mysore wars; the 1792 coalition of Marathas, Nizam and Company against Tipu; three Anglo-Maratha wars (the First ended in 1782 with the Treaty of Salbai; then 1803-05 and 1817-19), after which the Peshwa was removed; under Hastings (1813-23) the Company claimed **paramountcy**.
6. **The final wave**: Afghanistan 1838-42, Sind 1843, Punjab 1849, the Doctrine of Lapse, and Awadh 1856. By 1857 the Company ruled directly about **63 per cent** of the territory and **78 per cent** of the population (NCERT).

## The argument

A fragmented polity helped in three ways: rivals could be set against each other (Mir Jafar at Plassey; the 1792 coalition), rulers needed hired soldiers and money, and local wars drained treasuries. But NCERT notes that the Company rarely attacked an unfamiliar territory directly and preferred political, economic and diplomatic methods first. Fragmentation made conquest possible; Company choices made it happen.


### 10. Exam: was the decline internal or external? Debate and answer writing

*Objective:* Present at least four historians' views from Spectrum, separate the empire/region axis from the internal/external axis, and build a 15-mark balanced answer.

## Two axes, not one

Beginners treat the debate as a single contest of 'internal or external'. Spectrum uses a different division first: the **empire-related (Mughal-centric)** view finds the causes inside the structure and functioning of the empire; the **region-related** view finds them in turmoil in different parts of the empire. It adds that decline was due to both. Then there is the **internal against external** axis most exam questions use: weaknesses within the empire against shocks from outside (Nadir Shah, Abdali, Europeans). Keep both axes in mind.

## The voices quoted in Spectrum

The six-historian table is in the master notes ("Historians' views at a glance"). Keep two contrasts. **Satish Chandra and Irfan Habib** stress deep economic and structural causes (the jagirdari crisis, agrarian pressure). **William Irvine and Sidney Owen** stress military inefficiency and Aurangzeb's religious policy. J.N. Sarkar's moral judgement ('rottenness at the core') is stronger than most modern accounts, and Jon Wilson stresses how Nadir Shah's occupation broke authority; present each as one view.

## What the sources support for 'internal against external'

- Internal: succession wars, factionalism, the jagir crisis, the Deccan policy, army decay. External: Nadir Shah's looting, loss of Punjab and the west of the Indus, Abdali's invasions, the Company's rise.
- Spectrum's own summary: the process started under Aurangzeb and accelerated after 1707; at Aurangzeb's death it could still have been checked; Sikhs, Marathas and Rajputs lacked the capacity to overthrow the empire; the empire might have survived under capable successors.

A fair conclusion for an answer: **internal weaknesses made the empire vulnerable, and external shocks speeded and finalised the process**. But keep the contested points marked as contested.

## Wider reading caution

Other scholars argue the 18th century was not only decline, because some regions prospered (names often linked: Muzaffar Alam and C.A. Bayly; verify before citing, not in the extracts). Spectrum itself says trade prospered while the rest stagnated.

## Model answer skeleton (15 marks, about 250 words)

1. Introduction (30 words): Aurangzeb's death in 1707; state both views.
2. Internal factors (70 words): succession, jagirdari crisis, nobles, Deccan policy, army.
3. External factors (60 words): Nadir Shah 1739, Abdali 1748-67, Panipat 1761, the Company.
4. Historians (50 words): two names.
5. Judgement (40 words): interaction; which factor you weigh more and why.

- Writing 'decline began in 1707'. Spectrum says it began in Aurangzeb's reign and picked up after 1707.

### Notes: Definitions

- Successor state: a Mughal province turned into a virtually independent, hereditary state by its governor, who did not openly challenge the emperor's sovereignty (Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad).
- Independent kingdom: a state that emerged because Mughal control over provinces was destabilised (Mysore, the Rajput states).
- New state: a state set up by rebels against the Mughal empire (Maratha, Sikh, Jat; Spectrum's summary adds Afghan states).
- Jagirdari crisis: too many nobles and ranks for the land available to assign as jagirs, producing bejagiri (rank without assignment) and pressure on peasants.
- Khalisa: land whose revenue went straight to the crown; its reduction added to the state's financial strain (Spectrum).
- Izara: revenue farming, the sale of the right to collect revenue to a contractor; introduced by Zulfikar Khan under Jahandar Shah.
- Chauth and sardeshmukhi: Maratha claims on revenue across India that the Marathas sought to have legally sanctioned by the emperor (Spectrum).
- Diwani: the right to collect land revenue; the Company received it for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765.
- Resident: a Company agent at an Indian court after Buxar who interfered in succession and appointments (NCERT).
- Subsidiary alliance: an arrangement in which the Indian ruler gave up his independent army and paid for Company troops; failure to pay led to loss of territory (NCERT).
- Paramountcy: the claim, from Lord Hastings' time, that the Company's authority was supreme and justified annexing or threatening any Indian kingdom (NCERT).
