## PH-021: Hooke law and stress-strain relations

Physics Paper I · Physics · Continuous media

### Bridge: Units, powers of ten and the small-quantity approximation

Elasticity problems in Indian textbooks and in the Optional papers mix **SI units** and the older **cgs units**, so every numerical begins with a conversion line.

- **Force:** the SI unit is the newton (N). The cgs unit is the dyne: 1 N = 10⁵ dyne. A load of 1 kg weight (kgf) is 9.8 N.
- **Stress and moduli:** the SI unit is the pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². The cgs unit is dyne/cm²: 1 dyne/cm² = 0.1 Pa, so **multiply a cgs modulus by 0.1 to get pascals**. A modulus of 2 × 10¹² dyne/cm² is 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. Prefixes: 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa and 1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa.
- **Percent and strain:** a strain of 0.1 % is 10⁻³. Strain has no unit, so 'per cent' is just a way of writing a small number.

**The small-quantity approximation.** When |x| is much smaller than 1, (1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + n x. For a strain of 10⁻³ the neglected term is about 10⁻⁶, a thousand times smaller. This is why a length L + ΔL can be treated as L when ΔL/L is tiny, and why tan θ ≈ θ for a small shear angle. Mathur's example of a wire pulled sideways (13.11) uses exactly this step: 2√(1 + (5 × 10⁻³)²) ≈ 2 + 25 × 10⁻⁶ m.

Keep three significant figures through a calculation and convert only once, at the start.

### Bridge: Reading a graph: slope and area

Two graph skills are used repeatedly in this lesson.

**Slope.** For a straight line through the origin, y = m x, the slope m is y divided by x at any point. On a stress-strain graph, stress is on the vertical axis and strain on the horizontal axis, so the slope of the straight part is stress/strain, which is the **modulus**. A steeper line means a stiffer material (a larger modulus), not a stronger one. Strength is read from how high the curve reaches, not from how steep it starts.

**Area.** A small strip of a graph of F against displacement l has height F and width dl, so its area F dl is the small amount of work done. The area under the whole curve from 0 to l is the total work, W = ∫₀ˡ F dl. For a straight line from the origin the shape is a triangle, and the area of a triangle is one half of base times height, so W = ½ F l.

Units confirm the idea: force (N) times length (m) gives joule; stress (Pa) times strain (no unit) gives Pa, which equals J/m³, an energy per unit volume.

### 2. Foundation: stress and strain, with units and sign conventions

*Objective:* Define tensile, compressive, shear and volumetric stress and strain, give their units and dimensions, and apply the sign conventions used in the rest of the lesson.

When forces act on a body that stays in equilibrium, the body is deformed. Inside the body, **restoring forces** appear that equal the applied force in size and oppose it in direction. The restoring force per unit area is the **stress**. For a force F applied perpendicular to a cross-section of area A:

$$ σ = F/A    (SI unit: N m⁻² = pascal, Pa;  dimensions ML⁻¹T⁻²) $$

Stress has the dimensions of pressure. It is **not a vector** (NCERT, points to ponder): a force on one side of a cut has a direction, but the stress at the cut does not. For a Physics Optional reader the precise statement is that stress is a **second-rank tensor** (in general 9 components, of which 6 are independent). The force per unit area on one chosen plane, the traction, is a vector; NCERT's line only means that stress is not a vector. For a wire hanging from a ceiling with a weight F at the lower end, the ceiling pulls up with F and the weight pulls down with F, yet the tension in any cross-section is F, not 2F, so σ = F/A.

**Strain** is the fractional change in a dimension, so it is a pure number. There are three basic kinds.

| Kind | Stress | Strain | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longitudinal (tensile or compressive) | F/A, force normal to the face | ε = ΔL/L | length (and slightly the width) |
| Shear | F/A, force tangential to the face | φ = Δx/L = tan θ ≈ θ | shape, not volume |
| Volumetric (hydraulic) | pressure p, normal and equal everywhere | ΔV/V | volume, not shape |

The approximation tan θ ≈ θ is good for small angles: at 10° the two differ by only 1.03 % (tan 10° = 0.17633, 10° = 0.17453 rad).

**Sign conventions used here.** Tension and elongation are positive; compression and shortening are negative. For pressure, p is positive when it squeezes, and the volume then decreases, so ΔV is negative. This is why the bulk modulus carries a minus sign, B = −p/(ΔV/V), and comes out positive. Every formula in this lesson uses magnitudes unless a sign is stated.

**Assumption.** The sources treat **homogeneous and isotropic** materials: the composition and elastic properties are the same at every point and in every direction. Metals as wires and rods behave this way; wood and many crystals do not.

### 3. Core: Hooke's law, Young's modulus and the wire as a spring

*Objective:* State Hooke's law, define Young's, shear and bulk moduli with correct formulae, derive k = YA/L for a wire, and avoid the 'more elastic' misconception.

**Hooke's law.** For small deformations the stress is proportional to the strain:

$$ stress = (modulus) × strain,    i.e.   σ = E ε $$

The constant E is the **modulus of elasticity**. It depends on the material (and temperature), not on the size or the load, and it has the dimensions of stress because strain has none. Hooke's law is **empirical**: it describes most metals over a small range, but not all materials. Rubber and aortic tissue (elastomers) stretch hugely and do not obey it over most of their range (NCERT 8.4). Hooke's own Latin phrase was 'ut tensio sic vis'. Mathur prints the year as 1679 and Upadhyaya as 1678; the books disagree, so it is safer not to quote a year.

Each kind of strain has its own modulus.

$$ Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = F L/(A ΔL)    (Young's modulus, longitudinal) $$

$$ η = (F/A)/θ = F L/(A Δx)    (shear modulus or modulus of rigidity) $$

$$ K = −p/(ΔV/V) = −p V/ΔV    (bulk modulus; 1/K is the compressibility) $$

The symbols differ between books: NCERT writes G for η and B for K; Mathur writes n for η. In answers, define the symbol you use.

**The wire as a spring.** Rearrange Y = F L/(A ΔL) as F = (Y A/L) ΔL. This has the form F = k ΔL with

$$ k = Y A/L $$

**Dimensional check:** Pa × m²/m = N/m. ✓. Note the difference: Y belongs to the material, k belongs to this particular wire. Doubling the length halves k; doubling the area doubles k. A steel wire with L = 1 m and A = 1 mm² has k = 2 × 10¹¹ × 10⁻⁶/1 = 2 × 10⁵ N/m, so a 98 N load stretches it by only 0.49 mm.

**'More elastic' does not mean 'stretches more'.** Rubber stretches far more than steel under the same load, but the physics meaning of 'more elastic' is the larger modulus: steel is more elastic than rubber (Upadhyaya 4.3; NCERT points to ponder 6). A material with a large Y needs a large force for a small change in length.

**Worked example (NCERT 8.1).** A steel rod of radius 10 mm and length 1.0 m is pulled by 100 kN; Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Stress σ = F/A = 10⁵/(π × 10⁻⁴) = 3.18 × 10⁸ Pa. Extension ΔL = σL/Y = 3.18 × 10⁸ × 1/2 × 10¹¹ = 1.59 × 10⁻³ m = 1.59 mm. Strain = 1.59 × 10⁻³, or 0.16 %.

### 4. Core: the stress-strain curve of a wire, ductile and brittle behaviour

*Objective:* Describe the stress-strain curve region by region, define proportional limit, elastic limit, yield, ultimate and fracture stress, and distinguish ductile, brittle and elastomer behaviour.

In a standard tensile test a wire or cylinder is loaded in small steps; at each step the force and the extension are recorded and a graph of stress against strain is drawn. The curve of a typical metal is shown in the diagram. This is the content of the 2024 Q3(c)(i) question.

**Region by region** (the letters follow the diagram of this lesson; the sources use different letters, mapped in the master notes).

1. **O to P: the straight line.** Hooke's law holds, and unloading brings the wire back to O. The slope is Y. The largest stress on this line is the **proportional limit**. For metals the strain here seldom exceeds about 1/1000 of the original length (Mathur 13.5).
2. **P to E: elastic but not linear.** The curve bends, stress and strain are no longer proportional, yet the wire still returns to its original length when unloaded. The largest stress for which this holds is the **elastic limit**. NCERT draws the proportional and elastic regions as O-A and A-B and calls B 'yield point (also known as elastic limit)'; Mathur separates the elastic limit from the yield point. For metals the two limits lie close together.
3. **Yield, E to Y and beyond.** The strain now grows rapidly for little or no extra stress. This is **plastic deformation**: if the load is removed at a point such as B (Mathur), the stress returns to zero along a line roughly parallel to OP, the path BC, ending at C where the stress is zero; OC is the **permanent set**. Mathur marks an upper and a lower yield point.
4. **Strain hardening up to U.** More stress is needed for more strain. The maximum stress, computed on the original area, is the **ultimate tensile strength**.
5. **Beyond U: necking and fracture at F.** A local thin 'neck' forms, the cross-section there shrinks, the force needed falls, and the wire breaks. The curve of engineering stress therefore turns downward even though the material is still deforming.

**Ductile and brittle.** If U and F are far apart (a long plastic region, as in copper or mild steel) the material is **ductile**. If they are close, so that the wire snaps with little plastic strain, it is **brittle** (glass is the example NCERT's introduction raises). **Elastomers** such as rubber give a large elastic range that is not linear and has no well-defined plastic region.

**Design use.** Engineers keep the working stress well below the elastic limit. The ratio of breaking stress to working stress is the **factor of safety**; Mathur prints 10 for the UK and 5 for the USA, which are dated figures and should not be quoted as current standards.

### 5. Core: strain energy per unit volume, derived

*Objective:* Derive W = ½ F l and u = ½ σ ε for a stretched wire, express u as ½ Y ε² and σ²/2Y, and explain why the work done by a hanging weight is twice the energy stored.

Setting: a wire of natural length L and area A, with Young's modulus Y, is stretched slowly (so that it is always in equilibrium) from extension 0 to l, within the elastic limit. Take the extension x as the coordinate, measured along the wire from its unstretched length. The stretching force is the applied force F(x) that balances the restoring force.

**Step 1: the force at extension x.** From Y = (F/A)/(x/L), F(x) = (Y A/L) x. (This is Hooke's law for the wire, F = kx with k = YA/L from module 3.)

**Step 2: the work for a small extra stretch dx.** dW = F(x) dx = (Y A/L) x dx.

**Step 3: add up from 0 to l.**

$$ W = ∫₀ˡ (Y A/L) x dx = (Y A/L)(l²/2) = ½ (Y A l/L) l = ½ F l $$

where F = Y A l/L is the final force. The work is half the final force times the final extension: the area of the triangle under the F-x line.

**Step 4: divide by the volume V = A L.**

$$ u = W/V = ½ (F/A)(l/L) = ½ σ ε $$

Using σ = Y ε, the same quantity is **u = ½ Y ε² = σ²/(2Y)**. The Mathur and Upadhyaya derivations are the same integral; Mathur repeats it for volume strain (u = ½ p ΔV/V = p²/2K) and shear strain (u = ½ η θ² = ½ σ_s θ).

**Dimensional check.** σ ε has the dimensions of stress, ML⁻¹T⁻², which are also the dimensions of energy per unit volume (ML²T⁻² ÷ L³). ✓ **Limiting case.** As ε → 0, u → 0 faster than σ; at fixed strain a stiffer material stores more energy, while at fixed stress it stores less (u = σ²/2Y). **Number.** For steel at ε = 10⁻³: u = ½ × 2 × 10¹¹ × 10⁻⁶ = 10⁵ J/m³.

**Resilience.** The energy stored in a bar of volume V strained to stress σ is σ²V/2Y; the largest value, at the elastic limit, is the **proof resilience** (Mathur 13.16).

**Why half?** Hang a weight Mg from a wire and let it come to rest at extension l (so Mg = k l). The weight lost potential energy Mg l = k l², but the wire stores only ½ k l². If the weight is released and left to oscillate, the other half is dissipated as it settles; if the load is instead lowered slowly by hand, the other half is work done on the hand. Upadhyaya's example 12 (F = 98 N, l = 0.3 mm) stores ½ × 98 × 3 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.47 × 10⁻² J, while the weight lost 2.94 × 10⁻² J. If the load is applied suddenly (released from the unstretched position), the extension reaches 2l at the lowest point of the first oscillation (the maximum extension), not at the first instant: Mg x = ½ k x² gives x = 2Mg/k. This 'sudden-load' result is standard but is not in the three extracts.

### 6. Core: wire problems - own weight, thermal stress and combinations

*Objective:* Solve wire problems in series and parallel, derive the extension of a wire hanging under its own weight, and derive thermal stress in a clamped rod.

**Two wires in series (the same tension).** Wires joined end to end carry the same tension W, so each has stress W/A_i and the extensions add. NCERT 8.2: a copper wire (2.2 m) and a steel wire (1.6 m), both 3.0 mm in diameter, stretch by 0.70 mm in total. Since W/A = Y_c ΔL_c/L_c = Y_s ΔL_s/L_s, ΔL_c/ΔL_s = (Y_s/Y_c)(L_c/L_s) = (2.0/1.1)(2.2/1.6) = 2.5, so ΔL_c = 0.50 mm, ΔL_s = 0.20 mm and W = A Y_c ΔL_c/L_c = π(1.5 × 10⁻³)² × 1.1 × 10¹¹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴/2.2 = 1.8 × 10² N.

**Two wires in parallel (the same extension).** Wires of equal length attached to the same rigid body stretch equally, so the loads share in proportion to Y_i A_i/L_i. This rule is the key to the 2021 question (module 8).

**A wire hanging under its own weight.** Coordinate: x measured **downward from the free lower end**, so the tension at distance x from the free end supports only the wire below it. Let the density be ρ, area A, length L.

1. Tension at x: T(x) = ρ A x g, so the stress is σ(x) = ρ g x.
2. The small element dx stretches by d(ΔL) = (σ(x)/Y) dx = (ρ g x/Y) dx.
3. Add: ΔL = ∫₀ᴸ (ρ g x/Y) dx = ρ g L²/(2Y).

**Check.** If the whole weight ρ A L g hung at the end, F L/(AY) = ρ g L²/Y; the true extension is exactly half that, because the upper part is stretched by a larger load. **Dimensions:** (kg m⁻³)(m s⁻²)(m²)/(N m⁻²) = m ✓. The stress is greatest at the top, σ_max = ρ g L. The wire breaks under its own weight when ρ g L reaches the breaking stress, giving L_max = σ_b/(ρ g): about 10 km for σ_b = 9.8 × 10⁸ Pa, ρ = 10⁴ kg/m³ (Mathur 13.10), and only 34 m for σ_b = 10⁶ Pa, ρ = 3 × 10³ (Upadhyaya example 6). For steel, ρ = 7800 kg/m³, Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, L = 100 m: ΔL = 1.91 mm.

**Thermal stress in a clamped rod.** The rod is held between rigid supports at both ends. Free thermal change would be ΔL = α L Δθ, where α is the coefficient of linear expansion. The clamps prevent it, so the strain that the clamps impose is ε = α Δθ, in magnitude, and

$$ σ = Y α Δθ,    F = Y A α Δθ $$

independent of L. **Sign:** on cooling, the rod would shorten, the clamps hold it at the original length, so the rod is in **tension**; on heating it is in **compression**. **Numbers:** Mathur 13.9 (steel wire of diameter 1.5 mm, Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ per °C, 10 °C fall) gives F = π(0.75 × 10⁻³)² × 2 × 10¹¹ × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 = 42.4 N. For Upadhyaya example 4 (iron wire, r = 1 mm, Y = 2.2 × 10¹¹, α = 11 × 10⁻⁶, 10 °C fall) the recomputed tension is 76.0 N, whereas the book prints 72.5 N.

### 7. Master: Poisson's ratio, volume change and the behaviour of real materials

*Objective:* Define Poisson's ratio, derive the volume change of a stretched wire, and list the real-material effects (after-effect, hysteresis, fatigue) with the limits of the sources' statements.

**Poisson's ratio.** A stretched wire becomes longer and thinner. The strain perpendicular to the force is the **lateral strain**; the strain along the force is the **longitudinal strain**. Within the elastic limit their ratio is a constant of the material:

$$ σ_P = (lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain) = (Δd/d)/(ΔL/L) $$

The books use σ for Poisson's ratio; to avoid confusion with stress, this lesson writes σ_P. It is a pure number. NCERT gives about 0.28 to 0.30 for steels and about 0.33 for aluminium alloys.

**Volume change of a stretched wire (derived here).** Setting: a cylinder of radius r and length L, longitudinal strain ε = ΔL/L (positive), lateral strain magnitude σ_P ε (the radius shrinks).

1. V = π r² L.
2. Take logarithms and differentiate: ΔV/V = 2Δr/r + ΔL/L (first order in small strains).
3. Δr/r = −σ_P ε and ΔL/L = ε, so

$$ ΔV/V = ε (1 − 2σ_P) $$

**Limiting case.** If the volume does not change, σ_P = 0.5. Upadhyaya example 11 reaches this directly for a rubber thread with V = π r² l constant: 2 Δr/r + Δl/l = 0, so |Δr/r|/(Δl/l) = ½. **Number.** σ_P = 0.3, ε = 10⁻³: ΔV/V = 10⁻³ × 0.4 = 4 × 10⁻⁴. The wider limits on Poisson's ratio and the relation Y = 3K(1 − 2σ_P) are deferred to PH-022.

**Real materials: three effects (Mathur 13.6).** Only a few solids (quartz, phosphor-bronze, silver, gold) recover almost at once; others creep back slowly. This delay is **elastic after-effect**, and glass is notorious for it. **Elastic hysteresis** is the lag of strain behind stress: in a loading-unloading cycle the strain is greater on unloading for the same stress, and the energy lost per cycle is the area of the loop (standard result, not stated in the extract). **Elastic fatigue** (Kelvin) is the reduction of a wire's ability to vibrate after prolonged vibration. Mathur also records that hammering strengthens and annealing weakens elastic properties, that impurities and temperature alter them, and that heating usually weakens them; these are qualitative textbook statements and are marked for review.

**Unit and sign summary.** σ_P: no unit. Y, η, K: Pa. Strain: no unit. Tension positive, compression negative.

### 8. Exam: writing the 10-mark answer and solving the 2021 question; where the torsion questions belong

*Objective:* Write a 10-mark theory answer on Hooke's law and the stress-strain diagram, solve the 2021 two-wire problem in full, and recognise which parts of the other three questions belong elsewhere.

**2024 Q3(c)(i), 10 marks (practice target, not an official allotment: about 12 minutes).** 'State and explain Hooke's law of elasticity. Briefly discuss the features of the stress-strain diagram for a wire undergoing increasing stress.' A well-structured answer has five blocks:

1. **Statement:** within the elastic limit stress is proportional to strain; σ = E ε; E is the modulus (Y, η or K for the three strains).
2. **Explanation:** define stress F/A and strain ΔL/L; write Y = FL/(AΔL); note the law is empirical and fails for elastomers.
3. **Diagram:** the labelled curve (module 4), with axes and units.
4. **Features, one line each:** proportional limit; elastic limit; yield; plastic region and permanent set; ultimate strength; necking; fracture; ductile versus brittle.
5. **Closing remark:** a one-line use (working stress below the elastic limit).

**2021 Q2(b), 15 marks (about 18 minutes).** A light rod 100 cm long hangs horizontally from two vertical wires of equal length at its ends: steel (A_s = 0.05 cm², Y_s = 2.0 × 10¹¹ N/m²) and brass (A_b = 0.1 cm², Y_b = 1.0 × 10¹¹ N/m²). Find where a weight W should hang so that (i) the stresses are equal, (ii) the strains are equal.

Setting: coordinate x along the rod from the **steel end**, L = 100 cm; the rod is light and in equilibrium; assume the wires stay vertical.

1. Moments about the brass end: T_s L = W(L − x), so T_s = W(L − x)/L. Moments about the steel end: T_b L = W x, so T_b = W x/L. (Check: T_s + T_b = W ✓.)
2. **(i) Equal stress:** T_s/A_s = T_b/A_b gives (L − x)/A_s = x/A_b, so x = L A_b/(A_s + A_b) = 100 × 0.1/0.15 = 66.7 cm from the steel end (33.3 cm from the brass end).
3. **(ii) Equal strain:** strain = T/(A Y), so (L − x)/(A_s Y_s) = x/(A_b Y_b). Here A_s Y_s = 0.05 × 2.0 = 0.10 and A_b Y_b = 0.1 × 1.0 = 0.10 (same units), so x = 50 cm, the midpoint.

**Limiting check.** If A_s → 0 in (i), x → L: the weight moves to the brass end so the steel wire carries no load. **Caution:** with unequal moduli the rod does not stay exactly horizontal in (i); the question treats the wires as equal in length, and the tilt is small.

**2020 Q1(c).** Assumption: the amplitude stays below the static extension e = mg/k = 2.5 cm, so the cord stays taut (a cord can only pull; cord mass neglected). The cord's spring constant is k = YA/L, from module 3: with Y = 0.05 × 10¹¹ dyne/cm² = 5 × 10⁸ Pa, r = 0.5 mm and L = 1 m, k = 392.7 N/m. The oscillation, T = 2π√(m/k) = 0.317 s, is worked in PH-035 (module 6).

**2020 Q2(b) and 2025 Q4(c)** are about the twisting of shafts and belong to **PH-022 (Day 10)**. Pre-check numbers, to be verified there: the first gives a twisting couple P/ω = 254.6 N m and a twist of about 3.96 × 10⁻³ rad (Mathur 13.19 prints 0.004 rad but then multiplies the angle by the length to quote 2.0 cm, which is not a physical displacement: Mathur multiplies the twist angle by the shaft length, 0.004 × 500 cm = 2.0 cm, which has no physical meaning here; the 'relative shift between the ends' is the relative angular twist θ = 3.96 × 10⁻³ rad, and if a linear value is wanted the rim arc is Rθ = 0.158 mm). UPSC 2020 Q2(b) reproduces Mathur 13.19 word for word, so do not copy the book's 2.0 cm. The second needs the hollow-shaft stiffness; with r_o = 2 r_i the mass falls by about 22.5 %.

### Notes: Definitions

- Elasticity: the tendency of a body to regain its original size and shape when the deforming force is removed; plasticity: the tendency to remain deformed.
- Stress: restoring force per unit area, σ = F/A, unit Pa, dimensions ML⁻¹T⁻²; normal (tensile or compressive) or tangential (shearing).
- Strain: fractional change in a dimension (ΔL/L), a shear angle (Δx/L = tan θ ≈ θ), or ΔV/V; dimensionless.
- Young's modulus Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L); shear modulus (modulus of rigidity) η = (F/A)/θ; bulk modulus K = −p/(ΔV/V), compressibility 1/K.
- Proportional limit, elastic limit, yield point, ultimate tensile strength, breaking (fracture) stress, permanent set, working stress, factor of safety.
- Proof resilience: the largest strain energy a body can store without permanent set, σ_E²V/2Y at the elastic limit.

### Notes: Equations

- σ = F/A;  ε = ΔL/L;  Y = FL/(AΔL);  k = YA/L (spring constant of a wire).
- η = F/(Aθ) = FL/(AΔx);  K = −pV/ΔV;  σ_P = (Δd/d)/(ΔL/L).
- Strain energy: W = ½Fl = YAl²/(2L);  u = ½σε = ½Yε² = σ²/(2Y);  analogues u = p²/2K and u = ½ηθ².
- Hanging wire: ΔL = ρgL²/(2Y);  L_max = σ_breaking/(ρg).
- Thermal stress, clamped rod: σ = Yα Δθ;  F = YAα Δθ (tension on cooling, compression on heating).
- Volume change of a stretched wire: ΔV/V = ε(1 − 2σ_P);  constant volume gives σ_P = 0.5.
- Sudden load on a wire: maximum extension 2Mg/k, twice the static extension (standard result, not in the extracts).

### Notes: Comparison of the three elastic moduli

- Each modulus is stress over strain for one kind of deformation; their inter-relations are PH-022.

### Notes: Labels on the stress-strain curve in the three sources

- The same physical features carry different letters and, partly, different names; always label your own diagram.

### Notes: Derivation summary: u = ½σε

- F(x) = (YA/L)x for extension x within the elastic limit.
- dW = F dx.
- W = ∫₀ˡ (YA/L) x dx = YAl²/(2L) = ½Fl.
- Divide by V = AL: u = ½(F/A)(l/L) = ½σε = ½Yε² = σ²/2Y.
- Check: dimensions of u equal those of stress; ε → 0 gives u → 0.

### Notes: Cause and effect: thermal stress

- Temperature change Δθ → free length change αLΔθ wanted.
- Rigid clamps forbid the change → the rod is held at its original length → a strain of magnitude αΔθ is imposed.
- Hooke's law → stress YαΔθ → force YAαΔθ, independent of the rod's length.
- Cooling → tension; heating → compression.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Stiffness (modulus, slope) versus strength (stress at yield or fracture): glass is stiff (Y of order 10¹¹ Pa) but brittle; stiffness and strength are different properties.
- Young's modulus Y (property of material, Pa) versus spring constant k = YA/L (property of the specimen, N/m).
- Elastic limit (recovery) versus proportional limit (linearity) versus yield (large plastic strain).
- Energy stored (½Fl) versus work done by a hanging weight (Mg l = Fl).
- 'More elastic' in everyday speech (stretches more) versus in physics (larger modulus).
- Diameter versus radius in A = πr²: the commonest numerical slip in this topic.

### Core concepts

- Stress is restoring force per unit area (Pa); strain is the fractional change in a dimension; both have a tensile, shear and volumetric form.
- Hooke's law: within the elastic limit stress is proportional to strain; the constant is a modulus; it is empirical and fails for elastomers.
- Y = FL/(AΔL); a wire behaves as a spring with k = YA/L.
- The stress-strain curve has proportional limit, elastic limit, yield, ultimate strength, necking and fracture; ductile materials have a long plastic region, brittle ones a short one.
- Strain energy per unit volume is ½σε = ½Yε² = σ²/2Y, the area under the stress-strain line.
- A clamped rod has thermal stress YαΔθ, independent of its length; a hanging wire stretches by ρgL²/2Y.

### Practice

**Q.** Give the SI unit and dimensions of stress and of strain.

**A.** Stress: pascal, ML⁻¹T⁻²; strain: no unit and no dimensions.

**Q.** Convert 8 × 10¹¹ dyne/cm² to pascals.

**A.** Multiply by 0.1: 8 × 10¹⁰ Pa.

**Q.** A wire is 2.0 m long and stretches by 1.0 mm. What is the strain?

**A.** ΔL/L = 1.0 × 10⁻³/2.0 = 5 × 10⁻⁴.

**Q.** Why is the shear strain Δx/L written as an angle for small deformations?

**A.** Because Δx/L = tan θ, and tan θ ≈ θ (in radians) for small θ (1 % error at 10°).

**Q.** Explain why steel is called more elastic than rubber although rubber stretches more.

**A.** Elasticity in physics is measured by the modulus: for the same stress steel gives a smaller strain, so its Y is larger.

**Q.** Why is the tension in a wire with a weight hanging from its lower end equal to F and not 2F?

**A.** The ceiling reaction and the weight are the two ends of the same tension; the tension is the internal force transmitted across any cross-section, equal to F.

**Q.** Distinguish proportional limit, elastic limit and yield point.

**A.** Proportional limit: end of linear stress-strain. Elastic limit: largest stress after which the wire fully recovers. Yield: the stress at which the strain grows rapidly for little extra stress.

**Q.** Why is the energy stored in a stretched wire half of the product of the final force and the extension?

**A.** The force rises from zero to its final value linearly with the extension, so the work is the area of a triangle, ½Fl.

### Memory skeleton

- Stress σ = F/A (Pa, ML⁻¹T⁻²); strain ΔL/L, θ, ΔV/V (dimensionless)
- Hooke's law: stress ∝ strain within the elastic limit; Y, η, K = −p/(ΔV/V)
- Wire as spring k = YA/L; stress-strain curve P, E, Y, U, F; ductile versus brittle
- Strain energy: W = ½Fl, u = ½σε = ½Yε² = σ²/2Y
- Wire under own weight: ΔL = ρgL²/(2Y); thermal stress σ = YαΔθ
- Poisson's ratio: ΔV/V = ε(1 − 2σ_P); constant volume gives 0.5
