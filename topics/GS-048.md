### Bridge: Who ruled India in 1939: Viceroy, Executive Council, ministries

In 1939 India was still ruled by the British Crown. At the top sat the **Viceroy** (the Crown's representative; Lord Linlithgow in 1939). He worked with an **Executive Council**, a small group of ministers-in-name at the Centre. Under the Government of India Act, 1935 (taught in GS-047), the provinces had elected assemblies and **ministries** (cabinets) answerable to them, but the federal part of the 1935 Act never came into force (GS-047 module 4), so the Centre still ran on the 1919 arrangements: the Viceroy and his Council controlled defence and foreign affairs and were not answerable to Indian legislators.

The Congress had won the 1937 provincial elections and formed ministries in most provinces (GS-053, module 7). So in September 1939 the Congress ran provincial governments, yet it had no say in the decision that mattered most: whether India was at war.

Two terms recur in every wartime offer. **Dominion status** means self-government inside the British Empire, as in Canada. **Constituent assembly** means a body of representatives chosen to write the constitution of a country. The Congress had officially demanded a constituent assembly since 1935 (see GS-159) and repeated the demand after the 1937 elections (GS-053 module 7); and, from 1929, wanted complete independence, not the first. Almost every proposal between 1939 and 1945 can be tested with one question: does it give real power at the Centre now, and who will write the constitution later?

### 1. Orientation: Quit India on one page

*Objective:* Hold the whole story of 1939 to 1945 in three questions before any detail.

## The movement in one paragraph
The **Quit India Movement** was the Congress's third great mass struggle (after Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience). Its demand was an **immediate end to British rule**. The AICC ratified the resolution at Bombay on 8 August 1942, Gandhi gave the call 'Do or Die', and in the early hours of 9 August 1942 the government arrested the entire top leadership. What followed was a largely leaderless explosion, an underground resistance and a few parallel governments, met by heavy repression.

## Three questions that organise the topic
1. **Why then?** The Second World War, the Congress ministries' resignation (1939), the failed offers of 1940 and the Cripps Mission (1942), wartime hardship, and the Japanese advance to India's borders.
2. **What happened?** The Wardha and Bombay decisions, the arrests, popular violence and sabotage, the underground, Ballia, Tamluk and Satara, repression, Gandhi's fast.
3. **What did it change?** Independence became the immediate agenda; the League gained ground while Congress leaders were jailed; talks resumed in 1944 and 1945.

### 2. Foundation: the war, India's position and the key terms

*Objective:* Define the wartime vocabulary and fix the war events that frame Indian decisions.

## The war events that matter for India
- **1 September 1939**: Germany attacks Poland. **3 September**: Britain declares war, and the Viceroy announces India's support for the war without consulting Indian opinion (Spectrum).
- **1940**: Hitler overruns Belgium, Holland and France, and Britain becomes more conciliatory (Spectrum).
- **June 1941**: Germany attacks the USSR.
- **December 1941**: Japan attacks Pearl Harbour.
- **March 1942**: Japan, having overrun almost all of South-East Asia, takes Rangoon (Spectrum). Japanese troops were now at India's eastern border.

## Terms to know
- **Axis and Allies**: the Axis were Germany, Italy and Japan; the Allies included Britain, the USA, the USSR and China (the USSR fought on the Allied side from June 1941 and the USA from December 1941, which also explains why the Communist line changed in 1941: module 8). Spectrum notes the Allies (USA, USSR, China) pressed Britain to win Indian cooperation.
- **Fascism and imperialism**: the Congress had long opposed both. It disliked Fascism and Nazism but could not support a 'war for freedom' fought by a power that denied India freedom.
- **Individual satyagraha**: a limited protest by selected individuals, not a mass movement (module 3).
- **Mass civil disobedience**: a movement of the whole population, as in 1930.
- **Parallel government**: a rival administration set up by rebels in an area where British authority has collapsed (module 7).
- **Underground**: activity carried on in hiding, because the organisation has been declared illegal.
- **Scorched earth** and **denial policy**: destroying boats, food or transport so an invader cannot use them. Spectrum records that boats were commandeered in Bengal and Orissa and that fears of a scorched-earth policy in Assam, Bengal and Orissa added to discontent.

## Guided example: the three players
NCERT calls the situation a three-way struggle: the Congress, the Muslim League and the British, not simply Indians against the British. Ask the three questions from GS-053 of each. The Congress: base across communities; goal, independence and a constituent assembly; method, conditional cooperation, then struggle. The League: base, a growing Muslim following; goal, separate states from March 1940; method, bargaining while the Congress is out of office. The British: goal, keep India in the war and keep control; method, offers that preserved defence and the Viceroy's powers.

Keep these three players in mind: every offer from 1940 to 1945 is a move in that triangle.

### 3. From war to individual satyagraha, 1939 to 1941

*Objective:* Explain the Congress response to the war, the 1939 resignations, the August Offer and the individual satyagraha.

## The conditional offer and the Wardha debate
The Congress disliked being drawn into the war unilaterally. It offered conditional support: a **constituent assembly after the war**, and **some form of genuinely responsible government at the Centre immediately**. Viceroy Linlithgow refused. At the Congress Working Committee (CWC) at Wardha (10-14 September 1939), Gandhi favoured unconditional moral support to Britain; Bose and the socialists (Narendra Dev, Jayaprakash Narayan) wanted to use the war to launch a movement; Nehru took a middle path: no Indian participation until India was free, but no exploiting of Britain's difficulty. Gandhi, isolated, accepted Nehru's line (Spectrum).

## The resignation of the ministries
On 17 October 1939 Linlithgow refused to define Britain's war aims beyond resisting aggression and planned to consult communities, parties and princes about modifying the 1935 Act. On 23 October the CWC rejected this, refused support for the war and called on the Congress ministries to resign. NCERT: the ministries resigned in October 1939 (GS-053 says the same; exact day-by-day resignations vary by province: verify). Jinnah asked Muslims to observe a 'Day of Deliverance' on 22 December 1939 (standard knowledge; verify). Spectrum argues that the British aim was to provoke a clash and then use emergency powers.

Why no mass struggle yet? Gandhi's group thought the Allied cause was just, feared communal riots, saw the Congress organisation as weak and the masses unready (Spectrum). At **Ramgarh (March 1940, Azad presiding)** the Congress demanded complete independence and left the form and timing of struggle to Gandhi. That same month the League passed its **Lahore resolution**, calling for independent states in the Muslim-majority north-west and east.

## The August Offer (August 1940)
After France fell, Linlithgow offered: dominion status as the objective; a larger Executive Council with an Indian majority; a post-war constituent assembly 'mainly' of Indians; no constitution without minority consent. The Congress rejected it ('dead as a doornail', Nehru); the League welcomed the minority veto. Spectrum's evaluation: it was the first time the right of Indians to frame their constitution was recognised. In July 1941 the Council was enlarged (8 Indians of 12) but the British kept defence, finance and home.

## Individual satyagraha (from October 1940)
Gandhi chose a limited form (Spectrum). Aims: show that patience was not weakness; show that Indians would not help the war, equating Nazism with the 'double autocracy' at home; give the government one more chance. Each satyagrahi made an anti-war speech; if not arrested, he repeated it and marched towards Delhi ('Delhi Chalo'). **Vinoba Bhave** was first and **Nehru** second. By May 1941, 25,000 had been convicted. The leaders were released in December 1941 as Japan advanced.

### 4. The Cripps Mission, March to April 1942, and why it failed

*Objective:* State the Cripps proposals, the objections of each party and the reasons for failure.

## Why the mission was sent
Japan's advance made an invasion of India look real and Indian support crucial. The USA, USSR and China pressed Britain for a settlement, and the Congress had said it would back the war effort if real power moved immediately and independence followed after the war (Spectrum). **Sir Stafford Cripps**, a left-wing Labour member of the War Cabinet who had supported the Indian cause, came in March 1942 (arrival about 22 March; departure in the second week of April; verify the exact dates). Nehru and Azad negotiated for the Congress.

## The Draft Declaration
1. After the war an **Indian Union with dominion status**, free to leave the Commonwealth.
2. A **constituent assembly** after the war: members elected by the provincial assemblies by proportional representation and nominated by the princes.
3. Britain would accept the constitution on two conditions: a province unwilling to join could keep a **separate constitution** and form a separate union; and a treaty would settle the transfer of power and protect minorities.
4. Meanwhile **defence would remain in British hands** and the Governor-General's powers would remain.

What was new compared with the August Offer: the constitution-making body would be wholly Indian ('solely' not 'mainly'), a concrete plan for it, a province's right to stay out (which Spectrum calls a blueprint for partition), and the right to leave the Commonwealth.

## Objections
| Party | Main objections |
|---|---|
| Congress | Dominion status not independence; princely states represented by nominees, not elected; right of provinces to secede; no immediate transfer of power or real share in defence |
| Muslim League | Single Union; the constituent-assembly machinery; Pakistan not explicitly conceded |
| Others | Liberals, Mahasabha (secession); depressed classes and Sikhs (fear of being left at the mercy of a majority) |

## Why it failed
The Congress insisted that an Indian should hold defence. NCERT describes the break as being over the appointment of an Indian Defence Member; Spectrum describes it as being over the Viceroy's veto and the Governor-General's supremacy. Both are the same deadlock: power at the Centre during the war. Cripps had talked of a 'cabinet' and 'national government' but later said he meant only an enlarged Executive Council. His 'take it or leave it' attitude and the hostility of Churchill, Amery, Linlithgow and the Commander-in-Chief closed the door (Spectrum). Gandhi's reported words were 'a post-dated cheque'; Azad feared that accepting a promise would remove the justification for a struggle. Spectrum also calls the mission a propaganda device for American and Chinese opinion.

The effect: a 'frustrated and embittered' India felt the time had come for a final assault (Spectrum).

### 5. Wardha, Bombay and the arrests of 9 August 1942

*Objective:* Explain why the Congress chose struggle in 1942 and trace the decisions from 14 July to 9 August.

## Why a struggle now (Spectrum)
1. Cripps's failure showed that Britain would not change; further silence would amount to accepting the British right to decide India's fate.
2. Popular discontent: rising prices, shortage of rice and salt, commandeering of boats, fears of scorched earth.
3. News of British defeats in South-East Asia; people withdrew deposits from banks and post offices.
4. British conduct in the retreat from Burma and Malaya, with separate evacuation roads for Europeans and Indians ('White Road' and 'Black Road'), exposed racial attitudes and cut British prestige.
5. The leadership wanted to prepare the masses for a possible Japanese invasion.

After Cripps left, Gandhi drafted a resolution calling for British withdrawal and for non-violent non-cooperation against any Japanese invasion. His reasoning (standard knowledge; verify): the British presence invited a Japanese attack; an orderly withdrawal would let India defend itself.

## 14 July 1942: Wardha
The CWC accepted the idea of a struggle at Wardha on **14 July 1942** and authorised Gandhi to lead a non-violent mass movement. Not every leader agreed: C. Rajagopalachari dissented (standard knowledge; verify), and Nehru and Azad were reportedly hesitant but accepted (verify).

## 7-8 August 1942: Gowalia Tank, Bombay
The **AICC** met at the Gowalia Tank maidan, Bombay. The resolution was proposed by Nehru and seconded by Patel (Spectrum) and ratified on **8 August 1942**. It: demanded an immediate end to British rule; declared that free India would defend itself against Fascism and imperialism; promised a provisional government after the British withdrawal; sanctioned a civil disobedience movement; and named Gandhi leader.

Gandhi's special instructions were stated at the meeting but never issued: government servants should not resign but declare allegiance to the Congress; soldiers should not leave the army but not fire on countrymen; students could leave studies if confident; peasants should pay rent depending on the zamindar's stand; princes should accept the people's sovereignty (Spectrum). Then came the 'mantra': **'Do or Die'**.

## 9 August 1942: the sweep
Gandhi had built tempo through individual satyagraha, organisational reform and propaganda, but the government would not wait. In the early hours of **9 August**, in a single sweep, all top leaders were arrested. The CWC, AICC and Provincial Congress Committees were declared unlawful under the **Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1908**, and public meetings were banned under rule 56 of the Defence of India Rules. Gandhi was taken to the Aga Khan Palace, Pune, and the CWC members to Ahmednagar Fort (standard knowledge; verify). The movement never began as planned: the leaders were jailed before they could issue the programme (see the question 'did it fail or succeed' in module 9).

### 6. The leaderless phase and the underground

*Objective:* Describe popular action after 9 August and the work of the underground leaders, including Aruna Asaf Ali and Usha Mehta.

## The public on the rampage
With the leaders gone, younger and more militant workers acted on their own. The crowds attacked symbols of authority (police stations, post offices, railway stations, courts), forcibly hoisted national flags on public buildings, blew up bridges, removed railway lines and cut telegraph lines. This was most intense in **eastern United Provinces and Bihar**; the other main storm centres were Midnapore, Maharashtra and Karnataka (Spectrum). Students struck, held processions, wrote illegal news-sheets (patrikas) and acted as couriers. Workers struck in Ahmedabad, Bombay, Jamshedpur, Ahmednagar and Poona.

Note the contrast with the instructions of module 5: Gandhi stressed non-violence, but, left without leaders, the movement had 'no restraint and violence became common' (Spectrum). Spectrum quotes the historian Sumit Sarkar: the 'Do or Die' mantra represents the militant mood of Gandhi. NCERT calls it genuinely a mass movement of hundreds of thousands, which especially energised the young, who left colleges for jail.

## Aruna Asaf Ali and the flag
Aruna Asaf Ali, then relatively unknown, hoisted the Congress flag at the Gowalia Tank maidan on the morning of **9 August**, after the arrests (Spectrum words it as presiding over 'the Congress committee session'; verify). She then went underground and became one of its most visible figures.

## The underground
Socialists, Forward Bloc members, Gandhi's ashramites, revolutionary nationalists and local groups worked in Bombay, Poona, Satara, Baroda, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, Andhra, the United Provinces, Bihar and Delhi (Spectrum). The names given: **Ram Manohar Lohia, Jayaprakash Narayan, Aruna Asaf Ali, Usha Mehta, Biju Patnaik, Chhotubhai Puranik, Achyut Patwardhan, Sucheta Kripalani, R.P. Goenka**. Their aims were to keep morale high, supply a line of command and guidance, and distribute arms and ammunition.

- **Usha Mehta** started an underground radio in Bombay (Spectrum). Standard knowledge, verify: it began broadcasting around 14 August 1942 as the 'Congress Radio', Lohia spoke on it, and the police traced it in about three months.
- **Jayaprakash Narayan** escaped from Hazaribagh jail in November 1942 (Diwali night; standard knowledge, verify) and helped organise resistance; later he and Lohia were arrested and ill-treated in custody (verify).
- Help came from many sides: businessmen (donations, shelter), students (couriers), villagers (refusing information), railway staff and pilots (carrying material) and even policemen (passing on secrets) (Spectrum).

## Check your understanding of 'leaderless'
The movement was leaderless at the top (the CWC and the AICC were in jail) but not directionless: the underground supplied some command, and the Congress had spent years preparing (module 9).

### 7. Parallel governments, repression, the fast and the famine

*Objective:* Compare the three parallel governments and describe repression, Gandhi's 1943 fast and the 1943 famine.

## Parallel governments (Spectrum; NCERT)
- **Ballia** (eastern United Provinces), **August 1942, for about a week**, under **Chittu Pandey**; it secured the release of many Congress leaders before being put down.
- **Tamluk** (Midnapore, Bengal), the **Jatiya Sarkar**, **December 1942 to September 1944**: cyclone relief, grants to schools, paddy supplied from the rich to the poor, volunteer groups (Vidyut Vahini). Matangini Hazra was shot dead leading a procession at Tamluk in September 1942 (standard knowledge; verify).
- **Satara** (Maharashtra), the **Prati Sarkar**, from **mid-1943**: leaders included Y.B. Chavan and Nana Patil; village libraries, people's courts (Nyayadan Mandals), prohibition and 'Gandhi marriages'. NCERT adds that it was dominated by kunbi peasants with Dalit support and ran its volunteer corps and village units until the 1946 elections despite repression and, later, Congress disapproval. Spectrum says to 1945 (a difference between books: see the master notes).

## Repression
Martial law was not proclaimed, but repression was severe: lathi charges, tear gas, firing, the press muzzled, cities taken over by the military, heavy fines on rebellious villages and mass flogging (Spectrum). Spectrum estimates about 10,000 killed; official figures were much lower and numbers are disputed (verify before quoting). Reportedly the Viceroy called it the most serious rebellion since 1857 (standard knowledge; verify). NCERT notes it took more than a year to suppress.

## Gandhi's fast, February 1943
Gandhi, imprisoned since 9 August, began a **21-day fast on 10 February 1943** (the 21 days and start date are standard knowledge; verify the end date, early March). Spectrum describes it as an answer to a government exhortation to condemn violence, directed against the violence of the State. It prompted hartals and protests at home and abroad, and three members of the Viceroy's Executive Council resigned (Spectrum; names M.S. Aney, N.R. Sarker and H.P. Mody are standard knowledge, verify). Effects: morale was raised, anti-British feeling heightened and the government's high-handedness exposed. Gandhi survived. Mahadev Desai died in detention in August 1942 and Kasturba Gandhi in February 1944 (standard knowledge; verify).

## The famine of 1943
The Bengal famine killed an estimated 1.5 to 3 million people (Spectrum), most in the south-west and east of Bengal. Causes listed: food diverted to feed a large army, rice imports from Burma and South-East Asia cut off, and mismanagement and profiteering with late rationing. It added to the case against the war government without being a Quit India event itself.

## Release
GS-053 teaches that Gandhi was released on health grounds on **6 May 1944**. NCERT lehs303 says June 1944: treat this as a source slip and learn 6 May (verify).

### 8. Who participated and who stood apart

*Objective:* State the position of each major group and give the reason Spectrum offers or standard knowledge supplies.

## Who took part (Spectrum)
Students and youth were at the front. Women, especially school and college girls, took part (Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kripalani, Usha Mehta). Workers struck and faced repression. Peasants of all strata were at the heart of the movement; some zamindars joined too. Peasants targeted symbols of authority and there was a complete absence of anti-zamindar violence. Lower-level officials in the police and administration took part, eroding loyalty. Muslims gave shelter to underground workers and there were no communal clashes during the movement. Businessmen helped with donations and shelter, though the upper classes and bureaucracy overall remained loyal.

## Who stayed away
- **The Communists** did not join. After Germany attacked the USSR in June 1941 they backed the British war against Germany: the 'Imperialist War' became the 'People's War' (Spectrum). Spectrum's summary adds that some communists took part, so individual action differed from party policy.
- **The Muslim League** opposed the movement, fearing that if the British left at once the minorities would be oppressed by the Hindus (Spectrum). Jinnah's slogan 'Divide and Quit' belongs to this period (standard knowledge; verify).
- **The Hindu Mahasabha** boycotted it (Spectrum). Standard knowledge (verify): its leader V.D. Savarkar urged cooperation in the war effort, and Shyama Prasad Mookerjee, a minister in Bengal, wrote to the Governor about checking the movement.
- **The princely states** gave a low-key response (Spectrum).

## Why this matters
Spectrum and NCERT agree on the consequence. NCERT: while Congress leaders were in jail, the League 'worked patiently', making a mark in Punjab and Sind where it had had scarcely any presence. On 23 March 1943 Pakistan Day was observed (Spectrum). Jinnah also objected to the 1944 Rajagopalachari Formula as accepting less than the two-nation theory (module 9).

## A caution on wording
'Opposed' or 'did not join' describes a political stand, not every member's conduct. For example, Spectrum's summary mentions that some communists participated. In an answer, write the party's position first and then add 'although individuals differed'.

### 9. Debates, consequences and evaluation

*Objective:* Explain the main historians' positions, the consequences up to 1945 and a balanced judgement of Quit India.

## The historians' debates
**1. Spontaneous or planned?** Spectrum's estimate says the element of spontaneity was higher than before, yet a degree of popular initiative had been sanctioned by the leadership and the Congress had prepared for a long time. A fair answer holds both: the arrests triggered the explosion, but the 1930s organisation, individual satyagraha and 'Do or Die' supplied the ground.

**2. How violent, and was it Gandhian?** The 'Do or Die' mantra and the sabotage have led historians such as Sumit Sarkar (quoted by Spectrum) to see a militant strain in Gandhi. Others stress the instructions of 8 August, the absence of anti-zamindar violence and the absence of communal clashes.

**3. Whose movement?** Nationalist accounts stress its all-India mass character. Marxist and subaltern accounts look at the peasants, workers and tribals acting on their own grievances and understanding (as NCERT shows for Satara). The Cambridge school stresses local factional politics (standard knowledge; verify the authors). Say 'some historians' unless you are sure of the name.

**4. Did it hasten independence?** Many argue it showed Britain could not rule without Indian consent. Others point to Britain's war-weakened economy, the 1945 Labour government and the INA and naval unrest (GS-049, to come on Day 13, not yet written) as equally important. A balanced answer says: Quit India was a necessary, not a sufficient, cause.

## Consequences, 1942 to 1945
- Independence was now the **immediate agenda**; Spectrum says there could be no retreat after it.
- **Loyalty to the government eroded**, including among lower officials; it was no longer possible to rule without Indian consent.
- **The League gained** while the Congress was banned (module 8). The Congress was weakened organisationally, with its leaders in jail until 1944-45.
- **The 1944 Rajagopalachari Formula** (Gandhi supported it): the League would endorse independence and join an interim government; after the war Muslim-majority areas would decide by plebiscite on a separate state, with a common centre for defence and communications. Jinnah wanted the two-nation theory accepted first and only Muslims voting; Savarkar condemned it.
- **Gandhi-Jinnah talks** in Bombay followed in September 1944 (standard knowledge; verify).
- The **Desai-Liaqat proposals** (Congress and League parity in a central executive, 20 per cent for minorities) did not become an agreement, but parity had far-reaching effects.
- The **Wavell Plan and the Simla Conference (June 1945)**: GS-053 module 8 has the summary. Wavell declared the talks broken, giving the League a virtual veto, which the 1945-46 elections confirmed.
- The **INA trials** and the Labour government belong to the next phase: GS-049 (to come on Day 13, not yet written).

## Evaluation
**Failure in the short term:** no transfer of power, harsh suppression, a divided Congress, a stronger League. **Success in the long term:** mass mobilisation, a generation of young leaders, and proof that British rule had lost consent. Balanced conclusion: a political success that was a military failure.

### 10. Examination application: the three verified questions

*Objective:* Convert the lesson into answer plans for one Prelims and two Mains questions.

## Prelims, 2021: 8 August 1942
The question asks which of four statements about 8 August 1942 is correct: (a) the AICC adopted the Quit India resolution; (b) the Viceroy's Executive Council was expanded to include more Indians; (c) the Congress ministries resigned in seven provinces; (d) Cripps proposed an Indian Union with full Dominion status after the war. Method: **anchor each statement to its own date.** (b) belongs to the August Offer (August 1940) and the July 1941 enlargement; (c) is October 1939; (d) is the Cripps proposal (March 1942). Only the AICC action is dated 8 August 1942 in this lesson (module 5). Date trap on (b): the August Offer was announced on 8 August 1940 (standard; verify), the same day and month in a different year, so check the year as well as the day. That is analysis, not an official key.

Elimination habit: if one option is a well-known event but with a wrong date, check whether the question names a date. 'With reference to the date' questions reward the single event the date belongs to.

## Mains, 2024 (10 marks): events leading to Quit India and results
Plan in 150 words: (1) **Context**: war, India dragged in without consultation, resignation of ministries 1939. (2) **Failed offers**: August Offer, individual satyagraha, Cripps. (3) **Hardships and threat**: prices, shortages, boat denial, Japanese advance and the retreat from Burma. (4) **Decision**: Wardha 14 July, Bombay 8 August, arrests 9 August. (5) **Results**: independence on the agenda, loyalty eroded, League gained, talks of 1944-45. Add the analytical line: Cripps's failure was the trigger, not the only cause.

## Mains, 2026 (10 marks): significance of Gandhian movements and the masses mobilised
This question is shared with GS-041, so here you analyse only the Quit India angle. Use a frame of **who joined and what it meant**: students, women, workers and peasants; lower officials; support from businessmen and villagers; even without leaders, thousands acted (the best proof of mass mobilisation). Then qualify: the League, the Communists, the Mahasabha and the princes stayed out. Close with significance: the mass base that made post-1945 British control impossible. Other Gandhian movements (Champaran, Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience) belong to other lessons; mention them only in one linking sentence.

## Common errors

### Notes: Why Quit India: cause and effect

- Cause: the war was declared for India without consulting Indians, so the Congress withdrew its ministries in 1939.
- Cause: the offers of 1940 and 1942 left defence and the Centre with the British, so they gave no real power.
- Cause: wartime shortages, rising prices and boat commandeering created popular discontent.
- Cause: the British collapse in South-East Asia and its racial conduct there destroyed prestige.
- Effect: independence became the immediate agenda and loyalty to the government eroded.
- Effect: the League gained ground while Congress leaders were in jail.
- Effect: negotiation in 1944-45 (Rajagopalachari Formula, Desai-Liaqat, Wavell) continued the deadlock.

### Notes: Source slips and book differences

- Gandhi's release: NCERT lehs303 prints June 1944; GS-053 teaches 6 May 1944 (standard knowledge; verify). Learn 6 May.
- Satara Prati Sarkar: Spectrum says to 1945, NCERT says till the 1946 elections. Both are presented as source differences; use 'till 1945-46'.
- The August Offer is dated only 'August 1940' in Spectrum; 8 August is standard knowledge (verify).
- Spectrum's ministry resignations: CWC resolution 23 October 1939; actual provincial resignations ran from late October into November (verify).
- The Lahore resolution of March 1940 is popularly called the Pakistan resolution though its text did not use the word (standard; verify).
- Casualty figures differ widely: Spectrum estimates about 10,000 killed; official figures were lower; treat as contested.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- August Offer (1940) versus Cripps (1942) versus Cabinet Mission (1946): three different proposals; the Cabinet Mission is GS-051 (Partition, Cabinet Mission and Independence Act), to come on Day 15, not yet written.
- Individual satyagraha (Oct 1940) versus Quit India (Aug 1942): limited, selected individuals versus a mass movement.
- Wardha 14 July (CWC) versus Bombay 8 August (AICC): the committee decides, the larger body ratifies.
- Wardha, September 1939 versus Wardha, 14 July 1942: two different CWC meetings under one place name (Spectrum prints both as 'Wardha').
- Rajagopalachari Formula (1944) versus Desai-Liaqat (1945) versus Wavell Plan (1945): different proposals, each ending without agreement.
- Congress ministries' resignation (1939) versus Quit India (1942): the first was a withdrawal from office, not a mass movement.
- Parallel governments (Ballia, Tamluk, Satara) versus the Provisional Government of Azad Hind (Bose, 1943): the first were Congress-linked and local; the second belongs to the INA topic (GS-049, Day 13, not yet written).

### Notes: Historians' views at a glance

- Spectrum's estimate: more spontaneous than earlier movements, yet prepared and partly sanctioned by the leadership.
- Sumit Sarkar, as quoted by Spectrum: 'Do or Die' shows Gandhi's militant mood.
- Nationalist reading: independence on the immediate agenda; all-India mass character (standard; verify for named authors).
- Subaltern and Marxist readings: people's own grievances and initiative; leadership caution (standard; verify).
- Cambridge reading: local and factional politics (standard; verify).
- A safe formula: say 'some historians' and give the evidence, not the name, if unsure.

- **foundationChecks:**

- **conceptual:**

    - **a:** Its promises of dominion status and a constituent assembly were for after the war, while defence and power at the Centre stayed with the British now.
  - - **q:** Explain why the Congress was willing to cooperate in 1941-42 only if power were transferred immediately.
    - **a:** Congress held that people would support the war only if they felt the war government was theirs, so a real share in defence and the Centre was essential (CWC resolution, December 1941).
  - - **q:** Distinguish individual satyagraha from the Quit India Movement.
    - **a:** Individual satyagraha was a limited, symbolic protest by selected persons and aimed to show patience was not weakness; Quit India was an all-people mass struggle for immediate British withdrawal.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A newspaper says 'Quit India failed because it was suppressed in a year'. Evaluate in three sentences.
    - **a:** In the short term it failed to bring a transfer of power and was suppressed. In the long term it put independence on the immediate agenda, eroded loyalty to the government and trained a new generation of leaders, so the judgement depends on the yardstick.
  - - **q:** Two students disagree: one says Quit India was Gandhian, the other says it was a violent revolt. Give evidence for both.
    - **a:** Gandhian: Gandhi's instructions stressed non-violence, and there was no anti-zamindar or communal violence. Violent: sabotage of railways and telegraphs, attacks on police stations, and Gandhi's own 'Do or Die'.
