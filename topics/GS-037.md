### Bridge: Words you need: boycott, swadeshi, national education, passive resistance, Extremist, split

Learn these six words before the story.

- **Boycott**: refusing to buy, use or deal with something as a protest. In 1905 it meant refusing foreign (British) cloth, salt and sugar; the Extremists wanted to extend it to schools, courts, councils and jobs.
- **Swadeshi**: *swa* (own) and *desh* (country): use and make goods of one's own country. It was both a boycott's other half and a programme to build Indian enterprise.
- **National education**: schools and colleges run by Indians, under national control, in Indian languages, as an alternative to government-controlled education (GS-038 module 5 has the National Council of Education of 1906).
- **Passive resistance**: peaceful refusal to cooperate with the government. Aurobindo's aim, as Spectrum quotes it, was to make the administration impossible by an organised refusal to help British commerce or officials. Notice that *passive* here means non-violent refusal, not inactivity.
- **Extremist (militant nationalist)**: the label used by Spectrum and Indian textbooks for the radical leaders who rejected prayer, petition and protest. The label is the historians' and the British officials', not a party name; the group was not an organisation. The matching label for the older leaders is **Moderate** (GS-036).
- **Split**: the break at the Surat session of the Congress in December 1907, after which the Congress was dominated by the Moderates and the Extremists worked from outside.

**Reading tip.** Bengal's 1905 events are told in GS-038. In this lesson, whenever you see the words 'movement' or 'agitation' ask which strand is speaking: the Moderate petitioners (up to 1905), the Extremist leaders (after 1905) or the revolutionaries (GS-039).

### 1. Orientation: where GS-036 ends and this lesson begins

*Objective:* State the Extremist claim in one line and place the main events from the 1890s to 1911 on a timeline.

GS-036 ended with the **Moderate phase** (1885 to about 1905): educated leaders who believed in prayer, petition and protest, the **drain theory** (GS-030) and gradual constitutional reform, and who thought British connection was in India's interest for now. By 1905 younger leaders thought this style had failed. GS-038 told the story of the partition of Bengal and its annulment. **This lesson starts from the people and the ideas**: who the Extremists were, why they appeared, what they believed, what they added to the movement and why it faded.

## The Extremist claim in one line

Spectrum (PDF p. 311): the militant nationalists tried to turn the anti-partition and Swadeshi movement into a **mass struggle**, raised the slogan of independence from foreign rule and gave the idea of independence the central place in India's politics, to be won by self-sacrifice. 'Political freedom,' Aurobindo said, 'is the lifebreath of a nation.'

## What is not in this lesson

**One caution for a beginner.** 'Extremist' sounds as if the leaders supported violence. Spectrum says the Extremists ranged from open members and secret sympathisers of violence to those opposed to any kind of political violence (PDF p. 319). Their central method was boycott and passive resistance, not bombs.

### 2. Foundation: why militancy grew, I: the political and economic causes

*Objective:* Explain the political and economic causes of militant nationalism and tie each to the repression chronology Spectrum gives.

Spectrum lists nine causes of militant nationalism (PDF pp. 303-306, summary p. 327). Read them as **pushes** (what the British did) and **pulls** (new confidence and ideas). This module takes the pushes; the pulls follow in module 3.

## 1. The true nature of British rule

The more militant among the politically conscious saw that the government was **not conceding any of their important demands**. They also came to feel that only an Indian government could lead India to progress. The Moderates' hope that Britain would reform itself (GS-036 module 6) looked less and less convincing.

## 2. Economic misery

The economic miseries of the 1890s exposed the exploitative character of colonial rule. Spectrum says severe famines killed **90 lakh persons between 1896 and 1900**, bubonic plague affected large areas of the Deccan, and there were large-scale riots in the Deccan (figures as stated by Spectrum; verify; GS-030 module 6 treats the famine question and the drain argument that gave the miseries a political meaning).

## 3. Rights taken away, not given

Spectrum says the nationalists saw that **instead of giving more rights the government was taking away even existing ones**. Use this chronology as a ready list:

| Year | Measure (Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| 1892 | Indian Councils Act, criticised as failing to satisfy nationalists (details in GS-036) |
| 1897 | Natu brothers deported without trial; Tilak and others imprisoned for sedition |
| 1898 | Section 124A widened and a new Section 153A added (Spectrum prints 156A; slip; verify) |
| 1899 | Number of Indian members in the Calcutta Corporation reduced |
| 1904 | Official Secrets Act curbed freedom of the press |
| 1904 | Indian Universities Act: more government control over universities, which the government called factories producing political revolutionaries |

Spectrum adds that British rule was no longer socially and culturally progressive: it was suppressing mass and technical education.

## 4. Curzon's reactionary policies

Spectrum says the sharp reaction to Curzon's seven-year rule arose because it was full of 'missions, commissions and omissions'. He refused to recognise India as a nation, described the nationalists' activities as 'letting off of gas' and spoke derogatorily of Indian character. The Official Secrets Act, the Indian Universities Act, the Calcutta Corporation Act and above all the **partition of Bengal** left no doubt, in Indian minds, that British rule was basically reactionary (GS-038 module 2 on Curzon's build-up; the 2020 Mains frame is in module 10).

## 5. Dissatisfaction with the Moderates

The younger elements in the Congress were dissatisfied with the achievements of the first 15 to 20 years and attacked prayer, petition and protest as '**political mendicancy**' (begging). NCERT adds that leaders in Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab, Pal, Tilak and Lajpat Rai, criticised the Moderates' 'politics of prayers' and stressed self-reliance and constructive work.

## How the causes link

Failed requests, hunger, fewer rights and an insulting viceroy made petitioning look useless. The partition was the **spark** that gave a ready-made militant school a mass issue.

### 3. Foundation: why militancy grew, II: confidence, education, the world and the militant school

*Objective:* Explain the pull factors: self-confidence, education, international events, reaction to westernisation, and the existing militant school and leadership.

The pushes of module 2 would have produced only anger if new ideas and confidence had not been available. Spectrum names these pulls.

## Growth of confidence and self-respect

There was growing faith in self-effort. Tilak, Aurobindo and Bipin Chandra Pal repeatedly urged nationalists to rely on the character and capacity of the Indian people. A feeling spread that the **masses** must be drawn into the battle because they could make the sacrifices that freedom needs.

## Growth of education and unemployment

Education increased awareness among the masses. At the same time, unemployment and underemployment among the educated drew attention to poverty and the underdeveloped economy under colonial rule (GS-035 on education and consciousness). Educated young people with no jobs became the audience for radical ideas.

## International influences

Spectrum gives a ladder of events that broke the myth of European invincibility:

- **Japan after 1868**: an Asian country industrialised without outside help.
- **Ethiopia's defeat of Italy (1896).**
- **The Boer Wars (1899-1902)**: the British faced reverses.
- **Japan's victory over Russia (1905).**
- Nationalist movements in **Ireland, Russia, Egypt, Turkey, Persia and China**.

The lesson drawn was that a united people willing to sacrifice could take on the mightiest of empires. Notice the dates: the last event, Japan over Russia, came in the very year of the partition.

## Reaction to westernisation

The new leadership felt the stranglehold of excessive westernisation and the colonial design of submerging Indian identity in the British Empire. Its inspiration was Indian. Vivekananda, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee and Dayananda Saraswati (GS-034) painted India's past in brighter colours than British ideologues and exploded the myth of Western superiority; Spectrum gives Dayananda's political message as 'India for the Indians'.

## An existing militant school

By 1900 a band of thinkers advocated a more militant approach: **Raj Narain Bose, Ashwini Kumar Dutta, Aurobindo Ghosh and Bipin Chandra Pal** in Bengal; **Vishnu Shastri Chiplunkar and Bal Gangadhar Tilak** in Maharashtra; and **Lala Lajpat Rai** in Punjab. Tilak emerged as the most outstanding representative.

## A trained leadership

Spectrum says that the new leadership could channel the political energy of the masses, and that the energy was released during the anti-partition movement, which took the form of the Swadeshi agitation.

An answer to 'Why did militant nationalism grow?' scores best with one internal push, one internal pull and one external cause, ending with the partition as the immediate spark (module 6).

### 4. Core: the Extremist leaders and their regions

*Objective:* Match each Extremist leader to a region and to the idea or act the extracts attach to him, and answer the 2018 identity question.

The Extremists were a **current**, not a party. Their leaders worked in different provinces, and their ideas differed. Spectrum's summary (PDF p. 328) names the leadership of the Extremist phase (1905-08): **Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lajpat Rai and Aurobindo Ghosh**. Textbooks often add the nickname 'Lal-Bal-Pal' for Lajpat Rai, Tilak and Pal (standard; verify; it is not in the extract).

## Region map (Spectrum, PDF pp. 306 and 309)

| Region | Leaders and local bodies |
|---|---|
| Bengal | Raj Narain Bose, Ashwini Kumar Dutta (Swadesh Bandhab Samiti, Barisal), Aurobindo Ghosh, Bipin Chandra Pal |
| Maharashtra (Poona and Bombay) | Vishnu Shastri Chiplunkar, Tilak |
| Punjab | Lala Lajpat Rai, Ajit Singh |
| Delhi | Syed Haider Raza |
| Madras | V.O. Chidambaram Pillai (Tirunelveli, with Subramania Siva) |

## The four main figures

- **Bal Gangadhar Tilak.** Spectrum calls him the most outstanding representative of the militant school. NCERT gives his slogan, 'Freedom is my birthright and I shall have it'. He used the Marathi paper *Kesari* as a strong critic of British rule (NCERT), organised the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals as vehicles of swadeshi propaganda, and led the spread of the movement outside Bengal; he saw in it a chance to organise a popular mass struggle (Spectrum PDF p. 316). For him swaraj meant some sort of self-government (PDF p. 319). His sedition trial is treated in module 9.
- **Aurobindo Ghosh.** First principal of the Bengal National College (GS-038 module 5). He wanted complete independence, and said 'political freedom is the lifebreath of a nation'. Spectrum quotes his purpose in passive resistance as making the administration impossible. He later retired from active politics (module 9). His link to revolutionary groups is GS-039.
- **Bipin Chandra Pal.** A Bengal leader who, with Aurobindo, urged reliance on Indian character; Spectrum says he and Aurobindo spoke of a Hindu nation and Hindu interests, which alienated many Muslims (module 5). He retired from active politics after 1908.
- **Lala Lajpat Rai.** The leader from Punjab; NCERT notes he was a leading member of the radical group critical of the politics of petitions and an active member of the Arya Samaj. With Ajit Singh he led strikes in Rawalpindi (module 8). Spectrum says he left for abroad after 1908. Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh were deported to Mandalay in May 1907 under Regulation III of 1818 (standard knowledge; verify).

## A leader identified by his writings (2018 Prelims frame)

The 2018 question describes a man who wrote biographies of Mazzini, Garibaldi, Shivaji and Shrikrishna, stayed in America for some time and was elected to the Central Assembly. The extracts do not give these details; **standard knowledge, flagged verify**, usually attributes them to Lajpat Rai (in the United States during the First World War period; elected to the Central Legislative Assembly in the 1920s). Analysis, not an official key. In the extracts Aurobindo is tied to the Bengal National College and passive resistance, Pal to Bengal and the Hindu-nation idea, and Motilal Nehru does not appear.

Deuskar (*Desher Katha*) and Subramania Siva and V.O. Chidambaram Pillai are placed in module 7.

### 5. Core: Extremist ideology and methods against Moderates

*Objective:* State the four tenets of the Extremist school, contrast them with the Moderates on nine points, and explain both strengths and limits.

## The four tenets (Spectrum PDF pp. 306-307, summary p. 327)

1. **Hatred for foreign rule**: since no hope could come from it, Indians should work out their own salvation.
2. **Swaraj as the goal** of the national movement.
3. **Direct political action** was needed.
4. **Belief in the capacity of the masses** to challenge authority, and in **personal sacrifice**: a true nationalist should be always ready for it.

## What changed compared with the Moderates

Spectrum's table of differences (PDF pp. 317-318) has nine points. Learn them in pairs.

In pairs (Moderates against Extremists): zamindars and upper middle class against the educated middle and lower middle classes; Western liberal thought against Indian heritage and Hindu symbols; belief in England's providential mission against rejecting it as an illusion; British link as in India's interest against a link that perpetuates exploitation; professed loyalty to the Crown against calling it unworthy; masses not ready against immense faith in the masses; constitutional reforms and a share in services against swaraj; constitutional means only against boycott and passive resistance; patriots who were not a comprador class against patriots who made sacrifices. The master notes carry the full table.

## Do not overstate the divide

- **Spectrum's balance.** It calls the Extremists' emphasis on mass participation and broadening the base a **progressive improvement** on Moderate politics, and says they raised patriotism from an 'academic pastime' to 'service and sacrifice'.
- **Inconsistency.** Extremist ideology and functioning lacked consistency. Its advocates ranged from open members and secret sympathisers of violence to opponents of any political violence. Tilak meant 'some sort of self-government' by swaraj; Aurobindo meant complete independence.
- **Moderate limits.** Spectrum says the Moderates had outlived their use: petitions and speeches had become obsolete, they failed to win the younger generation, and they had not worked among the masses. At Calcutta 1906 and in the Swadeshi movement they discovered they were not its leaders.
- **Moderate strength.** Moderates remained a possible front line of defence against repression, a point both sides missed at Surat (GS-038 module 7).

## Revivalism and its price

Spectrum says the politically progressive Extremists proved **social reactionaries** with revivalist and obscurantist undertones. Evidence it gives:

- Tilak opposed the **Age of Consent Bill** (the 1891 Act raised the age of consent for girls from 10 to 12; Spectrum calls it the marriageable age, a slip already flagged in GS-034), though his objection was mainly that such reform should come from a self-governing people, not under alien rule.
- He organised the **Ganapati and Shivaji festivals** as national festivals and supported **anti-cow-killing campaigns**, which portrayed him as a Hindu nationalist.
- Pal and Aurobindo spoke of a Hindu nation and Hindu interests.

Spectrum concludes that this alienated many Muslims and promoted an unhealthy relation between politics and religion, whose bitter harvest India reaped later. State this as Spectrum's interpretation; other historians stress government policy as a cause of Muslim distance (GS-038 module 8).

Spectrum quotes Tilak: the Extremists of today will be the Moderates of tomorrow, as the Moderates of today were the Extremists of yesterday; labels describe a stage, not a person.

### 6. Core: how the Extremists took charge and their programme

*Objective:* Explain why the Extremists gained influence over the Swadeshi movement in Bengal, what the Calcutta 1906 session decided, and what their passive resistance programme contained.

GS-038 told how the campaign moved from petitions to boycott. This module takes only the leadership change and the programme.

## Three reasons the Extremists gained influence after 1905

Spectrum gives three:

1. The Moderate-led movement had **failed to yield results**.
2. The **divisive tactics** of the governments of both the Bengals (the two new provinces) embittered the nationalists.
3. The government resorted to **suppressive measures**: corporal punishment of students, a ban on the public singing of Vande Mataram, restriction on public meetings, prosecution and long imprisonment of swadeshi workers, clashes between police and people, arrests and deportation of leaders, and suppression of the press.

Repression, in other words, pushed people towards the leaders who offered a stronger answer.

## Congress sessions as stepping stones

| Session | What happened (as in Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| Benaras, 1905 (Gokhale) | Moderate-Extremist differences came out; compromise resolution condemning the partition and Curzon and supporting the Bengal movement |
| Calcutta, 1906 (Naoroji) | Goal declared as 'self-government or swaraj like the United Kingdom or the colonies'; resolution on swadeshi, boycott and national education; swaraj named for the first time but undefined |

Many textbooks speak of **four resolutions** at Calcutta: swaraj, swadeshi, boycott and national education. Spectrum's text matches that grouping, but it describes the goal and one resolution on the other three; treat the number 'four' as standard knowledge, verify.

## The passive resistance programme

Encouraged by Calcutta, the Extremists called for **wide passive resistance** in addition to swadeshi and boycott. It covered a boycott of **government schools and colleges, government service, courts, legislative councils, municipalities and government titles**. Aurobindo's purpose, as Spectrum quotes it, was to make the administration impossible through an organised refusal to do anything that helps either British commerce or British officialdom.

## The independence idea

The Extremists tried to transform the anti-partition and Swadeshi movement into a **mass struggle** and gave the slogan of independence from foreign rule; goal and method were joined by the notion of **self-sacrifice**.

## What the Extremists did *not* do

They formed no **party or organisation** (module 9), did not agree on one meaning of swaraj (module 5), and did not control the Congress after Surat (GS-038 module 7).

### 7. Core: swadeshi in practice: enterprise, crafts and the press

*Objective:* Describe swadeshi enterprise, the revival of crafts and the press, point to GS-038 for stores, education and culture, and state the weaknesses.

Political leaders turned swadeshi into a way of life. GS-038 modules 5 and 6 carry the methods list, the stores, the national education bodies and the art, song and science of the movement; this module adds enterprise, crafts, the press and the criticism.

## Swadeshi or indigenous enterprise

Spectrum says swadeshi spirit led to Indian **textile mills, soap and match factories, tanneries, banks, insurance companies and shops**. The weakness is stated too: these enterprises rested more on **patriotic zeal than on business acumen**. Examples named:

- **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai** founded the **Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company** at Tuticorin and challenged the British India Steam Navigation Company. Notice how this connects to the crushed shipbuilding industry in GS-030 module 4.
- **Bengal Chemicals** was established by Prafullachandra Ray in **1893** (as stated by Spectrum; often dated 1892; verify), well before the partition of 1905.

## Revival of crafts

The movement boosted swadeshi crafts: samitis trained people in swadeshi crafts (GS-038), and the melas and stores listed in GS-038 module 5 supported handicrafts. NCERT states the aims of the Swadeshi movement: self-help, swadeshi enterprise, national education and use of Indian languages. This is what the 2019 statement on 'the revival of indigenous artisan crafts and industries' points to (module 10).

## Education, stores and culture: revise in GS-038

GS-038 modules 5 and 6 list the enterprises, stores, national education bodies and the art, song and science of the movement; revise them there. Keep three exam points: (1) Spectrum says the enterprises rested more on patriotic zeal than on business acumen; (2) V.O. Chidambaram Pillai founded the Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company at Tuticorin, which links to the shipbuilding laws of 1813-14 (GS-030 module 4); (3) the National Council of Education of 15 August 1906 was a product of the movement (2019 PYQ).

## The press and literature

Sakharam Ganesh Deuskar's *Desher Katha* (1904) popularised swadeshi in a popular idiom and the ideas of Naoroji and Ranade (the economic critique of GS-030). Spectrum quotes Ishita Banerjee-Dube that it warned against the colonial state's 'hypnotic conquest of the mind'; it sold over 15,000 copies before being banned in 1910 and inspired swadeshi street plays and folk songs.

## A note on 7 August

The Boycott Resolution was passed at the Calcutta Town Hall on **7 August 1905**, the formal proclamation of the Swadeshi Movement (Spectrum). Analysis, not an official key: Spectrum supports 7 August 1905; standard sources say National Handloom Day was first observed on 7 August 2015 to commemorate the Swadeshi Movement (verify in a government release), which makes Statement II the stated reason for Statement I in the 2023 question.

### 8. Core: mass participation, its limits and the all-India spread

*Objective:* Identify who joined the movement and who stayed away, and describe how it spread beyond Bengal.

Spectrum's section on the 'extent of mass participation' (PDF pp. 314-316) is a favourite Mains source. Learn it group by group, with the limit for each.

## Who took part

- **Students.** Students propagated swadeshi and picketed shops selling foreign goods in Bengal, Maharashtra (especially Poona) and parts of the South (Guntur, Madras, Salem). Colleges could be disaffiliated or lose grants; guilty students could lose jobs and scholarships or face fines, expulsion, arrest or beating.
- **Women.** Traditionally home-centred women, especially of the urban middle classes, took active part in processions and picketing, and from then on played a significant role in the national movement.
- **Labour.** Some strikes began over rising prices and racial insult, mainly in foreign-owned companies. Spectrum gives:
  - September 1905: over 250 Bengali clerks of the Burn Company, Howrah, walked out over a derogatory work rule.
  - July 1906: an East Indian Railway strike led to a Railwaymen's Union.
  - 1906-08: frequent jute mill strikes (Spectrum's phrase 'affecting 18 out of 18 mills' is odd; treat the detail as verify).
  - Subramania Siva and Chidambaram Pillai led strikes in a foreign-owned cotton mill at Tuticorin and Tirunelveli.
  - Rawalpindi: arsenal and railway workers struck under Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh.
  By summer 1908 labour unrest subsided under strict action.
- **Others.** Some zamindari sections and the urban lower middle classes joined.

## Who stayed away

- **Muslims.** Some Muslims participated: Barrister Abdul Rasul, Liaqat Hussain, Guznavi and Maulana Azad (who joined a revolutionary group). But most upper- and middle-class Muslims stayed away or, led by Nawab Salimullah of Dacca, supported the partition on the plea that it would give a Muslim-majority East Bengal. Spectrum adds government help for the Muslim League (it says 'propped up on December 30, 1905'; its own p. 324 and NCERT give December 1906 at Dacca, the standard date; p. 315 is a slip) and says Hindu festivals and goddesses evoked by leaders tended to exclude Muslims. It says the movement could not win the **Muslim peasantry** because of a conscious government policy of divide and rule, helped by an overlap of class and community at places.
- **Peasants.** Spectrum's evaluation says the movement largely remained confined to the upper and middle classes and zamindars and failed to reach the masses, especially the peasantry. Compare GS-052 on later peasant politics.

## All-India aspect

Movements in support of Bengal's unity and the swadeshi and boycott agitation were organised in many places: Poona and Bombay under **Tilak**; Punjab under **Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh**; Delhi under **Syed Haider Raza**; Madras under **Chidambaram Pillai**. NCERT adds that in deltaic Andhra it was known as the **Vandemataram movement**. Tilak saw in this a new chapter: a challenge and an opportunity to organise a popular mass struggle to unite the country in a bond of common sympathy.

In short, the movement was **broad but shallow**: a real gain in new groups, but not a peasant movement or a Hindu-Muslim one.

### 9. Master: Surat, repression, 1911, why it faded and why it still mattered

*Objective:* Recap Surat, repression and 1911 briefly with links, and give a balanced account of why the open phase faded and why it was a turning point.

GS-038 modules 7 and 9 tell the Surat split and the 1911 annulment in full. Here is the **minimum recap**, then the evaluation that is the Mains payload.

## Recap in five lines

- **Surat, December 1907:** a dispute over the venue (Nagpur or Surat), the president (Tilak or Lajpat Rai against Rashbehari Ghosh) and whether to repeat the swadeshi, boycott and national education resolutions ended in a split; the Moderates, now dominant, reaffirmed self-government within the Empire by constitutional means. (NCERT hess208 prints December 1915, when the Bombay session eased their return; the formal reunion is dated Lucknow, December 1916; verify.)
- **Repression (1907-10):** five laws: the Seditious Meetings Act (1907), the Explosive Substances Act (1908), the Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908), the Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act (1908) and the Indian Press Act (1910); Tilak was convicted for sedition over his *Kesari* writings after the Muzaffarpur bomb and sentenced to six years' transportation to Mandalay. Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh had been deported to Mandalay in May 1907 under Regulation III of 1818 (standard knowledge; verify). Standard sources date his trial to 1908; Spectrum prints 1909 (slip; verify).
- **Government strategy (Morley):** 'rallying' the Moderates by repression, conciliation and suppression; the 1909 reforms (GS-050) are the conciliation.
- **After 1908:** Aurobindo and Bipin Chandra Pal left active politics, Lajpat Rai went abroad, and the national movement declined for a time; Tilak was released in 1914 and picked up the threads.
- **1911:** the partition was annulled mainly to curb the 'menace' of revolutionary terrorism (Spectrum), with the capital moved to Delhi (GS-038 module 9).

## Why the open phase was almost over by 1908 (Spectrum)

1. Severe government repression and the loss of leaders (arrested, deported or retired): the movement was left **leaderless**.
2. No effective organisation or party structure; Surat quarrels did harm.
3. Many techniques later seen in Gandhian politics (non-cooperation, passive resistance, filling jails, social reform, constructive work) appeared but lacked a disciplined focus and stayed ideas.
4. It aroused the people but could not channel the energy.
5. A narrow social base: upper and middle classes and zamindars, little of the peasantry.
6. A mass movement is hard to sustain at a high pitch for long.

## Why it was nonetheless a turning point

Spectrum says it was a 'leap forward' in several ways:

- Hitherto untouched groups (students, women, workers, some urban and rural sections) took part.
- All major strands of the later movement appeared: from conservative moderation to extremism, from revolutionary activity to incipient socialism, from petitions and prayers to passive resistance and non-cooperation.
- The richness of the movement reached art, literature, science and industry.
- People were aroused and learnt bold political positions and new forms of work.
- The campaign undermined the hegemony of colonial ideas and institutions, and the future struggle drew heavily on this experience.

## A balanced judgement

The movement lacked organisation and mass depth and left a communal wedge, yet it **changed the form of Indian politics**: mass participation, boycott, national education and self-reliance became the stock of later movements (GS-043). ### Notes: Definitions

  Source: [Drishti IAS, Swadeshi and Boycott Movement (To the Points, Paper 1)](https://www.drishtiias.com/to-the-points/paper1/swadeshi-and-boycott-movement)
- **F-DR-FADE1908 (draft):** By 1908 the Swadeshi movement was almost over in its open phase because of the government's violent repression.
  Source: [Drishti IAS, Swadeshi and Boycott Movement (To the Points, Paper 1)](https://www.drishtiias.com/to-the-points/paper1/swadeshi-and-boycott-movement)
- **F-DR-PART1911 (draft):** The partition of Bengal was annulled in 1911 by Lord Hardinge, primarily (as this source puts it) to curb revolutionary terrorism.
  Source: [Drishti IAS, Swadeshi and Boycott Movement (To the Points, Paper 1)](https://www.drishtiias.com/to-the-points/paper1/swadeshi-and-boycott-movement)
- **F-VR-SURAT (draft):** The Congress session at Surat on 26 December 1907 ended in a split. Rash Behari Ghosh presided; the Moderates were led by Gopal Krishna Gokhale and the Extremists by Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Lala Lajpat Rai. Tilak was later sentenced to six years in Mandalay jail.
