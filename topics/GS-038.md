## GS-038: Partition of Bengal and reversal

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Reading dates, centuries and British offices

Every event in this lesson is dated in the **Common Era (CE)**, the year-count that older books write as AD. Dates before the year 1 are written BCE (Before the Common Era) and count backwards; none appear here, so every year below is CE.

**Centuries are counted in blocks of one hundred that start at year 1.** The 19th century is 1801 to 1900; the 20th century is 1901 to 2000. So the famines of 1896-1900 belong to the 19th century and the partition of 1905 belongs to the opening decade of the 20th century. A **decade** is ten years; 'the 1890s' means 1890 to 1899.

Three British offices appear again and again:

- **Viceroy (also Governor-General)**: the Crown's representative in India, living in India and running the Government of India. Curzon was Viceroy at the time of the partition plan (January 1899 to November 1905; standard months, verify).
- **Secretary of State for India**: a British cabinet minister in London who supervised the Indian administration. John Morley held this post when the 1909 reforms were planned.
- **Province and capital**: a province was a large administrative unit of British India with its own headquarters, called its capital. Bengal's capital was Calcutta; the 1905 plan gave the new Eastern Bengal and Assam province a capital at Dacca.

Finally, a spelling warning. In this lesson 'partition of Bengal' (1905) is a division of a *province* by the British. The later 'Partition' of 1947 divided the *country*. They are different events and the examiner will not use one name for the other.

### Bridge: Six words you must know before reading further

- **Moderates**: the Congress leaders of 1885 to about 1905 who believed in constitutional agitation, that is, petitions, speeches and resolutions within the law. Their critics called the approach 'prayer, petition and protest' and sneered at it as 'political mendicancy' (begging). Spectrum's label for them is the standard exam word.
- **Extremists** (also called militant or radical nationalists): the younger leaders who wanted swaraj (self-rule) as the goal and direct action, including boycott and passive resistance. Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai and Aurobindo Ghosh are the names to remember. 'Extremist' is a label used by the British and by textbooks; the leaders themselves saw their method as patriotic self-reliance.
- **Swadeshi**: 'of one's own country'. The idea of using and making Indian goods and building Indian institutions.
- **Boycott**: a deliberate refusal to buy, use or deal with something, here foreign (mainly British) goods, schools and, later, offices.
- **Samiti**: a volunteer association or corps. In Bengal samitis organised lectures, physical training, famine relief and Swadeshi schools.
- **Separate electorate**: a voting system in which seats are reserved for a community and only members of that community may vote for them. A **joint electorate** has all voters voting together, possibly with some seats reserved. This difference decides the Muslim League story in module 8.

One more term: **annulment** means cancelling a decision. The British annulled the partition in 1911.

### 1. Orientation: the story in one page

*Objective:* State in five sentences what the partition of Bengal was, how people reacted, how it ended, and which other lessons cover neighbouring ground.

## The story in five sentences

1. In 1905 the British Viceroy, **Lord Curzon**, divided the large province of **Bengal** into two units, officially for administrative convenience.
2. Indians, led at first by Moderate leaders and then by militant nationalists, read the plan as a way to **weaken Bengal**, which was the nerve centre of Indian nationalism, and they opposed it with a new weapon: **boycott of foreign goods**.
3. The campaign grew into the **Swadeshi movement**, which used samitis, national schools, indigenous enterprise, festivals and the press, and which spread beyond Bengal.
4. The Congress split at **Surat in 1907** between Moderates and Extremists while the government mixed repression with limited reforms (1909), including **separate electorates for Muslims**.
5. In **1911** the British annulled the partition and shifted the imperial capital to Delhi, but the province was not simply put back as it was.

### 2. Background: from the Moderate phase to Curzon

*Objective:* Explain why a more militant politics emerged by 1905 and which Curzon-era measures made the partition look like the last straw.

## The Moderate phase in four lines (primer; taught fully in GS-036, to come on Day 14, not yet written)

The Indian National Congress first met at Bombay in December 1885 (72 delegates, W.C. Bonnerjee president, Spectrum ch. 11). For about twenty years Naoroji, Mehta, Banerjea, Gokhale and others used **constitutional agitation**: resolutions, petitions and meetings to build opinion in India and Britain, criticising the economic drain and asking for wider councils and Indianisation of the services. The weakness, as Spectrum puts it, was a narrow social base: the masses played a passive role.

## Why militant nationalism grew

Group the causes as **disappointment, confidence and pressure**.

- **Disappointment.** The Indian Councils Act of 1892 was seen as inadequate; younger leaders called 'prayer, petition and protest' political mendicancy.
- **Economic misery.** Famines reported to have killed 90 lakh persons between 1896 and 1900; plague in parts of the Deccan.
- **Shrinking rights.** The Natu brothers' deportation and Tilak's prosecution (1897), the 1898 sedition-law additions, fewer Indian members in the Calcutta Corporation (1899), the Official Secrets and Indian Universities Acts (1904).
- **Confidence.** Japan's victory over Russia (1905), Ethiopia's defeat of Italy (1896) and British reverses in the Boer wars weakened the myth of European invincibility; Vivekananda, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee and Dayananda painted India's past proudly.

## Curzon: the accumulation

George Nathaniel Curzon was Viceroy from January 1899 to November 1905 (standard months; verify). Spectrum's account of the Indian reaction names four measures: the **Calcutta Corporation Act (1899)**, the **Official Secrets Act (1904)**, the **Indian Universities Act (1904)** and, above all, the **partition of Bengal (1905)**. It also records his dismissal of nationalist speeches as 'letting off of gas' and his 1900 remark that the Congress was tottering to its fall.

**Reading rule.** To evaluate Curzon give both sides: the official argument that a very large province needed better administration (true to some extent, says Spectrum) and the Indian reading that the measures together were reactionary. Other Curzon measures (North-West Frontier Province, 1901; Ancient Monuments Preservation Act, 1904) are standard items not in the extracts (module 10).

The chain: economic distress, shrinking rights, loss of faith in petitions and new confidence produced a leadership ready for direct action, and the partition supplied the occasion.


### 3. The 1905 partition: what was divided, how, and why

*Objective:* State the provinces, capitals and population figures of the 1905 scheme, do the arithmetic that makes the figures consistent, and separate the official reason from the political motive.

## Dates

Spectrum records that the decision was made public in **December 1903**, formally **announced in July 1905** and came into force on **16 October 1905**.

## The two units

| | Bengal (reconstituted) | Eastern Bengal and Assam |
|---|---|---|
| Parts | Western Bengal plus Bihar and Orissa | East Bengal plus Assam |
| Capital | Calcutta | Dacca |
| Population (Spectrum) | 54 million | 31 million |
| Religion (Spectrum) | 42 million Hindus out of 54 million | 18 million Muslims out of 31 million |
| Language (Spectrum) | 17 million Bengali speakers; 37 million Hindi and Oriya speakers | Not given by Spectrum (East Bengal Bengali-speaking, Assam mostly Assamese: standard knowledge, verify) |

## Doing the arithmetic

- **Bengal's Hindu share**: 42 / 54 is about 78 per cent. **Eastern unit's Muslim share**: 18 / 31 is about 58 per cent. One side was a clear Hindu majority and the other a Muslim majority, which is why Spectrum treats the plan as division by religion.
- **Bengali speakers in the new Bengal**: 17 / 54 is about 31 per cent; 37 million spoke Hindi or Oriya, so Bengali speakers became a minority in the unit that kept the name Bengal. This is Spectrum's point about division by language.
- **Consistency check.** The old province had 78 million people (Spectrum); the two new units add up to 54 + 31 = 85 million. The 7 million difference is roughly what Assam, which was *not* part of the old Bengal, added to the eastern unit. (An inference from the source's own numbers, not a sourced figure.)

## The official reason

Bengal, with about 78 million people (about a quarter of British India, per Spectrum), was said to be too large to administer, and Assam would develop better under a direct administration. Spectrum accepts this was *true to some extent*. NCERT calls it 'administrative convenience' and asks whose convenience: that of British officials and businessmen.

## The political motive, and the evidence

Both books say the real motive was to **curtail the influence of Bengali politicians and split the Bengali people**. Three pieces of evidence:

1. **The shape of the division.** NCERT notes that instead of removing the *non-Bengali* areas from Bengal, the government separated *East Bengal* and merged it with Assam.
2. **An official's own words.** Spectrum quotes Risley, home secretary to the Government of India, in 1904: 'Bengal united is a power. Bengal divided will pull in several different ways... One of our main objects is to split up and thereby to weaken a solid body of opponents to our rule.'
3. **Curzon's appeal to Muslims.** Spectrum says Curzon argued that Dacca could be the capital of a Muslim-majority province giving Muslims a unity they had not had for a long time, which Spectrum reads as propping up Muslim communalists against the Congress.

**Interpretive caution.** The motive is an *inference* from words and effects, supported by Risley's quote, not a confession by Curzon. Write 'the government's own officials described one aim as weakening a solid body of opponents', not 'Curzon admitted divide and rule'.


### 4. The anti-partition campaign: from petitions to boycott

*Objective:* Narrate the campaign in sequence (Moderate phase 1903-1905, 7 August, 16 October, spread outside Bengal) and state the Congress position in 1905.

## Phase 1: the Moderate campaign (1903 to 1905)

While the plan was only a proposal, **Surendranath Banerjea, K.K. Mitra and Prithwishchandra Ray** used petitions, meetings, memoranda, pamphlets and newspapers such as **Hitabadi, Sanjibani and Bengalee** (Spectrum) to create educated opinion in India and England. The government ignored it and announced the partition in July 1905.

## The turn: protest meetings and the boycott pledge

Within days of the announcement, protest meetings were held in small towns all over Bengal, and it was at these meetings that the pledge to boycott foreign goods was first taken. On **7 August 1905**, a massive meeting at the **Calcutta Town Hall** passed the **Boycott Resolution**, which Spectrum treats as the formal proclamation of the Swadeshi movement. Leaders then went to other parts of Bengal to spread the message of boycotting **Manchester cloth and Liverpool salt**: notice that the targets name British manufacturing towns.

## 16 October 1905: the day of mourning

On the day the partition took effect people observed a day of mourning: fasting, bathing in the Ganga and walking barefoot in processions singing **Bande Mataram**. Rabindranath Tagore and Ramendrasundar Trivedi called for **Rakshabandhan** on that day so that people tied rakhis on each other's wrists as a symbol of unity of the two halves of Bengal. Source slip: Spectrum prints the date as 'October 6' on the same page that gives 16 October for the partition; the unity-day date is 16 October. Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose addressed huge gatherings and Rs 50,000 was reported raised within hours; crowds sang Tagore's 'Amar Sonar Bangla', later the national anthem of Bangladesh.

## Spread beyond Bengal

Spectrum names Tilak (Poona, Bombay), Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh (Punjab), Syed Haider Raza (Delhi) and Chidambaram Pillai (Madras); NCERT adds that in deltaic Andhra it was called the Vandemataram Movement.

## The Congress position

At its **1905 session (Benaras, Gokhale presiding)** the Congress condemned the partition and Curzon's reactionary policies and supported the anti-partition and Swadeshi movement *of Bengal*. The Extremists (Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal, Aurobindo) wanted to carry it outside Bengal and beyond boycott of goods into a political mass struggle for swaraj; the Moderates, dominant in the Congress, would not go that far. The compromise was a milder resolution that avoided a split for the moment. Calcutta 1906 (Naoroji; goal of swaraj) is in module 7.

Spectrum gives three reasons the Extremists gained influence: the Moderate campaign had failed, the divisive tactics of the governments of both Bengals had embittered nationalists, and repression (student punishments, a ban on public singing of Vande Mataram, curbs on meetings and the press) followed.

### 5. Methods of the movement: boycott, samitis, national education, press

*Objective:* List the forms of struggle with at least one concrete example each, and state who led the samitis and the national education programme.

Spectrum's 'new forms of struggle': learn each with one example.

## 1. Boycott of foreign goods

Boycott included the public burning of foreign cloth, boycott of foreign-made salt and sugar, priests refusing to ritualise marriages involving foreign goods, and washermen refusing to wash foreign clothes. Spectrum says this form met with great success at the practical and popular level. Notice how it used *social pressure within communities*, not only individual choice.

## 2. Public meetings and processions

Major methods of mass mobilisation and popular expression.

## 3. Samitis (volunteer corps)

The **Swadesh Bandhab Samiti** of **Ashwini Kumar Dutta at Barisal** was a very popular and powerful means of mobilisation. In Tirunelveli (Tamil Nadu), **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai, Subramania Siva** and some lawyers formed the **Swadeshi Sangam**. Samitis spread political consciousness through **magic lantern lectures and Swadeshi songs**, gave physical and moral training, did **social work during famines and epidemics**, organised schools, taught Swadeshi crafts and ran **arbitration courts**.

## 4. Festivals and melas

Traditional festivals became a medium for political messages. Tilak's **Ganapati and Shivaji festivals** carried Swadeshi propaganda in western India and in Bengal; traditional folk theatre in Bengal was used too. Hold this point; module 8 returns to its cost.

## 5. Self-reliance (atma shakti)

Atma shakti meant re-asserting national dignity and confidence and the social and economic regeneration of villages, including campaigns against caste oppression, early marriage, dowry and alcohol.

## 6. National education

When the government tried to stop student participation by threatening grants, affiliation and scholarships of nationalist institutions, the response was a movement for **national schools**. **Raja Subodh Mullick** gave Rs 1,00,000 towards a national university. The **National Council of Education** was set up on **15 August 1906**, with the **Bengal National College** (first principal **Aurobindo Ghosh**, first president Rashbehari Ghosh) and the **Bengal Technical Institute**. Education was to be literary, scientific and technical, under national control and through the **vernacular**; funds were even raised to send students to Japan. Spectrum also credits **Satishchandra Mukherjee**, whose Dawn (paper, 1897) and **Dawn Society (1902)** had already spread self-help in industry and education; Spectrum (p. 313) also names Tagore's Shantiniketan school among the inspirations for such national education.

## 7. The press and literature

The Moderate-phase newspapers named earlier carried the campaign (Hitabadi, Sanjibani, Bengalee); NCERT shows Tilak's Marathi **Kesari** as a strong critic of British rule. Sakharam Ganesh Deuskar's **Desher Katha (1904)** popularised Swadeshi in everyday language (over 15,000 copies before it was banned in 1910). The government answered with the Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908 and the Indian Press Act, 1910.

## 8. Passive resistance programme (after Calcutta 1906)

Encouraged by the Calcutta session, the Extremists called for boycott of government schools, colleges, services, courts, councils, municipalities and titles. Aurobindo's purpose, as Spectrum quotes it, was to make the administration impossible by organised refusal to help British commerce or officialdom.

### 6. Swadeshi as a social, economic and cultural movement (Mains angle)

*Objective:* Build a three-part answer showing that Swadeshi was more than a political protest, with evidence and limits for each part.

Separate the movement into dimensions and give each a fact and a limit.

## Economic dimension

- **Evidence.** Swadeshi textile mills, soap and match factories, tanneries, banks, insurance companies and shops appeared. V.O. Chidambaram Pillai's **Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company** at Tuticorin challenged the British India Steam Navigation Company. Earlier groundwork: Prafullachandra Ray's **Bengal Chemicals (1893)**, Swadeshi stores such as Tagore's Swadeshi Bhandar (1897), Jogeshchandra Chaudhuri's Indian Stores (1901) and Sarala Debi's Lakshmir Bhandar (1903).
- **Limit.** Spectrum says these enterprises rested more on patriotic zeal than on business acumen. Consumers and goods supply could not keep up, and the boycott's economic effect is hard to measure from these pages.

## Cultural dimension

- **Evidence.** Songs by Tagore, Rajnikant Sen, Dwijendralal Ray, Mukunda Das and others; Tagore's 'Amar Sonar Bangla'; Subramania Bharati's *Sudesha Geetham* in Tamil. **Abanindranath Tagore** broke the dominance of Victorian naturalism in painting, drawing on Ajanta, Mughal and Rajput art; **Nandalal Bose** was the first recipient of a scholarship from the Indian Society of Oriental Art (founded 1907). In science, **Jagdish Chandra Bose and Prafullachandra Ray** pioneered original research.
- **Limit.** The cultural idiom was often Hindu (festivals, goddesses, symbols), which Spectrum says tended to exclude Muslims (module 8).

## Social dimension

- **Evidence.** Atma shakti campaigns against caste oppression, early marriage, dowry and drink; samitis gave famine and epidemic relief; the social base widened to students, women, certain zamindar sections, the lower middle class and some workers. Women of urban middle classes took part in processions and picketing, and Spectrum says they would play a significant role thereafter. Workers joined too (a clerks' walkout in 1905, railway and jute-mill strikes in 1906 to 1908, strikes at Tuticorin and Tirunelveli led by Subramania Siva and Chidambaram Pillai); labour unrest subsided under strict action by summer 1908.
- **Limit.** The movement remained largely confined to the upper and middle classes and zamindars and did not reach the masses, especially the peasantry. Spectrum adds that it failed to win the Muslim peasantry because of a conscious government policy of divide and rule, helped by an overlap of class and community in places.

## Political dimension and the answer frame

The movement threw up non-cooperation, passive resistance, filling jails and constructive work, which Spectrum says later came to be associated with Gandhian politics, but it failed to give them a disciplined focus: the **legacy line**.

**Answer frame.** One opening sentence (partition as trigger), one short paragraph per dimension with a named fact, a 'limits' paragraph (narrow base, Muslim distance, repression, weak organisation) and the legacy line.


### 7. Moderates against Extremists: Surat 1907 and the repression

*Objective:* Explain the disagreement over the pace and techniques of the movement, narrate the Surat split and the government's response, and state what happened to the leaders.

## What the two sides wanted

| Question | Moderates | Extremists |
|---|---|---|
| Where should boycott operate, and what should it cover? | Bengal only; mainly foreign cloth and liquor | All parts of the country; goods plus schools, colleges, courts, councils, services, municipalities |
| Method | Constitutional only | Passive resistance and boycott, 'extra-constitutional' |
| Goal | Self-government within the Empire | Swaraj (Tilak: self-government of some kind; Aurobindo: complete independence, per Spectrum) |

A fuller comparison is to come in GS-037 (Day 15) and GS-053 (Day 10), not yet written; this table carries only what the Surat dispute needs. NCERT adds that the Moderates opposed boycott because they thought it involved the use of force.

## The run-up

- **Benaras, December 1905** (Gokhale presiding): a mild compromise resolution (module 4).
- **Calcutta, December 1906**: Extremists wanted Tilak or Lajpat Rai as president; Moderates proposed Dadabhai Naoroji, who was elected. The goal 'swarajya or self-government' like the UK or Australia and Canada was adopted, with resolutions on Swadeshi, boycott and national education. Its meaning was left open, so each side read it its own way.
- After Calcutta the Extremists called for wide passive resistance; the Moderates, encouraged by news of council reforms, decided to tone down the Calcutta programme.

## Surat, December 1907

The Extremists wanted **Nagpur**, with Tilak or Lajpat Rai as president and the Calcutta resolutions repeated. The Moderates chose **Surat**, in Tilak's home province of Bombay, because a leader of the host province could not be president, so Tilak was excluded; they wanted **Rashbehari Ghosh** as president and meant to drop the resolutions. Both sides took rigid positions and the split followed. The Congress, now dominated by Moderates, reaffirmed **self-government within the British Empire** by constitutional methods only. NCERT: the Moderates dominated and Tilak's followers worked from outside. NCERT hess208 prints the reunion as December 1915, but Spectrum places the Moderate-Extremist reunion at Lucknow in **December 1916**, which is the standard date (verify the NCERT year).

Spectrum's balanced verdict: each side misread the other. The Moderates missed that the council reforms were meant to isolate the Extremists; the Extremists missed that Moderates could be a front line against repression; neither realised that only a broad-based movement could succeed against a strong imperialist power.

## Government strategy and repression

Morley's policy of 'rallying' the Moderates, described by Spectrum as repression, conciliation and suppression: first mild repression of Extremists to frighten the Moderates, then concessions to Moderates, then full suppression of Extremists. Five laws between 1907 and 1911 included the Seditious Meetings Act (1907), the Indian Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act (1908), the Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908) and the Indian Press Act (1910); Spectrum's summary adds the Explosive Substances Act (1908). **Tilak** was tried for sedition over his writings in *Kesari* about a Muzaffarpur bomb and sentenced to six years' transportation to Mandalay. Tilak was tried and sentenced in July 1908 (standard; Spectrum's 1909 is a slip). Aurobindo and Bipin Chandra Pal retired from active politics and Lajpat Rai went abroad; Tilak was released in 1914. After 1908 the movement as a whole declined for a time.

### 8. Muslim League, separate electorates and the communal dimension

*Objective:* Place the Simla Deputation, the Muslim League and the 1909 reforms in sequence, state what the sources say about Muslim participation in Swadeshi, and write about it without over-simplifying.

## Muslim responses to the partition and Swadeshi

Spectrum records a mixed picture. Some Muslims took part (Barrister Abdul Rasul, Liaqat Hussain, Guznavi, and Maulana Azad, who joined a revolutionary group). But **most upper and middle class Muslims stayed away** or, led by **Nawab Salimullah of Dacca**, supported the partition for the Muslim-majority East Bengal it gave them. A second factor: Swadeshi leaders evoked **Hindu festivals and goddesses**, which tended to exclude Muslims. Hold two points together: the movement did not capture the Muslim peasantry, and the reason was government policy, class-community overlap and the movement's religious idiom together, not one cause alone.

## The sequence 1906 to 1909

| When | What | Source |
|---|---|---|
| October 1906 | A group of Muslim elites, the **Simla Deputation** led by the **Aga Khan**, meets Viceroy Minto and demands **separate electorates** and representation above their numerical strength | Spectrum |
| December 1906 | The **All India Muslim League** is formed at **Dacca** by landlords and nawabs (Nawab Salimullah with Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Waqar-ul-Mulk); it supports partition and separate electorates | NCERT, Spectrum |
| 1909 | Indian Councils Act (Morley-Minto reforms): separate electorates for Muslims | Spectrum, NCERT |

**Source slip.** Spectrum p. 315 says the League was 'propped up on December 30, 1905'; the same book (p. 324) and NCERT put its formation in December 1906 at Dacca. The League was founded on 30 December 1906; the year 1905 is the slip.

## The 1909 reforms: what to know now

Elections were introduced for non-official members (indirectly, through electoral colleges); the number of elected members increased; and **for the first time separate electorates for Muslims** were established for election to the central council (also in provincial councils; verify), with representation above their population share and a lower income qualification for Muslim voters. Spectrum quotes Sumit Sarkar's seat breakdown for the 69-member Imperial Legislative Council (arithmetic in the master notes). One Indian, Satyendra Sinha, joined the Viceroy's executive council. The rest of this constitutional story is GS-050, to come on Day 14, not yet written.

## How to word the evaluation

Spectrum judges the reforms as aimed at dividing nationalist ranks and rallying Moderates and Muslims against nationalism, and describes separate electorates as appeasing a small section of the Muslim elite. NCERT notes the incentive it created: politicians could gather a following by distributing favours to their own religious groups. The **intention** (divide) and the **effect** (a political barrier between communities, in Nehru's words quoted by Spectrum) are separate claims and both are sourced.

**Cross-link.** The 1947 division of the country is GS-051, to come on Day 15, not yet written.

### 9. 1911: annulment, the Delhi Durbar, capital shift, legacy and historiography

*Objective:* State what was decided in 1911, what it left unchanged, the reasons given for annulment, and the main interpretations of the whole episode.

## What happened in 1911

Spectrum: the partition was annulled in 1911 **mainly to curb the menace of revolutionary terrorism**. The annulment came as a shock to the Muslim political elite. The capital was shifted to Delhi **as a sop to the Muslims**, since Delhi was associated with Muslim glory, but the Muslims were not pleased. **Bihar and Orissa were taken out of Bengal, and Assam was made a separate province.**

The following details are standard textbook content that is not in the extracts, so mark them verify: the announcement was made by King George V at the **Delhi Durbar of December 1911** (commonly dated 12 December); the Viceroy was **Lord Hardinge**; the reorganisation took effect in **1912**; the new capital, New Delhi, was inaugurated much later, in 1931. Dacca University, often described as a consolation to Bengal's Muslims, was promised in 1912 and founded in 1921 (standard; verify).

## What was reversed and what was not

| Element | After 1911 |
|---|---|
| Bengali-speaking divisions split between two provinces | Reunited (Sylhet stayed with Assam; verify): the 1905 split of East and West Bengal ended |
| Bihar and Orissa | Separated from Bengal (a separate province) |
| Assam | Made a separate province (it had been joined with East Bengal in 1905) |
| Imperial capital | Moved from Calcutta to Delhi |

This is why examiners say the annulment 'did not restore the old Bengal'. Bengal reunited but lost Bihar and Orissa, and Calcutta lost the capital.

## Why annul?

The source gives one reason: the revolutionary menace. Other reasons often discussed (the strength and cost of the protest, British prestige) are interpretations, not sourced facts. The open Swadeshi phase had largely ended by 1908, so the reversal came after the mass phase, partly as a response to revolutionary violence.

## Legacy

1. **A new type of politics**: boycott, national education, samitis and passive resistance entered the repertoire; Spectrum says all major trends of the later movement, from conservative moderation to revolutionary activity and incipient socialism, emerged in this phase.
2. **A wider social base**: students, women, workers and parts of the middle classes.
3. **The end of Moderate leadership of mass action**: the Moderates 'outlived their utility' (Spectrum), though they still controlled the Congress after Surat.
4. **A communal wedge**: Muslim League, separate electorates, a Hindu-flavoured idiom; Spectrum says the link between religion and politics bore bitter fruit later.
5. **A precedent**: Bengal was divided again in 1947 on religious lines (standard knowledge; GS-051 is to come on Day 15, not yet written; verify). Do not link 1905 and 1947 as cause and effect without argument.

## Historiography: how to write 'historians differ'

- **Nationalist reading**: divide and rule; the reversal was a victory for popular agitation.
- **Class reading**: the pattern Spectrum describes (upper and middle class base, little peasant reach, Muslim peasantry unmoved) is a class-and-community reading; it cites Sumit Sarkar.
- **Communal reading**: some see the movement's Hindu symbolism as a cause of Muslim distance; others stress government policy. The sources present both, so a balanced answer does too.

Cite only scholars the extracts name (Sumit Sarkar, Ishita Banerjee-Dube).

### 10. Examination application: the Curzon PYQ and answer plans

## The verified PYQ

**2020 Mains GS I Q3 (10 marks): 'Evaluate the policies of Lord Curzon and their long term implications on the national movement.'** Directive: *evaluate* means judge with evidence and reach a conclusion. This is analysis, not a model answer or an official key.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Partition of Bengal (1905): the division of the Bengal province by Viceroy Curzon into Bengal (Western Bengal plus Bihar and Orissa, capital Calcutta) and Eastern Bengal and Assam (capital Dacca); effective 16 October 1905.
- Swadeshi movement: the movement that grew out of the anti-partition campaign, built on boycott of foreign goods, indigenous enterprise, national education and self-reliance.
- Boycott Resolution: passed at the Calcutta Town Hall on 7 August 1905; treated by Spectrum as the formal proclamation of the Swadeshi movement.
- Moderates: Congress leaders of the constitutional-agitation school (1885 to about 1905). Extremists: militant nationalists who wanted swaraj and direct action.
- Samiti: a volunteer corps (for example Swadesh Bandhab Samiti, Barisal).
- National Council of Education: set up on 15 August 1906 to organise literary, scientific and technical education on national lines under national control.
- Separate electorate: seats reserved for a community voted for only by that community; introduced for Muslims in the central council by the 1909 reforms (also in provincial councils; verify).
- Annulment: cancellation of the partition in 1911.

### Notes: Chronology of the Swadeshi phase

- December 1885: Congress founded at Bombay (72 delegates, W.C. Bonnerjee president).
- January 1899 to November 1905: Curzon as Viceroy (standard months; verify); 1899 Calcutta Corporation Act; 1904 Official Secrets Act and Indian Universities Act.
- December 1903: partition plan becomes public. July 1905: formal announcement.
- 7 August 1905: Boycott Resolution, Calcutta Town Hall.
- 16 October 1905: partition effective; day of mourning and Rakshabandhan (Spectrum prints 6 October by slip).
- December 1905: Benaras Congress (Gokhale): condemns partition, supports Bengal's movement.
- 15 August 1906: National Council of Education.
- October 1906: Simla Deputation. December 1906: Muslim League at Dacca; Calcutta Congress (Naoroji) adopts swaraj.
- December 1907: Surat split.
- 1907 to 1911: Seditious Meetings Act 1907; Newspapers Act, Criminal Law Amendment Act and Explosive Substances Act 1908; Indian Press Act 1910.
- 1909: Morley-Minto reforms. 1911: annulment and capital shift to Delhi (announced at the Delhi Durbar; verify date). December 1916: Moderates and Extremists reunited at Lucknow (Spectrum; NCERT hess208 prints December 1915, verify).

### Notes: Cause-effect chain

- Famine and distress plus shrinking rights plus disappointment with petitions led to a militant school of thought by about 1905.
- Partition (1905) plus Moderate failure to stop it led to the boycott and Swadeshi movement.
- Boycott and national education led to government repression of students and press.
- Dispute over extension and techniques led to the Surat split (1907); the split plus repression weakened the open movement by 1908.
- Government carrot-and-stick led to the 1909 reforms with separate electorates.
- Revolutionary violence and continuing unrest led to annulment in 1911 (Spectrum: mainly to curb revolutionary terrorism) and the shift of capital.

### Notes: Methods of Swadeshi with examples

- Boycott: public burning of foreign cloth; refusal by priests and washermen.
- Samitis: Swadesh Bandhab Samiti (Ashwini Kumar Dutta, Barisal); Swadeshi Sangam (V.O. Chidambaram Pillai, Subramania Siva, Tirunelveli).
- National education: Bengal National College (Aurobindo first principal), Bengal Technical Institute, Subodh Mullick's gift of Rs 1,00,000, Dawn Society (Satishchandra Mukherjee, 1902).
- Enterprise: Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company (Tuticorin), Bengal Chemicals (1893, Prafullachandra Ray), Swadeshi stores.
- Culture: Tagore's songs, Abanindranath Tagore's painting, Nandalal Bose, Subramania Bharati, Desher Katha (1904).
- Festivals: Ganapati and Shivaji festivals (Tilak); folk theatre in Bengal.

### Notes: Repressive and constitutional measures of 1907 to 1911

- Seditious Meetings Act, 1907.
- Indian Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908.
- Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1908.
- Explosive Substances Act, 1908 (listed in Spectrum's summary).
- Indian Press Act, 1910.
- Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto reforms): elected non-officials (indirect), separate electorates for Muslims, one Indian (Satyendra Sinha) on the Viceroy's executive council.
- Imperial Legislative Council after 1909 (Spectrum, citing Sumit Sarkar): 69 members = 37 officials + 32 non-officials (5 nominated + 27 elected); of the 27 elected, 8 seats were reserved for Muslims, 4 for British capitalists, 2 for landlords and 13 were general (check: 37 + 32 = 69; 5 + 27 = 32; 8 + 4 + 2 + 13 = 27).

### Notes: Source slips and cautions

- Spectrum p. 309: Rakshabandhan date printed as 6 October 1905; the partition took effect on 16 October 1905 and the unity day was that date.
- Spectrum p. 315: Muslim League 'propped up on December 30, 1905'; Spectrum p. 324 and NCERT: December 1906 at Dacca.
- Spectrum p. 322: Tilak tried 'in 1909'; Tilak was tried and sentenced in July 1908 (standard; Spectrum's 1909 is a slip).
- Spectrum's 78 million (old province) against 54 + 31 = 85 million (two new units): reconciled by Assam's addition (inference).
- 'Divide and rule' is an inference from evidence (Risley's quote, the shape of the division); do not present as proven intent.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Partition of Bengal (1905, province) against Partition of India (1947, country).
- Moderate-led anti-partition campaign (petitions, 1903 to 1905) against Extremist-led boycott phase (after 1905).
- Benaras 1905 (compromise resolution), Calcutta 1906 (swaraj as goal, Naoroji), Surat 1907 (split).
- Muslim League formation (1906) against the Simla Deputation (October 1906) against separate electorates (1909 Act).
- Annulment of partition (1911) against the Delhi capital shift (announced 1911, effective 1912; verify).

### Core concepts

- The partition was officially administrative but, on the evidence cited by Spectrum and NCERT, aimed at weakening Bengali nationalism.
- The movement moved from Moderate petitions to Extremist boycott after 7 August 1905.
- Swadeshi worked through boycott, samitis, national education, festivals, enterprise and the press.
- Swadeshi was social, economic and cultural as well as political, but its base was narrow.
- Surat 1907 split the Congress; the government used repression and the 1909 reforms.
- The 1911 annulment reunited Bengali areas but separated Bihar and Orissa, made Assam separate and moved the capital to Delhi.

**Q.** Why does the evidence on 'divide and rule' require care?
