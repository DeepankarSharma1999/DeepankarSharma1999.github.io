### Bridge: Small fractional changes: logarithms and error propagation

Many exam parts ask what happens when a size changes by a few per cent. The tool is the **fractional change**, written δx/x (0.01 means 1%).

**Rule.** If a quantity is Q = k xᵖ yᵠ (k a constant), take the natural logarithm: ln Q = ln k + p ln x + q ln y. Differentiating gives dQ/Q = p dx/x + q dy/y. So **a power p multiplies the fractional change by p**. For Poiseuille, Q ∝ a⁴, so a 1% change in radius changes Q by about 4%; because Q ∝ 1/L, a +1% change in L changes Q by −1%.

**Warning.** The rule is exact only for very small changes. For a 10% change use the exact power: (0.9)⁴ = 0.6561, not 1 − 0.4 = 0.6. When errors in several measurements add, the *worst case* adds their absolute sizes; for independent random errors add their squares and take the square root.

**Example.** η = πPr⁴/(8Ql). If r is 1% too large and Q is 2% too small, η is about 4% + 2% = 6% too large.

$$ v(r) = ΔP (a² − r²) / (4ηL),   v_max = ΔP a² / (4ηL),   v_mean = v_max / 2 $$

$$ Q = π ΔP a⁴ / (8ηL),   R_h = ΔP / Q = 8ηL / (π a⁴) $$

### 2. Core: resistance, scaling and sensitivity (the r⁴ law at work)

*Objective:* Use R_h = 8ηL/(πa⁴) to predict how flow, pressure and power change when a, L, η or ΔP change, and compare the exact answer with the small-change estimate.

**Resistance form.** Poiseuille's equation reads ΔP = R_h Q, with R_h = 8ηL/(πa⁴) (Mathur calls it the effective viscous resistance and writes Q = P/R, like I = V/R). *Unit:* Pa s m⁻³ (= [ML⁻⁴T⁻¹]). *Dimension check:* [ML⁻¹T⁻¹][L]/[L⁴] = ML⁻⁴T⁻¹, and (ML⁻¹T⁻²)/(L³T⁻¹) = ML⁻⁴T⁻¹ ✓.

**Scaling rules (everything else fixed).** Q ∝ ΔP; Q ∝ a⁴; Q ∝ 1/η; Q ∝ 1/L. Equivalently R_h ∝ L, ∝ η, ∝ 1/a⁴.

**Exact versus small-change.** For a *finite* change use ratios and powers. For a *small* change use fractional differences:

$$ δQ/Q ≈ δΔP/ΔP + 4 δa/a − δη/η − δL/L $$

*Why the 4:* take the logarithm of Q = πΔP a⁴/(8ηL) and differentiate: d ln Q = d ln ΔP + 4 d ln a − d ln η − d ln L.

**Worked comparison (2026 question, new angle).** Radius narrows by 10%, Q, η, L fixed. Exact: ΔP₂/ΔP₁ = (1/0.9)⁴ = 1.5242, a rise of **52.4%**. Small-change estimate: 4 × 10% = 40%, which is wrong by 12 percentage points because 10% is not small and the changes compound. For a 5% narrowing: exact 1/0.95⁴ = 1.2277 (22.8%), estimate 20%. For 1%: exact 4.06%, estimate 4%. *Rule:* the linear rule is safe only when the change is a few per cent; in an examination use the exact power law. Two wrong answers to avoid: 40% (linear), 34.4% (this is 1 − 0.9⁴, the *fall of flow at fixed pressure*).

**Same data, other questions.**

- Flow at fixed ΔP after a 10% narrowing: Q₂/Q₁ = 0.9⁴ = 0.6561.
- Pressure (and, by module 4, heart power) at fixed Q rises by the same factor 1.524.
- **Stenosis in a part of the artery** (series idea, module 3): if only 1 cm of a 10 cm vessel is narrowed to half its radius, the narrowed part has 16 times the resistance per length, so R_total/R₀ = 0.9 + 0.1 × 16 = **2.5**. At fixed ΔP the flow falls to 40%; to keep Q the pressure must rise by 150%.

**Blood: sourced facts and illustration.** NCERT (extract p. 12) states that blood is thicker than water, with η about 2.7 mPa s at 37 °C in Table 9.2, and that its viscosity relative to water stays constant between 0 °C and 37 °C. *Illustrative, not physiological data:* a straight rigid tube a = 2.0 mm, L = 0.10 m, Q = 2.0 mL/s of fluid with η = 2.7 mPa s needs ΔP = 8ηLQ/(πa⁴) = **85.9 Pa**, mean speed 0.159 m/s, and with an assumed ρ = 1060 kg m⁻³ (standard value, verify) Re = ρvD/η = **250**. Real arteries are elastic and pulsatile and blood is not exactly Newtonian (standard knowledge, verify), so Poiseuille's formula is a model; the 2026 question explicitly lets you assume it.

**Traps.** Mixing radius and diameter (factor 16); treating "blood pressure" as absolute pressure rather than the pressure difference driving the flow; applying the linear rule to a 10% change.

### 3. Core: capillaries in series and in parallel (derivation)

*Objective:* Derive the series and parallel flow formulae from Q = ΔP/R_h, including the 2024 form Q = (πP/8η)(l₁/r₁⁴ + l₂/r₂⁴)⁻¹, and the pressure-sharing rule.

**Setting.** Tubes 1 and 2 carry the same Newtonian liquid (η), each with its own radius a_i and length L_i, each in steady laminar flow. Resistances R_i = 8ηL_i/(πa_i⁴). Ignore losses at the junctions and assume each tube is long enough to be fully developed (flagged in module 7).

**Series (end to end).** Incompressible liquid cannot pile up at the junction, so the same volume per second passes through both (Mathur: like the same current through resistors in series):

$$ Q₁ = Q₂ = Q $$

The pressure drops add, because the outlet pressure of tube 1 is the inlet pressure of tube 2:

$$ P = p₁ + p₂ $$

Apply Poiseuille to each: p₁ = R₁Q and p₂ = R₂Q. Then P = (R₁ + R₂)Q, so **R_series = R₁ + R₂** and

$$ Q = P / (R₁ + R₂) = P / [8ηL₁/(πa₁⁴) + 8ηL₂/(πa₂⁴)] = (πP/8η) (L₁/a₁⁴ + L₂/a₂⁴)⁻¹ $$

This is the 2024 result (with l for L and r for a). *Pressure sharing:* p_i = P R_i/(R₁ + R₂) ∝ L_i/a_i⁴; the **narrow, long tube takes most of the pressure drop**. For n tubes: R = ΣR_i.

**Parallel (side by side).** Both tubes see the same pressure difference P between the common ends; the flows add:

$$ Q = Q₁ + Q₂ = P/R₁ + P/R₂ $$

so **1/R_parallel = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂**, that is

$$ Q = (πP/8η) (a₁⁴/L₁ + a₂⁴/L₂) $$

*Flow sharing:* Q_i/Q = (1/R_i)/(1/R) ∝ a_i⁴/L_i; **the wide, short tube takes most of the flow.**

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* πP/(8η) has dimension [ML⁻¹T⁻²]/[ML⁻¹T⁻¹] = T⁻¹, and (L/a⁴)⁻¹ has dimension L³, so Q has L³T⁻¹ ✓. *Limits:* L₂ → 0 in series leaves Q = πPa₁⁴/(8ηL₁) (tube 2 vanishes) ✓; identical tubes in parallel give Q = 2 × single ✓, in series half ✓.

**Numbers (recomputed).** η = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s, ΔP = 2000 Pa; tube 1: a = 1.0 mm, L = 0.10 m; tube 2: a = 0.5 mm, L = 0.20 m. R₁ = 2.546 × 10⁸, R₂ = 8.149 × 10⁹ Pa s m⁻³.

- Series: Q = 2.38 × 10⁻⁷ m³ s⁻¹; p₁ = 60.6 Pa, p₂ = 1939.4 Pa (sum 2000 ✓).
- Parallel: Q = 8.10 × 10⁻⁶ m³ s⁻¹, of which tube 1 carries 7.85 × 10⁻⁶ (97.0%).

**Design lesson.** Replace one tube of radius a by four tubes of radius a/2 (same total cross-section area) in parallel: R = R₀(16)/4 = 4R₀. Splitting a flow into thinner channels at equal area raises resistance, because the wall surface and the r⁴ penalty grow.

**Traps.** In series the *pressure differences* differ and the flows are equal; in parallel the flows differ and the pressure differences are equal. The junction pressure in a series pair is not atmospheric. The formulas do not apply if either tube is turbulent.

### 4. Core: energy, power and viscous dissipation

*Objective:* Show that the power needed to drive Poiseuille flow is ΔP Q = R_h Q², that it equals the viscous dissipation, and find where in the tube the heat appears.

**Question.** PH-023 module 8 noted that in a uniform viscous pipe the pressure energy is lost as heat. Here that is proved, with a number.

**Step 1: power supplied by the pressure.** The net pressure force on the fluid in the tube is ΔP × (cross-section πa²). Per second the fluid column advances by the mean speed v̄ = Q/(πa²). Power = force × speed:

$$ W = ΔP · πa² · v̄ = ΔP · Q $$

*Units:* Pa × m³ s⁻¹ = N m⁻² × m³ s⁻¹ = J s⁻¹ = W ✓.

**Step 2: other forms.** With ΔP = R_h Q: **W = R_h Q² = ΔP²/R_h**. At fixed Q the power is proportional to R_h; at fixed ΔP it is proportional to 1/R_h.

**Step 3: where does it go?** Take steady flow in a uniform horizontal tube. The velocity profile is the same at inlet and outlet, so the kinetic-energy flux (energy per second) entering equals that leaving, and the potential energy is unchanged. By the work-energy theorem the pressure work must therefore be removed as **heat by viscous friction**: W_dissipated = ΔP Q.

**Step 4: independent local check.** In a shell between r and r + dr the viscous stress is τ = η|dv/dr| and the rate of shear is |dv/dr|, so the dissipation per unit volume is τ × (shear rate) = η (dv/dr)² (standard result, used here and then checked against Step 1). Use dv/dr = −ΔP r/(2ηL) from PH-023 module 4:

$$ η (dv/dr)² = η ΔP² r² / (4η²L²) = ΔP² r² / (4ηL²) $$

Multiply by the shell volume 2πrL dr and add all shells from 0 to a:

$$ W = ∫₀ᵃ [ΔP² r² / (4ηL²)] 2πrL dr = [πΔP²/(2ηL)] ∫₀ᵃ r³ dr = [πΔP²/(2ηL)] (a⁴/4) = π ΔP² a⁴ / (8ηL) $$

Since Q = πΔP a⁴/(8ηL), this is **ΔP · Q** ✓: the local and global accounts agree.

**Step 5: where the heat appears.** The integrand ∝ r³ means the dissipation per unit tube length inside radius r is proportional to r⁴. Fraction inside r = (r/a)⁴. Half is produced outside r = a/2^(1/4) = 0.841 a, in the outer 16% of the radius, because the velocity gradient (zero on the axis) is largest at the wall.

**Interpretation of the 2019 question (PH-023 module 8).** Per unit mass the dissipated energy is ΔP/ρ; this is the 6.25 × 10⁻³ J kg⁻¹ of that question, now justified: it is not kinetic energy gained but friction lost.

**Numbers.** Water tube of PH-023 (a = 1.0 mm, L = 0.50 m, ΔP = 2000 Pa, Q = 1.571 × 10⁻⁶ m³ s⁻¹): W = 2000 × 1.571 × 10⁻⁶ = **3.14 × 10⁻³ W**. *Illustrative circulation:* Q = 5 L/min = 8.33 × 10⁻⁵ m³ s⁻¹ driven by a mean pressure difference of 100 mmHg (= 13 332 Pa; round textbook-style values, verify) gives W = **1.11 W**. A 10% narrowing of a uniform vessel at the same Q raises its own dissipation by 1.524 (module 2).

**Limits.** ΔP → 0 gives W → 0. Doubling L at fixed Q doubles W; halving a at fixed Q multiplies W by 16.

**Trap.** Do not equate ΔP Q with the kinetic energy gained. In a uniform tube the kinetic energy does not change; the work becomes heat. Module 5 shows the one place where part of ΔP does go into kinetic energy: the entrance.

### 5. Master: Mathur's corrections for a real capillary (end and kinetic energy)

*Objective:* Derive Mathur's kinetic-energy correction P′ = P − ρQ²/(π²r⁴), state the end correction l + 1.64 r exactly as sourced, and judge when they matter.

**Why corrections exist.** The ideal derivation assumes fully developed parabolic flow from end to end. Mathur (PDF pp. 806-808) names two departures in a real capillary of radius r and length l, carrying volume rate Q under measured pressure difference P.

**Correction 1: the entrance (end correction).** Near the inlet the liquid is accelerating and not yet streamline. Mathur says this is removed by replacing l by the effective length **l + a′ with a′ almost invariably 1.64 r**, a correction independent of tube length but depending on the radius. The source gives this value without a derivation, so treat 1.64 as an empirical number to quote, not to derive.

**Correction 2: kinetic energy of the issuing liquid (derivation).** Part of P must supply the kinetic energy carried out by the liquid, so only P′ < P fights viscosity. Assume the liquid enters from a wide vessel, where its speed and so the inlet kinetic-energy flux are about zero. Energy per second: P Q = P′Q + (kinetic energy flux).

1. Shell between x and x + dx: volume rate 2πx dx v, mass rate 2πx dx v ρ.
2. Kinetic energy per second of the shell: ½ (2πρ x v dx) v² = πρ x v³ dx.
3. The profile is v = P′(r² − x²)/(4ηl), so v³ = [P′/(4ηl)]³ (r² − x²)³.
4. Integrate: ∫₀ʳ x (r² − x²)³ dx = r⁸/8 (put u = r² − x², du = −2x dx: ½∫₀^(r²) u³ du = r⁸/8).
5. So KE flux = πρ [P′/(4ηl)]³ r⁸/8.
6. From Q = πP′r⁴/(8ηl): P′/(ηl) = 8Q/(πr⁴), so [P′/(4ηl)]³ = 8Q³/(π³r¹²).
7. KE flux = πρ · 8Q³/(π³r¹²) · r⁸/8 = ρQ³/(π²r⁴).
8. Energy balance: P Q = P′Q + ρQ³/(π²r⁴), hence

$$ P′ = P − ρ Q² / (π² r⁴) $$

Mathur adds that the constant is "very nearly 1" for real apparatus, writing P′ = P − kρQ²/(π²r⁴), and that Hagenbach, Couette and Wilberforce (among others) found the simple form gives very good results. With both corrections the viscosity becomes

$$ η = π (P − b) r⁴ / [8 Q (l + 1.64 r)],   b = kρQ²/(π²r⁴),  k ≈ 1 $$

**Reading the result (our analysis).** ρQ²/(π²r⁴) = ρ v̄², where v̄ = Q/(πr²). The kinetic energy carried out per unit volume discharged (KE flux / Q) is ½ρv̄² for a flat profile and ρv̄² for the parabola, exactly twice as much (the algebra above shows it). Both corrections are independent of tube length, while P ∝ l at fixed Q: **a long, thin tube makes both negligible**, which is why Poiseuille's method uses one.

**How big?** Mathur's example 15.21 (water, r = 0.52 mm, l = 0.602 m, head 0.4 m, 40 cm³ in 5 min): b = 24.6 Pa against P = 3920 Pa (0.63%), and 1.64 r/l = 0.14%. Together they move η from 1.402 to 1.391 × 10⁻³ Pa s (−0.8%). Contrast the short wide water tube of PH-023 (a = 1 mm, L = 0.5 m, ΔP = 2000 Pa): ρv̄² = 250 Pa is 12.5% of ΔP; solving P′ = P − ρQ²/(π²a⁴) self-consistently gives Q = 1.412 × 10⁻⁶ instead of 1.571 × 10⁻⁶ m³ s⁻¹, **10.1% lower**. Mathur's correction is the simplified model, so treat this as an estimate.

**Limits and traps.** When ρ → 0 (or Q → 0) b → 0 and the ideal formula returns. Do not subtract b from ΔP when only the ratio Q ∝ ΔP is being used in a scaling question such as the 2026 one; scaling questions assume the ideal law. Do not add 1.64r to the radius: it is added to the length.

### 6. Core: measuring viscosity with a capillary (method, relation, errors)

*Objective:* Describe the constant-head capillary method, obtain η from measured Q, and rank the error sources using the fourth-power sensitivity; derive the draining-vessel variant.

**Method (Mathur, PDF p. 806).** Keep a constant liquid head h above a horizontal capillary of radius r and length l (an overflow tube holds the level). Collect the liquid that trickles out in time t in a weighed beaker. Then Q = (mass/ρ)/t, the driving pressure is P = hρg, and

$$ η = π P r⁴ / (8 Q l) $$

Mathur says this simple method suits only liquids of low viscosity such as water.

**Worked example (Mathur 15.21, recomputed).** 40 cm³ in 5 min: Q = 40 × 10⁻⁶/300 = 1.333 × 10⁻⁷ m³ s⁻¹; P = 0.4 × 1000 × 9.8 = 3920 Pa; r = 0.52 mm, l = 0.602 m. Then η = π × 3920 × (0.52 × 10⁻³)⁴/(8 × 1.333 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.602) = **1.402 × 10⁻³ Pa s** (Mathur prints 1.4 × 10⁻³). Check: Re = ρ v̄ D/η = 116, laminar.

**Error propagation.** Take logarithms of η = πPr⁴/(8Ql):

$$ δη/η = δP/P + 4 δr/r + δQ/Q + δl/l   (worst case) $$

With assumed uncertainties r 1%, Q 1%, head 0.5%, l 0.2%: worst case 4 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.2 = **5.7%**; adding in quadrature (independent errors) √(16 + 1 + 0.25 + 0.04) = **4.2%**. Radius supplies 4 of the 5.7 points. To hold η to 1%, r must be known to 0.25%. This is why the bore, not the length, decides the quality of a capillary viscometer. (The usual way of measuring the bore, for example with a mercury thread, is standard practice not given in the extract; verify.)

**Other error sources.**

- *Entrance and kinetic-energy effects:* use P′ and l + 1.64 r (module 5); small for long thin tubes.
- *Turbulence:* check Re (module 7).
- *Temperature:* NCERT says liquid viscosity falls as temperature rises (water 1.0 mPa s at 20 °C, 0.3 at 100 °C), so hold the temperature steady and record it.
- *Head drift, tilt, bubbles, partial blockage:* each alters P or r⁴.
- *Gases:* Poiseuille's liquid formula is not directly usable, because a gas is compressible, so only the mass flow is constant, not Q. Mathur treats this separately (Meyer's formula, his section 15.20); that formula is not covered here.

**Draining-vessel variant (derivation).** A vessel of cross-section A empties through a horizontal capillary; h is the height of liquid above the capillary (measured from its axis). Volume leaves at Q = −A dh/dt, and Q = πρg h a⁴/(8ηl) (pressure ρgh). Hence

$$ dh/dt = −h/τ,   τ = 8ηlA/(πρ g a⁴) $$

so h = h₀ e^(−t/τ) and the time to fall from h₁ to h₂ is t = τ ln(h₁/h₂) (natural logarithm). Mathur's example 15.13 evaluates this with 2.3026 × log₁₀, which equals ln, so the logarithm is not a slip. His printed time constant (15.20, and the working of 15.13) omits ρ: Mathur omits ρ because water in CGS has ρ = 1; keep it for other liquids or SI. For half the height (the liquid volume above the capillary level halves) t = τ ln 2. Doubling the capillary radius shortens every time by 16.

**Numbers.** A = 20 cm², l = 10 cm, a = 0.50 mm, η = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s: τ = 831.5 s and t(½) = **576.4 s = 9 min 36 s**, matching the answer printed with Mathur's exercise 40. Mathur example 15.13 (vessel radius 7 cm, capillary 10 cm long, 0.2 mm radius, η = 0.01 CGS, 50 to 25 cm) gives about **48.14 h** (1.7329 × 10⁵ s, g = 980) by our recomputation (Mathur prints 1.734 × 10⁵ s = 48.16 h; the difference is arithmetic rounding, since π cancels when A = π × 7²).

### 7. Master: when Poiseuille's formula fails (Reynolds limit and others)

*Objective:* Test a given flow against the Reynolds limit, reconcile Mathur's radius-based k with the diameter-based Re of PH-023, and list the other assumptions that break.

**Reynolds recap (PH-023 module 7).** Re = ρ v̄ D/η with D the diameter; roughly Re below about 2000 is laminar in a straight pipe (standard figure, verify).

**Mathur's version (PDF p. 803).** Mathur writes the critical velocity as v_c = kη/(ρ r) with *r the radius* and k "usually taken to be 1000" for narrow tubes, and he derives the same form by matching dimensions (η^a ρ^b r^c gives a = 1, b = −1, c = −1). Because D = 2r, Re_c = ρ v_c (2r)/η = 2k = **2000**. The two statements agree; the **convention trap** is to use radius with the diameter threshold, an error of a factor 2 in Re. Mathur also notes that the more viscous and lighter the liquid and the narrower the tube, the more orderly the flow, and that a perfectly inviscid liquid has zero critical velocity.

**Laminar limit on flow rate.** In a tube v̄ = Q/(πa²), so Re = ρ(Q/πa²)(2a)/η = 2ρQ/(πaη). Setting Re = 2000: Q_max = Re_c π a η/(2ρ) ∝ a, while Poiseuille's Q ∝ a⁴. For water (η = 1.0 × 10⁻³, ρ = 1000) in a = 1.0 mm, Q_max = **3.14 × 10⁻⁶ m³ s⁻¹**: the PH-023 example (1.571 × 10⁻⁶) is at half the limit. Wide pipes become turbulent at modest pressure differences, so Poiseuille's law is a narrow-tube law.

**Checked examples.** The 2021 alcohol capillary (module 8): Re = 272. Mathur's water viscometer: 116. The illustrative artery of module 2: 250. All laminar.

**Other assumptions that break.**

1. *Entrance length.* Flow takes some distance to become parabolic. A common estimate is L_e ≈ 0.06 Re D (standard, **verify**); for Re = 272 and D = 1 mm this is 16 mm, small against a 200 mm tube, consistent with Mathur's small 1.64 r correction.
2. *Compressible fluids.* For gases only the mass flow is constant (Mathur §15.20); Poiseuille's volume formula is not valid as it stands.
3. *Elastic walls, pulsation, non-Newtonian liquids* (blood in real arteries): the formula becomes an approximation (standard knowledge, verify).
4. *Non-circular cross-sections.* The circular-tube result does not apply. For an ellipse of semi-axes A and B, textbooks give Q = πA³B³ΔP/[4ηL(A² + B²)] (**standard formula, not in the sources, verify before use**). Limit check: A = B = a gives πa⁶ΔP/(8ηLa²) = πΔPa⁴/(8ηL) ✓, so it reduces to Poiseuille.
5. *Slip.* The no-slip condition fails for very narrow channels or rarefied gases (standard; flagged).

**Decision routine for any tube problem.** (i) Compute Q from Poiseuille, (ii) compute v̄ and Re, (iii) if Re exceeds about 2000 the laminar result is not reliable (2000 to 4000 is transitional), (iv) if accuracy matters, estimate b = ρv̄² against ΔP.

**Traps.** Using a radius-based threshold of 2000; trusting Poiseuille on a wide pipe because ΔP is small; using the formula for air.

### Notes: Definitions and symbols

- Hydraulic (viscous) resistance R_h = ΔP/Q = 8ηL/(πa⁴), unit Pa s m⁻³; Mathur calls it the effective viscous resistance.
- Series: same Q, pressure drops add, R = R₁ + R₂. Parallel: same ΔP, flows add, 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.
- Viscous dissipation rate in the tube W = ΔP Q = R_h Q² = ΔP²/R_h (watts).
- Effective pressure P′ = P − kρQ²/(π²r⁴) (Mathur, k about 1): the part of P that overcomes viscosity.
- Effective length l + 1.64 r (Mathur's end correction).
- Symbol map: this lesson a, r, L, ΔP versus Mathur r, x, l, P.

### Notes: Key equations

- Q = πΔP a⁴/(8ηL); v_max = ΔP a²/(4ηL); v_mean = v_max/2 (PH-023 modules 4 and 5).
- Series: Q = (πP/8η)(Σ L_i/a_i⁴)⁻¹, p_i = P R_i/ΣR. Parallel: Q = (πP/8η)Σ a_i⁴/L_i, Q_i/Q = (a_i⁴/L_i)/Σ(a_j⁴/L_j).
- Sensitivity: δQ/Q ≈ δΔP/ΔP + 4δa/a − δη/η − δL/L (small changes only).
- Viscometer: η = πPr⁴/(8Ql), P = hρg; corrected η = π(P − b)r⁴/[8Q(l + 1.64r)].
- Draining: dh/dt = −h/τ, τ = 8ηlA/(πρga⁴), t = τ ln(h₁/h₂).
- Laminar limit: Re = 2ρQ/(πaη) < about 2000, so Q_max = 2000πaη/(2ρ).

### Notes: Series versus parallel

- Use the electrical twin, but remember R_h ∝ 1/a⁴, not 1/a².

### Notes: Power and dissipation in six lines

- Power of the pressure force: W = ΔP πa² v̄ = ΔP Q.
- Uniform tube, steady: kinetic-energy flux in = out, so W is converted to heat.
- Local rate per unit volume: η(dv/dr)², with dv/dr = −ΔP r/(2ηL), equal to ΔP² r²/(4ηL²).
- Integrate over shells 2πrL dr from 0 to a: [πΔP²/(2ηL)] a⁴/4.
- Result πΔP²a⁴/(8ηL) = ΔP Q: local and global accounts agree.
- Fraction of dissipation inside radius r is (r/a)⁴; half is outside 0.841 a.

### Notes: Mathur's sourced facts on the real capillary

- Mathur lists three Poiseuille assumptions: steady streamline flow parallel to the axis, uniform pressure over a cross-section, stationary liquid at the wall.
- The constant-head experiment suits low-viscosity liquids such as water; P = hρg and η = πPr⁴/(8Ql).
- End correction: effective length l + a′ with a′ almost always 1.64 r, independent of l (empirical; no derivation in the source).
- Kinetic-energy correction: P′ = P − kρQ²/(π²r⁴), k very nearly 1; Hagenbach, Couette and Wilberforce found it gives very good results.
- Critical velocity v_c = kη/(ρr), k about 1000 for narrow tubes (radius-based); Re_c = 2k = 2000 on diameter.
- Poiseuille's liquid formula cannot be applied directly to a gas: only the mass flow is constant; Meyer's formula is Mathur's separate treatment.

### Notes: Where the formula fails

- Turbulent flow (Re above about 2000 on diameter): the pressure drop rises faster than linearly with Q.
- Entrance region: not yet parabolic; estimate L_e ≈ 0.06 Re D (standard, verify).
- Gases and other compressible flow: volume rate not constant along the tube.
- Elastic, pulsatile, non-Newtonian flow (real blood vessels): model only.
- Non-circular tubes: a different formula (elliptical form in module 7, verify).
- Scaling problems such as the 2026 question assume the ideal law and ignore the corrections.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Series versus parallel: flows equal versus pressure differences equal.
- Radius versus diameter in a⁴ and in Re: factors 16 and 2.
- Mathur's radius-based k = 1000 versus diameter-based Re = 2000.
- ΔP Q (power) versus ρv̄² (kinetic energy carried out per unit volume discharged, parabolic profile): in a uniform tube the power becomes heat, not speed.
- Pressure difference ΔP (drives the flow) versus absolute or 'blood' pressure.
- Poise (CGS, 0.1 Pa s) in Mathur's older examples versus Pa s.

    - **task:** Show that the power to drive Poiseuille flow equals ΔP Q and equals the viscous dissipation. Practice target, not an official allotment: 10 marks about 12 minutes.
