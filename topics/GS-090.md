## GS-090: Bhakti and Sufi literature

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Bhakti and Sufi literature and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Tamil and Kannada beginnings

Some of the earliest bhakti movements, around the sixth century, were led by the Alvars, devotees of Vishnu, and Nayanars, devotees of Shiva, who travelled singing Tamil hymns and identified shrines as sacred. Some historians see protest against caste in their poems, and the Divyaprabandham's 'Tamil Veda' label placed Tamil devotion on a par with Sanskrit scripture. Women's voices stand out: Andal's love for Vishnu and Karaikkal Ammaiyar's ascetic verses defying ideals of feminine beauty. Chola rulers later supported these traditions and their compilation. In twelfth-century Karnataka, Basavanna's Virashaivas questioned ritual, caste and the idea of rebirth, expressing their critique in short Kannada vachanas.

### Sufi poetry in local tongues

Sufis organised around khanqahs and silsilas; in India the Chishtis became the most influential because they adapted to local practice. Their devotion centred on ziyarat to tombs and on sama, mystical music. Those around the Chishtis in Delhi spoke Hindavi; Baba Farid's local-language verses entered the Guru Granth Sahib. Masnavis such as Jayasi's Padmavat used human love to speak of divine love. Around Bijapur, Dakhani poems were probably sung by women while grinding grain or spinning, along with lullabies (lurinama) and wedding songs (shadinama), possibly inspired by Kannada vachanas and Marathi abhangs. Amir Khusrau's qaul gave Chishti sama a lasting form.

### Kabir, Guru Nanak and Mirabai

Kabir, of the fourteenth–fifteenth centuries, spoke of the Ultimate Reality using Islamic, Vedantic and yogic terms, and wrote both in sant bhasha and in ulatbansi, upside-down sayings that resist literal reading. Baba Guru Nanak (1469–1539) taught a nirguna bhakti of remembering the Divine Name through Punjabi shabads sung with Mardana on the rabab, and organised congregational worship. Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth; Guru Gobind Singh added Guru Tegh Bahadur's compositions, forming the Guru Granth Sahib. Mirabai, a Rajput princess from Merta married into Mewar's Sisodia family, recognised Krishna as her lover; her bhajans, transmitted orally for centuries, are the main basis of her biography.
