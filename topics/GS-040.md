### Bridge: Electorates: general, separate and weightage

An **electorate** is the group of people who are allowed to vote for a seat. In a **general (joint) electorate** all voters of an area vote together for any candidate. In a **separate electorate** the voters are listed by community, and members of a community vote only for candidates of their own community. A **reserved seat** is a seat set aside for a group.

A **proportion of seats** is the share of the seats given to a group. **Weightage** is a bonus: a group in a minority in some region gets more seats than its share of the population would give, so as to protect its voice. Spectrum names 'a fixed proportion of seats' for Muslims in the Pact; the standard figures (one-third and weightage) are outside the extract and are flagged verify.

Why does this matter? Supporters of separate electorates said that minorities would not otherwise get a voice; critics said that separating voters also separated politics. Spectrum records both views of the Pact (module 7), and GS-038 module 8 has the origin of the demand in 1906 and 1909.

### Bridge: Reading months and years across 1914 to 1920

This topic has several events inside one year, so you need months as well as years. The practical method is a **ladder**: write the year, then the months in order. For 1917 the ladder is June (arrest), August (declaration), September (release). For 1916 it is April (Tilak's league), September (Besant's league), December (Lucknow).

A **session** of the Congress is its annual meeting, held usually in December at a different city each year, with a **president** who presides. A **league** is a standing body that works through the year. Mixing these two is a common error: Home Rule Leagues did not wait for sessions.

When two books give different years for the same event, learn the one you can support and write the other with a 'verify' tag. In this lesson the Lucknow session is December 1916, and NCERT's 1915 reunion date is a slip.

### 1. Orientation: the story of 1914 to 1919 in one page

*Objective:* State in one breath what the Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow Pact were, when they happened and what the government said in reply.

## The story in one page

In 1914 Britain went to war in Europe and India was pulled in. Most of the Moderate and Extremist leaders supported the war effort, but both groups also hoped for self-government as a reward. Between 1915 and 1917 two leaders, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** and **Annie Besant**, started campaigns called **Home Rule Leagues**, modelled on the Irish demand for self-rule. They worked all year round, not only at the annual Congress session. In December 1916 the Congress met at **Lucknow**, took back the Extremists who had left in 1907, and signed a joint agreement with the Muslim League, called the **Lucknow Pact**. In 1917 the government arrested Besant, faced protests, and on 20 August 1917 the Secretary of State, Edwin Montagu, announced that the policy was gradual progress towards **responsible government**.

GS-038 explained the split at Surat (1907) and the Muslim League's separate-electorate demand, and GS-039 covered the revolutionary groups. GS-053 module 4 gives this period in the whole chronology; this lesson goes deeper and does not retell those earlier topics.

### 2. Foundation: words, the war and why the moment was right

*Objective:* Define the words used in this topic and explain why the First World War created the opportunity for Home Rule.

## Words you need

- **Home rule:** self-government, as in the demand of the Irish Home Rule League that Tilak and Besant took as model. The Leagues asked for self-government for India inside the British empire (Spectrum: 'within the British commonwealth').
- **Responsible government:** a government answerable to elected representatives, not only to authorities in London.
- **Separate electorate:** an arrangement in which voters of a community vote only for candidates of that community (bridge GS-040-B1).
- **Executive council:** the group of advisers who run the administration under the Viceroy or a governor, as distinct from the legislative council that debates and passes laws.
- **Reunion:** here, the return of the Extremists to the Congress after the 1907 split at Surat (GS-038 module 7).
- **Passive resistance:** a form of protest by refusing co-operation without violence; Tilak advocated it in 1917.

## How the three groups saw the war

Spectrum records a three-fold response: the Moderates supported the empire as a duty; the Extremists, including Tilak (released in June 1914), supported the war effort in the mistaken belief that Britain would repay loyalty with self-government; and the revolutionaries tried to use the war to strike at British rule (GS-039). Home Rule was the Indian response that Spectrum describes as less charged, but more effective, than the romantic Ghadr adventure of Indians living abroad.

## Why a campaign now

Spectrum lists six factors:

1. A section of nationalists felt popular pressure was needed to win concessions.
2. Moderates were disillusioned with the Morley-Minto reforms.
3. Wartime miseries, high taxation and rising prices made people ready to protest.
4. The war between imperialist powers, with their propaganda against each other, exposed the myth of white superiority.
5. Tilak was ready to lead after his release and had made conciliatory gestures.
6. Annie Besant, a theosophist of Irish descent who came to India in 1893 (Spectrum prints 'since 1896'; verify), wanted to build a home rule movement.

NCERT adds that the war raised defence expenditure and army recruitment and that returning soldiers had seen how imperialist powers treated Asian and African peoples. It also lists the 1917 Russian Revolution among the inspirations. The Russian Revolution is counted by Spectrum as a further advantage for the Home Rule campaign.

### 3. Core: the road to the Leagues, 1914 to 1916

*Objective:* Explain how Tilak and Besant prepared the ground, what the Congress decided in 1915 and where Gandhi stood.

## Tilak after prison

Tilak was released in June 1914. Spectrum says he made conciliatory gestures to the government, assuring it of his loyalty, and to the Moderates, saying that, like the Irish Home Rulers, he wanted a reform of the administration, not the overthrow of the government. He also admitted that acts of violence had only slowed political progress. He urged Indians to help the British in their hour of crisis. These gestures prepared the ground for the reunion (module 6).

## Besant and the campaign for self-government

Annie Besant, who came to India in 1893 (Spectrum prints 'since 1896'; verify), enlarged her activities. By early 1915 she was campaigning for self-government after the war, on the lines of the white colonies, through her newspapers **New India** and **Commonweal**, public meetings and conferences.

Both leaders understood a key point. For a movement to succeed, they needed the sanction of the Moderate-dominated Congress and the full co-operation of the Extremists. At the 1914 session they failed to reach a Moderate-Extremist understanding, so they decided to revive political activity on their own.

## The Congress of 1915

At the 1915 annual session Spectrum says Tilak's and Besant's efforts had some success. The Congress decided that the Extremists could be admitted to the Congress. Besant failed to get the Congress to approve her scheme of Home Rule Leagues, but the Congress did commit itself to a programme of educative propaganda and a revival of local-level Congress committees.

Besant, unwilling to wait for long, laid down a condition: if the Congress did not carry out its commitments she would be free to set up her own league. There was no response from the Congress, so she did.

**Source slip to keep straight.** NCERT (Class VIII, hess208) prints that the two groups 'reunited in December 1915'. Spectrum separates two steps: the Congress decided in 1915 to admit the Extremists, and the actual reunion took place at Lucknow in 1916 (module 6). GS-038 module 7 already flagged this; learn Lucknow, December 1916, as the date of the visible reunion.

## Where was Gandhi?

Gandhi returned to India in January 1915. Spectrum says he decided to tour the country for a year without taking any position on political matters, and he joined no political organisation unless it accepted non-violent satyagraha. Spectrum also says he did not favour the Home Rule agitation, since he thought it was the wrong time to press for Home Rule when Britain was in the middle of a war. NCERT (lehs303) records his first big public appearance at the opening of the Banaras Hindu University in February 1916, where Annie Besant was among the invitees, and his meeting with a peasant from Champaran at the Lucknow Congress in December 1916 (GS-041, Day 12, not yet written, will take up Champaran). So in 1915-16 Gandhi was visible but outside the Home Rule effort.

### 4. Core: Tilak's League and Besant's League compared

*Objective:* Describe the two Home Rule Leagues, their areas and programmes, and who joined and who stayed away.

## Why two leagues

Tilak and Besant set up separate leagues to avoid friction. Besant said that some of Tilak's supporters were not at ease with her and some of her own supporters were not at ease with Tilak. They coordinated by confining their work to their specific areas and co-operated where they could.

## The two leagues side by side

| Point | Tilak's league | Besant's league |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Indian Home Rule League | All India Home Rule League |
| Started | April 1916 | September 1916 |
| First meeting or base | First meeting at Belgaum; headquarters Poona | Madras |
| Area | Maharashtra (excluding Bombay city), Karnataka, Central Provinces and Berar | The rest of India, including Bombay city |
| Size | Six branches | 200 branches, loosely organised |
| Key people | Tilak | George Arundale (organising secretary), B. P. Wadia, C. P. Ramaswamy Aiyar |
| Demands | Swarajya, linguistic states, education in the vernacular | Self-government for India |

**Which league covered Bombay city?** Besant's. This is a good examination trap, since Tilak was a Bombay Presidency leader.

## The programme

The aim was to bring home rule to the common man as a message of self-government. The leagues used political education and discussion: public meetings, libraries and reading rooms with books on national politics, conferences, classes for students on politics, propaganda by newspapers, pamphlets, posters, illustrated postcards, plays and religious songs, fund collection, social work and participation in local government. Spectrum notes this reached a wider public than earlier campaigns and drew in the 'politically backward' regions of Gujarat and Sindh.

## Who joined

Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, Bhulabhai Desai, Chittaranjan Das, K. M. Munshi, B. Chakravarti, Saifuddin Kitchlew, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Tej Bahadur Sapru and Lala Lajpat Rai are all named by Spectrum. Some became heads of branches of Besant's league, and **Jinnah led the Bombay division**. Many Moderate Congressmen disillusioned with Congress inactivity and some members of Gokhale's Servants of India Society also joined.

## Who stayed away

Spectrum says Anglo-Indians, most Muslims and non-Brahmins from the South stayed away, as they felt Home Rule would mean rule of the Hindu majority and mainly of the high castes. This is the first sign of a limit that module 9 returns to. Note the contrast: Jinnah, a Muslim leader, led the Bombay branch, but most Muslims did not join; this is a general pattern, not an absolute rule.

## Gandhi and the leagues

Gandhi stayed out in 1915-16 (module 3). The league he joined later is the same All India Home Rule League, of which he accepted the presidentship in 1920 (module 8).

### 5. Core: repression, Besant's arrest and the Montagu declaration

*Objective:* Sequence the arrest of Besant, the protests, the declaration of 20 August 1917 and the release, and explain what the declaration meant.

## Government response

The government came down with severe repression, especially in Madras, where students were prohibited from attending political meetings. A case was started against Tilak, but the High Court rescinded it. Tilak was barred from entering Punjab and Delhi.

## The arrest of Besant, June 1917

In June 1917 Annie Besant and her associates B. P. Wadia and George Arundale were arrested. This led to a nationwide protest. In a dramatic gesture Sir S. Subramania Aiyar renounced his knighthood, and Tilak advocated passive resistance. Spectrum says the repression only hardened the agitators' attitude. It quotes Montagu's remark that the Government of India, by interning Besant, was like Shiva, who cut his wife into fifty-two pieces only to find that he had fifty-two wives. Spectrum also notes that many Moderates joined after the arrest. Besant was released in September 1917.

## The August Declaration

On **20 August 1917**, in the House of Commons, the Secretary of State for India, **Edwin Samuel Montagu**, announced the government's policy as 'an increasing participation of Indians in every branch of administration and gradual development of self-governing institutions with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire'.

## What the words meant

- **Self-government became a policy.** Demand for self-government could not now be called seditious, unlike Morley's 1909 statement that the reforms were not meant to give self-government.
- **'Responsible government' implied** that rulers would answer to the elected representatives, not only to London.
- **But it was not independence or even dominion status.** Spectrum says the British had no intention of handing power to legislatures with an Indian majority. To make the executive responsible in some degree, the idea of **dyarchy** would be developed (dyarchy was introduced in the provinces by the 1919 Act; GS-050, Day 14, not yet written, covers it).

## Indian objections

1. No time frame was given.
2. The government alone would decide the nature and the timing of advance, so the British would decide what was good for Indians.

## Putting the three events in order

| Month | Event | Cause or effect |
|---|---|---|
| June 1917 | Besant, Wadia and Arundale arrested | Government attempt to crush the agitation |
| 20 August 1917 | Montagu's declaration | Concession, partly under Home Rule and Lucknow pressure |
| September 1917 | Besant released | Calming gesture |

Spectrum lists the August declaration among the positive gains of the Home Rule movement, since the declaration and the Montford reforms were influenced by the agitation. Note that the declaration came while Besant was still interned, so it was not a reward for her release; the sequence shows pressure first, concession second, release third.

### 6. Core: Lucknow 1916, the Moderate-Extremist reunion

*Objective:* Explain why the Congress took back the Extremists at Lucknow in December 1916 and who presided.

## The session

The Lucknow session of the Congress in December 1916 was presided over by a Moderate, **Ambika Charan Majumdar**. It finally readmitted the Extremists led by Tilak to the Congress. Remember the earlier step: the 1915 session had agreed to admit them (module 3), and the formal reunion was visible at Lucknow.

## Why the reunion happened

Spectrum gives four factors:

1. **Old controversies had become meaningless.** The Surat disputes of 1907 over venue and president (GS-038 module 7) had lost relevance.
2. **Both sides realised the split had led to political inactivity.**
3. **Besant and Tilak made vigorous efforts.** To allay Moderate suspicion, Tilak declared that he supported administrative reform and not overthrow of the government, and he denounced violence.
4. **Pherozeshah Mehta's death.** Mehta had led the Moderate opposition to the Extremists, so his death removed an obstacle. (Mehta died in 1915; the year is standard knowledge, verify.)

Spectrum also quotes A. C. Majumdar, who said that after nearly ten years of painful separation both wings of the nationalist party had realised that united they stand, divided they fall.

## What the reunion achieved

Spectrum says the Moderate-Extremist reunion revived the Congress as an effective instrument of Indian nationalism. The Home Rule effort of Tilak and Besant helped to bring it about. Tilak's followers now returned to the Congress, which meant that the Congress could draw on the energy of the Extremist wing, as well as the experience of the Moderates.

## Connect to the earlier split

| Event | Date | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Surat | December 1907 | Split between Moderates and Extremists (GS-038) |
| Congress session | 1915 | Decision to admit Extremists |
| Lucknow | December 1916 | Extremists readmitted, under the presidency of a Moderate |

NCERT Class VIII's phrase 'reunited in December 1915' is a source slip in this respect: Spectrum places the readmission at Lucknow in 1916, and NCERT Class XII dates the Lucknow Congress to December 1916.

## Why the reunion matters for the next lesson

The same session saw the first joint demands of the Congress and the Muslim League (module 7). The reunion made it possible for the Congress to speak with one voice, and the League's new leaders were ready to join it.

### 7. Core: the Lucknow Pact, reasons and terms

*Objective:* Explain why the Muslim League moved closer to the Congress, state the terms of the Pact and apply Spectrum's critical comments.

## Why the League's attitude changed

Spectrum says the League, now dominated by younger militant nationalists, was coming closer to the Congress and turning anti-imperialist. It gives five reasons:

1. **Britain's refusal to help Turkey**, whose ruler the Khalifa claimed religio-political leadership of Muslims, in its wars (the Balkans 1912-13 and Italy 1911-12), angered Muslims.
2. **The annulment of the partition of Bengal in 1911** annoyed sections that had supported it (GS-038 module 9).
3. **The refusal to set up a university at Aligarh** with power to affiliate colleges all over India alienated some.
4. **A shift in the League's goal.** The younger members were moving beyond the Aligarh school. The League's Calcutta session of 1912 committed it to work with other groups for a system of self-government suited to India, if consistent with protection of Indian Muslims' interests.
5. **War-time repression.** Maulana Azad's *Al Hilal* and Mohammad Ali's *Comrade* were suppressed and the Ali brothers, Azad and Hasrat Mohani were interned, creating anti-imperialist feeling.

## The terms

| Party | What it accepted |
|---|---|
| Muslim League | To present joint constitutional demands with the Congress |
| Congress | The League's position on separate electorates, to continue until any one community demanded joint electorates |
| Both | A fixed proportion of seats for Muslims in legislatures at the all-India and provincial levels |

**Joint demands (Spectrum):** the government should declare it would confer self-government on Indians at an early date; representative assemblies at the centre and the provinces should be expanded, with an elected majority and more powers; the term of the legislative council should be five years; the Secretary of State's salary should be paid by the British treasury, not Indian funds; and half the members of the Viceroy's and provincial governors' executive councils should be Indians.

**Facts outside the extract (verify):** standard books say that Muslims were given one-third of the elected Indian seats in the Imperial Legislative Council and that provinces where Muslims were a minority received **weightage** (more seats than population alone would give). Spectrum states only a 'fixed proportion'. NCERT says Jinnah played an important role in the making of the Lucknow Pact; he was then a member of both the Congress and the League (standard, verify).

## Critical comments in Spectrum

- Half the executive was to be elected by the legislature, but the executive as a whole would not be responsible to it. The legislature could not remove the elected half, yet budgets depended on its approval, so deadlock was likely.
- The demands were a significantly expanded version of the Morley-Minto reforms.
- Accepting separate electorates meant Congress and League came together as separate political entities, which Spectrum calls a major landmark in the evolution of the two-nation theory.
- Leaders came together but no effort was made to bring the masses of the two communities together.
- But accepting separate electorates also showed a serious desire to allay minority fears of majority domination, and it generated much enthusiasm.

## A trap

The Pact does not introduce dyarchy or the Montford reforms; those came after the August 1917 statement (module 8). The Pact was a set of demands, not a law.

### 8. Core: why Home Rule faded, and the Swarajya Sabha of 1920

*Objective:* List the reasons for decline, link them to the 1918 and 1920 events, and answer the verified 2018 Prelims question.

## Seven reasons for decline by 1919

Spectrum says the agitation was short-lived and had petered out by 1919. Its reasons:

1. Lack of effective organisation.
2. Communal riots in 1917-18.
3. The Moderates who joined after Besant's arrest were pacified by Montagu's statement of August 1917 and by her release.
4. The talk of passive resistance by the Extremists kept Moderates away from September 1918.
5. The **Montagu-Chelmsford reforms**, announced in July 1918, divided nationalist ranks; Besant herself vacillated over the reforms and over passive resistance.
6. Tilak went to England in September 1918 for a libel case against Valentine Chirol, who had described Tilak as responsible for agitational politics in his book *Indian Unrest*; with Besant unable to give a lead and Tilak abroad, the movement was leaderless.
7. Gandhi's new approach was gathering momentum, and the mass movement pushed Home Rule aside.

## From Montagu to Montford

In line with the August 1917 statement, the government announced reforms in July 1918, known as the Montagu-Chelmsford or Montford reforms, and the Government of India Act, 1919 followed. It introduced dyarchy in the provinces (Spectrum). GS-050 (Day 14, not yet written) will cover the Act's details.

## The 1920 change of name

Spectrum adds in a bracket that in 1920 Gandhi accepted the presidentship of the All India Home Rule League and changed its name to **Swarajya Sabha**; within a year the League joined the Indian National Congress. This tells us how the handover of energy worked: the League's structure passed to Gandhi's leadership and then merged into the Congress.

## The verified 2018 question

**Prelims GS I 2018, Q79:** 'In 1920, which of the following changed its name to Swarajya Sabha?' The options were the All India Home Rule League, the Hindu Mahasabha, the South Indian Liberal Federation and the Servants of India Society.

Analysis, not an official key: Spectrum names the All India Home Rule League as the body that changed its name to Swarajya Sabha under Gandhi in 1920, so the first option agrees with the extract. The other three can be set aside by their identities: the Hindu Mahasabha was a Hindu organisation (formed in the 1910s; verify), the South Indian Liberal Federation (the 'Justice' movement) represented non-Brahmin interests in the south (standard knowledge, verify), and the Servants of India Society was Gokhale's association, whose members Spectrum says joined the Home Rule campaign.

## Linking the pieces

The decline of the Leagues did not mean the work was wasted. Spectrum lists positive gains (module 9), and the leagues' organisation passed into the Gandhian phase.

### 9. Master: assessment, gains, limits and the debates

*Objective:* Weigh the achievements and limitations of the Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow Pact and prepare balanced answers.

## Gains of the Home Rule movement (Spectrum)

1. It shifted the emphasis from the educated elite to the masses and deflected the movement permanently from the Moderate course.
2. It created an organisational link between town and country, which proved important when the movement entered its mass phase.
3. It created a generation of ardent nationalists.
4. It prepared the masses for Gandhian-style politics.
5. The August 1917 declaration and the Montford reforms were influenced by the agitation.
6. The efforts of Tilak and Besant helped the reunion at Lucknow.
7. It gave a new dimension and urgency to the national movement.

Spectrum sums it up as a transition from the deliberative and rather inactive Congress to the Gandhian phase.

## Limits

- **Narrow social base.** The Leagues named Gujarat and Sindh among their successes, but Anglo-Indians, most Muslims and non-Brahmins of the South stayed away, fearing a Hindu-majority and high-caste rule (module 4).
- **Weak organisation.** Besant's league was loosely organised, and Spectrum puts lack of effective organisation first among the reasons for decline.
- **Dependence on two leaders.** When one was interned or abroad, the movement stalled (module 8).
- **Quick concession.** The government's concession in August 1917 pacified Moderates, who had joined after the arrest.

## Evaluating the Lucknow Pact

A balanced answer states both Spectrum views:

| In favour | Against |
|---|---|
| A united front of the Congress and the League, with young League leaders turning anti-imperial | Separate electorates accepted by Congress: separate political entities |
| A serious effort to reassure minorities about majority domination | Only leaders came together; masses were not brought together |
| Enthusiasm among the people; the government responded with the August declaration | The scheme of executive and legislature risked deadlock |
| Joint demands moved beyond Morley-Minto (interpretation, not a Spectrum view) | Spectrum: an expanded Morley-Minto scheme, not a new principle |

Spectrum notes the government itself reacted by promising self-government in time, as in the 1917 statement. The conclusion for answers is that the Pact was a tactical success for unity and a strategic risk for the long-term place of separate electorates in Indian politics; GS-050 (Day 14) and GS-051 (Day 15), not yet written, follow this thread.

## Chronology reminders for GS-053

Use the anchor sequence: Surat 1907, Home Rule leagues 1916, Lucknow 1916, August Declaration 1917, Montford 1918-19, Swarajya Sabha 1920. GS-053 module 4 has the same frame.

## Debates and cautions

- Was Home Rule 'a response to the war' or an extension of Extremist politics? Spectrum calls it the response to the war in a less charged but more effective form than Ghadr, and a manifestation of 'aggressive politics'; the two descriptions are compatible.
- Did it fail? It faded organisationally by 1919, but its gains were passed into later movements (module 8).


### 10. Exam: answer frames, traps and unverified facts

*Objective:* Plan answers for typical Prelims and Mains questions on this topic and know which facts need outside checking.

## Prelims patterns

- Who started which league, where and when.
- The areas and branch counts of the two leagues.
- The terms of the Lucknow Pact and which party accepted what.
- The sequence of Besant's arrest, the August Declaration and the release.

## Mains frames

**Home Rule Leagues (15 marks).** Introduction (war context and Irish model); the two leagues and their areas; methods; response of the government (arrest of Besant); gains (masses, town-country link, influence on Montagu); limits (narrow base, weak organisation, decline by 1919); conclusion linking to Gandhi's phase.

**Lucknow Pact (10 marks).** Context (the reunion and the League's shift); terms; significance (united front, minority reassurance, enthusiasm); limits (separate electorates, leader-level unity, executive-legislature deadlock); judgement.

**August Declaration (10 marks).** The words; the change from Morley's 1909 stance; its limits (no time frame, government decides); link to dyarchy and Montford reforms.

## Traps

- **Wrong league area.** Besant's league covered Bombay city; Tilak's excluded it.
- **Date slips.** NCERT's 'December 1915' reunion; the correct date for Lucknow is December 1916. GS-038 flagged the same slip.
- **Arrest and declaration.** Besant was arrested in June 1917, the declaration came on 20 August 1917, and she was released in September 1917.
- **'Pact introduced dyarchy'.** False; dyarchy came through the 1919 Act.
- **'Gandhi founded the Leagues'.** False; he took the presidentship of the League in 1920.
- **'One-half of the Viceroy's council'.** Spectrum says half of the members of the executive councils of the Viceroy and governors would be Indians, and its critique says half the executive would be elected by the legislature. Learn 'half Indians' and note the slight difference in wording.

### Notes: Lucknow Pact: terms and joint demands

- Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims, to continue until any one community demanded joint electorates.
- Muslims received a fixed proportion of seats in all-India and provincial legislatures (Spectrum); the one-third and weightage figures are standard knowledge to verify.
- Joint demand: early declaration of self-government.
- Joint demand: expanded assemblies with an elected majority and more powers.
- Joint demand: five-year term for the legislative council.
- Joint demand: the Secretary of State's salary to be paid by the British treasury.
- Joint demand: half of the executive councils of the Viceroy and governors to be Indians.

### Notes: Reasons for the League's move towards the Congress

- Britain's refusal to help Turkey angered Muslims.
- Annulment of the partition of Bengal in 1911 annoyed supporters.
- Refusal of an affiliating university at Aligarh.
- Younger members outgrew the Aligarh school; Calcutta session of 1912 accepted work for self-government.
- Wartime repression and the interning of the Ali brothers, Azad and Hasrat Mohani.

### Notes: Reasons for the reunion at Lucknow

- Old controversies lost meaning.
- Both sides saw the split had caused inaction.
- Tilak and Besant's efforts; Tilak's reassurance about non-violence.
- Death of Pherozeshah Mehta.

### Notes: Montagu's August Declaration

- Made on 20 August 1917 in the House of Commons by Edwin Montagu.
- Aim: progressive realisation of responsible government as an integral part of the British Empire.
- Self-government became a policy and agitation for it could not be called seditious.
- Objections: no time frame; the British would decide timing and nature.
- Led to the Montford reforms and dyarchy (Government of India Act, 1919).

### Notes: Reasons for the decline of Home Rule

- Lack of effective organisation.
- Communal riots of 1917-18.
- Moderates pacified by the August Declaration and Besant's release.
- Passive resistance talk kept Moderates away from September 1918.
- Montford reforms divided the nationalists.
- Tilak abroad for the Chirol case; Besant unable to lead.
- Gandhi's mass approach.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Tilak's league (Poona, Maharashtra without Bombay city) versus Besant's league (Madras, rest of India with Bombay city).
- Surat 1907 (split) versus Lucknow 1916 (reunion).
- Lucknow Pact (joint demands) versus Montford reforms (government measure).
- Montagu's declaration (August 1917) versus Montagu-Chelmsford reforms (July 1918).
- Home Rule League versus Swarajya Sabha (the League's name from 1920).

- 1917: Besant arrested June, Montagu's declaration 20 August (responsible government, no time frame), Besant released September; Montford reforms July 1918, Act 1919.
- Faded by 1919 (riots, pacified Moderates, Tilak abroad for the Chirol case, Gandhi's mass approach); 1920 renamed Swarajya Sabha under Gandhi.
