### Bridge: What a Constituent Assembly is, and how it differs from a legislature

## Two bodies that sound alike

A **constitution** is the set of basic rules of a country: it says who makes decisions, how they are chosen and what they may never do (see GS-158, bridge 1). Somebody has to write those rules the first time. The body that does so is called a **Constituent Assembly**: a group of people brought together for one special job, which is to *frame* (draft and adopt) a constitution.

A **legislature** is different. Parliament, the Lok Sabha and a State legislative assembly are ordinary law-making bodies. They exist *because a constitution created them*, and they work *inside* the rules of that constitution. NCERT makes the same point: if Parliament has the authority to make laws, some higher document must have given it that authority in the first place. Parliament therefore cannot be the author of the constitution it lives under.

Think of a school. The students' council is created by the school's rulebook and passes everyday decisions. The group that first wrote the rulebook was a different, one-time committee.

## The Indian twist

India's Constituent Assembly was unusual after August 1947. The Indian Independence Act 1947 made it **both** a constituent body (writing the Constitution) and a legislative body (passing ordinary laws for the Dominion). The two functions were kept apart on separate days, with different presiding officers. You will meet this in module 4. Remember the clean idea now: *constituent = writes the rulebook; legislative = makes laws under a rulebook*.

### Bridge: Reading dates and building a chronology

## Why dates cause trouble here

This topic is full of dates that sit close together (9 and 13 December 1946; 26 November 1949 and 26 January 1950). Beginners often swap them. Two habits fix this.

**Habit 1: read dates as day, month, year, all in CE.** CE means Common Era, the ordinary calendar year count we use today (1946 means 1946 years after the starting point of the calendar). All dates in this lesson are CE. Month names are written out so you do not confuse 4/11/1948 (4 November) with an American-style 11 April.

**Habit 2: separate four different kinds of event.** Students lose marks by treating these as one:
- a **meeting or session** (the Assembly sat);
- a **reading** (a stage in which the draft is debated: first, second, third);
- **adoption** (the Assembly formally passes the final text);
- **enforcement / commencement** (the Constitution begins to operate as law).

## Your four anchor dates

Memorise these four first and hang everything else on them:

1. **9 December 1946**: first meeting.
2. **29 August 1947**: Drafting Committee set up.
3. **26 November 1949**: Constitution adopted.
4. **26 January 1950**: Constitution comes into force (most provisions).

Then place the smaller dates before, between or after these anchors.

### 1. Orientation: the storyline in one page

*Objective:* State in your own words who wrote the Constitution, when, in how many stages, and why the topic is split into demand, composition, working, drafting, adoption and criticism.

## The whole story in six lines

1. From 1934 to 1946 Indians first imagined, then demanded, and finally obtained the right to write their own constitution through an elected body.
2. In 1946 the **Cabinet Mission Plan** laid down how that body, the **Constituent Assembly**, would be formed: 389 seats, indirectly elected, partly nominated.
3. The Assembly met first on **9 December 1946**. Its guiding idea was the **Objectives Resolution** (moved 13 December 1946, adopted 22 January 1947).
4. After Partition and the **Indian Independence Act 1947**, the Assembly became sovereign, shrank to 299 members and also became the country's first Parliament.
5. A **Drafting Committee** under Dr. B. R. Ambedkar (set up 29 August 1947) produced the draft; the Assembly debated it in three readings.
6. The Constitution was **adopted on 26 November 1949** and came into force on **26 January 1950**.

## Why UPSC cares

Prelims asks short factual questions: who presided, which committee, which date, how many members. Mains (GS II) asks you to explain the making of the Constitution and to judge it: was the Assembly representative, was it sovereign, did it work well? Both types depend on the same skeleton. The rest of this lesson hangs details on it.

## What you do not need yet

Do not try to memorise the table of 70 princely-state seats or every minor committee on a first pass. You first need the skeleton above, the four anchor dates in bridge 2, and the names in bridge 3. The deep detail is in the later modules and the master notes.

### 2. The demand: from M. N. Roy to the Cabinet Mission

*Objective:* Place the steps of the demand (1934, 1935, 1938, 1940, 1942, 1946) in order and say what each step added.

## Why a demand was needed at all

Until 1947 India's rules were written by the British Parliament (the Acts you studied in GS-158). Indian leaders argued that a free India's constitution must be written by Indians. A Constituent Assembly (bridge 1) was the means.

## The steps, one at a time

- **1934: the idea.** M. N. Roy, a pioneer of the communist movement in India, put forward the idea of a Constituent Assembly for India for the first time.
- **1935: the official demand.** The Indian National Congress (INC) officially demanded a Constituent Assembly to frame India's Constitution for the first time.
- **1938: the principle of adult franchise.** Jawaharlal Nehru, on behalf of the INC, declared that the Constitution of free India must be framed, without outside interference, by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise. (*Adult franchise* means every adult citizen has the right to vote.)
- **1940: the August Offer.** The British Government accepted the demand **in principle**. Note the words "in principle": accepting an idea is not the same as setting it up.
- **1942: the Cripps proposals.** Sir Stafford Cripps, a Member of the British Cabinet, brought a draft proposal on framing an independent Constitution after World War II. The **Muslim League rejected** it because it wanted India divided into two autonomous states with two separate Constituent Assemblies.
- **1946: the Cabinet Mission.** A Cabinet Mission of three members (Lord Pethick-Lawrence, Sir Stafford Cripps, A. V. Alexander) reached India on 24 March 1946 and published its plan on 16 May 1946. It rejected the idea of two Constituent Assemblies but offered a scheme that more or less satisfied the Muslim League.

## A useful reading of the pattern

Notice who made each move: first an individual idea (Roy), then a party demand (INC), then a statement of principle about *how* (adult franchise, no outside interference), then British acceptance, then a failed attempt (Cripps) and finally a workable plan (Cabinet Mission). NCERT adds the deeper reason: Nehru saw the demand as a claim to full self-determination, a nation casting off the shell of its past political structure and fashioning its own.

## Two traps

- The Congress *formally* demanded in **1935**, not 1934. 1934 is Roy's idea.
- The 1938 statement asked for adult franchise, but the Assembly that actually met was **not** elected by adult franchise (module 3). The gap between the demand and the outcome is the root of a major criticism (module 7).

### 3. Composition and election of the Assembly

*Objective:* Reproduce the Cabinet Mission scheme (389 seats, how they were split and filled), the 1946 election result and the post-Partition strength of 299.

## The scheme

The Assembly was constituted in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. Its features, as Laxmikanth lists them:

1. **Total strength 389.** British India: **296**; princely states: **93**. Of the 296, **292** came from the eleven governors' provinces and **4** from the four Chief Commissioners' provinces, one each.
2. **Seats by population.** Each province and princely state (or group of small states) got seats in proportion to population, roughly **one seat per million**.
3. **Seats by community.** Seats in each British province were divided among three communities in proportion to population: **Muslims, Sikhs and General** (everyone else).
4. **Method.** Each community's representatives were elected by that community's members in the provincial legislative assembly, by **proportional representation with the single transferable vote**.
5. **Princely states.** Representatives were to be **nominated by the heads** of the states (Laxmikanth). NCERT words it more loosely: the method for the states was to be determined by consultation.

## What this made the Assembly

Partly elected and partly nominated, and **indirectly elected**: voters elected the provincial assemblies, and those assemblies elected the members. Those provincial assemblies themselves had been elected on a **limited franchise** under the Government of India Act 1935 (a franchise tied to tax, property and education). It is therefore wrong to say the Assembly was chosen by universal adult franchise.

## The 1946 election

Elections for the 296 British Indian seats took place in **July-August 1946**. Result: **Congress 208, Muslim League 73, others 15** (small groups and independents). The 93 princely-state seats were **not filled at first** because the states stayed away.

## After Partition

When members from areas that became Pakistan left, the Assembly's strength fell to **299**: **229** for the Indian provinces (down from 296) and **70** for the princely states (down from 93). NCERT adds that the Congress then held about **82 per cent** of the seats and that the Assembly included **28 members from the Scheduled Castes**. (Laxmikanth's community table for the original 296 shows 31 Scheduled Castes; the two figures refer to different moments, so quote the source you are using.)

## Representation without a vote of the people

Laxmikanth stresses that although it was not directly elected, the Assembly included representatives of nearly every section: Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Parsis, Anglo-Indians, Indian Christians, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, including women, and almost all leading figures with the exception of Mahatma Gandhi.

### 4. Working I: first meeting, Objectives Resolution, and the Independence Act

*Objective:* Describe the first meeting, state the Objectives Resolution's content and fate, and list the three changes made by the Independence Act 1947.

## The first meeting: 9 December 1946

The Assembly met for the first time on **9 December 1946**. The **Muslim League boycotted** it and insisted on a separate Pakistan, so only **211 members** attended. **Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha**, the oldest member, was elected temporary President, following the French practice of the eldest member presiding at the start. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected President later; H. C. Mukherjee and V. T. Krishnamachari became the two Vice-Presidents.

## The Objectives Resolution

On **13 December 1946**, Jawaharlal Nehru moved the historic **Objectives Resolution**. It laid down the fundamentals and philosophy of the constitutional structure. In summary:

- India is to be an **independent sovereign republic**;
- a **Union** of British Indian territories, Indian states and other willing territories;
- territories are **autonomous units** with residuary powers;
- all power flows **from the people**;
- guarantees of **justice** (social, economic, political), **equality** and **freedoms** of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and action, subject to law and public morality;
- **safeguards** for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed and other backward classes;
- the **integrity** of the Republic's territory and its sovereign rights;
- India's rightful place in the world and its contribution to **world peace**.

It was **unanimously adopted on 22 January 1947**. A modified version forms the **Preamble**. NCERT calls it the best summary of the principles the nationalist movement brought to the Assembly.

## Princely states join

The states that had stayed away gradually joined. On 28 April 1947, representatives of six states (Baroda, Bikaner, Jaipur, Patiala, Rewa, Udaipur) were present. After the Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 (Partition), most other states sent members, and Muslim League members from the Indian Dominion also entered.

## Three changes by the Indian Independence Act 1947

1. The Assembly became a **fully sovereign body** that could frame any Constitution it pleased and could abrogate or alter any British law relating to India.
2. It became a **legislative body** as well: the first Parliament of free India (Dominion Legislature). When it met to make the Constitution, Dr. Rajendra Prasad presided; when it met as the legislature, **G. V. Mavalankar** presided. The Assembly first met as the Dominion Legislature on 17 November 1947. The two functions continued until **26 November 1949**.
3. Members from areas included in Pakistan **withdrew**, reducing the strength to **299**.

## Other tasks it performed

Ratified India's Commonwealth membership (May 1949); adopted the national flag (22 July 1947); adopted the national anthem and national song (24 January 1950); elected Dr. Rajendra Prasad as the first President of India (24 January 1950).

### 5. Committees, the Drafting Committee and the people behind the draft

*Objective:* Name the major committees with their chairs, list the Drafting Committee members, and state the roles of Ambedkar and B. N. Rau.

## Why committees

The Assembly worked through committees. Each committee studied one subject and drafted provisions, which the full Assembly then debated. NCERT notes that these committees were usually chaired by Nehru, Rajendra Prasad, Patel or Ambedkar, men who often disagreed but worked together. Laxmikanth counts **eight major** committees (and many minor ones).

## The eight major committees and their chairs

| Committee | Chair |
|---|---|
| Union Powers | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Union Constitution | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Provincial Constitution | Sardar Patel |
| Drafting | Dr. B. R. Ambedkar |
| Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas | Sardar Patel |
| Rules of Procedure | Dr. Rajendra Prasad |
| States Committee (negotiating with states) | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Steering | Dr. Rajendra Prasad |

The Advisory Committee had five sub-committees, including **Fundamental Rights (J. B. Kripalani)** and **Minorities (H. C. Mukherjee)**. Minor committees include Finance and Staff (Prasad), Credentials (Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar), National Flag ad hoc (Prasad) and the Special Committee to Examine the Draft Constitution (Nehru).

## The Drafting Committee

Set up on **29 August 1947**, this was the most important committee, tasked with preparing the draft. It had **seven members**: Dr. B. R. Ambedkar (Chairman), N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar, Dr. K. M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, N. Madhava Rau (replacing B. L. Mitter, who resigned from ill-health) and T. T. Krishnamachari (replacing D. P. Khaitan, who died in 1948).

Its output: a **first draft published in February 1948**; the people were given **eight months** to comment; a **second draft published in October 1948**. Laxmikanth says it took under six months to prepare its draft and sat for **141 days**.

## Ambedkar

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar was Chairman of the Drafting Committee and, as the then Law Minister, **piloted the Draft Constitution** through the Assembly. He is recognised as the "Father of the Constitution of India" and the "chief architect". Dr. Rajendra Prasad paid tribute on 26 November 1949 to his zeal despite poor health.

## The officials

- **Sir B. N. Rau**: constitutional (legal) adviser to the Assembly. Standard accounts credit him with preparing the preliminary draft in October 1947, which the Drafting Committee then examined. This detail is not in the extracts used here; treat it as standard knowledge to verify.
- **H. V. R. Iyengar**: Secretary. **S. N. Mukerjee**: chief draftsman.

## A pre-Assembly body

On 8 July 1946, while elections were still on, the Congress appointed an **Experts Committee** under Nehru to prepare material for the Assembly. Granville Austin credited it with setting India on the road to the Constitution.

### 6. Readings, adoption, enforcement and the time taken

*Objective:* Narrate the three readings with dates, give adoption and commencement, and recall the numbers on sessions, days and cost.

## The three readings

- **First reading.** Dr. Ambedkar introduced the final draft on **4 November 1948**. A general discussion lasted five days, until 9 November 1948.
- **Second reading.** Clause-by-clause consideration ran from **15 November 1948 to 17 October 1949**. About **7,653 amendments** were proposed and **2,473** were actually discussed.
- **Third reading.** Began **14 November 1949**. Ambedkar moved that the Constitution as settled by the Assembly be passed. It was declared passed on **26 November 1949**.

## Adoption

The motion passed on 26 November 1949. This is the date named in the Preamble on which the people of India in the Constituent Assembly "adopted, enacted and gave to themselves" the Constitution. As adopted, it had a Preamble, **395 Articles** and **8 Schedules**; the Preamble was enacted after the rest.

**A source difference to watch.** Laxmikanth's main text says 284 of the 299 members were present on 26 November and signed. NCERT, and a note in Laxmikanth's own session table, place the **signing by 284 members on 24 January 1950**, the Assembly's final meeting. For safety, remember: adopted 26 November 1949; signed 24 January 1950 (NCERT); say "as per the source you cite" if asked in Mains.

## Enforcement

Some provisions (on citizenship, elections, the provisional parliament, transitional matters and the short title; Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392 and 393) came into force on **26 November 1949** itself. The rest, the major part, came into force on **26 January 1950**, called the date of commencement and celebrated as Republic Day. The date was chosen because on 26 January 1930 Purna Swaraj Day had been celebrated, after the INC's Lahore Session (December 1929). On commencement, the Indian Independence Act 1947 and the Government of India Act 1935 were **repealed**; the Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act 1949 continued.

## Sessions, time and cost

- **Eleven sessions**, from 9-23 December 1946 (first) to 14-26 November 1949 (eleventh), plus a final meeting on 24 January 1950.
- Total time: **2 years, 11 months and 18 days** (Laxmikanth). NCERT says the Assembly met for **166 days** over two years and eleven months. The Draft Constitution itself was considered for **114 days**; the Drafting Committee sat **141 days**. These are different counts of different things.
- The framers studied the constitutions of about **60 countries**. The cost was **Rs 64 lakh**.
- For comparison: the US framers took under 4 months (7 Articles); Canada about 2 years 6 months (147 Articles); Australia about 9 years (128 Articles); South Africa 1 year (153 Articles).

## Hindi text

The Constitution as adopted had no authoritative Hindi text. The 58th Amendment Act 1987 inserted Article 394A (Part XXII) to provide for one.

### 7. Criticism, the provisional parliament and borrowed features

*Objective:* List the six criticisms with the point each makes, explain how defenders answer them, describe the provisional parliament and name the main borrowed features.

## Six criticisms (Laxmikanth)

1. **Not representative.** Members were not directly elected on universal adult franchise.
2. **Not sovereign.** It was created by British proposals and, critics said, met with British permission.
3. **Time-consuming.** The US framers took about four months. Naziruddin Ahmed mocked the Drafting Committee as a "Drifting Committee".
4. **Congress dominated.** Granville Austin described it as a one-party body in an essentially one-party country.
5. **Lawyer-politician dominated.** Other sections were thin; critics linked this to the Constitution's bulk and complicated language.
6. **Hindu dominated.** Viscount Simon and Winston Churchill made such remarks.

## How defenders respond

NCERT and later chapters give a balanced view.

- **On representation:** NCERT admits the Assembly was not elected by universal suffrage but says there was a serious attempt to make it representative: members of all religions, 28 members from the Scheduled Castes and a Congress that itself spanned many shades of opinion. Chapter 10 separates the *voice* of representation (weak, because of restricted franchise) from the *opinion* of representation (broader, because the debates covered a vast range of views).
- **On time:** Considering about 60 constitutions, 7,653 proposed amendments and a Partition, defenders find the pace reasonable. Compare the Australian figure of about nine years.
- **On sovereignty:** the Independence Act 1947 made the Assembly fully sovereign (module 4).
- **On quality of debate:** NCERT praises the "public reason" of the debates, open to press and public, and notes that only universal suffrage passed almost without debate.

## The provisional parliament

On 24 January 1950 the Assembly held its final session but did not dissolve. From **26 January 1950** it served as the **provisional parliament** until a new Parliament was formed after the first general elections of 1951-52. Laxmikanth's note says the provisional parliament ceased on 17 April 1952 and the first elected two-House Parliament came into being in May 1952.

## Borrowed features (brief)

The country-by-country table (Britain, USA, Ireland, France, Canada) is in Master notes: 'Borrowed features (NCERT table, brief)'. In short: the parliamentary system came from Britain, Fundamental Rights and judicial review from the USA, Directive Principles from Ireland and a strong-centre federation from Canada. Ambedkar's reply to the charge of imitation was that the only new thing in a late constitution is the variation that fits local needs. The fuller list is in GS-158 and the salient-features chapter, not repeated here.

### 8. Examination application: question shapes, traps and elimination

*Objective:* Recognise the typical question shapes and use elimination on the two verified past questions.

## What UPSC asks

- **Who/which** facts: presiding officers, chairs, members.
- **Date/sequence** facts: first meeting, Objectives Resolution, Drafting Committee, adoption, enforcement.
- **Number** facts: 389, 299, 395 Articles, 8 Schedules, 7 members.
- **Statement + reason** items that put a true event with a wrong date.
- **Mains judgment**: representative? sovereign? effective?

## Worked example 1: PYQ 2023 (Constitution Day)

The question gives two statements about Constitution Day. Statement I says it is celebrated on 26 November to promote constitutional values. Statement II says that on 26 November 1949 the Assembly set up the Drafting Committee under Dr. Ambedkar. Method: test Statement II against your anchor dates. The Drafting Committee was set up on **29 August 1947**, whereas 26 November 1949 is the date of **adoption**. So Statement II conflicts with this module. Statement I rests on the observance of Constitution Day on 26 November, which is general knowledge outside the extracts used here. On that analysis the pattern "I correct, II incorrect" fits, but this is our reasoning, not an official key.

## Worked example 2: PYQ 2024 (provisional president)

The question asks who was the provisional (temporary) President before Dr. Rajendra Prasad took over. Method: recall the first meeting on 9 December 1946 and the oldest-member (French) practice. Laxmikanth names **Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha**. The distractors are real Assembly figures: Rajagopalachari, Ambedkar (Drafting Committee chair) and T. T. Krishnamachari (a Drafting Committee member). Analysis points to Sinha; this is not an official key.

## Common traps

1. Mixing **adoption (26 Nov 1949)** with **commencement (26 Jan 1950)**.
2. Mixing **Drafting Committee set-up (29 Aug 1947)** with **Objectives Resolution (13 Dec 1946)**.
3. Giving Ambedkar every role. He chaired the Drafting Committee; Prasad was President; Rau was adviser.
4. Using 389 for the final strength instead of 299.
5. Saying the Assembly was directly elected, or that princely states were elected by adult franchise.
6. Confusing Nehru's committees with Patel's.

## Elimination habits

If an option attributes the Objectives Resolution to Ambedkar or gives 1950 as the date the Constitution was adopted, discard it. Check numbers against the sum rules (292 + 4 = 296; 296 + 93 = 389; 229 + 70 = 299).

### Notes: Key definitions

- Constituent Assembly: a body formed to frame (draft and adopt) a constitution, as distinct from a legislature that makes ordinary laws under one.
- Objectives Resolution: Nehru's resolution of 13 December 1946, adopted 22 January 1947, stating the fundamentals of the future constitution; its modified version is the Preamble.
- Indirect election: members were elected by the provincial legislative assemblies, not by the people directly.
- Proportional representation by single transferable vote: the method by which each community's members in a provincial assembly elected the community's representatives.
- Provisional parliament: the Constituent Assembly functioning as the country's Parliament from 26 January 1950 until the new Parliament was formed after the 1951-52 elections.
- Adoption versus commencement: adoption is the Assembly passing the final text (26 November 1949); commencement is the date it came into force (26 January 1950 for most provisions).

### Notes: Chronology

- 1934: M. N. Roy puts forward the idea of a Constituent Assembly for India.
- 1935: the INC officially demands a Constituent Assembly.
- 1938: Nehru, for the INC, says the Constitution must be framed by an Assembly elected on adult franchise.
- 1940: the August Offer accepts the demand in principle.
- 1942: Cripps proposals; rejected by the Muslim League.
- 24 March 1946: Cabinet Mission reaches India; 16 May 1946: it publishes its plan.
- 8 July 1946: Congress appoints its Experts Committee.
- July-August 1946: elections to the 296 British Indian seats (Congress 208, League 73, others 15).
- 9 December 1946: first meeting (211 members; League boycott; Sinha temporary President).
- 13 December 1946: Objectives Resolution moved; 22 January 1947: adopted unanimously.
- 3 June 1947: Mountbatten Plan; 22 July 1947: national flag adopted.
- 14 August 1947: Assembly reassembles for divided India (NCERT); 29 August 1947: Drafting Committee set up.
- 17 November 1947: first meeting as Dominion Legislature; Mavalankar elected Speaker.
- February 1948: first draft published; October 1948: second draft published.
- 4 November 1948: first reading begins; 15 November 1948 to 17 October 1949: second reading.
- 14 November 1949: third reading begins; 26 November 1949: Constitution passed/adopted.
- 24 January 1950: final session; signing (NCERT); anthem and song adopted; Prasad elected first President.
- 26 January 1950: commencement; 1951-52: first general elections; provisional parliament ends in April 1952.

### Notes: Composition at a glance

- Seats and strength under the 1946 scheme and after Partition.
- Check totals: 292 + 4 = 296; 296 + 93 = 389; 229 + 70 = 299.

### Notes: Committees and chairs

- Union Powers Committee: Jawaharlal Nehru.
- Union Constitution Committee: Jawaharlal Nehru.
- Provincial Constitution Committee: Sardar Patel.
- Drafting Committee: Dr. B. R. Ambedkar (7 members; set up 29 August 1947).
- Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas: Sardar Patel; Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee: J. B. Kripalani; Minorities Sub-Committee: H. C. Mukherjee.
- Rules of Procedure Committee and Steering Committee: Dr. Rajendra Prasad.
- States Committee (negotiating with the states): Jawaharlal Nehru.
- Drafting Committee members: Ambedkar, N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar, K. M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, N. Madhava Rau (for B. L. Mitter), T. T. Krishnamachari (for D. P. Khaitan).

### Notes: Readings of the draft

- First reading: 4 November 1948, final draft introduced; general discussion of five days to 9 November 1948.
- Second reading: 15 November 1948 to 17 October 1949, clause by clause; 7,653 amendments proposed, 2,473 discussed.
- Third reading: from 14 November 1949; motion that the Constitution as settled be passed; declared passed 26 November 1949.
- As adopted: Preamble, 395 Articles, 8 Schedules.

### Notes: Effects of the Indian Independence Act 1947 on the Assembly

- Made the Assembly fully sovereign; it could alter or abrogate any British law relating to India.
- Made it a legislative body as well; Prasad presided as constituent body, G. V. Mavalankar as legislature; both functions until 26 November 1949.
- Muslim League members from Pakistan areas withdrew; strength fell from 389 to 299.

### Notes: Criticisms and responses

- Indirect election on limited franchise -> charge 'not representative' -> defence: all communities represented, Scheduled Castes included, debates reflected wide opinion.
- British origin of the scheme -> charge 'not sovereign' -> the Independence Act 1947 made it fully sovereign.
- Long duration compared with the US -> charge 'time-consuming' -> defence: scale of debate (7,653 amendments proposed), study of about 60 constitutions, Partition conditions.
- Dominance of one party and of lawyers -> charges of one-party and lawyer-politician domination -> defence: Congress itself diverse; deliberation by public reason.
- Hindu majority among members -> charge 'Hindu dominated' -> defence: all religious communities had representatives (Table of community strength: Hindus 163, Muslims 80 of 296).

### Notes: Other Assembly decisions and the officials

- Commonwealth membership ratified: May 1949.
- National flag adopted: 22 July 1947. National anthem and national song adopted: 24 January 1950.
- Prasad elected first President of India: 24 January 1950.
- Symbol of the Assembly: the elephant.
- Constitutional adviser: Sir B. N. Rau. Secretary: H. V. R. Iyengar. Chief draftsman: S. N. Mukerjee.
- Calligrapher of the original English text: Prem Behari Narain Raizada; decoration by Shantiniketan artists including Nand Lal Bose and Beohar Rammanohar Sinha; Hindi calligraphy by Vasant Krishan Vaidya.
- Cost: Rs 64 lakh. Sessions: 11, plus the final meeting of 24 January 1950.

### Notes: Enforcement and commencement

- Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392 and 393 came into force on 26 November 1949.
- The remaining provisions came into force on 26 January 1950, the date of commencement (Republic Day), chosen for Purna Swaraj Day of 1930.
- Article 394: commencement; Article 395: repeals (Indian Independence Act 1947 and Government of India Act 1935); Article 393: short title; Article 394A (58th Amendment 1987): authoritative Hindi text.

### Notes: Borrowed features (NCERT table, brief)

- Provisions the framers adapted from other constitutions, as tabulated by NCERT. The full list belongs to GS-158 and the salient-features chapter.
- Moved from module 7 (full NCERT summary): NCERT's table: from **Britain**: parliamentary government, rule of law, Speaker, law-making procedure, first-past-the-post; from the **USA**: Fundamental Rights, judicial review, judicial independence; from **Ireland**: Directive Principles; from **France**: liberty, equality, fraternity; from **Canada**: a federal system with a strong centre and residuary powers. Ambedkar's reply to the charge of imitation was that the only new thing in a late constitution is the variation that fits local needs.

### Notes: Commonly confused pairs

- Adoption (26 November 1949) versus commencement (26 January 1950).
- Drafting Committee set-up (29 August 1947) versus Objectives Resolution (13 December 1946).
- Temporary President Sachchidananda Sinha versus permanent President Rajendra Prasad.
- Ambedkar (Drafting Committee chair) versus Rau (adviser) versus Iyengar (secretary).
- V. T. Krishnamachari (Vice-President) versus T. T. Krishnamachari (Drafting Committee member).
- 389 (1946 design) versus 299 (post-Partition strength).
- Constituent role (Prasad presiding) versus legislative role (Mavalankar presiding).
