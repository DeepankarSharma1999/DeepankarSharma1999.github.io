### Bridge: Three forces on a falling sphere and a free-body diagram

A **free-body diagram** is a sketch of one object with an arrow for every force on it. Take a sphere of radius a and density ρ falling through a fluid of density σ. Choose **downward as positive**.

- **Weight** W = mg = (4/3)πa³ρg, downward. (Volume of a sphere is (4/3)πa³; mass is volume times density.)
- **Buoyancy** B = (4/3)πa³σg, upward. Archimedes' principle: the fluid pushes up with the weight of the fluid the sphere displaces.
- **Drag** F, upward while the sphere falls. It is zero at rest and grows with speed. The viscous-drag law is Stokes' law, F = 6πηav (module 2).

Newton's second law down the page: m dv/dt = W − B − F. The first two terms are constant; only the drag changes. That is why the speed settles at a constant value (the **terminal velocity**): the drag grows until it cancels W − B.

If σ > ρ the sphere is lighter than the fluid it displaces, W − B is negative and the sphere rises (an air bubble in oil).

### Bridge: Minimum calculus and units for this lesson

**Derivative.** Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = dv/dt. **Separation of variables.** An equation of the form dv/dt = (v_t − v)/τ, with τ and v_t constants, is solved by moving all v to one side and all t to the other: dv/(v_t − v) = dt/τ. Then integrate both sides. Useful integrals: ∫ dv/(v_t − v) = −ln(v_t − v) + C (the minus sign comes from the derivative of the inner term −v), ∫ dt = t, and ∫ e^(−t/τ) dt = −τ e^(−t/τ).

**The exponential.** e⁰ = 1, e⁻¹ = 0.368, e⁻² = 0.135, e⁻³ = 0.0498, e⁻⁵ = 0.0067. The natural log undoes it: ln 10 = 2.303 and ln 100 = 4.605.

**Units.** Viscosity η has SI unit Pa s = N s m⁻² = kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ (dimensions [ML⁻¹T⁻¹], PH-023 module 3). The CGS unit is the **poise**, g cm⁻¹ s⁻¹: **1 poise = 0.1 Pa s**, so air at 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ poise is 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s. Density: 1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Keep one unit system from the first line of every calculation; g = 9.8 m s⁻² = 980 cm s⁻².

### Bridge: The Reynolds number idea, in one paragraph

PH-023 module 7 defined the **Reynolds number** Re = ρvD/η for a pipe. For a sphere falling in a fluid of density σ, the same idea gives Re = σ v L/η, where L is a length that describes the sphere. It is a pure number: inertia of the displaced fluid divided by viscous resistance. If Re is small, viscosity controls the flow around the sphere and the drag is proportional to speed (Stokes). If Re is large, the fluid cannot follow the sphere smoothly, eddies form behind it and the drag grows roughly with the square of speed.

**Convention warning.** Mathur's limit (module 5) uses the radius, Re_a = σva/η. Many books use the diameter, Re_d = σv(2a)/η = 2Re_a. Always state which one you use. A sphere radius gives Re_a; a pipe uses the diameter.

### 1. Orientation: what Stokes' law gives, and what this lesson adds to PH-023

*Objective:* State Stokes' law, the terminal-velocity formula and the one validity condition, and know which parts are new compared with PH-023 module 6.

## The picture in one paragraph

A small sphere moves slowly through a viscous fluid. The fluid layer touching it moves with it (no slip), the layers farther out are dragged along, and the resulting relative motion between layers produces a retarding force opposite to the motion. For a sphere of radius a and speed v in a fluid of viscosity η, the force is

$$ F = 6π η a v $$

It is proportional to speed, so a falling sphere speeds up until the drag (plus buoyancy) balances the weight, and then falls at the **terminal velocity**

$$ v_t = 2 a² (ρ − σ) g / (9 η) $$

where ρ is the density of the sphere and σ the density of the fluid (the symbols NCERT and Mathur use; PH-023 module 6 called them ρₛ and ρ_f).

## What PH-023 already gave (linked, not repeated)

PH-023 module 6 states Stokes' law as in NCERT 9.5.1, gives the dimensional-analysis argument for F ∝ ηav, derives v_t from force balance, quotes v(t) = v_t(1 − e^(−t/t_c)) with t_c = 2a²ρₛ/(9η) (called τ in this lesson), and works one glycerine example. Treat those as recalled. PH-023 module 7 gives the Reynolds number; PH-024 gives Poiseuille; PH-025 (Day 13) gives Bernoulli's ideal-fluid equation.

### 2. Foundation: why the drag is 6πηav, a scaling argument and the drag coefficient

*Objective:* Explain from a scaling argument and from dimensions why F is proportional to ηav, and convert Stokes' law into the form C_D = 24/Re.

## What can and cannot be shown here

The full derivation of F = 6πηav solves the equations of viscous flow (Navier-Stokes) around a sphere at very small Re. NCERT states the law and says it will not derive it (extract p. 13). Mathur gives a dimensional-analysis deduction and says the number 6π was found by Stokes (PDF p. 810). The full derivation is beyond this lesson's scope; this module gives the parts that can be done with school algebra, and the exact factor is quoted. (Mathur's wording that Stokes found 6π 'experimentally' differs from the usual account that it follows from theory; standard knowledge, verify.)

## Scaling argument (new)

Near the sphere, the fluid speed falls from v at the surface to zero over a distance of the order of the sphere's size a. So the velocity gradient is of order v/a, and Newton's law of viscosity (PH-023 module 3) gives a viscous stress of order ηv/a. That stress acts over the sphere's surface, of order a². Force of order (ηv/a)(a²) = η a v. Every factor appears once, in agreement with the dimensional result below, and the exact solution only fixes the number 6π.

## Dimensional argument, recalled in one line

Assume F depends on η, a, v only: F = k ηˣ aʸ vᶻ. Matching M, T and L gives x = y = z = 1 (full steps in PH-023 module 6; Mathur's version is the same, PDF p. 810). Dimensional analysis cannot give k.

What it also cannot do is say when the law fails. Add the fluid density σ to the list of variables and the dimensionless group is Re. Then F = η a v f(Re) with f(Re) → 6π as Re → 0.

## The drag coefficient form (new)

For fast flow, engineers write the drag as F = ½ C_D σ (πa²) v², with πa² the frontal area. Equate this to Stokes' drag and use Re_d = σ v (2a)/η:

$$ ½ C_D σ π a² v² = 6π η a v  ⇒  C_D = 12 η /(σ a v) = 24 η /(σ (2a) v) = 24 / Re_d $$

Check: 12η/(σav): multiply and divide by 2: 24η/(σ·2a·v) = 24/Re_d ✓. So at small Re_d the drag coefficient falls as 1/Re, and the drag is linear in v. At large Re_d, C_D flattens at about 0.4 to 0.5 for a smooth sphere (standard knowledge, verify), and the drag is quadratic in v (module 5).

## Reading the formula

- Doubling the radius doubles the drag at the same speed, not quadruples it: the drag follows the *size*, not the area. Contrast the quadratic regime, where it follows the area.
- Viscosity η is the whole story: no η, no Stokes drag.

### 3. Core: the equation of motion, with every integration step (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Solve m dv/dt = W − B − 6πηav for v(t), define the time constant τ, and check the answer in the limits t ≪ τ and t ≫ τ.

## Assumptions and signs

The sphere (radius a, density ρ) is released from rest in a large body of still fluid (density σ, viscosity η). Down is positive; v(0) = 0. Stokes' drag holds throughout (small Re); the fluid dragged along is ignored for now (the added-mass caveat is in module 4). Re must be checked at the end.

## Steps

1. Newton's second law for the sphere, using the three forces of bridge module B1:

$$ m dv/dt = (4/3)π a³ ρ g − (4/3)π a³ σ g − 6π η a v,   m = (4/3)π a³ ρ $$

2. The first two terms are constants. Define v_t as the value of v that makes the right side zero (the terminal velocity from the force balance, module 1):

$$ (4/3)π a³ (ρ − σ) g = 6π η a v_t ,   v_t = 2a²(ρ − σ)g /(9η) $$

3. Replace the constant: m dv/dt = 6πηa v_t − 6πηa v = 6πηa (v_t − v). Divide by m and define the **time constant**

$$ τ = m /(6π η a) = (4/3)π a³ ρ /(6π η a) = 2 a² ρ /(9η),   dv/dt = (v_t − v)/τ $$

4. Separate the variables: dv/(v_t − v) = dt/τ.
5. Integrate from the start (t = 0, v = 0) to (t, v):

$$ ∫₀^v dv′/(v_t − v′) = ∫₀^t dt′/τ  ⇒  −ln(v_t − v) + ln(v_t) = t/τ $$

The minus sign arises because d/dv[−ln(v_t − v)] = 1/(v_t − v). The lower limit gives the term ln v_t.
6. Combine the logs: ln[v_t/(v_t − v)] = t/τ. Take the exponential: v_t/(v_t − v) = e^(t/τ), so v_t − v = v_t e^(−t/τ):

$$ v(t) = v_t (1 − e^(−t/τ)) $$

## Checks

*Dimensions:* τ = kg /(kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ · m) = s ✓. *Initial slope:* d/dt at t = 0 gives v_t/τ = g(1 − σ/ρ): this is the free-fall acceleration reduced by buoyancy, and indeed v_t/τ = [2a²(ρ − σ)g/9η]·[9η/(2a²ρ)] = g(ρ − σ)/ρ ✓. So **v_t = g(1 − σ/ρ) τ**: the terminal velocity is the initial acceleration times τ. *t ≪ τ:* 1 − e^(−u) ≈ u, so v ≈ v_t t/τ = g(1 − σ/ρ)t, free fall with buoyancy. *t ≫ τ:* v → v_t. *Limit τ → 0 for a very small sphere:* it reaches v_t almost instantly.

## Numbers: a drizzle drop

Water drop, a = 0.050 mm, in air (σ ≈ 1.2 kg m⁻³ neglected against ρ = 1000; η = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s):

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| v_t = 2a²ρg/(9η) | 0.302 m/s |
| τ = 2a²ρ/(9η) | 30.9 ms |
| v at t = τ | 0.632 v_t = 0.191 m/s |
| v at t = 3τ (93 ms) | 0.950 v_t |
| v at t = 5τ (154 ms) | 0.993 v_t |

Re_a = σ v_t a/η = 1.0, so this drop sits at the edge of the Stokes regime (module 5).

### 4. Core: distance fallen, the 90 per cent time, added mass and limiting cases (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Integrate v(t) to get the distance x(t), find time and distance to reach 90 and 99 per cent of v_t, and state the added-mass caveat and the rising-bubble and air limits.

## Distance fallen (every step)

Start from v(t) = v_t(1 − e^(−t/τ)) (module 3) and x(0) = 0, x measured downward.

1. x(t) = ∫₀^t v dt′ = v_t ∫₀^t (1 − e^(−t′/τ)) dt′.
2. ∫₀^t 1 dt′ = t, and ∫₀^t e^(−t′/τ) dt′ = [−τ e^(−t′/τ)]₀^t = τ(1 − e^(−t/τ)).
3. Subtract:

$$ x(t) = v_t [ t − τ (1 − e^(−t/τ)) ] $$

*Dimension:* v_t × time = length ✓. *Small t:* expand e^(−u) = 1 − u + u²/2 − …, so t − τ(1 − e^(−t/τ)) ≈ t²/(2τ) and x ≈ ½(v_t/τ)t² = ½ g(1 − σ/ρ)t²: uniform acceleration ✓. *Large t:* x → v_t(t − τ): after the transient the sphere has covered the distance of steady fall at v_t, minus a lag v_tτ.

## Time and distance to 90 and 99 per cent (Mathur exercise 47)

Set v = fv_t: e^(−t/τ) = 1 − f, so t = −τ ln(1 − f). For 90 per cent, t = τ ln 10 = 2.303τ; for 99 per cent, t = τ ln 100 = 4.605τ. Put these into x(t):

$$ x₉₀ = v_t τ (ln 10 − 0.9) = 1.403 v_t τ,   x₉₉ = v_t τ (ln 100 − 0.99) = 3.615 v_t τ $$

For the drizzle drop of module 3 (v_t = 0.302 m/s, τ = 30.9 ms, v_tτ = 9.34 mm): t₉₀ = 71 ms, x₉₀ = 13.1 mm; t₉₉ = 142 ms, x₉₉ = 33.7 mm.

## Added mass (standard, verify)

The equation m dv/dt = … ignores the fluid the sphere must push aside as it accelerates. For a sphere in an ideal fluid, the standard result is that the effective inertia is m + ½σV, with V = (4/3)πa³ (the **added mass**). A history (Basset) term also exists; both are beyond the minimum (flagged). The effect:

- The terminal velocity v_t is **unchanged** (at v_t the acceleration is zero, so inertia drops out).
- The time constant becomes τ′ = (m + ½σV)/(6πηa) = 2a²(ρ + σ/2)/(9η).

For steel in glycerine (ρ = 7800, σ = 1260 kg m⁻³): τ = 4.70 ms rises to 5.08 ms (+8.1 per cent). For a water drop in air σ/ρ ≈ 10⁻³ and the correction is negligible. For a light bubble the added mass dominates the inertia.

## Limiting cases

| Case | Result |
|---|---|
| σ → 0 (sphere in air) | buoyancy negligible; v_t = 2a²ρg/(9η) |
| σ = ρ | v_t = 0: neutral buoyancy, the sphere hangs |
| σ > ρ (rising bubble) | v_t < 0: the sphere rises; same exponential law with v_t negative |
| η → ∞ | v_t → 0 and τ → 0: the sphere hardly moves |
| η → 0 | v_t → ∞ in the formula: it fails, since Re grows (module 5) |

Rising bubble: an air bubble of a = 0.5 mm in glycerine (σ = 1260, η = 0.83 Pa s, air density neglected) gives v_t = −8.27 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹, i.e. it rises at 0.83 mm/s, Re_a = 6.3 × 10⁻⁴. A mobile bubble surface lowers the drag, so a real bubble may rise faster (Hadamard-Rybczynski, standard, verify).

### 5. Core: where Stokes' law holds, and what replaces it (Reynolds number, Oseen, walls)

*Objective:* State the assumptions of Stokes' law, compute Re_a for a given case, and decide whether the formula can be used.

## Mathur's list of assumptions (PDF p. 810)

(i) the body is perfectly rigid and smooth; (ii) the medium is infinite and homogeneous as far as the body is concerned, meaning the gaps between the medium's molecules are far smaller than the body; (iii) no slip at the surface; (iv) no eddies or waves, so the speed stays below a critical value. Mathur quotes Stokes' suggested critical speed η/(σa) and Arnold's lower value 0.6 η/(σa) (a the radius, σ the fluid density). In terms of Re_a = σva/η: **Stokes' law is reliable for Re_a up to about 0.6**.

## Applying the test to cases computed in this lesson

| Case | v_t from Stokes | Re_a = σ v a/η | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steel ball a = 1.5 mm in glycerine | 3.86 cm/s | 0.088 | valid |
| Fog droplet a = 10 µm in air | 1.21 cm/s | 0.008 | valid |
| 2018 drop, a = 0.04 mm in air | 19.4 cm/s | 0.52 | at the edge |
| Drizzle a = 0.050 mm in air | 30.2 cm/s | 1.0 | marginal, beyond 0.6 |
| 2018 drop after 100 coalesce | 4.17 m/s | 52 | not valid |
| 2017 drop, a = 1 cm, medium of density 1.21 kg m⁻³ (typical of air; the question does not name the medium) | 1.2 × 10⁴ m/s | 8.1 × 10⁶ | absurd |

A water drop in air reaches Re_a = 0.6 at a = 0.042 mm and Re_a = 1 at 0.050 mm.

## The Oseen correction (standard, verify)

Oseen improved Stokes' result for small but finite Re: F ≈ 6πηav(1 + (3/16)Re_d), with Re_d = σv(2a)/η. The correction is about 5 per cent at Re_d = 0.27 and 19 per cent at Re_d = 1. Beyond Re_d of about 1 the drag keeps growing faster than the linear law (the actual curve is in a fluid-mechanics text, not the syllabus minimum).

## The quadratic regime

For Re_d from about 10³ up to about 2 × 10⁵ a smooth sphere has a nearly constant C_D of 0.4 to 0.5 (standard, verify), so F = ½C_Dσπa²v² and the force balance W − B = F gives

$$ v_t = √[ 8 a g (ρ − σ) /(3 C_D σ) ] $$

(derivation: (4/3)πa³(ρ − σ)g = ½C_Dσπa²v² ⇒ v² = 8a(ρ − σ)g/(3C_Dσ) ✓). Contrast: v_t ∝ a² in the Stokes regime but ∝ √a in this one. A 1 mm radius raindrop would fall at 121 m/s by Stokes' law; with C_D = 0.47 the estimate is 6.8 m/s (Re_d ≈ 900, borderline of the quoted C_D range).

## Wall and end effects (Ladenburg)

Stokes' law assumes unbounded fluid. In a jar of radius R and depth h the observed speed is lower than the unbounded value v∞. Mathur gives the corrections (PDF pp. 811 and 812):

$$ v∞ = v (1 + 2.4 a/R)(1 + 3.3 a/h) $$

each factor above 1 (h the depth of liquid; Mathur writes m), so the unbounded speed is larger than the observed one. (Validity limit of the factors, a small compared with R and m, is standard; verify.) Module 6 applies them.

## Tiny droplets

When the droplet is only a few mean free paths across (assumption ii fails) the drag falls below Stokes' value; the Cunningham slip correction is the standard fix (named only; verify).

### 6. Core: the falling-ball viscometer, with corrections and a worked measurement

*Objective:* Describe the experiment, compute η with and without the wall and end corrections, and state the main sources of error.

## Method (Mathur sections 15.17 to 15.18, PDF pp. 811 and 812)

A small ball (steel ball-bearing) of accurately measured radius a is dropped centrally into a tall jar of the liquid. The time t to cover a distance S (about 5 cm in Mathur's description) between two marks, placed well below the top and well above the bottom, is measured. If the ball has reached v_t before the first mark, v = S/t, and the terminal-velocity formula inverted gives

$$ η = 2 a² (ρ − σ) g t /(9 S) $$

**Is the speed terminal?** Mathur's Gibson-Jacobs form uses three marks and checks that the times between successive marks are equal; if the ball is still speeding up, a smaller ball is tried. Because a²t stays constant for a ball in a given liquid, one can repeat with several balls and plot a² against 1/t: a straight line whose slope is a²t, averaging out random errors.

## Corrections

With the observed speed v the unbounded speed is v∞ = v(1 + 2.4a/R)(1 + 3.3a/h) (module 5; h the depth of liquid, Mathur writes m), so

$$ η = 2 a² (ρ − σ) g t /[ 9 S (1 + 2.4a/R)(1 + 3.3a/h) ] $$

(OCR note: Mathur's printed line for the wall-effect speed reads as if t multiplies; the correct reading is S(1 + 2.4a/R)/t, and the combined formula above is consistent with it.)

## Worked measurement (original numbers)

Steel ball a = 2.0 mm, ρ = 7800 kg m⁻³; oil σ = 950 kg m⁻³; jar radius R = 3.0 cm, oil depth h = 25 cm; S = 20.0 cm covered in t = 5.0 s; g = 9.8.

1. v = S/t = 0.200/5.0 = 0.0400 m/s.
2. Without corrections: η = 2(2.0 × 10⁻³)²(7800 − 950)(9.8)/(9 × 0.0400) = 2 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 6850 × 9.8/0.360 = 1.49 Pa s.
3. Wall factor: 1 + 2.4 × 2.0 × 10⁻³/0.030 = 1.160. End factor: 1 + 3.3 × 2.0 × 10⁻³/0.25 = 1.0264.
4. v∞ = 0.0400 × 1.160 × 1.0264 = 0.0476 m/s, so η = 1.49 × 0.0400/0.0476 = **1.25 Pa s = 12.5 poise**.
5. Check Re_a = σ v∞ a/η = 950 × 0.0476 × 2.0 × 10⁻³/1.25 = 0.072 (valid). The corrections lowered η by 16 per cent: the wall effect dominates here because a/R = 0.067 is not small.

## Sensitivity

η ∝ a²t/S: a 1 per cent error in a gives 2 per cent in η, in t 1 per cent. Keep the temperature steady (liquid η falls as temperature rises, PH-023 module 3), hence Mathur's water bath.

## Units

With a in cm, ρ in g cm⁻³, g = 980 cm s⁻² and S in cm, η comes out in poise; divide by 10 for Pa s. NCERT Example 9.9 run backwards (copper ball, 6.5 cm/s) gives η = 0.99 Pa s ✓.

### 7. Core: raindrops, fog, coalescence, bubbles, sedimentation and electrophoresis

*Objective:* Apply v_t ∝ a² to coalescing drops, fog and clouds, and state (flagged) how the same balance extends to centrifuges and charged particles.

## Coalescence: n equal drops make one

Volume is conserved: n(4/3)πa³ = (4/3)πA³, so A = n^(1/3) a. Since v_t ∝ radius squared in the Stokes regime,

$$ v_n / v₁ = (A/a)² = n^(2/3) $$

Mathur Example 15.23 (n = 2, v = 1 m/s): 2^(2/3) = 1.5874, so 1.587 m/s (Mathur prints 1.588, a last-digit rounding difference). Example 15.24: n = 8, 10 m/s gives 8^(2/3) = 4, so 40 m/s ✓. Mathur exercise 50 (n = 2, 5 cm/s) gives 7.94 cm/s; the printed 7.98 is a slip. The 2018 verified question has n = 100: factor 100^(2/3) = 21.54. This result is valid only while Re_a stays small (module 5).

**Trap:** the radius grows as n^(1/3), not n, and the speed as n^(2/3), not n.

## Fog, mist and clouds

With η = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s and a water droplet (σ of air neglected):

| Droplet radius | v_t | Time to fall 100 m | Re_a |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 µm (fog) | 1.21 cm/s | 2.3 hours | 0.008 |
| 1 µm (mist) | 0.121 mm/s | about 9.6 days | 8 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 50 µm (drizzle) | 0.30 m/s | 5.5 minutes | 1.0 |

Because v_t ∝ a², a hundredfold smaller radius gives a ten-thousandfold smaller speed. A gentle updraught of a few cm/s is enough to hold fog drops, which is why cloud particles seem to float (Mathur exercise 49 asks this; the argument is standard). For the 1 µm row the Cunningham slip correction would raise the real speed (module 5; verify).

## Millikan's oil drop (named as an application; standard, verify)

Mathur notes (PDF p. 812) that with η of air known, a falling drop's radius follows from v_t, which is how Millikan found the drop radius. Inverting: a = √[9ηv_t/(2(ρ − σ)g)]. For an oil drop (ρ = 900 kg m⁻³) with v_t = 0.12 mm/s: a = 1.05 µm. The full experiment (charge of the drop, electric field, corrections) belongs to modern physics, not here.

## Sedimentation, centrifuges and electrophoresis (standard, verify)

The same balance governs settling: in a centrifuge g becomes ω²r, so v = 2a²(ρ − σ)ω²r/(9η). A charged particle in a field E has qE = 6πηav, so v = qE/(6πηa) (the mobility is v/E).

### 8. Master: Stokes against Poiseuille and Bernoulli, the trap list and the source slips

*Objective:* Place Stokes' law beside PH-024 and PH-025, avoid the standard traps, and recognise the printed slips in Mathur's exercises.

## Where Stokes sits among Days 11 to 14

The full side-by-side table (situation, result, size law, energy, validity) is in the master notes; the key contrasts are below.

**Common thread.** The two viscous laws are linear (drag ∝ v, ΔP ∝ Q). Bernoulli's terms are quadratic (½ρv²). When Re_d rises above about 10³ the sphere's drag becomes quadratic too, F = ½C_Dσπa²v²: the Bernoulli-scale dynamic pressure ½σv² times a frontal area and a coefficient (module 5). So the three lessons are three regimes of one subject.

**Why Bernoulli's equation does not give the drag.** For a sphere in an ideal fluid it predicts zero net drag (d'Alembert's paradox, standard, verify), so the drag in Stokes' regime is entirely a viscous effect: the viscous term, absent in PH-025, is what produces F.

## Trap list

1. **Radius against diameter** in v_t: the formula uses the radius a; with diameter d, v_t = d²(ρ − σ)g/(18η).
2. **Density against relative density**: ρ − σ needs kg m⁻³ (or g cm⁻³ throughout); a relative density of 7.8 is not 7.8 kg m⁻³.
3. **CGS against SI**: 1 poise = 0.1 Pa s; g = 980 cm s⁻².
4. **Forgetting buoyancy** when σ is not negligible (steel in oil), or including it for drops in air where it is 0.1 per cent.
5. **Using Stokes' law beyond its Re** and trusting the number. Always compute Re_a.
6. **Coalescence**: radius ∝ n^(1/3), speed ∝ n^(2/3).
7. **Correction direction**: the wall correction raises the speed (v∞ > v) and lowers η.

### Notes: Definitions and symbols

- Stokes' drag: the retarding force F = 6πηav on a sphere of radius a moving at small speed v through a fluid of viscosity η (opposite to the velocity).
- Terminal velocity v_t: the constant speed reached when drag plus buoyancy equals the weight, so that the net force and the acceleration are zero.
- Symbols: ρ is the density of the sphere, σ the density of the fluid (NCERT and Mathur); PH-023 wrote ρₛ and ρ_f for the same quantities.
- Time constant τ = m/(6πηa) = 2a²ρ/(9η): the time in which the gap between v and v_t shrinks by a factor e.
- Reynolds number for a sphere: Re_a = σva/η (radius) or Re_d = σv(2a)/η (diameter); Re_d = 2Re_a.

### Notes: Core equations

- F = 6πηav (NCERT Eq. 9.17; Mathur section 15.17).
- v_t = 2a²(ρ − σ)g/(9η) (NCERT Eq. 9.18; Mathur).
- m dv/dt = (4/3)πa³(ρ − σ)g − 6πηav, so v(t) = v_t(1 − e^(−t/τ)).
- x(t) = v_t[t − τ(1 − e^(−t/τ))]; t₉₀ = τ ln 10 = 2.303τ; x₉₀ = 1.403 v_tτ; t₉₉ = 4.605τ; x₉₉ = 3.615 v_tτ.
- Falling-ball viscosity: η = 2a²(ρ − σ)g t/(9S), with corrections 1/[(1 + 2.4a/R)(1 + 3.3a/h)] (Mathur; h = depth of liquid, which Mathur writes m).
- Coalescence of n equal drops: A = n^(1/3)a, v_n = n^(2/3)v₁ (Stokes regime).
- Quadratic regime (standard, verify): F = ½C_Dσπa²v², v_t = √[8ag(ρ − σ)/(3C_Dσ)], C_D about 0.47 for a smooth sphere.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: velocity against time

- Assumptions: Stokes drag, still unbounded fluid, v(0) = 0, down positive.
- m dv/dt = 6πηa(v_t − v); τ = m/(6πηa).
- dv/(v_t − v) = dt/τ; integrate: −ln(v_t − v) + ln v_t = t/τ.
- v_t/(v_t − v) = e^(t/τ), so v = v_t(1 − e^(−t/τ)).
- Checks: dimension of τ is time; initial slope v_t/τ = g(1 − σ/ρ); v → v_t for t ≫ τ.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Terminal velocity is not zero acceleration from the start: the sphere accelerates for about 5τ first.
- Buoyancy and drag are different upward forces: buoyancy is constant, drag depends on v.
- Poise and Pa s differ by a factor of 10: 1 Pa s = 10 poise.
- Stokes' drag is linear in a, not in the area: contrast with the quadratic regime.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- Re_a above about 0.6: Stokes fails; the drag exceeds 6πηav.
- Bounded fluid: the wall and end corrections apply (Ladenburg), the observed speed is lower.
- Non-rigid bodies (bubbles, large drops) and very small drops (slip) change the factor 6π (standard; verify).
- Non-spherical bodies: a different numerical factor.

    - **task:** Show why Stokes' drag is proportional to ηav (scaling and dimensional analysis), state the exact result, and convert it to C_D = 24/Re_d. Practice target, not an official allotment: 10 marks about 12 minutes.
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: rigid sphere, unbounded fluid, no slip, steady slow motion; F depends on η, a, v only (small Re).
      - Scaling: velocity gradient near the surface ~ v/a, stress ~ ηv/a, area ~ a², so F ~ ηav.
