## GS-089: Sangam and classical Sanskrit literature

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Sangam and classical Sanskrit literature and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Sanskrit: from Veda to kavya and drama

Panini standardised Sanskrit grammar around the fifth century BCE. Vedic literature divides into sruti (heard: Vedas, Brahmanas, Aranyakas, Upanishads) and smriti (remembered: Vedangas, epics, Puranas). The Ramayana, credited to Valmiki as adikavi, and the Mahabharata, whose Bhagavad Gita lies in the sixth of eighteen parvas, are composed in the kavya tradition. After the Vedic age, Sanskrit produced drama, verse and prose. Bhasa drew plots from the epics; Kalidasa, the Gupta-era master of the Meghadutam and Abhijnanashakuntalam, followed Natyashastra conventions; Shudraka wrote Mrichchhakatikam and Bhavabhuti Uttararamacharitam. NCERT notes that Sanskrit drama relied on royal patronage and an elite audience.

### Sangam and early Tamil literature

Tamil is the oldest of the Dravidian literary languages, the rest emerging only in the first millennium CE. Ancient Tamil literature is called Sangam literature; NCERT names Agastyam as the earliest known work and Tolkappiyam as a grammatical treatise. Its poetics divide love (akam) from war, kingship and public life (puram), and the five tinai each carry a flower name and a landscape mood. The epic Silappadikaram is prized for its picture of Tamil towns, religions, peoples, dance and music, while Manimekalai turns towards Buddhism. Purananuru poems show that in ancient Tamilakam, chiefs who shared wealth with bards were celebrated and misers despised, giving historians a social ideal in which generosity, not accumulation, earned respect.

### Reading texts as historians

Historians ask whether a text is in Sanskrit, the language of priests and elites, or in Prakrit, Pali or Tamil, more likely used by ordinary people; whether it was ritual mantra or a story to be retold; who composed it and for whom; and when and where. The Mahabharata illustrates the method. Its Sanskrit is simpler than Vedic Sanskrit, so it was probably widely understood. Scholars separate narrative from didactic sections, which were probably added later, and the tradition calls it itihasa. The story probably began with charioteer-bards (sutas) and was written down by Brahmanas from the fifth century BCE. Sukthankar's critical edition showed shared elements across regional manuscripts alongside many regional variations.

### Worked example

Q: 'Sangam literature and classical Sanskrit literature offer different windows into early Indian society.' Discuss. Frame: languages and audiences (Sanskrit elite vs Tamil bardic); genres (Sanskrit epic, drama, kavya vs akam/puram, tinai, Tamil epics); social values (dharma norms vs Purananuru ideal of the generous chief); method — language, genre, authorship and date checks, using the Mahabharata's critical edition as a model.

### Recall

- - **q:** Name the five Sangam tinai.
  - **a:** Kurinchi, Mullai, Marutam, Neytal and Palai.
- - **q:** Who led the critical edition of the Mahabharata?
  - **a:** V.S. Sukthankar, from 1919; it took 47 years.
- - **q:** In which language did Sanskrit plays render dialogue of women and minor characters?
  - **a:** Prakrit.

### Common trap

Assuming Sanskrit was the common spoken language of ancient India. NCERT says people spoke Prakrits, the Buddha taught in the people's language, Pali carried Buddhist literature, and Prakrit was used even inside Sanskrit plays for women and minor characters.
