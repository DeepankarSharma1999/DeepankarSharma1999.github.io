## GS-098: Earthquakes and volcanoes

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Powers of ten and a scale that jumps by multiplying

Some scales add and some multiply. A ruler adds: 7 cm is one centimetre more than 6 cm. The earthquake magnitude scale **multiplies**: each whole step upward stands for a much bigger jump in the thing measured. Such a scale is called a **logarithmic** scale. You do not need any mathematics beyond powers of ten to use it.

A **power of ten** is ten multiplied by itself. 10 x 10 = 100 (ten squared, written 10 with a small 2). 10 x 10 x 10 = 1,000 (ten cubed). So the exponent (the small number) counts how many tens you multiply.

The standard reading of the magnitude scale, which is **standard knowledge outside the NCERT extract and should be verified**, is this: one whole magnitude step means the ground-motion amplitude on the seismograph is ten times larger, and the energy released is about 32 times larger (this is 10 raised to the power 1.5, which is 31.6, and the lesson rounds it as 'about 32'). Two steps therefore mean amplitude 10 x 10 = 100 times and energy 31.6 x 31.6 = 1,000 times (exactly 10 to the power 3).

Worked example. A magnitude 7 quake compared with a magnitude 5 quake is two steps higher, so its amplitude is 100 times larger and its energy about 1,000 times larger. Going from magnitude 5 to 6 is one step: energy about 32 times, which is why a 'small' difference in the headline number hides a very large difference in energy.

Why this matters for the examination: NCERT only says that magnitude 'relates to the energy released' and runs from 0 to 10, and that intensity (1 to 12) counts visible damage. A question that asks which scale is logarithmic or what a rise of one unit means is a standard-knowledge question, so the figures above are given with the verify flag.

### 1. Orientation: two outlets for the earth's inner energy

*Objective:* Say in one minute what an earthquake and a volcano are, what the earlier lessons already covered, and what is new in this lesson.

## Two ways the earth lets energy out

The earth's inside is hot, under pressure and slowly moving. Two things happen at the surface because of this, and this lesson is about both.

- An **earthquake** is the shaking of the earth. Energy is released along a break in the rocks and travels outwards as waves (NCERT kegy203, PDF p2).
- A **volcano** is a place where gases, ashes and/or molten rock (lava) escape to the ground (NCERT, PDF p6).

Both are **endogenic** processes, which means they are driven from inside the earth. NCERT's opening page reminds you that the surface is shaped by endogenic and **exogenic** processes together (exogenic means driven from outside, such as wind, water and ice), and that the character of a region is not understood if the endogenic part is ignored (PDF p1).

## What you already have, and what is new

Day 18 lesson GS-096 already taught the focus, epicentre, P, S and surface waves, seismograph, shadow zones and the two scales in brief; Day 19 lesson GS-097 taught the plates and their boundaries. This lesson uses them without re-teaching.

New here:

1. The five **kinds** of earthquake, the two ways to **measure** one, and a little arithmetic to read a magnitude (module 3 and the bridge GS-098-B1).
2. The **effects** of earthquakes, including tsunami, and **where** earthquakes strike, with India (modules 4 and 5).
3. What comes out of a **volcano**, the kinds of volcano and the **landforms** they and their hidden (intrusive) cousins build (modules 6 to 8).
4. Where volcanoes are, famous eruptions, hazards, and the ten official questions (modules 9 and 10).

## The one pattern to hold in mind

Leong's book notes that the world distribution of earthquakes coincides very closely with that of volcanoes (PDF p55). Both sit in the weak, folded or faulted parts of the crust. If you keep only one sentence from this lesson, keep this one: **the shaking belts and the erupting belts are mostly the same belts.** The reason is the plate boundaries of Day 19 lesson GS-097.

### 2. Foundation: eight words and a model of how a fault slips

*Objective:* Define the eight terms in the list (fault, focus and epicentre, magma and lava, active volcano, igneous rock, extrusive and intrusive, pyroclast, seismograph) and describe how a fault releases energy.

## Eight words, in the order you need them

1. **Fault**: a sharp break in the crustal rocks. Rocks along a fault tend to move in opposite directions (NCERT kegy203, PDF p2).
2. **Focus (hypocentre) and epicentre**: the focus is the point where the energy is released; the epicentre is the point on the surface nearest to it, directly above it, and the first to feel the waves (PDF p2).
3. **Magma and lava**: NCERT calls the material in the upper mantle magma and, once it starts moving towards the crust, lava (PDF p6). Standard usage, and Leong p45, keep magma for molten rock anywhere below the surface, including inside the crust, and say lava only once it pours out on the surface. Use the standard pair in answers.
4. **Active volcano**: one that is releasing, or has recently released, gases, ash and/or lava (PDF p6). Leong adds the words **dormant** and **extinct**, which module 9 tests.
5. **Igneous rock**: rock formed when lava or magma cools. NCERT sorts igneous rocks into **volcanic rocks**, which cool at the surface, and **plutonic rocks**, which cool inside the crust (PDF p7).
6. **Extrusive and intrusive**: Leong says magma that reaches the surface and solidifies makes **extrusive** landforms, while magma that cools inside the crust makes **intrusive** landforms (PDF p45).
7. **Pyroclast**: a solid fragment hurled out of a vent; Leong lists cinders (lapilli), scoria, pumice and volcanic bombs (PDF p49).
8. **Seismograph**: the instrument that records the waves reaching the surface. It records; it is not itself a scale (module 3).

## A model of how a fault slips

NCERT describes it in one chain of ideas (PDF p2). The rocks on the two sides of a fault want to move apart. The weight of the rock lying above presses them together, so **friction** locks them. The tendency to move eventually overcomes the friction. The blocks, which have been deformed, then slide past each other abruptly, and the stored energy is released as waves travelling in all directions.

An everyday analogy (original): drag a heavy book across a table with a long rubber band. The band stretches and stores energy; then the book suddenly jumps. The pulling is the slow plate motion, the friction is the lock, and the jump is the earthquake.

## Where magma comes from

Recall Day 18 lesson GS-096: molten rock finds its way to the surface from the weak mantle zone, the **asthenosphere** (NCERT PDF p6). A volcano shows something from the mantle; an earthquake is the crust letting go of stress. One cause: the earth's moving interior.

### 3. Core: five kinds of earthquake, and two ways to measure one

*Objective:* Name NCERT's five kinds of earthquake with their triggers, and distinguish magnitude (energy, Richter) from intensity (damage, Mercalli).

## Five kinds, by cause (NCERT, PDF pp3-4)

| Kind | What triggers it |
|---|---|
| **Tectonic** | Sliding of rocks along a fault plane; the most common kind |
| **Volcanic** | A special class of tectonic earthquake, confined to areas of active volcanoes |
| **Collapse** | In areas of intense mining, roofs of underground mines collapse, causing minor tremors |
| **Explosion** | Ground shaking from the explosion of chemical or nuclear devices |
| **Reservoir-induced** | Earthquakes in the areas of large reservoirs |

NCERT gives a trigger for the tectonic, collapse and explosion kinds, and only a place (active volcanoes, large reservoirs) for the other two. Standard accounts attribute reservoir quakes to the weight and seepage of impounded water and cite Koyna (Maharashtra, 1967) as the Indian case (**outside the extract, verify**). A reading rule that fits NCERT's own wording: tectonic and volcanic quakes are natural; the other three are tied to mines, explosions or reservoirs, that is, to human works. The rule holds for all five rows of the table.

## How often

NCERT (PDF p5): quakes of magnitude 8 and above occur about once in one to two years, tiny ones almost every minute. Leong (PDF p54): more than 50,000 are recorded each year. These are different slices of one range; do not force the numbers to match.

## Two ways to measure

NCERT (PDF p4) says earthquakes are scaled either by **magnitude** or by **intensity**.

| | Magnitude | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | Richter scale | Mercalli scale (after an Italian seismologist) |
| Measures | The energy released at the source | The visible damage caused, place by place |
| NCERT range | 0 to 10 | 1 to 12 |
| Values per earthquake | One | Many: it changes with distance and ground |

**A seismograph is the instrument that records the waves; it is not a scale.** Leong (PDF p54) loosely says the seismograph measures intensity and the Richter scale gives magnitude; use NCERT's cleaner statement.

## One quake, many intensities (a guided example)

One earthquake: a brick house 30 km from the epicentre cracks badly (high intensity); at 400 km a hanging lamp only swings (low). Magnitude is one number for the event; intensity differs at each place. Equal magnitudes under an empty desert and under a crowded city of weak walls differ greatly in damage: write *same magnitude, different damage*. (Original illustration.)

## What the numbers do not tell you (all flagged)

- NCERT's 0 to 10 is a teaching range; the scale has no fixed top, and modern practice uses a related moment-magnitude scale (**verify**).
- Each step up in magnitude means about ten times the amplitude and about 32 times the energy (bridge GS-098-B1; **verify**).
- Leong says the largest earthquake recorded is 8.6; that figure is dated, and larger events are on record (**verify**). Do not quote it as a record.
- NCERT (PDF p4): the shaking lasts seconds, but effects can be devastating above magnitude 5; treat '5' as a rough threshold.

### 4. Core: the effects of an earthquake, and tsunami

*Objective:* List NCERT's twelve immediate effects, split them into landform-related and life-and-property effects, and explain how, where and with what results a tsunami forms.

## NCERT's twelve immediate effects (PDF p4)

(i) Ground shaking; (ii) differential ground settlement; (iii) land and mud slides; (iv) soil liquefaction; (v) ground lurching; (vi) avalanches; (vii) ground displacement; (viii) floods from dam and levee failures; (ix) fires; (x) structural collapse; (xi) falling objects; (xii) tsunami.

NCERT sorts them: the **first six** have some bearing on **landforms**; the **others** are the effects that cause immediate concern to the life and property of people. NCERT groups them by position in its list, not by a stated test: (i) to (vi) are the landform group, so soil liquefaction (the ground behaving like a liquid when shaken) is in it. Ground displacement (vii) sounds like a landform effect but NCERT places it in the second group.

Leong (PDF p54) adds fires after gas mains break, buckled roads and railways, cut cables, fissures and landslides, with damage thinning out from the epicentre like ripples.

## Tsunami: what the book says

NCERT (PDF p4): tsunami waves are **generated by the tremors**; the tsunami is not the earthquake itself. A tsunami happens only if the **epicentre is below oceanic waters** and the magnitude is sufficiently high.

Leong (PDF p54) calls tsunamis 'gigantic tidal waves'; they have nothing to do with the tide, and the word is Japanese (Leong p54). Use 'tsunami' in answers.

## How the wave forms (standard knowledge; verify)

The usual mechanism is a sudden vertical movement of a large patch of sea floor, typically at a subduction zone where one plate is forced under another (Day 19 lesson GS-097). The water above is pushed up or dropped, and the disturbance spreads as a series of very long waves. In deep water the wave is low and fast; in shallow water near the coast it slows and grows tall. Large undersea landslides and eruptions can also cause tsunamis.

Leong's Krakatoa case (PDF p51) is volcanic: the 1883 eruption set up waves over 30 m high that drowned 36,000 people in coastal Indonesia. His Lisbon case (PDF p55): in 1755 an abrupt subsidence of the Atlantic floor west of Lisbon set up waves as high as 11 m.

## Where

Tsunamis follow the plate-boundary belts of Day 19: the Pacific rim above all, and any boundary under the sea. Leong's examples: the most destructive tsunami in history followed the December 2004 earthquake in the Indian Ocean (Banda Aceh in Indonesia is Leong's named place; over 230,000 deaths in all, across the affected countries); a tsunami in Japan in 2011 led to a nuclear crisis at a coastal plant and an economic crisis in the country (PDF p54). The 2017 Mains question states that the 2004 tsunami brought havoc on fourteen countries including India.

## Consequences (build the answer in four layers)

1. **Life and health**: drowning, injury, disease, displaced families.
2. **Property and infrastructure**: ports, roads, power, coastal industry; Japan 2011 shows a nuclear dimension.
3. **Economy**: fisheries, tourism, farmland salted by seawater (verify), national budgets.
4. **Environment**: coastal erosion, damaged reefs and mangroves (verify).

## Preparedness (the 2017 question mentions NDMA 2010; verify the guideline details)

Standard elements: a **tsunami early-warning system** (India's Tsunami Early Warning Centre, housed at INCOIS, Hyderabad, was set up after the 2004 disaster; operational 2007, verify), coastal zone regulation, sound building and evacuation, mangrove cover, community drills. State these as 'typical content of the NDMA approach', not as exact clauses.

### 5. Core: where earthquakes strike, and India's exposure

*Objective:* State the world distribution of earthquakes with Leong's shares, explain it from plate boundaries, and write a sourced paragraph on why India is vulnerable, flagging what is outside the extracts.

## The world pattern (Leong, PDF p55)

Leong says the world distribution of earthquakes **coincides very closely with that of volcanoes**. The regions of greatest seismicity are the Circum-Pacific areas, with the epicentres and the most frequent occurrences along the Pacific Ring of Fire; as many as **70 per cent** of earthquakes occur in the Circum-Pacific belt, and another **20 per cent** in the Mediterranean-Himalayan belt, which includes Asia Minor, the Himalayas and parts of north-west China. Elsewhere the crust is relatively stable, though nowhere is immune.

Check: 70 + 20 = 90, leaving about 10 per cent elsewhere; Leong writes 'it is said', so treat the shares as approximate.

## Why these belts (link to Day 19 lesson GS-097)

- **Pacific rim**: plates descend at trenches (subduction), so quakes are frequent and some are deep.
- **Mediterranean-Himalayan belt**: plates collide; the Indian plate has pushed into Eurasia since about 40-50 million years ago (NCERT, via GS-097).
- **Ridges**: plates move apart and the central parts erupt often (NCERT kegy203 p7); shallow foci come from kegy204 via Day 19 lesson GS-097.

Keep the triangle *rim, collision belt, ridge*; draw the world map with the 70 and 20 per cent labels.

## India: what the extracts give

- NCERT (PDF p5): the continental crust is as much as 70 km thick in the Himalayan region, the largest crust thickness the book gives (the mean for continental crust is around 30 km).
- Day 19 lesson GS-097: the Himalaya are young, weak and flexible, with faults and folds, and are still rising as the Indian plate pushes north.
- NCERT's photograph on PDF p5 shows the damaged Aman Setu at Uri after an earthquake; no date or magnitude is given.

The lesson's own inference (not an NCERT sentence): a plate still pressing against Asia keeps loading its northern margin with stress, and Leong puts the Himalayas in a major earthquake belt. Peninsular India is the older, more stable block (NCERT kegy102 via GS-097), but 'nowhere is immune' (Leong).

## Standard-knowledge additions (all flagged verify)

- India's building code divides the country into **seismic zones**, with the highest-hazard zone including the Himalayan belt, the North-East, the Kutch region and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. A revised code reported in 2025 introduced a Zone VI covering the Himalayan arc; verify before quoting zone numbers.
- Frequently cited Indian earthquakes: Koyna (1967), Uttarkashi (1991), Latur (1993), Chamoli (1999), Bhuj (2001), Kashmir (2005), Sikkim (2011). Do not add casualty or magnitude figures from memory.

## Building a vulnerability answer (2021 Mains GS III)

Use four lenses: **physical** (plate collision, young mountains, thick crust, fault systems), **human** (dense settlements and weak buildings on soft ground), **economic** (infrastructure and housing loss, recovery cost) and **preparedness** (building codes, drills and relief; the exact time of a coming quake cannot be predicted, standard knowledge, verify). The 2021 question limits events to the last three decades (about 1991 onward): choose from Uttarkashi to Sikkim; Koyna 1967 is outside that window and is better kept as the reservoir-induced example. Mark details to verify.

### 6. Core: what comes out of a volcano, and two kinds of lava

*Objective:* List NCERT's volcanic products, separate gases, liquids and solids, and contrast basic and acid lava on silica, fluidity, speed, shape and explosiveness.

## The product list (NCERT, PDF p6)

The material that reaches the ground includes:

- **lava flows**,
- **pyroclastic debris**,
- **volcanic bombs**,
- **ash and dust**, and
- **gases** such as **nitrogen compounds**, **sulphur compounds** and minor amounts of chlorine (the extract spells it 'chlorene'), hydrogen and argon.

Sorted by state: liquid (lava), solid (pyroclastic debris, bombs, ash and dust) and gas. The 2024 Prelims item asked which of four named products are volcanic; all four of its entries (pyroclastic debris, ash and dust, nitrogen compounds, sulphur compounds) appear in NCERT's list. Leong gives a wider gas list: carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulphide ('sulphurated hydrogen'), small nitrogen amounts, chlorine and other volatiles (PDF p47), so a product that is missing from NCERT's list may still be volcanic; do not call it false only because NCERT omits it.

## Sizes of solid fragments (Leong, PDF p49)

The finest particles are **volcanic dust**, which can circle the world several times; ash falling as 'black snow' can bury houses. Coarser fragments are **pyroclasts**: cinders or lapilli, scoria, pumice and volcanic bombs. Krakatoa's dust (1883) caused brilliant sunsets round the world (PDF p51).

## Magma, gas and the push upward

Magma rises from the weak zone of the mantle (GS-096). Leong notes that the gases dissolved in magma make the lava more mobile and more explosive (PDF p47). Think of a shaken soda bottle (original analogy).

## Two kinds of lava (Leong, PDF p47)

| Feature | Basic lava | Acid lava |
|---|---|---|
| Silica | Poor in silica; rich in iron and magnesium | High percentage of silica |
| Colour and density | Dark, like basalt | Light-coloured, low density |
| Fluidity | Highly fluid | Highly viscous (thick and sticky) |
| Travel | Flows quietly, 16-48 km per hour, spreads far in thin sheets | Flows slowly, seldom travels far |
| Resulting volcano | Gentle, wide shield or dome | Steep-sided cone |
| Eruption style | Not very explosive | Loud explosions; bombs; sometimes a spine or plug (Mt. Pelee) |

Leong also calls basic lavas the hottest (about 1,000 degrees Celsius) and gives acid lava a 'high melting point'; the second claim runs against the usual teaching that silica-rich lavas erupt cooler, so mark it **check** and learn only the fluid-versus-viscous contrast.

## Why viscosity matters

Fluid lava lets gas escape quietly and spreads far; viscous lava traps gas, plugs the vent, and ends in an explosion. This one idea explains most of the next module. Quote the 16-48 km per hour as 'Leong gives' (verify).

### 7. Core: shield, composite, caldera, flood basalt and ridge volcanoes

*Objective:* Describe NCERT's five volcano types with Leong's additions (cinder cone, lava dome, spine), and test the 'fluid means gentle' rule against its exceptions.

NCERT classifies volcanoes by the **nature of the eruption and the form developed at the surface** (PDF p6).

## 1. Shield volcanoes (NCERT p6-7; Leong p48)

Apart from basalt flows, shield volcanoes are the largest on earth. They are made mostly of **basalt**, a very fluid lava, so they are **not steep** and have low explosivity; they turn explosive if water gets into the vent. The Hawaiian volcanoes are the standard examples; Leong names Mauna Loa and Kilauea. NCERT adds that the fountain of lava can throw out a cone at the vent that develops into a **cinder cone**.

## 2. Composite volcanoes (NCERT p7; Leong p49-50)

Cooler, more viscous lavas, often explosive eruptions, with large amounts of pyroclastic material and ash. These pile up round the vent in layers, so the mountain looks 'composite'. Leong calls them **strato-volcanoes**, the highest and most common; he names Vesuvius, Fuji, Popocatepetl and Chimborazo, and notes that Etna has hundreds of side vents called **parasitic cones**.

## 3. Caldera (NCERT p7; Leong p50)

The **most explosive** kind. They tend to collapse on themselves instead of building a tall structure, leaving a large depression, the **caldera**. NCERT reasons that this shows a huge magma chamber close to the surface. Leong adds that water may collect in a caldera to make a lake, such as Lake Toba (Sumatra) or Crater Lake (Oregon).

## 4. Flood basalt provinces (NCERT p7)

These outpour highly fluid lava that flows long distances and covers thousands of square kilometres in thick sheets. Individual flows can exceed 50 m in thickness and extend for hundreds of kilometres. The **Deccan Traps** are a far larger flood basalt province, now covering most of the Maharashtra plateau and once much more. Leong's equivalents: lava plains and basalt plateaux (Snake Basin, north-west Deccan, Iceland).

## 5. Mid-ocean ridge volcanoes (NCERT p7)

A system of mid-ocean ridges more than **70,000 km long** runs through all the ocean basins; the central portion has frequent eruptions.

## Leong's smaller forms (PDF p49)

**Ash and cinder cones** form from less fluid lavas that explode: steep slopes, a large crater, usually small (seldom over 300 m), often in groups (Paricutin in Mexico). A **spine or plug** forms when lava is so viscous that it hardens in the vent (Mt. Pelee).

## The rule, and its exceptions

Rule of thumb: the more fluid the lava, the gentler the slope and the quieter the eruption; the more viscous, the steeper and more explosive. Test it:

- Shield: fluid, gentle. Fits.
- Composite: viscous, explosive. Fits.
- Flood basalt: fits the fluid-gentle half of the rule but builds a plateau, not a cone.
- Caldera: a collapse form, not a lava type. It is a result, not a lava category.
- Cinder cone: NCERT p7 has fluid basalt fountaining a cinder cone at a shield vent; Leong p49 ties cinder cones to less fluid, explosive lava. Either way the steep slope is a pile of fragments, not of flows, so the rule does not apply.
- Mid-ocean ridge: defined by **place**, not by lava thickness.

Use the rule only for shield versus composite.

### 8. Core: intrusive landforms, geysers and hot springs

*Objective:* Describe batholith, laccolith, lapolith, phacolith, sill, sheet and dyke by shape and position, with the Indian cases NCERT names, and explain geysers and hot springs.

Magma that cools **inside the crust** makes intrusive forms; they show on the surface only when the cover above is worn away (denudation). NCERT PDF pp7-8 and Leong PDF pp45-46 describe the same family.

## Large bodies

- **Batholith**: a large mass of magmatic material that cools deep in the crust and forms large domes; granitic; the cooled part of magma chambers. It appears on the surface only after denudation removes the covering. Leong adds that their mode of origin is still debated.
- **Laccolith** (NCERT 'lacolith'): a large dome-shaped body with a **level base**, connected to a pipe-like conduit below; it arches up the rocks above. NCERT says the Karnataka plateau is spotted with granite domal hills, mostly exfoliated, that are examples of laccoliths or batholiths.
- **Lapolith** (Leong 'lopolith'): saucer-shaped, **concave to the sky**.
- **Phacolith**: a wavy lens of intrusive rock at the crest of an anticline or the bottom of a syncline, fed by a conduit from a magma chamber.

## Sheets and walls

- **Sill**: a near-horizontal body; NCERT calls the thinner ones **sheets** and the thick horizontal ones **sills**. Leong says a sill is magma injected along the bedding planes of sedimentary rocks.
- **Dyke**: lava that finds its way through cracks and fissures and solidifies almost **perpendicular** to the ground as a wall-like structure. NCERT says dykes are the most commonly found intrusive forms in **western Maharashtra** and are considered the **feeders** of the eruptions that built the **Deccan Traps**. Leong adds that a dyke may stand as an upstanding wall or a shallow trench after erosion, depending on whether it resists erosion more or less than the surrounding rock.

## A text sketch

Redraw the figure: volcano and vertical dykes, a sill between layers, a laccolith (flat base, domed top), a saucer lapolith, a phacolith lens, and the deep batholith.

## Geysers and hot springs (Leong, PDF pp53-54)

A **geyser** is a fountain of hot water and superheated steam; Leong says it may spout up to about 45 m. It occurs in a thermal or volcanic region where the water below is heated beyond boiling point; the jet is usually emitted with an explosion, often triggered by gases seeping out of heated rocks. Leong says almost all the world's geysers are in three areas: Iceland, Rotorua on North Island of New Zealand, and Yellowstone Park in the USA (Kamchatka and northern Chile also have large fields, verify), where 'Old Faithful' erupts about every 63 minutes on average (as in Leong; verify, recent intervals are longer).

A **hot spring** is water heated at depth that rises gently to the surface without an explosion. Such water carries dissolved minerals; Iceland has thousands of springs, some used to heat houses and pools.

A geyser is an explosive jet, a hot spring a quiet flow; both signal underground heat, neither is a volcano.

### 9. Master: where volcanoes stand, famous eruptions and hazards

*Objective:* Describe the world pattern of volcanoes from Leong, tell the three famous eruptions apart, list volcanic hazards and benefits, and handle the active-dormant-extinct trap.

## The world pattern (Leong, PDF pp52-53)

Leong says there are well over 500 active volcanoes and thousands of dormant and extinct ones. They lie along coastal mountain ranges, as offshore islands and in the midst of oceans, with few in continental interiors. The **Pacific Ring of Fire** holds about two-thirds of the world's volcanoes (his estimate). Read the ring as one chain: from the Aleutian islands to Kamchatka, Japan, the Philippines and Indonesia (Java and Sumatra), then south to the Solomon Islands, New Hebrides, Tonga and North Island of New Zealand; on the other side the chain runs from the Andes through Central America (Guatemala, Costa Rica, Nicaragua) and Mexico up to Alaska.

Elsewhere: in the Atlantic, Iceland and the Azores are active, while Madeira, Ascension, St. Helena, Cape Verde and the Canary Islands are listed by Leong as dormant or extinct (see the checks below). Mediterranean volcanoes follow the Alpine folds: Vesuvius, Etna, Stromboli, Vulcano, the Aegean islands. **The Himalayas have no active volcano.** In Africa, volcanoes lie along the East African Rift (Kilimanjaro and Mt. Kenya, probably extinct) and Mt. Cameroon is active.

**Checks on the book's numbers.** (1) Leong says the Pacific chain extends 'almost 3,200 km', but its end points, the Aleutians and North Island of New Zealand, are about 90 degrees of latitude (about 10,000 km) apart; mark it check. (2) His counts (about 100 Philippines, 40 Andes, 35 Japan, over 70 Indonesia) add to 245, about half of 'well over 500'; later sources give higher counts for Japan and Indonesia (verify). (3) The Canary Islands (La Palma, 2021) and Cape Verde (Fogo, 2014-15) have erupted since; verify. 'Dormant or extinct' can change.

## Three eruptions to tell apart (details in the Master Notes)

- **Vesuvius, AD 79** (above the Bay of Naples, Italy): Pompeii buried under about 6 m of ash; Herculaneum overwhelmed by a mudflow of ash and rain about 15 m thick.
- **Krakatoa, 1883** (small island in the Sunda Strait, midway between Java and Sumatra): the greatest explosion known; two-thirds of the island collapsed, forming a submarine caldera; waves over 30 m high drowned 36,000 people; fine dust caused sunsets round the world.
- **Mt. Pelee, 1902** (Martinique, West Indies): a glowing avalanche (nuee ardente) destroyed St. Pierre, killing nearly all of about 30,000 people; a lava spine later rose about 300 m.

## Hazards and benefits

Hazards in these cases: lava flows; hot glowing avalanches; ash fall; mudflows when rain meets ash; tsunamis from collapse or explosion; dust colouring skies worldwide. Benefits: fertile ash (farmers returned to Vesuvius), geothermal heat and hot springs (Iceland), crater and caldera lakes. 

## The active, dormant, extinct trap

Leong defines **active** as erupting frequently or recently (Cameroon: 1909, 1922, 1954, 1959), **dormant** as known to erupt and showing signs of possible future eruption, and **extinct** as no eruption in historic times while keeping volcanic features (the Jos Plateau). But he warns that Vesuvius and Krakatoa were thought extinct and then erupted violently.

### Notes: Core terms

- Fault: a sharp break in the crustal rocks along which blocks move (NCERT kegy203 p2).
- Focus (hypocentre): the point where the energy is released. Epicentre: the point on the surface directly above the focus, first to feel the waves.
- Magnitude: the size of the earthquake source, related to the energy released (Richter scale). Intensity: the visible damage at a place (Mercalli scale).
- Magma: molten rock below the surface, including inside the crust (standard usage and Leong p45); lava: magma once it pours out on the surface. NCERT p6 is looser: lava once it starts moving towards the crust.
- Volcanic rock: igneous rock cooled at the surface. Plutonic rock: igneous rock cooled in the crust (NCERT p7).
- Pyroclast: a solid fragment thrown from a vent (cinder or lapilli, scoria, pumice, bomb); pyroclastic debris, ash and dust are NCERT's solid products.
- Caldera: the large depression left when a volcano collapses on itself after a violent eruption.
- Tsunami: a sea wave generated by tremors beneath the ocean; not the earthquake itself (NCERT p4).

### Notes: Easily confused pairs

- Focus versus epicentre: depth point versus surface point above it.
- Magnitude versus intensity: energy versus damage; Richter versus Mercalli.
- Seismograph versus scale: the instrument records; scales interpret.
- Magma versus lava: molten rock below the surface (including inside the crust) versus the same material poured out on the surface (standard usage; NCERT p6 words it more loosely).
- Volcanic versus plutonic rock: cooled at the surface versus inside the crust.
- Sill versus dyke: horizontal versus near-vertical; laccolith versus lapolith: domed versus saucer-shaped.
- Geyser versus hot spring: explosive jet versus quiet flow.
- Caldera versus crater: a huge collapse depression versus a smaller vent depression (Leong).

### Core concepts

- Earthquakes and volcanoes are two outlets of internal energy and cluster on the same belts.
- Magnitude is energy at the source (one value); intensity is damage at a place (many values).
- A tsunami is a wave generated by tremors under the ocean, not the earthquake itself.
- Lava composition (silica, viscosity) controls whether a volcano is broad and quiet or steep and explosive.
- Magma that cools inside the crust makes intrusive forms; the Deccan Traps are the outpoured part and dykes are the feeders.

### Practice

**Q.** Define fault, focus and epicentre in one line each.

**A.** A fault is a sharp break in crustal rocks; the focus is where the energy is released; the epicentre is the surface point directly above the focus.

**Q.** What is the difference between magma and lava in standard usage, and how does NCERT word it?

**A.** Magma is molten rock below the surface (also inside the crust); lava is the same material poured out on the surface (standard usage; Leong p45). NCERT p6 calls it lava once it starts moving towards the crust.

**Q.** What is a pyroclast? Name two kinds.

**A.** A solid fragment hurled from a volcanic vent, such as a cinder (lapilli), scoria, pumice or volcanic bomb.

**Q.** What does a seismograph do?

**A.** It records the earthquake waves that reach the surface; it is an instrument, not a scale.

**Q.** Why can two earthquakes of the same magnitude cause very different damage?

**A.** Intensity depends on distance from the epicentre, the ground, the buildings and the population exposed, none of which magnitude captures.

**Q.** Why is the rule 'fluid lava means gentle volcano' only a guide?

**A.** Flood basalts are fluid but form plateaux, a caldera is a collapse form, cinder cones are steep piles of fragments (the rule is about lava flows), and ridge volcanoes are defined by place.

**Q.** Why do earthquake belts and volcano belts overlap?

**A.** Both lie along weak, folded or faulted parts of the crust at plate boundaries (Leong p55; GS-097).

**Q.** Why is a tsunami not simply a big wave caused by wind?

**A.** It is generated by sea-floor tremors or eruptions, not by wind; NCERT requires an oceanic epicentre and sufficient magnitude.

**Q.** A news item says a magnitude 6 earthquake struck a remote mountain valley and a magnitude 5.5 one struck a crowded coastal city. Write two sentences on why the second may be the greater disaster.

**A.** Magnitude measures energy at the source, but disaster depends on intensity at a place: the city has more people, buildings and infrastructure exposed, possibly on softer ground, so the intensity and losses can be higher despite the lower magnitude.

**Q.** A volcano near a coast produces thick, sticky lava, loud explosions and a steep cone. Name the lava type and the volcano type, and list two hazards.

**A.** Acid (viscous) lava building a composite (strato) volcano; hazards: ash fall, glowing avalanches or mudflows, and possibly tsunami if it collapses.
