### Bridge: What a constitution, government, legislature, executive and judiciary are

## The words you need before anything else

A **constitution** is the set of basic rules of a country: it says who gets to make decisions, how those decision-makers are chosen, and what they may never do to the people. NCERT describes four jobs a constitution does: it gives basic rules so that a diverse society can coordinate; it says who has the power to decide (for example, that Parliament makes laws); it sets limits on government, usually through fundamental rights; and it enables the government to pursue the goals of society, such as removing inequality. It also expresses the identity of a people.

A **government** is the machinery that runs the country under those rules. It has three parts, called **organs**:

- The **legislature** makes laws. In India today this is Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) and the state legislatures. In British India the equivalents were called **legislative councils**.
- The **executive** carries out the laws and runs day-to-day administration: today the President, Prime Minister and ministers; in British India the Governor-General (later Viceroy), his **executive council** and the governors of provinces.
- The **judiciary** decides disputes and interprets the law: today the Supreme Court and High Courts; in British India the Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774) and later the Federal Court (1937).

Two more words: a **presidency** was one of the three original British administrative regions (Bengal, Bombay, Madras), and a **province** is the later general term for a British Indian administrative unit, the ancestor of today's states. **Responsible government** means an executive that must answer to, and can be removed by, an elected legislature; most of 1773–1947 is the story of how slowly Britain moved towards it.

### Bridge: Reading a chronology: BCE/CE, century, decade, Act versus Charter Act versus Regulation

## Reading a chronology without getting lost

Historians count years with **BCE** (Before the Common Era) and **CE** (Common Era); every date in this topic is CE, so you can ignore the labels here. A **century** is a block of one hundred years named one higher than its digits: 1773 is in the 18th century, 1858 in the 19th, 1947 in the 20th. A **decade** is a ten-year block: "the 1930s" means 1930–1939.

In this topic three kinds of law appear. An **Act** is a law passed by the British Parliament in London (for example the Regulating Act of 1773). A **Charter Act** is the Act that renewed the East India Company's charter, its royal licence to trade and govern, which Parliament did roughly every twenty years (1793, 1813, 1833, 1853); each renewal was used to change the rules of government. A **Regulation** is a rule made in India by the Governor-General's government before 1833; after the Charter Act of 1833 such laws were called Acts.

Reading technique: when you meet a year, place it in one of the two phases first (Company rule 1773–1858 or Crown rule 1858–1947), then ask which Act is nearest. Build the timeline on paper as you read: 1773, 1784, 1793, 1813, 1833, 1853 | 1858, 1861, 1892, 1909, 1919, 1935, 1947. Say the gaps aloud: "roughly every twenty years under the Company, then irregular under the Crown." Patterns like this are what make dates stick.

### 1. Orientation: the storyline from Company to Crown to Independence

*Objective:* Place the 1773–1947 Acts in two phases and know the four threads to track through every Act.

## What "constitutional development" means here

A constitution is the set of basic rules that says who governs, how they are chosen, and what they may not do (Bridge 1). Between 1773 and 1947 India had no constitution of its own. Instead the British Parliament in London passed a chain of laws, each called an **Act**, that fixed how India was to be governed. Each Act changed the machinery a little: who headed the executive, whether a legislature existed, who sat in it, and how much power the provinces had. UPSC calls this chain "constitutional development" because the Constitution of 1950 was built on this inherited machinery.

## Two phases, one storyline

- **Company rule (1773–1858).** The East India Company came in 1600 as traders under a charter from Queen Elizabeth I. In 1765, after the Battle of Buxar (1764), it obtained the *diwani* (the right to collect revenue and decide civil cases) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa and became a territorial power. From 1773 the British Parliament began to regulate, then control, then take over the Company's political work.
- **Crown rule (1858–1947).** After the Revolt of 1857 the British Crown governed India directly. Indians were slowly associated first with law-making councils, then with the executive; provinces gained autonomy step by step; the Indian Independence Act of 1947 ended British rule.

## Four threads to follow through every Act

1. **Executive**: who runs the administration (Governor-General, his council, later ministers).
2. **Legislature**: is there a separate law-making body, and how much can it do?
3. **Representation**: who gets a seat or a vote, and on what basis (nomination, indirect election, direct election, separate electorates)?
4. **Provincial power**: does power flow to the centre (centralisation) or back to the provinces (devolution)?

A fifth thread is the direction of travel: centralisation from 1773 to its peak in 1833, then a reversal from 1861 that ends in provincial autonomy in 1937.

For each Act ask: what problem was it answering, what did it do for the *first time*, and what did it change in the four threads?

### 2. Company rule I: Regulating Act 1773 to Charter Act 1793

*Objective:* Explain how Parliament first regulated and then controlled the Company, and define 'double government' and 'Charter Act'.

## Regulating Act of 1773: Parliament steps in

By the 1770s the Company collected Bengal's revenue but was badly run. The Regulating Act of 1773 was the British Government's first step to control and regulate the Company's affairs in India. Laxmikanth gives three reasons for its importance: it recognised for the first time that the Company had political and administrative functions, not only trade; it was the first attempt at parliamentary control; and it laid the foundation of a central administration in India.

Its features:

- The Governor of Bengal became **Governor-General of Bengal**, assisted by an Executive Council of four members. Warren Hastings was the first.
- The governors of Bombay and Madras were made **subordinate** to him; earlier the three presidencies were independent of one another. This is the first step of centralisation.
- A **Supreme Court at Calcutta** (set up 1774) with one chief justice and three other judges.
- Company servants were barred from private trade and bribes.
- The Court of Directors (the Company's governing body) had to report to the British Government on revenue, civil and military affairs.

## Amending Act of 1781 (Act of Settlement)

It repaired defects of 1773: the Governor-General, his Council and Company servants were exempted from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction for official acts, and revenue matters were excluded from it.

## Pitt's India Act of 1784: double government

Introduced by Prime Minister William Pitt, it drew a line between the Company's **commercial** functions (left with the Court of Directors) and its **political** functions (placed under a new **Board of Control** in London). Two bodies now supervised India, hence "double government". Two things became true for the first time: the Company's territories were called "British possessions in India", and the British Government gained supreme control over the Company's civil, military and revenue administration.

## Act of 1786 and Charter Act of 1793

The Act of 1786 gave Cornwallis power to override his council and the post of Commander-in-Chief. The Charter Act of 1793 extended the overriding power to all future Governors-General, tightened control over Bombay and Madras, and renewed the Company's trade monopoly for twenty years.

### 3. Company rule II: Charter Acts of 1813, 1833 and 1853

*Objective:* Distinguish the three Charter Acts by their 'firsts': end of monopoly and Crown sovereignty, Governor-General of India, and a separate legislative council.

## Charter Act of 1813: the monopoly breaks

- Ended the Company's trade monopoly in India; Indian trade was opened to all British merchants. The monopoly over **tea** and **trade with China** continued.
- Asserted the **sovereignty of the British Crown** over the Company's territories in India.
- Allowed Christian missionaries into India and provided for western education.
- Authorised local governments to impose taxes.

## Charter Act of 1833: the peak of centralisation

- The Governor-General of Bengal became **Governor-General of India**, holding all civil and military powers. For the first time there was a Government of India with authority over the whole British territory in India. Lord William Bentinck was the first.
- Bombay and Madras lost their legislative powers; the Governor-General of India got exclusive law-making power for all British India. Laws under earlier Acts were **Regulations**; laws under this Act were **Acts**.
- The Company ceased to be a commercial body and became purely administrative, holding its territories "in trust" for the Crown.
- It attempted open competition for civil service posts and said Indians should not be barred from office, but the Court of Directors blocked this.

## Charter Act of 1853: legislation becomes a separate function

The last of the Charter Acts, and a constitutional landmark.

- For the first time the **legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General's council were separated. Six new "legislative councillors" were added, forming the Indian (Central) Legislative Council, which worked like a mini-Parliament with parliamentary procedure. Legislation was treated for the first time as a special function needing special machinery.
- **Open competition** for the covenanted (higher) civil service, now open to Indians; the Macaulay Committee on the Indian Civil Service followed in 1854.
- The Company's rule was extended without a fixed period, so Parliament could end it at will.
- First **local representation** in the Central Legislative Council: four of the six new members were appointed by the provincial governments of Madras, Bombay, Bengal and Agra.

Thread check: by 1853 the executive thread has one all-India head (1833), the legislature thread has a separate council (1853), the representation thread has only British officials, and the provincial thread is at its most centralised.

### 4. Crown rule begins: 1858, 1861, 1892 and 1909

*Objective:* Describe the transfer to the Crown and the step-by-step association of Indians with councils, ending with separate electorates in 1909.

## Government of India Act of 1858: the Crown takes over

Passed after the Revolt of 1857, this "Act for the Better Government of India" abolished the East India Company and transferred its territories, powers and revenues to the British Crown.

- India was to be governed by, and in the name of, Her Majesty. The Governor-General of India was also designated **Viceroy**, the Crown's direct representative; Lord Canning was the first.
- **Double government ended**: both the Board of Control and the Court of Directors were abolished.
- A new office, **Secretary of State for India**, a member of the British Cabinet answerable to Parliament, received full control over Indian administration, assisted by a 15-member advisory **Council of India** which he chaired.

Laxmikanth (quoting Kashyap) stresses that this improved supervision *in England* without substantially changing government *in India*.

## Indian Councils Act of 1861: Indians enter law-making

After 1857 the British sought Indian cooperation. Three Acts (1861, 1892, 1909) followed this "policy of association".

- The Viceroy nominated some Indians as non-official members of his expanded council; in 1862 Canning nominated three, including the Raja of Benaras and the Maharaja of Patiala.
- **Decentralisation began**: legislative powers were restored to Bombay and Madras, reversing the centralising trend that had peaked in 1833. New councils were created for Bengal (1862), North-Western Provinces (1886) and Punjab (1897); Laxmikanth traces this devolution to provincial autonomy in 1937.
- The **portfolio system** started by Canning in 1859 (each council member in charge of departments) got legal recognition.
- The Viceroy could issue **ordinances** without the council during an emergency, valid for six months.

## Indian Councils Act of 1892

- More additional (non-official) members in central and provincial councils, but the official majority was kept.
- Councils could **discuss the budget** and **ask questions** of the executive.
- Non-official members were nominated on the recommendation of bodies such as provincial councils, municipalities and universities: a limited, indirect use of election, though the word was not used.

## Indian Councils Act of 1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms)

- The Central legislative council grew from 16 to 60 members.
- Official majority kept at the centre; **non-official majority** allowed in provinces; supplementary questions and budget resolutions permitted.
- Indians were associated with the **executive councils** for the first time: Satyendra Prasad Sinha became Law Member of the Viceroy's executive council.
- **Separate electorates** for Muslims: Muslim members elected only by Muslim voters. Minto is called the father of communal electorates; the Muslim League (founded at Dacca, 1906) had demanded this.

### 5. The 1919 Act, Simon Commission, Round Tables, Communal Award and Poona Pact

*Objective:* Define dyarchy, list the 'firsts' of 1919, and connect the Simon Commission, Round Table Conferences, Communal Award and Poona Pact to the 1935 Act.

## The August Declaration and the 1919 Act

On 20 August 1917 the British Government declared for the first time that its goal was the gradual introduction of **responsible government** in India, an executive answerable to an elected legislature. The Government of India Act of 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, in force from 1921) followed.

- **Central and provincial subjects separated**; each legislature made laws on its own list. Yet the overall structure stayed centralised and unitary.
- **Dyarchy in the provinces**: provincial subjects were split into *transferred* subjects (administered by the Governor with ministers responsible to the legislative council) and *reserved* subjects (administered by the Governor and his executive council, not responsible to the council). "Dyarchy" comes from Greek *di-arche*, double rule. Laxmikanth calls the experiment largely unsuccessful. The extract does not list which subjects fell in which half; standard textbooks place law and order, land revenue and justice among reserved subjects and local self-government, education and health among transferred ones (confirm in review).
- **Bicameralism and direct elections** introduced for the first time: the central legislature became two Houses, a Council of State (upper) and a Legislative Assembly (lower), most members directly elected.
- Three of the six members of the Viceroy's executive council were to be Indian.
- Separate electorates extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans.
- A limited **franchise** based on property, tax or education.
- Provincial budgets separated from the central budget; a statutory commission to review the Act after ten years.

## Simon Commission (1927–1930)

Appointed in November 1927 under Sir John Simon: seven members, all British, so Indian parties boycotted it ("Simon Go Back"). Its 1930 report recommended abolishing dyarchy, extending responsible government in the provinces, a federation of British India and the princely states, and continuing communal electorates.

## Round Table Conferences

Three Round Table Conferences with British, British Indian and princely-state representatives followed, then a White Paper and a Joint Select Committee whose recommendations became the 1935 Act.

## Communal Award and Poona Pact (1932)

In August 1932 Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald's **Communal Award** continued separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans and extended them to the depressed classes (Scheduled Castes). Gandhi, then in Yerawada Jail at Poona, began a fast unto death against this extension. The resulting **Poona Pact** between Congress leaders and leaders of the depressed classes kept a joint Hindu electorate but gave **reserved seats** to the depressed classes. This "joint electorate plus reserved seats" formula is the one independent India kept.

### 6. Government of India Act 1935 and Indian Independence Act 1947

*Objective:* List the structural features of the 1935 Act, explain which parts operated and which never did, and state what the 1947 Act ended and created.

## Government of India Act of 1935

A long and detailed Act (321 Sections, 10 Schedules), described as the second milestone towards fully responsible government.

- **All-India Federation** of provinces and princely states, with powers divided into three lists: Federal (59 items), Provincial (54) and Concurrent (36); residuary powers went to the Viceroy. The federation never came into being because the princely states did not join.
- **Provincial autonomy** replaced dyarchy in the provinces: provinces became autonomous units with responsible government, the Governor acting on the advice of ministers answerable to the provincial legislature. It began in 1937, when Congress formed ministries in 7 of 11 provinces. Only the Congress ministries resigned, in 1939, when Britain refused to promise independence after the war; provincial autonomy did not end everywhere, and it continued in other provinces such as Bengal, Punjab and Sind. In the provinces the Congress ministries left, the Governors took over under Section 93 of the 1935 Act (Governor's rule); the Section 93 detail and the names of the continuing provinces are standard knowledge, not in the extract, so verify them.
- Dyarchy at the centre was provided for but never operated.
- **Bicameralism** in six of eleven provinces: Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United Provinces.
- Separate electorates extended further, to depressed classes, women and labour.
- The Council of India of 1858 was abolished.
- Provided for a **Reserve Bank of India**, Federal, Provincial and Joint Public Service Commissions, and a **Federal Court** (set up 1937).

## Indian Independence Act of 1947

Context: Attlee's statement of 20 February 1947, Muslim League agitation for partition, and the **Mountbatten Plan** of 3 June 1947. The Act (Royal Assent 18 July 1947) gave the plan effect.

- British rule ended; India became an independent and sovereign state from 15 August 1947.
- Two dominions, India and Pakistan, each with the right to secede from the British Commonwealth.
- The office of Viceroy was abolished; each dominion got a **Governor-General** appointed by the King on the advice of the dominion cabinet. The British Government had no further responsibility for either.
- The **Constituent Assemblies** could frame and adopt any constitution, repeal any British Act including this one, and legislate for their territories meanwhile; no later British Act applied unless the dominion adopted it.
- The office of Secretary of State for India was abolished.
- **British paramountcy** over the princely states lapsed; states were free to join India, join Pakistan, or remain independent.
- Until new constitutions were framed, each dominion was governed under the **1935 Act**, which it could modify.
- The Governor-General and provincial governors became constitutional (nominal) heads acting on ministerial advice; the title "Emperor of India" was dropped.

Mountbatten was the first Governor-General of the Indian Dominion.

### 7. Master view: four threads and what the 1950 Constitution inherited

*Objective:* Narrate the evolution of executive, legislature, representation and provincial power as continuous threads and state what the Constitution borrowed from the 1935 Act and abroad.

## Thread 1: Executive

Governor-General of Bengal (1773) → of India (1833) → Viceroy (1858) → nominal head on ministerial advice (1947). Control from London: Board of Control (1784) → Secretary of State with Council of India (1858) → Council abolished (1935) → Secretary of State abolished (1947). Indians in the executive: Sinha (1909); three of six members (1919); responsible provincial ministers (1937).

## Thread 2: Legislature

No separate legislature until 1853; Indians nominated (1861); budget and questions (1892); larger councils (1909); bicameral centre (1919); six bicameral provinces (1935).

## Thread 3: Representation

Nomination (1861) → indirect "recommendation" (1892) → separate electorates for Muslims (1909) → direct elections, more separate electorates, limited franchise (1919) → Communal Award and Poona Pact (1932), franchise of about 10 per cent (1935). Communal representation grew with every Act after 1909; the Constitution of 1950 rejected separate electorates and kept the Poona Pact model of reserved seats with a joint electorate.

## Thread 4: Provincial power

Centralisation: presidencies subordinated (1773), sole legislative power to the Governor-General (1833). Devolution: provincial legislatures restored (1861), subject lists and dyarchy (1919), provincial autonomy (1935). The Constituent Assembly itself was elected indirectly by assemblies set up under the 1935 Act (NCERT).

## What the 1950 Constitution inherited

The 1935 Act is the largest single structural source of the Constitution. Standard textbooks list from it the federal scheme with three lists, the office of Governor, the Federal Court model, public service commissions and emergency provisions. (This list comes from Laxmikanth's chapter on salient features, which is not among today's extracts; it is flagged for review and is covered again in GS-165.) The Assembly also borrowed from abroad, as NCERT tabulates: parliamentary government, rule of law, the Speaker and law-making procedure from Britain; Fundamental Rights, judicial review and judicial independence from the USA; Directive Principles from Ireland; liberty, equality and fraternity from France; a strong centre and residuary powers from Canada. Ambedkar's defence: the only new things possible so late are variations that suit the country's needs.

### 8. Examination application: question shapes, traps and elimination

*Objective:* Recognise the four Prelims question shapes for this topic, the standard traps, and the Mains structure examiners expect.

## How UPSC asks this topic

Prelims questions come in four shapes. (1) *Which Act did X?* The 2023 question on which Act made the Governor-General of Bengal Governor-General of India is this type; module 3 points to 1833. (2) *Statements about one Act.* The 2019 question on the Charter Act of 1813 gave three statements; two of them (end of monopoly except tea and China trade; assertion of Crown sovereignty) match module 3, while "revenues of India now controlled by the British Parliament" belongs to a later stage: control of Indian revenues by the Crown and Parliament came with the Government of India Act of 1858 (module 4), when the Company's territories, powers and revenues passed to the Crown, so the statement is anachronistic for 1813. (3) *Define a term.* The 2017 question asked what "dyarchy" refers to; module 5 defines it as the division of provincial subjects into two categories, not two Houses or two capitals. (4) *Classify items.* The 2022 question asked which were "reserved" subjects under 1919; the extract does not list subjects, so this is a known gap flagged in completeness.

Mains asks for evolution and evaluation: "trace", "examine", "to what extent". The expected structure is chronological threads (module 7), not Act-by-Act lists. Phrases like "second milestone", "legalised communalism", "policy of association" and "provincial autonomy" are high-value vocabulary.

## Examiner traps to expect

- Swapping 1813 (sovereignty asserted) with 1858 (Crown takes over).
- Swapping 1833 (Governor-General of India) with 1858 (Viceroy).
- Attributing the Board of Control to 1773 instead of 1784.
- Confusing "first Indians in the legislative council" (1861, nominated) with "first Indian in the executive council" (1909).
- Treating 1909 as the Act that introduced direct elections; that was 1919.
- Reading "federation" in 1935 as something that operated; it never did.

Elimination: check each statement against the "first time" register. A feature attached to an Act one step too early or too late is almost always the planted error.

### Notes: Key definitions

- Constitution: the basic rules that say who governs, how they are chosen and what they may not do; also expresses the aspirations and identity of a people (NCERT).
- Diwani: the right to collect revenue and administer civil justice, obtained by the Company for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765.
- Charter Act: an Act of the British Parliament renewing the East India Company's charter, used in 1793, 1813, 1833 and 1853 to alter the system of government.
- Double government (1784): supervision of India by two bodies, the Court of Directors (commercial) and the Board of Control (political); ended in 1858.
- Dyarchy (1919): division of provincial subjects into transferred (ministers responsible to the council) and reserved (Governor and executive council).
- Separate electorate: a system in which members of one community are elected only by voters of that community; introduced for Muslims in 1909.
- Responsible government: an executive answerable to and removable by an elected legislature; declared as the British objective on 20 August 1917.
- Provincial autonomy (1935): provinces acting as autonomous units with ministers responsible to the provincial legislature; in force from 1937; the Congress ministries resigned in 1939 and their provinces came under the Governor's rule (Section 93), while provincial autonomy continued in other provinces such as Bengal, Punjab and Sind (verify).

### Notes: Chronology of Acts

- 1600 Company chartered by Elizabeth I; 1765 diwani of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa.
- 1773 Regulating Act: Governor-General of Bengal, Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774), Bombay and Madras subordinated.
- 1781 Amending Act (Act of Settlement): limits on Supreme Court jurisdiction.
- 1784 Pitt's India Act: Board of Control; double government; 'British possessions in India'.
- 1786 Act: Cornwallis given overriding power and Commander-in-Chief post.
- 1793 Charter Act: overriding power extended; monopoly renewed 20 years.
- 1813 Charter Act: monopoly ended except tea and China; Crown sovereignty asserted; missionaries; western education.
- 1833 Charter Act: Governor-General of India (Bentinck); Company purely administrative; Regulations become Acts.
- 1853 Charter Act: separate legislative council; open competition; local representation; no fixed term for Company rule.
- 1858 Government of India Act: Company abolished; Viceroy (Canning); Secretary of State for India; Council of India.
- 1861 Indian Councils Act: Indians nominated; provincial legislatures restored; portfolio system; ordinances.
- 1892 Indian Councils Act: budget discussion, questions; indirect election via recommendation.
- 1909 Morley-Minto: council of 60; separate electorates; Indian in executive council (Sinha).
- 1917 August Declaration; 1919 Montagu-Chelmsford Act (in force 1921): dyarchy, bicameralism, direct elections.

- 1935 Government of India Act: federation (never formed), provincial autonomy (1937–39), three lists, Federal Court (1937), RBI.
- 1947 Indian Independence Act: two dominions from 15 August 1947; Viceroy and Secretary of State abolished; paramountcy lapsed.

### Notes: Who was first

- First Governor-General of Bengal: Warren Hastings (1773 Act).
- First Governor-General of India: Lord William Bentinck (1833 Act).
- First Viceroy of India: Lord Canning (1858 Act).
- First Indians nominated to the Viceroy's legislative council (1862): Raja of Benaras, Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao.
- First Indian in the Viceroy's executive council: Satyendra Prasad Sinha, Law Member (1909 Act).
- First Governor-General of the Dominion of India: Lord Mountbatten; first Prime Minister: Jawaharlal Nehru.

### Notes: Centralisation versus devolution

- Centralisation ran from 1773 to its peak in 1833; devolution ran from 1861 to provincial autonomy in 1935 (in force 1937).

### Notes: Representation: how Indians entered the councils

- 1853: four of six legislative councillors appointed by provincial governments (local, not Indian, representation).
- 1861: Indians nominated as non-official members.
- 1892: non-officials nominated on recommendation of local bodies, universities, chambers; indirect election in practice.
- 1909: separate electorates for Muslims; non-official majority in provinces; Indian in executive council.
- 1919: direct elections; bicameral centre; separate electorates for Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans; franchise by property, tax or education.
- 1932: Communal Award extends separate electorates to depressed classes; Poona Pact replaces this with reserved seats in a joint electorate.
- 1935: separate electorates also for depressed classes, women and labour; about 10 per cent franchise.
- 1950: universal adult suffrage; separate electorates abolished; reserved seats retained.

### Notes: Cause and effect chains

- Company misrule in Bengal after 1765 → Regulating Act 1773 → clash of Supreme Court with Government → Amending Act 1781.
- Revolt of 1857 → Government of India Act 1858 (Crown rule) → need for Indian cooperation → Councils Acts 1861, 1892, 1909.
- August Declaration 1917 → Government of India Act 1919 → statutory review clause → Simon Commission 1927 → boycott → Round Table Conferences → White Paper → Joint Select Committee → Government of India Act 1935.
- Communal Award 1932 → Gandhi's fast → Poona Pact → reserved seats with joint electorate, retained in 1950.
- Attlee's statement (20 February 1947) → League agitation → Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947) → Indian Independence Act 1947.

### Notes: Legacy in the 1950 Constitution

- From the 1935 Act (standard textbook list, flagged for review): federal scheme with lists, office of Governor, judiciary, public service commissions, emergency provisions, administrative detail.
- From Britain (NCERT): parliamentary form, first-past-the-post, rule of law, Speaker, law-making procedure.
- From the USA: Fundamental Rights, judicial review, independence of the judiciary.
- From Ireland: Directive Principles; from France: liberty, equality, fraternity; from Canada: strong centre and residuary powers.
- The Constituent Assembly (first sitting 9 December 1946) was elected indirectly by provincial assemblies formed under the 1935 Act; it adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, in force 26 January 1950.

### Notes: Commonly confused pairs

- 1813 asserted Crown sovereignty; 1858 transferred government to the Crown.
- 1833 created the Governor-General of India; 1858 renamed him Viceroy.
- 1861 nominated Indians to the legislative council; 1909 put an Indian in the executive council.
- 1892 used indirect 'recommendation'; 1919 introduced direct elections.
- Dyarchy was introduced in the provinces by 1919 and abolished there by 1935, which proposed it at the centre instead (never operated).
- The Board of Control (1784) and the Court of Directors were both abolished in 1858; the Council of India (1858) was abolished in 1935.

### Memory skeleton

- Two phases: Company rule 1773-1858, Crown rule 1858-1947
- Company Acts: 1773, 1784, 1813, 1833, 1853
- Crown and councils: 1858, 1861, 1892, 1909
- Dyarchy 1919 and the road to 1935 (Simon, Round Tables, Communal Award, Poona Pact)
- 1935 Act: three lists, provincial autonomy, federation never formed
- 1947 Independence Act; 1935 Act as interim constitution
- Four threads: executive, legislature, representation, provincial power

### Last-week summary

- 1773 GG of Bengal + Supreme Court Calcutta (1774); 1784 Board of Control, double government; 1813 monopoly ends except tea and China, Crown sovereignty asserted.
- 1833 GG of India (Bentinck), Regulations become Acts; 1853 separate legislative council, open competition; 1858 Company abolished, Viceroy (Canning), Secretary of State + 15-member Council of India.
- Representation: 1861 Indians nominated; 1892 indirect recommendation; 1909 separate electorates, Sinha as Law Member; 1919 direct elections, bicameral centre, dyarchy (in force 1921).
- 1935: lists 59/54/36, provincial autonomy (1937), Federal Court (1937), federation and central dyarchy never operated; 1947: two dominions from 15 August, paramountcy lapsed.
