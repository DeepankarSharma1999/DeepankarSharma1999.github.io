### Bridge: Bridge: element, mineral, rock and waves in plain words

## Four everyday ideas, defined once

An **element** is a pure substance made of one kind of atom: oxygen, silicon, iron and gold are elements. An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element. A **compound** is two or more elements joined in a fixed ratio, such as silicon dioxide (silica) made of silicon and oxygen.

A **mineral** is a natural substance, usually a compound, with a definite chemical make-up and definite physical properties (hardness, colour, shape). NCERT Class XII puts it as a natural substance of organic or inorganic origin with definite chemical and physical properties (legy205, PDF p1). A **rock** is a mixture of minerals packed together; Leong defines rocks as aggregates of minerals (PDF p29). So the ladder is element, then mineral, then rock.

A **wave** is a disturbance that moves through a material and carries energy. A sound wave is the simplest example: the air is squeezed and stretched, but the air itself does not travel from the speaker to your ear. In an **earthquake wave** the material is rock. Two directions matter: the direction the wave travels, and the direction the rock particles shake. If they shake along the travel direction the wave is a push-and-pull wave; if they shake across it the wave is a side-to-side wave. The two kinds behave very differently in liquids, which is the key to the whole lesson.

### 1. Orientation: the earth as layers, and how they are read

*Objective:* State in one minute what the earth is made of, in layers, and name the two big ways scientists learn about layers nobody can visit.

## The picture first

NCERT opens by asking whether the earth is a solid ball like a cricket ball or a hollow ball with a thick cover of rock. The answer is neither. The earth is built in **layers**: a thin hard skin (the **crust**), a very thick rocky layer (the **mantle**) and a metal-rich centre (the **core**). No person has been to the centre and none can go, so every statement about the inside is built from evidence, mostly indirect (NCERT kegy203, PDF p1).

## Words to fix before anything else

- **Crust**: the outermost solid, brittle part of the earth.
- **Mantle**: the thick layer below the crust, beginning at a boundary called the Moho and reaching 2,900 km depth (NCERT).
- **Core**: the innermost part, made mainly of iron and nickel.
- **Lithosphere**: in NCERT, the crust plus the uppermost mantle, 10-200 km thick. Leong uses the word for the crust alone, so expect both uses in books; learn the NCERT meaning for the examination.
- **Magma** is molten rock below the surface; **lava** is the same material once it is moving towards, or has reached, the surface (NCERT p6).
- **Seismic waves** are the vibrations an earthquake sends out. **Seismograph**: the instrument that records them.
- **Mineral** and **rock**: see bridge GS-096-B1.

## The whole lesson in one table

| Layer | Where it is (NCERT) | State |
|---|---|---|
| Crust | oceanic crust about 5 km thick on average, continental about 30 km, up to about 70 km in the Himalayan region | solid, brittle |
| Mantle | from the Moho to 2,900 km depth; upper part includes the weak asthenosphere | solid, with a weak zone in the upper part |
| Core | 2,900 km to the centre; Earth radius about 6,378 km | outer core liquid, inner core solid |

## The two ways of reading the inside

1. **Sampling**: mines, deep drill holes and volcanoes bring up real material (direct evidence).
2. **Listening**: earthquake waves travel through the whole planet and change speed and direction as they cross different materials. Reading them is the most important indirect source (module 3 and 4).

## Scope and plan

Plate movement and the landforms of volcanoes and earthquakes are a later lesson; here they appear only as **messengers from below**. First pass: modules 1 to 5, then 7 and 8. Modules 6, 9 and 10 can follow on a later sitting.

### 2. Foundation: direct and indirect sources of knowledge about the interior

*Objective:* List the direct and indirect sources of information about the earth's interior and state what each can and cannot tell.

## Why the evidence is mostly indirect

The earth's radius is about 6,378 km (NCERT), and nobody can reach the centre or bring back samples. NCERT therefore says most knowledge rests on **estimates and inferences**, with a small part from direct observation and analysis of materials (kegy203, PDF p1).

## Direct sources: real material in the hand

- **Mining.** Surface rock and mining areas supply the most easily available solid earth material. Gold mines in South Africa are as deep as 3 to 4 km; going deeper is not possible because it becomes too hot.
- **Deep drilling.** The Deep Ocean Drilling Project and the Integrated Ocean Drilling Project drill into the crust. NCERT names the deepest drill at Kola, 12 km, and places it in the Arctic Ocean (the borehole is usually described as on the Kola Peninsula of Russia, **verify**). Twelve km is only 0.19 per cent of the 6,378 km radius, so the deepest hole barely scratches the crust.
- **Volcanic eruptions.** Magma thrown onto the surface can be analysed in a laboratory, but it is difficult to say from what depth it came.

## Indirect sources: reasoning from properties

1. **Temperature, pressure and density.** From mining it is known that all three **increase with depth** (NCERT p2). Knowing the total thickness of the earth, scientists estimated their values at each depth. Leong (PDF p47) gives two disagreeing rates: about 1 degree Celsius for every 20 m of descent, which is 50 degrees per km, and 1 degree Fahrenheit for every 65 ft, which is about 28 degrees Celsius per km (python). The usual mean gradient is about 25 to 30 degrees Celsius per km (standard knowledge, **verify**), so the Fahrenheit figure is the plausible one and the 20 m figure looks like a conversion slip (see the Master Notes). With 25-30 degrees per km a 3 km mine is then roughly 75 to 90 degrees hotter than the surface rock, which is why deep mines are so hot. Extended blindly to 2,900 km the rule would give tens of thousands of degrees, but Leong's own estimate for the core is 1,927 degrees Celsius, so the rise must slow down with depth. This is an inference, not a measurement.
2. **Meteors.** Meteorites that reach the earth are solid bodies made of material the same as or similar to our planet's. They are not from the earth's interior, but their material and structure are similar, so they are a clue.
3. **Gravity.** Gravity is greater near the poles and less at the equator, because the equator is farther from the centre. Readings also differ with the mass of material below. A reading that differs from the expected value is a **gravity anomaly**, and it tells about the distribution of mass in the crust.
4. **Magnetic surveys.** These show the distribution of magnetic materials in the crustal part.
5. **Seismic activity.** The study of seismic waves gives, in NCERT's words, a complete picture of the layered interior. It is the most important indirect source and is taught in modules 3 and 4.

An exam pair to keep: NCERT's exercise treats volcanoes as a *direct* source and earthquake waves as indirect. Direct sources reach only 3-12 km of depth; gravity and magnetic surveys speak only about the crust.

### 3. Core: earthquake waves, P, S and surface

*Objective:* Describe how an earthquake releases energy, distinguish P, S and surface waves by speed, motion and the materials they cross, and read a seismograph curve.

## What an earthquake is

An earthquake, in simple words, is the shaking of the earth, caused by the release of energy that travels outwards as waves (NCERT, kegy203, PDF p2). The release happens along a **fault**, a sharp break in the crustal rocks. The rock on either side of a fault tends to slide in opposite directions, but the weight of the rock above locks them by friction. When the tendency to move overcomes friction, the blocks slip abruptly and the energy is let out.

- **Focus** (or **hypocentre**): the point inside the earth where the energy is released.
- **Epicentre**: the point on the surface directly above the focus, nearest to it. It is the first place to feel the waves.

NCERT notes that all natural earthquakes occur in the lithosphere (taken there as the part up to 200 km). This is NCERT's simplification: deep-focus earthquakes reach about 700 km (standard knowledge, **verify**). The recording instrument is the **seismograph**.

## Two families of waves

**Body waves** start at the focus and move in all directions through the body of the earth. **Surface waves** are made when body waves reach and interact with the surface rocks; they travel along the surface, arrive last and are the most destructive, displacing rock and collapsing structures.

## The two body waves

| Feature | P-wave (primary) | S-wave (secondary) |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival | first, because it is faster | later, with a time lag |
| Particle motion | parallel to the direction of travel: stretches and squeezes the material | perpendicular to the direction of travel: makes crests and troughs |
| Materials crossed | solids, liquids and gases (like sound) | **solids only** |
| Why it matters | enters every layer | its disappearance reveals liquid zones |

The S-wave rule is the most valuable fact in the chapter: because S-waves cannot cross a liquid, any region they fail to reach must contain liquid on their path.

## Speed and bending

NCERT says the velocity of a wave changes as it passes through materials of different density, and that the denser the material, the higher the velocity. (This is NCERT's simple rule; real speed also depends on how rigid the material is, standard knowledge, **verify**.) Waves also change direction: **reflection** makes a wave rebound from a boundary, **refraction** makes it bend into a new direction. Scientists infer these changes from the seismograph record and use them to locate the boundaries inside.

## Measuring size

- **Magnitude** (Richter scale) relates to the energy released; written as a number from 0 to 10 in NCERT. Quakes of magnitude 8 and more are rare (NCERT: one in 1 to 2 years).
- **Intensity** (Mercalli scale) measures visible damage, 1 to 12.

## Reading a seismograph curve

NCERT Figure 3.1 shows a curve with three distinct sections, which fit the arrival order: P first, then S, then the largest swing of the surface waves. The farther the station, the larger the P-to-S gap (standard knowledge, **verify**).

### 4. Core: shadow zones and what they prove about the core

*Objective:* State the 105 and 145 degree pattern for P and S shadow zones, and use it to argue that the outer core is liquid.

## The observation

Earthquake waves are recorded at seismographs all over the world, yet there are specific areas where certain waves are not reported. Such an area is a **shadow zone**. Each earthquake has its own shadow zone, and you can draw it for any quake once you know the epicentre (NCERT, PDF p3).

To describe positions, use **angular distance**: the angle at the earth's centre between the epicentre and a station, measured along the surface. The epicentre itself is 0 degrees; the point exactly opposite on the globe is 180 degrees.

## The NCERT pattern

- Within **105 degrees** of the epicentre, both P- and S-waves are recorded.
- Beyond **145 degrees**, P-waves are recorded but S-waves are not.
- Between **105 and 145 degrees** neither is recorded: this is the shadow zone for both.
- Every station beyond 105 degrees fails to receive S-waves, so the S-wave shadow is the whole region beyond 105 degrees; the P-wave shadow is only the band from 105 to 145 degrees.

| Angular distance from epicentre | P-waves | S-waves |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 105 degrees | recorded | recorded |
| 105 to 145 degrees | not recorded | not recorded |
| 145 to 180 degrees | recorded | not recorded |

## The reasoning

1. S-waves vanish for every station beyond 105 degrees. S-waves cannot travel through liquid. So the waves heading towards the far side must have met a **liquid** layer. This layer is the outer core (NCERT: the outer core is liquid).
2. P-waves do cross liquids, but they bend as they enter the different material of the core, leaving a P-shadow band (105 to 145 degrees) before reappearing beyond 145 degrees. This explanation is standard knowledge (**verify**); NCERT says only that reflection and refraction change wave direction and helped reveal the structure.
3. Together the shadows locate the core-mantle boundary at 2,900 km (NCERT p6) and show a liquid outer core. NCERT states that the inner core is solid; the wave evidence for that is standard knowledge (**verify**).

## How big is the shadow?

NCERT says the S-wave shadow is "a little over 40 per cent" of the surface. Clean geometry for a 105 degree boundary gives about 37 per cent (derivation in the Master Notes, 'Worked arithmetic'), so use it in answers as "more than a third; NCERT: over 40 per cent".

A hand-drawn figure: a circle with the epicentre at the top, the 105 and 145 degree bands marked on both sides, and the far cap beyond 145 degrees labelled P only.

### 5. Core: crust, mantle and core with their boundaries and figures

*Objective:* Describe each layer with its thickness, state and composition, define a discontinuity, and separate the lithosphere and asthenosphere from crust and mantle.

## The crust

The crust is the outermost, solid, brittle part. Its thickness varies: the **oceanic crust** is thinner (mean about 5 km) and the **continental crust** thicker (about 30 km), and under major mountain systems it is thicker still, as much as 70 km in the Himalayan region (NCERT, PDF p5). Leong gives the range as 5-6 km under the oceans to as much as 48 km under some parts of the continents (PDF p28); for the Himalayas use NCERT.

Leong also splits the crust by rock type. The upper part is granite-like and forms the continents; it is rich in silica and alumina, so it is called **sial** (average density 2.7). The lower part is denser basalt-like rock forming the ocean floors, rich in silica, iron and magnesium, called **sima** (density 3.0). The lighter continents 'float' on the denser sima. (The sial and sima labels are older textbook terms; treat them as Leong's vocabulary.)

## The mantle

The mantle extends from the **Moho discontinuity** to a depth of 2,900 km. Its upper portion is the **asthenosphere** ('astheno' means weak), said by NCERT to extend up to about 400 km; it is the main source of magma that reaches the surface in eruptions. The crust and the uppermost part of the mantle together form the **lithosphere** (thickness 10-200 km). The lower mantle is solid. Leong calls the mantle the mesosphere, very dense and rich in olivine.

## The core

The core-mantle boundary lies at 2,900 km. The outer core is liquid and the inner core solid; the material is very heavy, mostly nickel and iron, which is why it is sometimes called the **nife** layer (NCERT; Leong agrees). Leong gives the core's radius as 3,476 km and its temperature as 1,927 degrees Celsius.

Check: 6,378 - 2,900 = 3,478 km, within 2 km of Leong's 3,476 km, so the books agree (more cross-checks in the Master Notes, 'Worked arithmetic').

## Discontinuities

A **discontinuity** is a boundary where wave speeds change abruptly. Three are used: the **Moho** (crust to mantle; named in NCERT and Leong), the **core-mantle boundary** at 2,900 km (often called the Gutenberg discontinuity, standard knowledge, **verify**) and the boundary between the liquid outer and solid inner core (often called the Lehmann discontinuity, at about 5,150 km depth; standard knowledge, **verify**).

Density, temperature and pressure all increase with depth (NCERT); the extracts give no mantle or core density or pressure figures, so learn only the trend (details in the Master Notes).


### 6. Core: volcanoes and magma as messengers from the mantle

*Objective:* Explain what volcanoes reveal about the interior, define magma, lava, plutonic and volcanic rock, and contrast fluid basic with sticky acid lava.

## Why a volcano belongs in this lesson

A **volcano** is a place where gases, ashes and/or molten rock (lava) escape to the ground. NCERT calls a volcano **active** if materials are being released or have been released in the recent past (PDF p6). For this lesson a volcano matters for one reason: it hands up rock from below. The landforms of volcanoes are taught later; here the question is what the product tells us.

## What the eruption product tells

- The mantle is denser than the crust and contains the weak **asthenosphere**; molten rock finds its way up from this zone (NCERT p6).
- Magma that moves towards the crust or reaches the surface is called **lava**. The ground receives lava flows, pyroclastic debris, volcanic bombs, ash and dust, and gases such as nitrogen and sulphur compounds. Leong adds that dissolved gases make lava more mobile and explosive (PDF p47).

Leong's older picture says the whole interior is 'semi-molten'. Modern NCERT teaching is sharper: the lower mantle is solid, only the outer core is liquid, and magma comes from pockets in a weak zone. Use NCERT's picture in answers.

## Two kinds of lava (Leong)

**Basic lava**: low in silica, rich in iron and magnesium, dark, the hottest (about 1,000 degrees Celsius) and very fluid; it spreads far in thin sheets and builds a gentle shield with quiet eruptions. **Acid lava**: high in silica, light, viscous and slow; it builds a steep cone, with loud explosions and sometimes a plug or spine.

## Where the magma cools: two kinds of igneous rock

Lava that cools on reaching the surface forms **volcanic** rocks; magma that cools within the crust forms **plutonic** rocks (NCERT p6). The forms taken by lava cooled inside the crust are called **intrusive forms** (batholith, laccolith, sill, dyke and others; names and Indian cases in the Master Notes).

## Indian pointers in the NCERT extract

- The **Deccan Traps** are a flood basalt province covering most of the Maharashtra plateau today; originally they covered a much larger area (NCERT p7). Flood basalts are highly fluid lava that flows for long distances and piles up thousands of square kilometres thick.
- **Dykes** are the most commonly found intrusive forms in the western Maharashtra area and are considered the feeders of the eruptions that built the Deccan Traps (NCERT p8).
- The Karnataka plateau is spotted with domal hills of granite; most, now exfoliated, are examples of lacoliths or batholiths (NCERT p8).

## A word on mantle plumes

A **mantle plume** (standard knowledge, **verify**; the NCERT extract does not mention it) is a narrow column of unusually hot rock rising slowly from deep in the mantle, which can melt near the top and feed volcanoes away from plate edges. The Deccan Traps are widely linked to one. Module 10 builds the answer.

Leong's counts of volcanoes and earthquakes by belt are in the Master Notes; the two zones overlap because both mark weak, folded or faulted parts of the crust.

### 7. Core: minerals, their properties and families

*Objective:* Define a mineral, list the properties used to tell minerals apart, name the main mineral families and apply the NCERT classification of minerals.

## Definition, with a warning

NCERT Class XII defines a **mineral** as a natural substance of organic or inorganic origin with definite chemical and physical properties (legy205, PDF p1). The words 'organic origin' are why coal and petroleum are listed as minerals in economic geography. A geologist's stricter definition (standard knowledge, **verify**): a naturally occurring, usually inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and a regular internal arrangement of atoms (crystal structure). Answer in the NCERT sense unless the question is clearly geological.

Rocks are aggregates of minerals (Leong, PDF p29). Granite is mainly quartz, feldspar and mica; limestone contains a bit of quartz and feldspar and much calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (Leong).

## Elements behind the minerals

A handful of elements make up most of the crust. Standard textbook knowledge (**verify**): eight elements make up about 98 per cent of the crust by weight, with oxygen first (about 47 per cent) and silicon second (about 28 per cent), then aluminium, iron, calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium. This is why silicon-oxygen minerals dominate. The extracts here do not print this list; learn the order oxygen, silicon as the safe core.

## Properties used to identify a mineral (standard knowledge, verify)

Colour (easy but often misleading), streak (colour of the powder), lustre (how it shines), crystal form, cleavage and fracture (how it breaks), hardness (the Mohs scale runs from 1, talc, to 10, diamond) and specific gravity (weight compared with an equal volume of water).

Leong supports one chain: rock colour comes from the minerals present. Granite is light because of quartz, pink or white feldspar and mica; basalt is dark because of ferro-magnesian minerals such as olivine, hornblende and biotite mica (PDF p30).

## Main mineral families (standard knowledge, verify)

- **Silicates** (feldspar, quartz, mica, olivine, pyroxene, amphibole): by far the most abundant.
- **Carbonates**: calcite (limestone, marble), dolomite.
- **Oxides**: haematite and magnetite (iron ores; NCERT XII names both as India's two main iron ores).
- **Sulphates and halides**: gypsum; rock salt.
- **Sulphides**: ores of lead, zinc, copper.
- **Native elements**: gold, copper, graphite, diamond.

## NCERT's economic classification (legy205, PDF pp1-2)

| Group | Sub-group | Examples in the extract |
|---|---|---|
| Metallic | Ferrous (iron-bearing) | iron ore, manganese, chromite |
| Metallic | Non-ferrous | copper, bauxite |
| Non-metallic | Organic in origin (fossil or mineral fuels) | coal, petroleum |
| Non-metallic | Inorganic | mica, limestone, graphite, dolomite |

Three characteristics of minerals (NCERT XII): they are **unevenly distributed** over space; there is an **inverse relation between quality and quantity** (good quality is scarce); and all minerals are **exhaustible** because they take very long to form and cannot be replenished at the time of need.

## Weathering and ore concentration

NCERT Class XI adds that weathering enriches ores: when rocks weather, some material is leached away by groundwater, and the concentration of the remaining valuable material (iron, manganese, aluminium, copper) rises. Without such enrichment many deposits would not be rich enough to mine, refine and use economically (kegy205, PDF p6).

### 8. Core: igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks and the rock cycle

*Objective:* Classify rocks by origin, texture and example, link grain size to cooling rate, and trace the rock cycle with the process at each step.

## Three families

Rocks are grouped by origin and appearance into **igneous**, **sedimentary** and **metamorphic** (Leong, PDF p31).

## Igneous rocks: cooled from molten rock

Igneous rocks form when magma cools and solidifies. They are normally crystalline, not layered and without fossils. They are called primary rocks because the other families are ultimately made from them (standard usage, **verify**). Two classes, by where cooling happens:

- **Plutonic (intrusive)**: cooled slowly deep in the crust, so large crystals grow. Granite, diorite and gabbro.
- **Volcanic (extrusive)**: poured out as lava and solidified rapidly, so crystals are small. Basalt is the common one and forms lava plateaux such as the Deccan plateau (Leong, PDF p31).

**Texture** (grain size) depends on cooling speed: very rapid cooling at the surface gives a glassy texture, moderately rapid a fine texture, slow cooling at depth a coarse texture (Leong, PDF p30). **Chemistry** gives a second axis: **acid** igneous rocks (high silica) such as granite are lighter in density and colour; **basic** rocks have more iron, aluminium and magnesium oxides and are denser and darker. Leong's silica table prints inconsistent ranges; the usual cut-offs are acid above about 66 per cent silica, basic about 45 to 52 per cent, ultrabasic below 45 (standard knowledge, **verify**).

## Sedimentary rocks: laid down in layers

Sedimentary rocks form from sediment accumulated over long periods, usually under water. They show **strata** (layers) from a few centimetres to many metres thick, are typically made of cemented grains and often contain **fossils** (Leong, PDF p32). Leong's three groups:

1. **Mechanically formed**: older rock pieces cemented together: sandstone, grit, conglomerate (rounded pebbles), breccia (angular fragments), and clay, shale or mudstone from the finest material.
2. **Organically formed**: from living remains: limestone and chalk (calcareous); peat, lignite and coal (carbonaceous, from compressed plants).
3. **Chemically formed**: precipitated from solutions: rock salt, gypsum, potash, nitrates.

The supply of sediment starts with weathering and erosion: rock is broken in place, agents remove it, and on gentler slopes the coarser pieces settle first and the finer later (NCERT kegy205, PDF pp5, 8).

## Metamorphic rocks: changed by heat and pressure

Any rock, igneous or sedimentary, may become metamorphic under great heat and pressure, especially during intense earth movements (Leong, PDF pp33-34). NCERT adds that folding, faulting and plate motion change pressure, volume and temperature and so induce metamorphism (kegy205, PDF p3). Leong's pairs:

clay becomes slate, limestone marble, sandstone quartzite, granite gneiss, shale schist and coal graphite. (In standard teaching shale first becomes slate, and schist is a higher-grade stage; **verify**.)

## The rock cycle

The three families are connected in a loop with no start and no finish:

1. Magma cools to **igneous** rock.
2. Weathering and erosion break any rock into **sediment**.
3. Sediment is deposited, compacted and cemented into **sedimentary** rock.
4. Heat and pressure change any rock to **metamorphic** rock.
5. Deep burial melts any rock back into **magma**.

Shortcuts exist: igneous rock can be metamorphosed directly, and sedimentary rock can be melted. Leong's own sentence for it: from eroded materials new rocks are formed, to be uplifted later into the next generation of mountains (PDF p36).

### 9. Master: porosity, resistance, India's rocks and mineral belts

*Objective:* Separate porosity from permeability, explain why rocks resist weathering differently, and link India's geology to its mineral belts and Gondwana coal.

## Resistance of rocks

NCERT: the intensity of exogenic processes depends on the type and structure of rocks. Structure includes folds, faults, joints, bedding planes, hardness of constituent minerals, chemical susceptibility and **permeability or impermeability**. A rock can resist one process and yield to another (kegy205, PDF pp4-5). Leong adds that softer rocks like clay and shale wear down much faster than hard granite (PDF p34).

## Porosity and permeability

These two words are confused every year. Learn the pair exactly (standard knowledge, **verify**):

- **Porosity**: the fraction of the rock volume that is empty space (pores). It tells how much water the rock can hold.
- **Permeability**: how easily water can move through the rock. It needs pores that are large enough and connected.

Chalk, a soft organic limestone (Leong, PDF p33), has many connected pores, so it absorbs water and lets it pass: **porous and permeable**. Clay holds a lot of water in extremely small, poorly linked pores, so water barely moves: **porous but nearly impermeable**. 'Impermeable' therefore does not mean 'non-porous', the point tested in 2025 (module 10).

## India's rocks and mineral belts (NCERT Class XII)

- The bulk of India's valuable minerals are products of pre-Palaeozoic age, mainly in the **metamorphic and igneous** rocks of peninsular India. The vast alluvial plain of north India has no minerals of economic use (legy205, PDF p1).
- Metallic minerals mostly occur in the **old crystalline rocks** of the peninsular plateau. Over 97 per cent of coal reserves are in the valleys of the Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari. Petroleum lies in the **sedimentary basins** of Assam, Gujarat and Mumbai High. Most major minerals lie east of a line joining Mangaluru and Kanpur (PDF p2).
- Three belts: **north-eastern plateau** (Chhotanagpur in Jharkhand, Odisha Plateau, West Bengal, parts of Chhattisgarh), **south-western plateau** (Karnataka, Goa, Tamil Nadu uplands, Kerala; no coal except Neyveli lignite) and **north-western** (along the Aravali in Rajasthan and Gujarat). Minerals of each belt are in the Master Notes.
- Coal occurs in rocks of two ages, **Gondwana** and tertiary. The Damodar Valley holds the key Gondwana coalfields (Jharia the largest, then Raniganj); tertiary coal is in the north-east (PDF p7).

## Gondwanaland

Leong's geological time table records that Gondwanaland (Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica) split in the Primary-Secondary era span (the table's layout is garbled in the extract, so the exact period is not used here). India's Gondwana connection shows in its Gondwana coal and in the similar ancient rocks of its old southern lands.

The Deccan Traps, the dykes of western Maharashtra and the granite domes of the Karnataka plateau (NCERT kegy203, pp7-8) are the standard Indian igneous examples.

## The two Prelims items

**2018 Q57 (three statements).** Statement 1: the earth's magnetic field has reversed every few hundred thousand years. Statement 2: at creation, more than 4,000 million years ago, the atmosphere had 54 per cent oxygen and no carbon dioxide. Statement 3: living organisms modified the early atmosphere. Facts beyond the extracts (standard knowledge, **verify**): the field has reversed at irregular intervals averaging a few hundred thousand years; the early atmosphere had very little free oxygen and plenty of carbon dioxide, and photosynthesising life later added oxygen. Analysis: statement 2 conflicts with this, while 1 and 3 fit it, so the likely answer is 1 and 3 only. Analysis only, not an official key.

**2025 Q28 (chalk and clay).** Statement I says chalk is very permeable and clay quite impermeable. Statement II: chalk is porous and so absorbs water. Statement III: clay is not at all porous. By module 9, chalk is porous and permeable, and clay is porous but poorly permeable, so III is incorrect. II is correct and explains I. The option that says only one of II and III is correct and it explains I fits the analysis.

## The four Mains questions: frames

**Primary rocks (2022, 10 marks).** Define primary (igneous) rocks as those formed by cooling of magma. Features: crystalline, non-layered, no fossils, hard. Types by place of cooling (plutonic, volcanic), silica (acid, basic) and texture. Examples: granite, basalt, gabbro; Deccan Traps and Karnataka granite hills. Role as the source of the other rocks.

**Mantle plume (2018, 10 marks).** Define as in module 6 (standard knowledge, **verify**). Roles in plate tectonics, which is taught later: volcanism away from plate edges (hotspots); flood basalts such as the Deccan Traps; a fixed plume under a moving plate leaves a volcano track that records plate motion; plumes may help break up continents such as Gondwanaland. State that plume theory is debated.

**Juno and the origin of the earth (2017, 10 marks).** Juno is NASA's Jupiter orbiter, launched 2011, in orbit from 2016 (standard knowledge, **verify**). It measures Jupiter's gravity, magnetic field and atmospheric water and ammonia, with clues to its core. Jupiter formed early and kept much of the solar nebula's gas, so its make-up tests how planets, including the earth, formed and evolved. Gravity, magnetism and waves are the same inference methods as module 2.

**Mining and GDP in a Gondwana country (2021, 10 marks).** Positives: old crystalline belts and Gondwana coal (module 9). Why the GDP share is low: uneven distribution, inverse quality and quantity and exhaustibility (NCERT XII), plus standard-knowledge reasons to verify (limited deep exploration, clearance and land delays, imports, little value addition, a services-led economy). Give no GDP percentage without a source.

## Traps to avoid

- Writing 'lithosphere is the crust' when NCERT includes the uppermost mantle.
- Reversing P and S properties.
- Saying S-waves are absent between 105 and 145 degrees only: they are absent beyond 105 degrees.
- Calling impermeable the same as non-porous.

### Notes: Core terms

- Crust: the outermost solid brittle layer; oceanic mean thickness about 5 km, continental about 30 km, up to about 70 km under the Himalayan region (NCERT).
- Mantle: from the Moho to 2,900 km depth; its upper weak part is the asthenosphere (extends up to about 400 km in NCERT) and is the main source of magma.
- Core: from 2,900 km to the centre; outer core liquid, inner core solid; mostly nickel and iron (nife).
- Lithosphere: NCERT, crust plus uppermost mantle, 10-200 km thick. Leong uses the word for the crust alone.
- Moho discontinuity: the boundary between crust and mantle. A discontinuity is a boundary where wave speed changes sharply.
- Focus (hypocentre): the point of energy release inside the earth. Epicentre: the surface point directly above it.
- Magma: molten rock below the surface. Lava: that material moving to, or at, the surface.
- Mineral: a natural substance of organic or inorganic origin with definite chemical and physical properties (NCERT XII). Rock: an aggregate of minerals (Leong).
- Gravity anomaly: the difference between the gravity reading at a place and the expected value; it points to uneven distribution of mass in the crust.

### Notes: Shadow zones: facts and arithmetic

- Within 105 degrees of the epicentre both P and S are recorded (NCERT).
- From 105 to 145 degrees neither is recorded: shadow zone for both.
- Beyond 145 degrees P is recorded and S is not; no S-wave arrives anywhere beyond 105 degrees.
- The S-wave shadow is much larger than the P-wave shadow (NCERT).
- Derived share of the globe, by (1 - cos(180 - X)) / 2: beyond 105 degrees = 37.1 per cent; the band 105 to 145 degrees = 28.0 per cent. NCERT says a little over 40 per cent for S: treat that as rough.
- Inference: S cannot cross liquid, so the zone it cannot reach contains liquid (the outer core); the core-mantle boundary lies at 2,900 km.

### Notes: Worked arithmetic (moved from modules 4 and 5)

- Share of the globe beyond angular distance X from the epicentre: (1 - cos(180 - X)) / 2, treating each boundary as a clean circle.
- S-wave shadow, beyond 105 degrees: (1 - cos 75 degrees) / 2 = 0.371, about 37 per cent. P-wave band, 105 to 145 degrees: (cos 105 - cos 145) / 2 = 0.280, about 28 per cent. S is the larger shadow, as NCERT says; its 'little over 40 per cent' is rough.
- Core radius: 6,378 - 2,900 = 3,478 km, within 2 km of Leong's 3,476 km, so the books agree.
- By radius the core is 55 per cent (0.545) and the mantle plus crust 45 per cent; volume goes as the cube of the radius, so the core is only 0.545 cubed = 16 per cent of the earth's volume (derived here).
- Leong's 35000 degrees Fahrenheit beside 1,927 degrees Celsius is a misprint: 1,927 degrees Celsius is 3,501 degrees Fahrenheit.
- Temperature gradient: 1 degree Celsius per 20 m = 50 degrees per km; 1 degree Fahrenheit per 65 ft = (1/1.8) per 19.8 m = about 28 degrees Celsius per km. Usual mean gradient about 25-30 degrees per km (verify): 3 km gives roughly 75-90 degrees.
- Density, temperature and pressure with depth: NCERT says all three increase. Numbers in the extracts are limited to Leong's sial 2.7 and sima 3.0, the near-surface temperature rate (module 2) and the core's 1,927 degrees Celsius; the extracts give no density or pressure figure for the mantle or core.

### Notes: Earthquake types, scales and effects (NCERT)

- Tectonic earthquakes: the most common, from sliding of rocks along a fault plane; volcanic earthquakes are a special class, confined to active volcano areas.
- Collapse earthquakes: roofs of underground mines fall in intense mining areas. Explosion earthquakes: chemical or nuclear explosions. Reservoir-induced earthquakes: in areas of large reservoirs.
- Magnitude scale (Richter): energy released, 0 to 10. Intensity scale (Mercalli): visible damage, 1 to 12.
- Effects listed by NCERT include ground shaking, differential settlement, landslides, soil liquefaction, ground lurching, avalanches, ground displacement, floods from dam and levee failures, fires, structural collapse, falling objects and tsunami.
- A tsunami occurs only if the epicentre is below oceanic waters and the magnitude is high enough; tsunamis are waves generated by the tremor, not the earthquake itself.
- Leong: about 70 per cent of earthquakes occur in the Circum-Pacific belt and about 20 per cent in the Mediterranean-Himalayan belt; over 50,000 earthquakes are recorded each year.

### Notes: Volcano types and intrusive forms (names only; landforms are a later lesson)

- Volcano types in NCERT: shield (basalt, fluid, Hawaii), composite (viscous, explosive, layers of lava and ash), caldera (collapsed depression after violent eruption), flood basalt province (very fluid lava; Deccan Traps), mid-ocean ridge volcanoes (a ridge system more than 70,000 km long).
- Intrusive forms in NCERT: batholith (large granitic body, cooled magma chamber), laccolith (dome with level base), lapolith (saucer shape), phacolith (wavy mass at fold crests or troughs), sill and sheet (near horizontal; thick ones are sills), dyke (wall-like, near vertical).
- India: dykes are most common in western Maharashtra and were feeders of the Deccan Traps; granite domal hills of the Karnataka plateau are lacoliths or batholiths (NCERT).
- Leong: acid lava is viscous and builds steep cones; basic lava at about 1,000 degrees Celsius is fluid and builds shield volcanoes; well over 500 active volcanoes, about two-thirds in the Ring of Fire; no active volcano in the Himalayas.

### Notes: India's mineral belts and rocks (NCERT Class XII)

- Bulk of valuable minerals: pre-Palaeozoic metamorphic and igneous rocks of peninsular India; the north Indian alluvial plain has no economically useful minerals.
- North-eastern plateau belt: Chhotanagpur (Jharkhand), Odisha Plateau, West Bengal, parts of Chhattisgarh: iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite, mica.
- South-western plateau belt: Karnataka, Goa, contiguous Tamil Nadu uplands and Kerala: high-grade iron ore, manganese, limestone; lacks coal except Neyveli lignite.
- North-western belt: along the Aravali in Rajasthan and Gujarat, Dharwar rocks: copper, zinc, building stones, gypsum, salt; Gujarat has petroleum.
- Coal: over 97 per cent of reserves are in the valleys of the Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari; Gondwana coal in the Damodar valley (Jharia largest, then Raniganj); tertiary coal in the north-east.
- Petroleum: sedimentary basins of Assam, Gujarat and Mumbai High; new reserves in the Krishna-Godavari and Kaveri basins.

### Notes: Easily confused pairs

- Magnitude (energy, Richter 0-10) versus intensity (damage, Mercalli 1-12).
- Focus (inside, where energy is released) versus epicentre (on the surface above it).
- Magma (below the surface) versus lava (at or moving to the surface).
- Lithosphere (NCERT: crust plus uppermost mantle) versus asthenosphere (weak upper mantle zone below it) versus mesosphere (Leong's name for the mantle).
- Plutonic (cooled inside the crust) versus volcanic (cooled at the surface).
- Porosity (empty space, storage) versus permeability (connected pores, transmission).
- Shadow zone of P (the 105 to 145 degree band) versus shadow zone of S (everything beyond 105 degrees).
- Mineral (definite composition and properties) versus rock (aggregate of minerals).

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - The interior is known mainly by indirect evidence, and seismic waves are the most important of it.
  - P-waves cross solids, liquids and gases; S-waves cross solids only; the S-wave shadow beyond 105 degrees shows a liquid outer core.
  - Earth is layered: crust, mantle, core, with the Moho and the 2,900 km boundary as the main discontinuities.
  - Rocks are aggregates of minerals; the three families are linked by the rock cycle.
  - India's valuable minerals lie mostly in the old crystalline rocks of the peninsula and in Gondwana coal.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - Earth radius about 6,378 km (NCERT); mantle boundary 2,900 km; core radius about 3,478 km by subtraction (Leong 3,476).
  - Oceanic crust mean about 5 km, continental about 30 km, up to about 70 km in the Himalayan region (NCERT).
  - Shadow zone: both P and S absent from 105 to 145 degrees; S absent beyond 105 degrees; P present beyond 145 degrees.
  - Lithosphere is 10 to 200 km thick and asthenosphere extends to about 400 km (NCERT).
  - Deepest drill: Kola, 12 km (NCERT); gold mines in South Africa 3 to 4 km deep.
  - Richter scale 0 to 10 (energy); Mercalli 1 to 12 (damage).
  - Over 97 per cent of coal reserves are in the Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari valleys (NCERT XII).

  - P versus S waves; body versus surface waves.
  - Lithosphere versus asthenosphere; NCERT versus Leong use of 'lithosphere'.
  - Igneous versus sedimentary versus metamorphic; plutonic versus volcanic.
  - Porosity versus permeability.
  - Mineral (NCERT XII, includes organic fuels) versus the stricter geological definition.

  - Saying S-waves are missing only in the 105 to 145 band; they are missing beyond 105 degrees.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-NCERT-INTERIOR-DRILL (verified):** NCERT: the earth's radius is about 6,378 km and no one can reach the centre to observe or collect samples; the deepest drill, at Kola, has so far reached a depth of 12 km.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-INTERIOR-INDIRECT (verified):** NCERT: mining shows that temperature, pressure and density increase with depth; meteors are solid bodies of material similar to the earth's but not from its interior; the other indirect sources are gravitation (gravity anomalies show how mass is distributed in the crust), the magnetic field (magnetic materials in the crust) and seismic activity, one of the most important sources.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-PS-WAVES (verified):** NCERT: P-waves move faster, are the first to arrive and travel through gaseous, liquid and solid materials; S-waves arrive with a time lag and can travel only through solid materials, which helped scientists understand the structure of the interior.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-SHADOW-ZONES (verified):** NCERT: seismographs within 105 degrees of the epicentre record both P and S-waves; beyond 145 degrees they record P-waves but not S-waves; the zone between 105 and 145 degrees is the shadow zone for both; the entire zone beyond 105 degrees receives no S-waves, so the S-wave shadow is much larger than the P-wave band.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CRUST (verified):** NCERT: the crust is the outermost solid, brittle part of the earth; the mean thickness is 5 km for oceanic crust and around 30 km for continental crust, and the continental crust is as much as 70 km thick in the Himalayan region.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-MANTLE (verified):** NCERT: the mantle extends from Moho's discontinuity to a depth of 2,900 km; its upper portion, the asthenosphere ('weak'), is considered to extend up to 400 km and is the main source of magma; the crust and uppermost mantle form the lithosphere, 10-200 km thick; the lower mantle is solid.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CORE (verified):** NCERT: earthquake wave velocities helped show the existence of the core; the core-mantle boundary lies at 2,900 km depth, the outer core is liquid and the inner core solid, and the core is made of very heavy material, mostly nickel and iron (the 'nife' layer).
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-IGNEOUS-FORMS (verified):** NCERT: lava released in eruptions cools into igneous rocks, classified as volcanic rocks (cooling at the surface) and plutonic rocks (cooling in the crust); the forms taken by lava cooled within the crust are called intrusive forms.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-DECCAN-DYKES (verified):** NCERT: the Deccan Traps, a flood basalt province covering most of the Maharashtra plateau today, are believed to have covered a much larger area initially; dykes, the most commonly found intrusive form in western Maharashtra, are considered the feeders of the eruptions that built the Deccan Traps.

- **F-NCERT-KARNATAKA-GRANITE (verified):** NCERT: the Karnataka plateau is spotted with domal hills of granite rocks, most of them now exfoliated, which are examples of lacoliths or batholiths.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Interior of the Earth (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-METAMORPHISM (verified):** NCERT: orogeny, epeirogeny, earthquakes and plate tectonics cause faulting and fracturing of the crust, and all these processes cause pressure, volume and temperature changes, which in turn induce metamorphism of rocks.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Geomorphic Processes (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-INDIA-OLD-ROCKS (verified):** NCERT (Class XII): the bulk of India's valuable minerals are products of pre-Palaeozoic age and are mainly associated with the metamorphic and igneous rocks of peninsular India; the vast alluvial plain of north India is devoid of minerals of economic use.

### Memory skeleton

- Evidence: direct (mining, drilling, volcanoes) and indirect (temperature-pressure-density, meteors, gravity, seismic waves)
- Earthquake waves: P, S, surface; focus and epicentre; Richter versus Mercalli
- Shadow zones: 105 and 145 degrees; S cannot cross liquid, so the outer core is liquid
- Layers: crust (Moho), mantle to 2,900 km with asthenosphere, core (nife); lithosphere 10-200 km
- Volcanoes and magma as messengers; intrusive forms
- Minerals (NCERT XII classes) and the three rock families joined by the rock cycle

### Last-week summary

- Radius about 6,378 km; crust 5 km oceanic, 30 km continental, 70 km Himalaya; Moho; mantle to 2,900 km; core about 3,478 km radius, nickel-iron, outer liquid, inner solid.
- Lithosphere 10-200 km (NCERT: crust plus uppermost mantle); asthenosphere to about 400 km, main magma source.
- P first, parallel, all media; S second, perpendicular, solids only; surface last, most destructive. Both within 105; neither 105-145; only P beyond 145.
- Richter 0-10 energy; Mercalli 1-12 damage. Plutonic slow coarse granite; volcanic fast fine basalt; limestone-marble, sandstone-quartzite, granite-gneiss, clay-slate, coal-graphite.
- Over 97 per cent of coal in Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi, Godavari valleys; minerals: metallic (ferrous, non-ferrous) and non-metallic (organic fuels, inorganic).
