## GS-162: Citizenship provisions

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: Citizen, alien, person, national and subject: the vocabulary

Before reading a single Article, fix five words.

**State** here means the political community and its government as an organised unit; a **citizen** is a full member of that community, with a legal bond of allegiance to it. **Allegiance** simply means loyalty owed to the State.

An **alien** (also called a **foreigner**) is a person on Indian soil who is a citizen of some other country. Laxmikanth divides aliens into **friendly aliens** (subjects of countries with cordial relations with India) and **enemy aliens** (subjects of a country at war with India). A **person** is the widest word: any human being within the territory, whether citizen or alien. This matters because the Constitution sometimes says 'any person' and sometimes says 'citizens', and a single word changes who can go to court.

**Nationality** is the status of belonging to a nation-state in international law; in everyday Indian use 'citizen' and 'national' usually point to the same status, which is why Article 9 talks about 'citizen of a foreign State'. A **subject** is the older word for a person owing allegiance to a monarch.

A helpful picture: three nested circles. The biggest is *persons* (everyone on Indian soil). Inside it sit *citizens* (full members). Outside the citizen circle but inside the person circle sit *aliens*. Rights written for 'persons' reach all three groups; rights written for 'citizens' reach only the inner circle.

### Bridge: Domicile, nationality, ordinary residence and illegal migrant: four easily confused terms

Four words appear again and again in this topic and are often mixed up.

**Domicile** means the place where a person has made a permanent home with the intention of staying. It is a matter of residence plus intention, not of ancestry or passport. Article 5 of the Constitution uses 'domicile in the territory of India' as the first test for citizenship at commencement. Domicile can be changed by moving and intending to settle; it is not the same as being a citizen. A foreigner can be domiciled in India (long-term home) without being an Indian citizen. Standard Supreme Court reading (the D. P. Joshi case of 1955, verify): domicile in this Constitution refers to domicile in the country, not in a particular State, which is one reason Indian law is spoken of as having a single domicile alongside single citizenship. States often issue 'domicile certificates' for admissions or jobs; those are residence-based administrative papers, not a separate legal citizenship.

**Nationality** is the legal bond with a nation-state; **citizenship** is its detailed legal form with rights and duties.

**Ordinary residence** means living in a place habitually, not merely visiting. The Citizenship Act uses periods of ordinary residence (for example seven years, twelve months) as conditions.

**Illegal migrant** is a statutory category: broadly a foreigner who entered India without a valid passport or travel document, or stayed beyond the period the permit allowed (standard knowledge; verify the exact definition in the Citizenship Act, as the extract only uses the words 'not being an illegal migrant').

### 1. Orientation: what citizenship is and why it matters

*Objective:* State the meaning of citizenship, separate citizens from aliens, and list the seven things the Constitution gives only to citizens.

## One-sentence meaning

NCERT defines **citizenship** as full and equal membership of a political community. In today's world, States give their members a collective political identity and certain rights; citizens expect rights from their State, and help and protection when they travel.

## Two kinds of people

Laxmikanth says India, like any modern State, has two kinds of people: **citizens** and **aliens**. Citizens are full members who owe allegiance to the State and enjoy all civil and political rights. Aliens are citizens of another State and do not enjoy all those rights. Friendly aliens come from countries with cordial relations; enemy aliens come from a country at war with India and have fewer rights. For example, enemy aliens lack the protection against arrest and detention in Article 22. (Vocabulary: see bridge GS-162-B1.)

## What citizenship gives you in India

Laxmikanth lists seven rights and privileges that belong to citizens and are denied to aliens:
1. Protection against discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth (Article 15).
2. Equality of opportunity in public employment (Article 16).
3. The freedoms of speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence and profession (Article 19).
4. Cultural and educational rights (Articles 29 and 30).
5. The right to vote in Lok Sabha and State Assembly elections.
6. The right to contest for Parliament and the State legislatures.
7. Eligibility for certain high offices: President, Vice-President, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, Governors, the Attorney General and Advocates General.

Along with rights come duties: paying taxes, respecting the national flag and anthem, defending the country (the formal list is Article 51A; see GS-166).

## A point UPSC likes

In India both a citizen by birth and a naturalised citizen can be President. In the USA only a citizen by birth is eligible. The Constitution of India does not make birth-citizenship a condition.

## The storyline of this lesson

Part II of the Constitution (Articles 5 to 11) says who were citizens on 26 January 1950 and lets Parliament make the rest of the law. Parliament did so in the **Citizenship Act, 1955**, which gives five ways to **acquire** and three ways to **lose** citizenship. India has **single citizenship**. A special category of **Overseas Citizens of India** exists. Amendments, the Assam story and the 2019 Act add the debates. Modules 2 to 8 follow this order.

### 2. Articles 5 to 11: citizens at commencement and Parliament's power

*Objective:* Name the four categories of persons who became citizens on 26 January 1950 and the role of Article 11.

## What Part II does and does not do

The Constitution deals with citizenship in **Articles 5 to 11 of Part II**. Laxmikanth stresses that it contains neither a permanent nor an elaborate scheme. It only identifies who **became** citizens at **commencement (26 January 1950)**. It does not deal with acquisition or loss afterwards; instead it empowers Parliament to legislate. (See bridge GS-162-B2 for 'domicile'.)

## The four categories (Articles 5 to 8)

**1. Persons domiciled in India (Article 5).** A person who had his domicile in India and satisfied any one of three conditions: born in India; or either parent born in India; or ordinarily resident in India for five years immediately before commencement.

**2. Migrants from Pakistan (Article 6).** A person who migrated to India from Pakistan became a citizen if he, or a parent or grandparent, was born in undivided India, and: (a) if he migrated before **19 July 1948**, he had been ordinarily resident in India since migration; or (b) if he migrated on or after 19 July 1948, he had been registered as a citizen, which required six months' residence before applying. Footnote: 19 July 1948 is when the permit system for such migration started.

**3. Migrants to Pakistan who returned (Article 7).** A person who migrated to Pakistan after **1 March 1947** but later returned for resettlement could become a citizen after six months' residence and registration. The extract's footnote says this covers migration before 26 January 1950; later migrants fall under the 1955 Act.

**4. Persons of Indian origin abroad (Article 8).** A person, or whose parent or grandparent was born in undivided India, who ordinarily resides outside India became a citizen if registered by an Indian diplomatic or consular representative, before or after commencement. This is the provision for overseas Indians.

Summary line: domiciled persons; migrants from Pakistan; migrants to Pakistan who returned; persons of Indian origin abroad.

## Articles 9, 10 and 11

- **Article 9:** a person who would be a citizen by Article 5, or deemed one by Article 6 or 8, is not a citizen if he has **voluntarily acquired** the citizenship of a foreign State. It is narrower than a general ban on dual citizenship (verify the text).
- **Article 10:** every person who is or is deemed a citizen continues to be one, subject to any law of Parliament.
- **Article 11:** Parliament may make any provision on **acquisition and termination** of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship.

## Table 6.2 at a glance

| Article | Subject |
|---|---|
| 5 | Citizenship at commencement |
| 6 | Certain migrants from Pakistan |
| 7 | Certain migrants to Pakistan |
| 8 | Persons of Indian origin residing outside India |
| 9 | Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship |

## Why Article 11 is the key

Because Article 11 lets Parliament decide the details, India's citizenship rules can be changed by an ordinary law (no Article 368 amendment is needed). That is why the Act has been amended many times (modules 3 to 7).

### 3. Citizenship Act, 1955: acquisition by birth and by descent

*Objective:* Apply the date-based rules for citizenship by birth and by descent to sample persons.

## The Act and its five ways

The **Citizenship Act, 1955** governs acquisition and loss after the Constitution began. Originally it also provided Commonwealth citizenship, repealed by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003. It prescribes **five ways** to acquire citizenship: **birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation of territory** (NCERT lists the same five). Memory cue: B-D-R-N-I.

## 1. By birth: three time bands

| Born in India | Citizen by birth if (source) |
|---|---|
| 26 Jan 1950 to 30 Jun 1987 | irrespective of the nationality of the parents |
| 1 Jul 1987 to 2 Dec 2004 | either parent is a citizen of India at the time of birth |
| 3 Dec 2004 onwards | both parents are citizens, or one is a citizen and the other is not an **illegal migrant** |

Exception: the children of **foreign diplomats** posted in India and of **enemy aliens** cannot acquire citizenship by birth. (The 1 July 1987 change is usually linked to the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1986, and the 3 December 2004 change to the 2003 Amendment; the extract gives the dates but not the Act names, so verify the names.)

## 2. By descent: persons born outside India

| Born outside India | Citizen by descent if (source) |
|---|---|
| 26 Jan 1950 to 9 Dec 1992 | father was a citizen of India at the time of birth |
| 10 Dec 1992 onwards | either parent was a citizen at the time of birth |
| 3 Dec 2004 onwards | additionally the birth must be **registered at an Indian consulate within one year** (or later with Central Government permission) |

For registration of a minor's birth, the application must carry the parents' written undertaking that the child does not hold another country's passport. A minor who is a citizen by descent and also a citizen of another country **ceases to be an Indian citizen** if he does not renounce the other citizenship within six months of attaining full age.

## Thinking aloud on three people

- *Asha, born in Chennai in 1982 to Sri Lankan parents.* Band 1: citizen by birth irrespective of parents.
- *Bilal, born in Dubai in 1999 to a mother who is an Indian citizen.* Descent after 1992: either parent suffices. Not a birth-in-India case.
- *Chen, born in Delhi in 2010 to a foreign diplomat.* Excluded: diplomats' children do not get citizenship by birth.

## Why the dates moved

The direction of change is clear from the table: automatic territorial birthright (the *jus soli* idea, citizenship from place of birth) was replaced by a parentage test (*jus sanguinis*, citizenship from blood or parents). This follows the concern with migration that Module 7 develops.

### 4. Registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory, and the Assam Accord clause

*Objective:* Tell registration and naturalisation apart by their conditions, and explain incorporation and the Assam Accord provisions.

## 3. By registration

The Central Government may, on application, register as a citizen any person who is **not an illegal migrant** and belongs to one of these classes:
- (a) a person of Indian origin ordinarily resident in India for **seven years** before applying;
- (b) a person of Indian origin ordinarily resident in a country or place outside undivided India;
- (c) a person **married to a citizen** and ordinarily resident in India for seven years before applying;
- (d) **minor children** of citizens;
- (e) a person of full age and capacity whose **parents are registered citizens**;
- (f) a person of full age whose parent was earlier a citizen of independent India (or who himself was), resident for **twelve months** before applying;
- (g) a person of full age registered as an **OCI cardholder for five years** and resident for twelve months (see module 6).

A person is of **Indian origin** if he, or either parent, was born in undivided India or in a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947. Every such person takes an **oath of allegiance**.

## 4. By naturalisation

A certificate of naturalisation can be granted by the Central Government to a person (not an illegal migrant) who:
1. is not a subject or citizen of a country that bars Indians from naturalising there;
2. undertakes to renounce his existing citizenship if accepted;
3. has resided or served a Government in India throughout the **12 months** before applying;
4. during the **14 years** before that 12-month period, has resided or served in India for aggregate periods of at least **11 years**;
5. is of good character;
6. has adequate knowledge of a language in the **Eighth Schedule** (22 languages at present, originally 14);
7. intends to reside in India, or serve a Government in India, an international organisation of which India is a member, or a society or company established in India.

All or any of these may be **waived** for persons who have rendered distinguished service to science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace or human progress. A naturalised citizen takes an oath to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution.

## 5. By incorporation of territory

If a foreign territory becomes part of India, the Government specifies who among its people become citizens from a notified date. Example: after Puducherry joined India, the **Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962** was issued under the Act.

## 6. The Assam Accord clause (1985)

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1985, inserted special provisions for persons covered by the **Assam Accord** (the foreigners' issue): (a) persons of Indian origin who came to Assam from Bangladesh **before 1 January 1966** and have ordinarily resided there are **deemed citizens from 1 January 1966**; (b) those who came **on or after 1 January 1966 but before 25 March 1971** and were detected as foreigners must register, and are deemed citizens after **ten years from detection**, with the rights of a citizen in between **except the right to vote**. (This is commonly cited as Section 6A of the Act; the section number is not in the extract, so verify.)

## Compare at a glance

| Feature | Registration | Naturalisation |
|---|---|---|
| Typical person | Indian origin, spouse, children, OCI | Foreigner with no Indian ancestry needed |
| Residence | 7 years (or 12 months for some) | 12 months plus 11 of previous 14 years |
| Waiver | Not described | Possible for distinguished service |

### 5. Loss of citizenship and single citizenship

*Objective:* Distinguish renunciation, termination and deprivation, and explain why India has single citizenship with listed exceptions.

## Three ways to lose citizenship

The Act prescribes three ways whether citizenship was acquired under the Act or earlier under the Constitution.

**1. Renunciation (voluntary, by declaration).** Any citizen of full age and capacity can declare that he renounces Indian citizenship; on **registration** of the declaration he ceases to be a citizen. If made during a war in which India is engaged, registration is withheld. When a person renounces, every **minor child** also loses citizenship, but on reaching **eighteen** may resume it.

**2. Termination (automatic, by law).** When a citizen **voluntarily** (consciously, knowingly, without duress or undue influence) acquires another country's citizenship, Indian citizenship **automatically terminates**. This does not apply during a war in which India is engaged.

**3. Deprivation (compulsory, by the Central Government).** The Central Government can deprive a citizen if:
- (a) citizenship was obtained by **fraud**;
- (b) the citizen has shown **disloyalty to the Constitution**;
- (c) the citizen **traded or communicated with the enemy** unlawfully during a war;
- (d) within **five years** of registration or naturalisation he was imprisoned in any country for **two years**; or
- (e) he has been ordinarily resident outside India for **seven years continuously**.

Footnote exceptions to (e): it does not apply to a student abroad, one in service of a government in India or of an international organisation of which India is a member, or one who has registered annually at an Indian consulate his intention to retain citizenship. (The procedure of notice and inquiry before deprivation is not in the extract; verify.)

**Who can be deprived.** Deprivation applies to citizens by naturalisation or registration (and Article 5(c) citizens), not to citizens by birth or descent (Section 10; standard knowledge, verify).

| Mode | Who acts | Voluntary? |
|---|---|---|
| Renunciation | the citizen (declaration) | yes |
| Termination | law, on voluntary foreign acquisition | acquisition is voluntary, loss is automatic |
| Deprivation | Central Government | no |

## Single citizenship

India is federal in structure, yet the Constitution provides only **one citizenship: Indian**. There is no separate State citizenship; citizens owe allegiance only to the Union. The USA and Switzerland adopted **double citizenship**; in the USA each person is a citizen of both the nation and a State, with two sets of rights, which can lead to States favouring their own citizens in voting, office and professions. In India all citizens enjoy the same civil and political rights everywhere. Canada, like India, has a single system. The framers' aim: fraternity and unity.

## Four listed exceptions to non-discrimination

1. **Article 16:** 16(2) bars residence discrimination in public employment; 16(3) lets Parliament alone prescribe residence within a State or UT as a condition for specified posts. The Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957 allowed this for non-Gazetted posts in Andhra Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Manipur and Tripura; it **expired in 1974**. Today Andhra Pradesh and Telangana are covered through **Article 371D**.
2. **Article 15** bars discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, but **not on residence**. So States may favour residents, for example fee concessions.
3. **Article 19** movement and residence are subject to protecting **Scheduled Tribe** interests, restricting outsiders in tribal areas.
4. Till 2019, **Jammu and Kashmir** could define permanent residents under **Article 35A**; the 2019 Presidential order superseded the 1954 order.

### 6. Overseas Citizenship of India: OCI, PIO and NRI

*Objective:* Explain how OCI arose, what the 2015 merger did, and why OCI is not dual citizenship.

## The story in dates

- **September 2000:** the Government set up a High Level Committee on the Indian Diaspora under **L. M. Singhvi**; it reported in **January 2002** and recommended amending the Act to give dual citizenship to Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) of certain countries.
- **2003 Amendment:** introduced **Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)** for PIOs of **16 specified countries** other than Pakistan and Bangladesh; removed Commonwealth citizenship.
- **2005 Amendment:** extended OCI to PIOs of all countries except **Pakistan and Bangladesh**, provided the home country allows dual citizenship.
- **PIO card scheme** started **19 August 2002**; **OCI card scheme** **2 December 2005**; both ran in parallel, causing confusion.
- **2015 Amendment:** merged the schemes into the **Overseas Citizen of India Cardholder** scheme; the PIO scheme was rescinded with effect from **9 January 2015** and existing PIO cardholders are deemed OCI cardholders from that date.

## Not dual citizenship

Laxmikanth stresses that OCI is **not** dual citizenship, because, on Laxmikanth's reading, the Constitution forbids dual citizenship (he cites Article 9, whose text only bars those claiming citizenship under Articles 5, 6 or 8 who voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship; the wider single-citizenship position rests also on the Citizenship Act, 1955; verify). An OCI cardholder is a foreign citizen with a long-term status.

## Who can be registered (as the 2015 Act states)

A person of full age who is a citizen of another country but (i) was a citizen of India on or after 26 January 1950; or (ii) was eligible to become one on that date; or (iii) belonged to a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947; or (iv) is a child, grandchild or great-grandchild of such a person; plus minor children; plus a **foreign spouse of a citizen or OCI cardholder** after the marriage has subsisted for at least **two years**. Anyone whose parent, grandparent or great-grandparent is or was a citizen of **Pakistan, Bangladesh or any country the Centre specifies** is not eligible.

## What an OCI cardholder cannot do

Not entitled to equality of opportunity in **public employment**; not eligible to be **President or Vice-President**; not eligible as **Supreme Court or High Court judge**; cannot be a **registered voter**; cannot be a member of **Parliament or a State legislature**; cannot hold public posts except as the Centre specifies.

## Cancellation

The Centre may cancel OCI registration for fraud, disaffection towards the Constitution, trading with the enemy in war, a sentence of two years within five years of registration, interests of sovereignty, security or public order, or the dissolution or bigamous second marriage of a spouse-based registration.

## Comparing three labels

| | NRI | PIO (scheme rescinded 2015) | OCI cardholder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Indian citizen ordinarily abroad with an Indian passport | Foreign-passport holder of Indian ancestry | Registered under the 1955 Act |
| Citizen of India? | Yes | No | No |
| Visa to visit | No | Yes (per Laxmikanth's table, which refers to a person of Indian origin without a card; PIO card holders were visa-exempt for 15 years; verify) | Lifelong multiple-entry; no visa |
| Path to citizenship | already a citizen | 7 years' ordinary residence | OCI for 5 years plus 12 months' residence |

## The Assam story, neutrally

The Assam Accord arose from Assam's long agitation about people entering from Bangladesh. Its dividing lines are 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971 (module 4). Later, an updated **National Register of Citizens (NRC)** for Assam was prepared under Supreme Court supervision; a final list was published on 31 August 2019, reported to include about 3.1 crore names and exclude about 19 lakh (verify the figures). The Supreme Court is reported to have upheld Section 6A of the Act in October 2024 by a 4:1 majority (verify). Whether to extend a nationwide NRC has been a separate political question; the Government has said no decision was taken (verify).

## The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (flag: verify against the Act)

As widely reported, the Act: (i) lets persons belonging to Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi or Christian communities from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan** who entered India on or before **31 December 2014** be treated as not 'illegal migrants'; (ii) reduces the residence needed for naturalisation of these groups from **eleven to five years**; (iii) does not apply to Sixth Schedule tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura or areas under the Inner Line permit system. Rules were reportedly notified in March 2024. Challenges were pending in the Supreme Court at last report (verify).

### 8. Rights, duties and the wider idea of citizenship

*Objective:* Sort constitutional rights into citizens-only and all-persons, and use NCERT's ideas on equal membership.

## Who gets what: citizens only versus all persons

**Citizens only (Laxmikanth's list):** Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30; the right to vote and to contest elections; eligibility for specified offices. Reasons from the text: Article 15(1) forbids discrimination by the State 'against any citizen'; Article 16 speaks of 'citizens'; Article 19 of 'all citizens'; Article 29 protects 'any section of citizens'. Article 30 speaks of 'minorities', which Laxmikanth places among citizen rights; read the words of the Article before relying on the point (standard knowledge; verify).

**All persons (including aliens) as GS-166 lists:** Articles 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 and 28. Exception noted by Laxmikanth: Article 22 protection against arrest does not cover enemy aliens.

**Duties:** the Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) bind citizens only (see GS-166 module 4).

## Comparison table: citizen versus alien

| Feature | Citizen | Alien |
|---|---|---|
| Membership | full member | citizen of another State |
| Civil and political rights | all | not all |
| Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30 | yes | no |
| Vote and contest | yes (Lok Sabha and Assemblies) | no |
| High offices | eligible | not eligible |
| Articles 14, 21 | yes | yes |
| Article 22 | yes | friendly: yes; enemy: no |
| Allegiance | to India | to own State |

## NCERT's wider ideas (for Mains dimensions)

- **Full and equal membership** also means citizen-to-citizen obligations and trusteeship of culture and resources.
- Disputes over migration within India ('Mumbai for Mumbaikars') test whether freedom of movement includes a right to live and work anywhere.
- **Equal rights need not mean uniform policy**; the aim is to make people more equal.
- **T. H. Marshall** (1950) saw citizenship as civil, political and social rights.
- Urban poor, slum dwellers and tribal groups show unequal capacity to use equal rights. The Supreme Court's 1985 decision in the Olga Tellis case read the right to livelihood into Article 21.
- **Stateless people** and refugees show the limit of national citizenship. India gave refuge to the Dalai Lama and followers in 1959. NCERT discusses **global citizenship** as a supplement, not a substitute, for national citizenship.
- Citizenship in a democracy is a **project**, constantly renegotiated.

### 9. Examination application: the two verified questions and traps

*Objective:* Decode the 2021 Prelims question and the 2026 Mains question using this lesson, and learn the main traps.

## Prelims 2021 (upsc-2021-prelims-gs-i-q89)

Three statements: (1) only one citizenship and one domicile; (2) only a citizen by birth can become Head of State; (3) a foreigner once granted citizenship cannot be deprived of it under any circumstances. (Analysis, not an official key.)

- **Statement 1.** Module 5 gives single citizenship from the source. 'One domicile' follows the reading in bridge B2: the Constitution speaks of domicile in India, not in a State.
- **Statement 2.** Conflicts with the source (module 1): in India a naturalised citizen can also be President. The word 'only' is the trap.
- **Statement 3.** Conflicts with module 5: deprivation exists (fraud, disloyalty, etc.). The phrase 'under any circumstances' is the trap.

On this analysis the statements consistent with the lesson are 1 alone, so look for the option '1 only'.

## Mains 2026 (upsc-2026-mains-gs-ii-q4), 10 marks

Question: is the right to vote a fundamental right; the Election Commission's position in revising electoral rolls; can it examine citizenship of voters? **The extract covers none of this directly except that voting is a citizen right.** The following is standard knowledge to verify:

- The right to vote is a **constitutional right** (Article 326 grants adult suffrage to citizens aged eighteen and above) with a statutory framework, but the Supreme Court has repeatedly described it as not a Fundamental Right in Part III (for example in the Jyoti Basu case of 1982). A 2013 PUCL ruling on NOTA linked casting a vote to freedom of expression. Verify wording.
- The Election Commission is under **Article 324** the authority for superintendence, direction and control of electoral rolls.
- Section 16 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 disqualifies non-citizens from enrolment in an electoral roll (standard knowledge; verify).
- Citizenship is a **qualification** for enrolment; the Citizenship Act and Rules decide who is a citizen. The Supreme Court has said a roll officer cannot treat an enrolled elector as a non-citizen without notice and a fair opportunity (the Lal Babu Hussein case, 1995; verify). Recent special revisions of rolls (such as the 2025 Bihar exercise) led to Supreme Court hearings; check the latest reports before writing.

## Traps

1. 'Only' and 'under any circumstances' statements.
2. Mixing registration with naturalisation residence periods.
3. Treating OCI as dual citizenship.
4. Saying birthright automatically gives citizenship after 1987.
5. Confusing termination (automatic) with deprivation (by the Centre).
6. Putting Article 21 in the citizen-only list.

- Alien: a citizen of another State on Indian soil; friendly aliens belong to countries with cordial relations, enemy aliens to a country at war with India.
- Person: any human being on Indian soil, citizen or alien; the widest category.
- Domicile: a permanent home with intention to stay; Article 5 uses it as a test, but domicile is not citizenship.
- Illegal migrant: a statutory category excluded from registration and naturalisation (exact definition to be verified in the Act).
- OCI cardholder: a foreign citizen of Indian origin registered under the 1955 Act; not a citizen and not a dual citizen.

### Notes: Articles 5 to 11 (Part II)

- Article 5: citizenship at commencement for persons domiciled in India who were born in India, or whose either parent was born in India, or who had been ordinarily resident for five years.
- Article 6: certain migrants from Pakistan; cut-off date 19 July 1948 separating residence-based and registration-based routes.
- Article 7: certain migrants to Pakistan who returned for resettlement after 1 March 1947.
- Article 8: persons of Indian origin living outside India, registered by an Indian consular officer.
- Article 9: voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship ends citizenship.
- Article 10: continuance of citizenship, subject to Parliament's law.
- Article 11: Parliament may regulate the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all related matters.

### Notes: Exceptions to single citizenship and uniformity

- Article 16: 16(2) bars residence discrimination in public employment; 16(3) lets Parliament alone prescribe residence for specified posts; the 1957 Act expired in 1974; Articles 371D covers Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.
- Article 15 does not bar preference by residence; States offer fee concessions to residents.
- Article 19: movement and residence curtailed for protection of Scheduled Tribes.
- Jammu and Kashmir permanent residents under Article 35A until 2019; the 2019 order superseded the 1954 order.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Termination (automatic by law) versus deprivation (Central Government order).
- Registration (mainly Indian origin, spouse, children, OCI) versus naturalisation (foreigner with long residence).
- OCI versus dual citizenship: OCI is a long-term status of a foreign citizen.
- Domicile versus citizenship: a foreigner can be domiciled in India.
- Rights of citizens (Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30) versus rights of persons (Articles 14, 21 and others).

### Notes: The 2019 Act debate in one frame (verify before quoting)

- Concern about migration and the Assam agitation produced the Accord, cut-off dates and later the NRC exercise.
- The 2019 Act created a religion-linked fast track for six communities from three neighbouring countries with a cut-off of 31 December 2014 (as widely reported).
- Supporters stress protection from religious persecution and the Partition legacy; critics stress equality, secularism and exclusions.
- The legal question is whether the classification is reasonable under Article 14; challenges were pending at last report; no outcome is asserted here.

### Core concepts

- Citizenship is full and equal membership of a political community.
- The Constitution (Articles 5 to 11) only names who were citizens at commencement; Parliament makes the rest (Article 11).
- The Citizenship Act 1955 has five modes of acquisition (BDRNI) and three modes of loss (RTD).
- India has single citizenship; OCI is not dual citizenship.
