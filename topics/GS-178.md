### Bridge: Electoral systems in plain words: constituency, first-past-the-post and proportional representation

A **constituency** is a geographical area whose voters elect representatives. If one person is elected from each constituency it is a **single-member constituency**. An **electorate** is the set of people entitled to vote. **Universal adult franchise** means every adult citizen above a stated age (18 in India) can vote, whatever their wealth, caste or sex, unless disqualified by law.

There are two broad ways to turn votes into seats.

1. **Territorial representation, also called first-past-the-post (FPTP).** The country is divided into constituencies, each sends one member, and the candidate who gets the most votes wins. The winner does not need more than half of all votes; being ahead of every rival is enough. It is simple, but a party can win many seats with a minority of votes, and small groups can be left without a voice.
2. **Proportional representation (PR).** Seats are shared in proportion to votes. One form is the **single transferable vote (STV)**, in which each voter ranks candidates 1, 2, 3 and the lowest candidate's ballots are passed on to the next preference until someone reaches the quota (GS-175 module 4 shows a hand count). The other form is the **list system**, where parties win seats in proportion to votes for their lists.

An **electoral college** is a small body of electors who choose one office-holder or a few representatives, instead of every citizen voting. When people vote for representatives and those representatives then elect someone else, the election is **indirect**. India uses direct FPTP for the Lok Sabha and indirect STV for the Rajya Sabha. You will see why in modules 2 and 3.

### Bridge: Party, whip, floor and defection in plain words

Most members of Parliament are elected as candidates of a **political party**, an organised group that contests elections under a common name and programme. Inside a House, the members of one party form that party's **legislature party** (the party's MPs in that House). The **floor** of the House is a way of saying 'the debate and the voting in the chamber', so 'crossing the floor' means moving from one side to the other.

A **whip** is a party officer who makes sure members attend and vote as the party decides. The written instruction sent to members on how to vote is also called a whip or a **direction**. Laxmikanth records that the office of whip is mentioned neither in the Constitution nor in the Rules of the House nor in any statute; it rests on parliamentary convention. Both ruling and opposition parties have whips.

**Defection** means a legislator leaving the party on whose ticket he was elected, or acting against its directions. Before 1985 a member could change parties freely after winning, which the reformers saw as damaging stability and encouraging bargaining for office. **Dissent** is different: it means disagreeing with your party's line on a question. One of the central debates about the Tenth Schedule (module 8) is whether the law can tell dissent apart from defection. For now remember the three words: **party** (the group), **whip** (the instruction) and **defection** (breaking from the party).

### 1. Orientation: Parliament's three parts and the map of this lesson

*Objective:* Name the three parts of Parliament, explain why the President is part of it, and see how this lesson is organised and linked to earlier ones.

## What Parliament is

**Parliament** is the legislative organ of the Union government: it makes laws and checks the executive. India chose the parliamentary ('Westminster') form of government, so Parliament sits at the centre of the political system. **Articles 79 to 122** in **Part V** deal with its organisation, composition, duration, officers, procedures, privileges and powers. (An *Article* is a numbered provision; a *Part* is a group of Articles; a *Schedule* is a list at the end of the Constitution.)

## Three parts, not two

Parliament consists of the **President**, the **Council of States** (Rajya Sabha, the Upper House) and the **House of the People** (Lok Sabha, the Lower House); the Hindi names were adopted in 1954. The Rajya Sabha represents the States and Union Territories; the Lok Sabha represents the people as a whole.

The President is not a member of either House, yet he is an integral part, for reasons Laxmikanth lists: a Bill passed by both Houses cannot become law without his assent; he summons and prorogues both Houses and dissolves the Lok Sabha; he addresses both Houses; and he issues ordinances when they are not in session. The framers followed the British pattern (the Crown is part of Parliament), not the American one. This is **President-in-Parliament**: the parliamentary system stresses interdependence of legislature and executive, the presidential system their separation.

### 2. Composition of the Rajya Sabha and the Lok Sabha

*Objective:* State the maximum and present strength of each House, who the members represent and how many are elected or nominated, and recognise where Laxmikanth's figures predate later changes.

## Rajya Sabha

The maximum strength of the Rajya Sabha is **250**: up to **238** representatives of the States and Union Territories (elected indirectly) and **12** nominated by the President. At the time Laxmikanth wrote it has **245** members. The **Fourth Schedule** allots seats to the States and Union Territories.

- **States.** Their representatives are elected by the *elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies*, by proportional representation using the single transferable vote. Seats are allotted by **population**, so Uttar Pradesh has 31 members and Tripura 1 (NCERT gives Sikkim as another State with one). In the United States every State has two Senators regardless of size, and the Senate has no nominated members.
- **Union Territories.** Their representatives are elected by an electoral college specially constituted for the purpose, again by STV. Laxmikanth says only three of the nine Union Territories (Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir) have seats; the January 2020 merger of Dadra and Nagar Haveli with Daman and Diu is reported to have left eight Union Territories (verify).
- **Nominated members.** The President nominates 12 persons with special knowledge or practical experience in art, literature, science and social service, so that eminent people have a place without contesting an election; NCERT carries a student's question about whom they represent.

**Source slip to correct.** Laxmikanth prints 229 members for States, 4 for Union Territories and 12 nominated; these mix two dates, counting Jammu and Kashmir's four seats with the States although the same page lists it as a Union Territory. After the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 the usual split is **225 States + 8 Union Territories (Delhi 3, Puducherry 1, Jammu and Kashmir 4) + 12 nominated = 245** (verify against the Rajya Sabha website). Check: 225 + 8 = 233; 233 + 12 = 245; 238 + 12 = 250.

## Lok Sabha

Laxmikanth gives the maximum as **552** (530 States, 20 Union Territories, 2 nominated Anglo-Indians) and the strength as **545** (530 + 13 + 2). After the **104th Amendment (2019)**, reported to have ended Anglo-Indian nomination from January 2020, the Lok Sabha has **543 elected members** and the elected maximum is 550 (530 + 20); verify. NCERT says there are 543 constituencies, unchanged since the 1971 census.

- **States.** Members are **directly elected** from territorial constituencies on universal adult franchise: every citizen above **18** who is not disqualified may vote (the **61st Amendment (1988)** lowered the age from 21).
- **Union Territories.** Parliament decides how they are represented; the Union Territories (Direct Election to the House of the People) Act, 1965 made them directly elected too.
- **Nominated members.** The President could nominate two Anglo-Indians if the community was not adequately represented; the provision, due to end in 1960, was extended repeatedly, to 2020 by the 95th Amendment (2009).

## Why two Houses?

NCERT's reasons: large and diverse countries need representation for every section and region, and a second chamber lets every decision be reconsidered. The Rajya Sabha is also the federal House, which is why its powers over the States are special (GS-175 module 8).

### 3. How members reach the Houses: constituencies, delimitation, reservation and the voting system

*Objective:* Explain the uniformity rules for constituencies, delimitation and the seat freeze, reservation of seats, and why the Lok Sabha uses first-past-the-post while the Rajya Sabha uses proportional representation.

## Territorial constituencies

For direct elections each State is divided into territorial constituencies. The Constitution makes two uniformity rules.

1. Each State gets Lok Sabha seats so that the ratio of seats to population is the **same for every State** (this does not apply to a State with a population under six million).
2. Within a State, the ratio of each constituency's population to its seats is the same.

So representation is uniform between States and between constituencies in a State. 'Population' means the population ascertained at the preceding census whose figures have been published.

## Readjustment and the freeze

After every census the allocation of seats to States and the division of States into constituencies is readjusted. Parliament has enacted Delimitation Commission Acts for this in 1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** froze the allocation and division at the **1971 level till 2000**, to encourage population-limiting measures. The **84th Amendment (2001)** extended the ban by 25 years (Laxmikanth says 'up to 2026'; the standard wording is until the first census taken after 2026, so verify). The same Amendment allowed readjustment inside States on the 1991 census; the **87th Amendment (2003)** changed this to the 2001 census, *without altering the number of seats allotted to each State*.

## Reservation of seats

The Constitution abandoned communal representation, but it reserves Lok Sabha seats for the **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** in proportion to population. It was meant to last ten years (to 1960) and has been extended every time; Laxmikanth says to 2020 by the 95th Amendment, and the 104th Amendment (2019) is reported to have extended it to 2030 (verify). Reserved seats are filled by votes of **all** voters in the constituency; there is no separate electorate, and an SC or ST person may also contest a general seat. The **106th Amendment (2023)**, the women's reservation law, is reported to reserve one-third of Lok Sabha and State Assembly seats for women, taking effect only after a census and delimitation (verify the current position).

## First-past-the-post and proportional representation

India uses **territorial representation (FPTP)** for the Lok Sabha: each constituency elects one member and the candidate with the most votes wins. Laxmikanth calls this a simple majority system; strictly, the winner needs only a plurality. Its weakness: it does not give due representation to minorities.

Proportional representation fixes that weakness. India uses its STV form for the **Rajya Sabha, the State legislative councils, and the elections of the President and Vice-President**. PR was not adopted for the Lok Sabha because of (1) its complexity for voters at low literacy and (2) its tendency to multiply parties, unsuited to stable parliamentary government; Laxmikanth adds that it is expensive, leaves no scope for by-elections, loosens voter-representative contact and makes the party more important than the voter.

For how an STV count works by hand, see GS-175 module 4; for the Presidential version, GS-174.

### 4. Duration of the Houses and how a seat is vacated

*Objective:* State the term of each House, how the Lok Sabha ends and can be extended, and list the five ways a member vacates a seat.

## Rajya Sabha: a continuing chamber

The Rajya Sabha (first constituted in 1952) is a **permanent body, not subject to dissolution**. **One-third** of its members retire **every second year**, and their seats are filled at the beginning of every third year; retiring members may be re-elected or renominated any number of times. The Constitution does not fix the term; the Representation of the People Act, 1951 provides **six years**. For the first batch, lots decided who retired. NCERT notes the advantage: even if the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the Rajya Sabha can be called to conduct urgent business.

## Lok Sabha: five years, but not for ever

The Lok Sabha is not a continuing chamber. Its normal term is **five years from the date of its first meeting after the general election**, after which it dissolves automatically. The President may dissolve it earlier (Laxmikanth adds that this cannot be challenged in a court of law: his statement, not settled law, since *S.R. Bommai* (1994) made dissolution of a State Assembly under Article 356 reviewable [verify]); NCERT says this happens if no party or coalition can form a government, or if the Prime Minister advises it. The term can be **extended only during a national emergency**, by a law of Parliament, for one year at a time, and the extension cannot continue beyond six months after the emergency ceases.

## How a seat is vacated

1. **Double membership.** A person cannot be in both Houses: if elected to both he must say within **10 days** which he keeps, otherwise his Rajya Sabha seat falls vacant; a sitting member of one House elected to the other loses the first seat; a person elected to two seats in one House must choose, or both become vacant. A person cannot be in Parliament and a State legislature together: the Parliament seat falls vacant unless he resigns the State seat within **14 days**.
2. **Disqualification**, including under the Tenth Schedule (module 8).
3. **Resignation** in writing to the Chairman or Speaker; the seat falls vacant when it is *accepted*, and the presiding officer may refuse if satisfied it is not voluntary or genuine.
4. **Absence** from **all meetings for sixty days** without permission, if the House declares the seat vacant; periods of prorogation or adjournment for more than four consecutive days are not counted.
5. **Other cases:** election declared void by a court; expulsion by the House; election as President or Vice-President; appointment as Governor.

If a disqualified person is elected, the Constitution lays down no procedure; the Representation of the People Act, 1951 lets the High Court declare the election void, with appeal to the Supreme Court.

## Test the rule

A sitting Lok Sabha member who is also elected to the Rajya Sabha loses his Lok Sabha seat, because a sitting member of one House elected to the other vacates the first. A member absent for 70 days, of which 20 fall in a period of prorogation, has only 50 countable days, so the 60-day threshold is not met.

### 5. Who can be a member: qualifications, disqualifications and who decides

*Objective:* State the qualifications for membership, list the constitutional and statutory disqualifications and say who decides ordinary disqualification and defection.

## Qualifications

The Constitution lays down four: the person must (1) be a **citizen of India**; (2) make and subscribe an **oath or affirmation** before a person authorised by the Election Commission, swearing true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India; (3) be at least **30 years** old for the Rajya Sabha and **25 years** for the Lok Sabha; and (4) possess other qualifications Parliament prescribes.

Under the **Representation of the People Act, 1951** he must be a registered **elector for a parliamentary constituency** (same for both Houses); the earlier rule that a Rajya Sabha candidate must be an elector of the State concerned was dispensed with in 2003, and the Supreme Court upheld the change in 2006. For a seat reserved for SCs or STs he must belong to such a community, but they may also contest unreserved seats. Compare the President and the Vice-President (35 years; GS-174, GS-175).

## Disqualifications under the Constitution (Article 102)

A person is disqualified from being chosen, or from being, a member if:

1. he holds an **office of profit** under the Union or a State government, except that of a minister or any other office Parliament has exempted;
2. he is of **unsound mind** and stands so declared by a court;
3. he is an **undischarged insolvent**;
4. he is **not a citizen**, or has voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship or is under an acknowledgement of allegiance to a foreign State; or
5. he is disqualified by a **law of Parliament**.

An **office of profit** is a government post that brings financial gain, creating a conflict of interest between the member and the executive he is meant to scrutinise. Standard knowledge (verify with the Constitution): the Constitution does **not define** the term; the **Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959** exempts a list of offices.

## Extra disqualifications under the 1951 Act

Laxmikanth lists: election offences or corrupt practices; **conviction with imprisonment of two or more years** (preventive detention is not a disqualification); failure to lodge election expenses; interest in government contracts, works or services; directorship or an office of profit in a corporation in which the government holds at least 25 per cent; dismissal from government service for corruption or disloyalty; conviction for promoting enmity between groups or for bribery; and punishment for preaching social crimes such as untouchability, dowry and sati. Standard knowledge (verify): the Supreme Court's 2013 *Lily Thomas* ruling removed the three-month protection, so disqualification follows conviction at once.

## Who decides

On any of the above, the **President's decision is final**, but he must obtain the **Election Commission's opinion** and act accordingly. **Defection** is different: it is decided by the **Chairman** (Rajya Sabha) or **Speaker** (Lok Sabha), not the President (module 8).

## Edge cases

A minister is not disqualified for an office of profit. A man held in preventive detention is not disqualified by that detention alone. A 29-year-old citizen who is a registered elector can contest the Lok Sabha but not the Rajya Sabha.

### 6. Sessions: summoning, adjournment, adjournment sine die, prorogation and dissolution

*Objective:* Explain each way a sitting, a session or a House is started or ended, who has the power and what happens to pending business.

## Summoning

The **President** from time to time **summons** each House to meet (Article 85; the standard wording adds 'at such time and place as he thinks fit', which a 2020 Prelims statement tested; verify the text). The President summons and prorogues the Houses on the advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74(1)); in practice the Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs proposes the dates [verify]. The maximum gap between two sessions is **six months**, so Parliament must meet at least **twice a year**. There are usually **three sessions**: **Budget** (roughly late January or February to April or May), **Monsoon** (July to September) and **Winter** (November to December). The Constitution fixes no minimum number of sittings and does not itself name three sessions; that is practice.

The session vocabulary (sitting, session, recess, lame-duck session) is taught in **GS-181 bridge module 1**; keep one point: a session runs from a House's first sitting to its prorogation (or the dissolution of the Lok Sabha), and recess is the gap between prorogation and the next session.

## Adjournment and adjournment sine die

An **adjournment** suspends the work of a sitting for a specified time: hours, days or weeks. **Adjournment sine die** terminates a sitting for an indefinite period, meaning without naming a date to meet again. The power of both lies with the **presiding officer**, who can also call a sitting before the date to which it was adjourned, or after the House was adjourned sine die.

## Prorogation

When the business of a session is complete, the presiding officer declares the House adjourned sine die. Within a few days the **President issues a notification of prorogation**, ending the session. The President can also prorogue a House while it is still in session. Pending Bills and other business are *not* affected by prorogation in India, but all pending *notices* (other than for introducing Bills) lapse and fresh notices are needed. In Britain prorogation ends pending business (subject to carry-over motions; verify), so do not carry that rule over.

## Dissolution

The Rajya Sabha, as a permanent House, is not dissolved. Only the **Lok Sabha** is. Dissolution ends the life of the House itself and a new House is constituted after a general election. It happens by **expiry of the term** (five years, or as extended in an emergency) or by the **President's decision**; once the Lok Sabha is dissolved before its time the dissolution is **irrevocable**. By convention the President dissolves on the advice of the Council of Ministers; the standard position is that he acts on advice save in exceptional circumstances (verify; a 2024 Prelims question tested it).

The Speaker does not leave office on dissolution; he continues until the new Lok Sabha meets (GS-183 module 2). The comparison table with adjournment and prorogation is in the master notes.

### 7. Lapse of Bills, quorum, voting and the joint sitting recap

*Objective:* Apply the six rules on lapse of Bills on dissolution, compute the quorum, state how decisions are taken and recall when a joint sitting can and cannot be held.

## What lapses on the dissolution of the Lok Sabha

On dissolution, all business pending before the Lok Sabha or its committees (Bills, motions, resolutions, notices, petitions) lapses and must be reintroduced in the new House. Some Bills survive. Laxmikanth's six rules:

| Situation of the Bill | Lapses? |
|---|---|
| Pending in the Lok Sabha (originated there or sent by the Rajya Sabha) | Yes |
| Passed by the Lok Sabha but pending in the Rajya Sabha | Yes |
| Not passed by the two Houses due to disagreement, and the President has notified a joint sitting before dissolution | No |
| Pending in the Rajya Sabha but not passed by the Lok Sabha | No |
| Passed by both Houses, pending the President's assent | No |
| Passed by both Houses but returned by the President for reconsideration | No |

Assurances pending before the Committee on Government Assurances also survive. GS-179 module 3 teaches the same rules; this table is a recap. Test: a Bill that originated in the Rajya Sabha, was passed there and is pending in the Lok Sabha lapses (first row); a Bill introduced in the Rajya Sabha and still there survives (fourth row).

## Quorum

**Quorum** is the minimum number of members present before a House can transact business: **one-tenth of the total number of members, including the presiding officer**, so at least **55** in the Lok Sabha and **25** in the Rajya Sabha. Check: 545 ÷ 10 = 54.5, rounded up to 55 (with 543 elected members, 54.3, also 55); 245 ÷ 10 = 24.5, rounded up to 25. Without quorum the presiding officer must **adjourn the House or suspend the meeting**.

## Voting

All matters in a House or joint sitting are decided by a **majority of members present and voting, excluding the presiding officer**, who votes only to break a tie. A few matters (impeachment of the President, constitutional amendment, removal of presiding officers) need a **special majority**. Proceedings stay valid despite any vacancy. For 'majority of all the then members' versus 'present and voting', see GS-183 bridge module 3.

## Joint sitting: a recap with the reasoning

Full rules are in GS-179 module 3. For the Mains question on when a joint sitting happens and when it cannot: it is summoned by the President to break a **deadlock between the Houses on a Bill**, and the Speaker presides. It is *unavailable* for **Money Bills** (the Lok Sabha prevails: the Rajya Sabha can only recommend and the Bill is deemed passed after 14 days) and for **Constitution Amendment Bills** (each House passes them separately by a special majority), and if the Bill lapsed on dissolution with no notification before it.

## Ministers and the Attorney General

Every minister and the Attorney General may speak and take part in the proceedings of either House, a joint sitting and any committee of which he is a member, **without a vote**; a minister who is not yet a member (allowed for six months) may take part in both Houses.

### 8. Master layer: the anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule)

*Objective:* State the grounds, exceptions and deciding authority under the Tenth Schedule, the changes of the 91st Amendment, the main criticisms and the points that need checking against recent rulings.

## Origin

The **52nd Amendment Act (1985)** provided for disqualification of members of Parliament and State legislatures for defection. It changed four Articles (101, 102, 190 and 191) and added the **Tenth Schedule**. The **91st Amendment (2003)** made one change in the Schedule: it removed the exception for a *split*.

## Grounds of disqualification

- A **member belonging to a party** is disqualified if (a) he **voluntarily gives up membership**, or (b) he **votes or abstains** contrary to his party's direction without prior permission and the party has not condoned it within **15 days**.
- An **independent** member is disqualified if he **joins a party after election**.
- A **nominated** member is disqualified if he **joins a party after six months** from taking his seat.

## Exceptions

1. **Merger.** A member who leaves because his party merged with another is not disqualified; a merger needs **two-thirds** of the party's members (Laxmikanth's words; the Schedule speaks of two-thirds of the *legislature party*; verify paragraph 4).
2. **Presiding officer.** A member elected presiding officer who gives up party membership, or rejoins it after leaving office, is exempt, for the dignity and impartiality of the office.

## Who decides, and the Supreme Court

The question is decided by the **presiding officer of the House**. The original Schedule made his decision final and barred the courts. In *Kihoto Hollohan* the Supreme Court upheld the Tenth Schedule but struck down paragraph 7, which barred the courts, because an amendment affecting the courts' jurisdiction needed ratification by the States. Paragraph 6's 'finality' survives only in a limited sense: the presiding officer acts as a tribunal and his decision is subject to judicial review (GS-183 module 5) [verify]. Laxmikanth prints the year as 1992 in chapter 22 and as 1993 in chapter 76; treat 1992 as the decision and 1993 as a reporting year (verify). He acts only on a **complaint from a member**, must give the accused a chance to explain and may refer the matter to the Committee of Privileges, so defection has no automatic effect.

## What the 91st Amendment added

The **split exemption** (one-third of a legislature party) is deleted, so only merger protects. A defector is **barred from becoming a minister** and from any **remunerative political post** (paid from public revenue or by a government-owned body); the extract does not state the length of the bar, and the standard text says until the end of the term or re-election, whichever is earlier (verify). The Amendment also capped the Council of Ministers at **15 per cent** of the Lok Sabha (State: 15 per cent of the Assembly, floor of 12 including the Chief Minister).

## Evaluation

Aims: curb defections motivated by office or money and strengthen democracy. Advantages: stability, orderly realignment by merger, less corruption, constitutional recognition of parties. Criticisms: it does not separate **dissent from defection**, restricting conscience and empowering party bosses; it banned retail (individual) defection but legalised wholesale (group) defection, still possible through the two-thirds merger exception even after the split exemption went in 2003; it ignores expulsion for acts outside the House; it treats independents and nominated members inconsistently; and the **presiding officer** may be partisan and untrained in law. NCERT adds that the law has not stopped defections but has strengthened party leaders and presiding officers.

## Recent developments: verify before use

Standard knowledge, flagged: courts have dealt with delay by Speakers, expelled members, whips and mergers; a 2020 ruling is reported to have urged Parliament to consider an independent tribunal and asked Speakers to decide within three months (also noted in GS-183). *Subhash Desai v Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra* (2023) is reported to hold that the political party, not the legislature party alone, appoints the whip, and that a 'split' is no defence after 2003 [verify]. Read each judgment before citing it.

## Mains skeleton (15 marks, 250 words): 'Parliament in India has declined into a rubber stamp of the Cabinet. Critically examine.'

### Notes: Leaders in Parliament and some language rules

- Leader of the House: the Prime Minister if a member of the Lok Sabha, or a minister nominated by him; the Rajya Sabha has its own, a minister and member of that House. Not mentioned in the Constitution; in the Rules.
- Leader of the Opposition: leader of the largest opposition party with not less than one-tenth of the seats of the House; statutory recognition in 1977; first recognised officially in 1969; salary and facilities of a Cabinet minister; the British Shadow Cabinet is the comparison. As reported, the Lok Sabha had no recognised Leader of the Opposition after 2014 and 2019 and had one after 2024 (verify).
- Whip: party officer who ensures attendance and discipline; based on convention, not on the Constitution, the Rules or a statute.
- Language: Hindi and English are the languages of business; the presiding officer can permit use of the mother tongue; simultaneous translation is provided; English continued after 1965 through the Official Languages Act, 1963.
- Voting procedure in the Lok Sabha: Aye or No; lobby cleared for three minutes thirty seconds on a challenge; division recorded by automatic vote recorder, slips or lobbies.

### Notes: Confused pairs and exceptions

- Adjournment (a sitting) versus prorogation (a session) versus dissolution (the Lok Sabha); and 'adjournment motion' (a device to raise an urgent matter, GS-181) is an entirely different thing.
- Prorogation lapses pending notices but not pending Bills in India; dissolution lapses Bills pending in the Lok Sabha.
- The Rajya Sabha member's six-year term is not the House's term: the House is permanent.
- Majority of all the then members, majority present and voting, and special majority are three different rules (GS-183 bridge module 3).
- Ordinary disqualification (President, after Election Commission's opinion) versus defection (presiding officer).
- Quorum counts the presiding officer; voting excludes him except in a tie.
- Exception: the Speaker does not vacate office when the Lok Sabha is dissolved, although the House's life ends.
- Exception: the Tenth Schedule exemption for merger needs two-thirds, and since the 91st Amendment removed the split exemption, one-third is no longer enough.

- **coreConcepts:**
  - Parliament is the President plus two Houses; the President is an integral part without being a member.
  - The Rajya Sabha is a permanent, indirectly elected House with 12 nominated members; the Lok Sabha is directly elected, lasts five years and can be dissolved.

  - - **id:** GS-178-F1
    - **q:** What are the three parts of Parliament?
    - **a:** The President, the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha).
  - - **id:** GS-178-F2
    - **q:** Who elects the Lok Sabha members from the States, and on what franchise?
    - **a:** The people directly, on universal adult franchise, for every citizen aged 18 or above who is not disqualified.
  - - **id:** GS-178-F3
    - **q:** What is the difference between a sitting and a session?
    - **a:** A sitting is one meeting on a day; a session is the period from the first sitting of a House to its prorogation (or dissolution).
  - - **id:** GS-178-F4
    - **q:** What is the quorum in each House and who is counted?
    - **a:** One-tenth of the total members including the presiding officer: 55 in the Lok Sabha and 25 in the Rajya Sabha.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **id:** GS-178-C1
    - **q:** Why is the Rajya Sabha called a permanent House even though each member serves six years?
    - **a:** Because it is never dissolved: one-third of its members retire every second year and are replaced, so the House as a body continues.
  - - **id:** GS-178-C2
    - **q:** Why did the Constitution not adopt proportional representation for the Lok Sabha?
    - **a:** It is complicated for voters at low literacy, and it tends to multiply parties, which is unsuited to stable parliamentary government.
  - - **id:** GS-178-C3
    - **q:** Why does a Bill pending only in the Rajya Sabha survive the dissolution of the Lok Sabha, while one pending in the Lok Sabha does not?
    - **a:** Dissolution ends the Lok Sabha's life, so business pending before it lapses; the Rajya Sabha is not dissolved, so a Bill alive only there continues.
  - - **id:** GS-178-C4
    - **q:** Why is ordinary disqualification decided by the President on the Election Commission's opinion while the Tenth Schedule is left to the presiding officer?
    - **a:** Ordinary disqualification goes to the President after the opinion of an independent constitutional body (the Election Commission); the Tenth Schedule gave defection to the presiding officer, though the Supreme Court made his decision reviewable.
- **application:**
  - - **id:** GS-178-A1
    - **q:** The Rajya Sabha has 245 members. Compute its quorum, showing the rounding.
    - **a:** 245 / 10 = 24.5, rounded up to 25.
  - - **id:** GS-178-A2
    - **q:** A member elected to a State Assembly is also elected to the Lok Sabha and does nothing. After how many days does what happen?
    - **a:** After 14 days, if he has not resigned from the State legislature, his seat in Parliament falls vacant.
  - - **id:** GS-178-A3
    - **q:** An independent member joins a party two years after election. A nominated member joins a party after one year of taking his seat. Which is disqualified?
    - **a:** Both: an independent joining a party after election is disqualified, and a nominated member joining after six months is disqualified.

Article 80(1): the Council of States consists of (a) twelve members to be nominated by the President and (b) not more than two hundred and thirty-eight representatives of the States and of the Union territories. Article 80(3): the nominated members are persons having special knowledge or practical experience in 'Literature, science, art and social service'. Article 80(4): the representatives of each State are elected by the elected members of the Legislative Assembly of the State by proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. Article 80(5): the representatives of the Union territories are chosen in such manner as Parliament may by law prescribe.
  Source: [The Constitution of India [as on 1st May, 2026], Legislative Department, Ministry of Law and Justice, Government of India](https://legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf)

### Last-week summary

- Rajya Sabha max 250 (238 + 12 nominated), STV by MLAs, permanent, one-third retire every second year, six-year term, age 30, quorum 25.
- Lok Sabha max 550 elected (530 States + 20 UTs), FPTP, five years from first meeting (emergency extension a year at a time), age 25, quorum 55.
- Sessions: gap at most six months, three sessions only by practice; adjournment (presiding officer, sitting), prorogation (President, session), dissolution (Lok Sabha only).
- Dissolution lapses Bills pending in, or passed by, the Lok Sabha; Bills pending only in the Rajya Sabha, awaiting assent, or notified for joint sitting survive.
- Disqualification: ordinary by the President on the EC's opinion (Art 103); defection by Chairman/Speaker; Tenth Schedule 1985, merger two-thirds, split exemption deleted 2003.
