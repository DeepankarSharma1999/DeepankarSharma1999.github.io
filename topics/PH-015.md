### Bridge: Vectors and the cross product

A **vector** is a quantity that needs both a size and a direction to be stated completely: velocity, force, position. A **scalar** needs only a size: mass, time, energy. We write vectors in bold or with an arrow; in this lesson a bold symbol such as **r**, **ω**, **L** is a vector and plain r, ω, L is its magnitude (size).

Every vector can be split into three **components** along the three perpendicular axes x, y, z. If **r** has components (x, y, z) then its magnitude is r = √(x² + y² + z²).

Two products matter here.

1. The **dot (scalar) product** gives a number: **a · b** = a b cos θ, where θ is the angle between them. It is zero when the vectors are perpendicular. In components, **a · b** = aₓbₓ + a_yb_y + a_zb_z.

2. The **cross (vector) product** gives a vector: **a × b** has magnitude a b sin θ and points perpendicular to both **a** and **b**, in the direction a right-handed screw advances when turned from **a** towards **b** (the "right-hand rule": curl the fingers of your right hand from **a** to **b**; the thumb gives the direction). Key facts: **a × b** = −**b × a** (the order matters), **a × a** = 0, and for the unit vectors **i × j** = **k**, **j × k** = **i**, **k × i** = **j**.

In components:

$$ a × b = (a_y b_z − a_z b_y) i + (a_z b_x − a_x b_z) j + (a_x b_y − a_y b_x) k $$

One identity is used repeatedly in rigid-body work, the "BAC−CAB" rule:

$$ a × (b × c) = b (a · c) − c (a · b) $$

Rotational quantities are all cross products: torque **τ** = **r × F**, angular momentum **L** = **r × p**, velocity of a point of a spinning body **v** = **ω × r**. If the cross product is new to you, practise with **i × j** = **k** until the right-hand rule feels automatic; every derivation in the lesson relies on it.

### Bridge: Matrices and eigenvalues in one page

A **matrix** is a rectangular array of numbers. A 3×3 matrix A acts on a column vector **v** (three numbers stacked) and produces a new vector A**v**: each component of the result is a dot product of one row of A with **v**. Example: the matrix with rows (0, −1, 0), (1, 0, 0), (0, 0, 1) turns (1, 0, 0) into (0, 1, 0): it rotates the x-axis onto the y-axis, i.e. it is a 90° rotation about z.

Terms used in this lesson:

- **Transpose** Aᵀ: rows and columns interchanged. (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ.
- **Identity** 1: ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere; 1**v** = **v**.
- **Orthogonal matrix**: AᵀA = 1, i.e. Aᵀ is the inverse of A. Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles, so every rotation is an orthogonal matrix.
- **Determinant** det A: a single number built from the entries. Facts we need: det(AB) = det A · det B; det(Aᵀ) = det A; for a 3×3 matrix det(−A) = (−1)³ det A = −det A; det 1 = 1. For an orthogonal matrix det A = ±1; rotations have det A = +1 (the value −1 means a mirror reflection is included, which a rigid body cannot do).
- **Symmetric matrix**: A = Aᵀ. The inertia tensor is symmetric.
- **Eigenvector and eigenvalue**: a non-zero vector **n** with A**n** = λ**n** is an eigenvector; the number λ is its eigenvalue. The matrix only stretches **n**, it does not turn it. Rewriting as (A − λ1)**n** = 0, a non-zero solution exists only if det(A − λ1) = 0; this cubic equation in λ is the **characteristic (secular) equation**. A real symmetric 3×3 matrix always has three real eigenvalues and three mutually perpendicular eigenvectors.

Why this matters: Euler's theorem says every rotation matrix has an eigenvector with eigenvalue exactly +1 (the axis), and the inertia tensor's eigenvectors are the principal axes of a body. You do not need to compute 3×3 determinants from scratch today; you need to recognise these words when they appear.

### Bridge: Radians and angular quantities

A **radian** is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius. Because the full circumference is 2πr, a complete turn is 2π radians, so 1 rad = 360°/(2π) ≈ 57.3°, a right angle is π/2 rad and a half turn is π rad.

The reason physics insists on radians is the relation

$$ s = r θ $$

between arc length s, radius r and angle θ; it holds only when θ is in radians. Differentiating with respect to time gives the speed of a point moving on the circle, v = r ω, where **angular velocity** ω = dθ/dt is measured in rad/s. A second differentiation gives tangential acceleration a_t = r α, where **angular acceleration** α = dω/dt is in rad/s².

Conversions you will use constantly: one revolution per second = 2π rad/s; N revolutions per minute (rpm) = 2πN/60 rad/s. Example: 300 rpm = 300 × 2π/60 = 10π ≈ 31.4 rad/s.

A radian is a ratio of two lengths, so it is **dimensionless**: the dimensions of ω are simply T⁻¹ and of α are T⁻². That is why v = rω has dimensions L × T⁻¹ = L T⁻¹, as a speed must. Checking dimensions like this ("a dimensional check") is a standard final step in every derivation in this lesson, and in the exam it earns credit and catches algebra slips.

Finally, the word "angular" in front of any quantity means "the rotational analogue of": angular displacement θ replaces distance s, angular velocity ω replaces velocity v, angular momentum L replaces momentum p, and torque τ replaces force F.

### 2. What a rigid body is and what motions it can have

*Objective:* Define a rigid body through its distance constraint, explain why real bodies are only approximately rigid, and distinguish translation, rotation about a fixed axis and rotation about a fixed point.

## Definition

A **rigid body** is a system of particles in which the distance between every pair of particles stays constant during the motion. If **r**ᵢ and **r**ⱼ are the position vectors of particles i and j, the condition is

$$ |r_i − r_j| = c_ij = constant for every pair (i, j) $$

A condition like this, which restricts the possible positions of the particles, is called a **constraint**. The rigid-body constraint is the strongest geometric constraint there is: once you know where three points of the body are, every other point is fixed.

## Why real bodies are "approximately rigid"

No real body is perfectly rigid. Every solid deforms a little under force (a steel beam bends, a spinning disc stretches outward), and sound travels through a solid at a finite speed, so a push at one end reaches the other end only after a delay. A body may be treated as rigid when these deformations are negligible compared with the motion being studied. Wheels, tops, gyroscopes, the Earth as a whole and small molecules in their lowest vibrational states are all treated as rigid in the relevant problems. Rubber, water or a chain cannot be, because their shape changes under the very forces that move them. Rigidity is therefore a **model**, chosen when it is accurate enough, not a property any body possesses exactly.

## The two kinds of motion

**Translation**: every particle of the body has the same velocity at every instant, so the body moves without changing its orientation. A block sliding down a slope translates. The whole body then moves like a single particle located at its **centre of mass**, with the whole mass concentrated there.

**Rotation about a fixed axis**: a straight line of the body (the axis) stays at rest and every other particle moves in a circle whose plane is perpendicular to the axis and whose centre lies on it. All particles turn through the same angle in the same time, so a single angular velocity ω describes the entire body. A ceiling fan, a door, a potter's wheel are examples.

**Rotation about a fixed point**: only one point is at rest, and the axis of rotation passes through that point but changes direction from moment to moment. A top spinning on its tip is the classic example; its axis slowly sweeps out a cone (**precession**, a later topic). This is the case that Euler's theorem (Module 4) is about.

**General motion**: a body that is neither pivoted nor fixed can translate and rotate at the same time: a rolling cylinder, a thrown spanner, a tumbling satellite. Chasles' theorem (Module 4) says this general motion is always a translation plus a rotation.

## Why the distinction matters

For a fixed axis the direction of **ω** never changes and the problem is one-dimensional: one angle θ(t). For a fixed point the direction of **ω** changes, angular momentum need not be parallel to **ω**, and the full machinery of the inertia tensor and Euler's equations is needed. Knowing which case you are in is the first decision in every rigid-body problem.

### 3. Counting degrees of freedom and describing a configuration

*Objective:* Define degrees of freedom, derive the number 6 for a free rigid body by the three-point argument, reduce it for a linear molecule, a fixed point and a fixed axis, and meet Euler angles as the three orientation coordinates.

## Definition

The **degrees of freedom** of a system is the number of independent coordinates needed to specify its configuration (the positions of all its parts) completely. "Independent" means none of them can be computed from the others.

A single free particle needs three numbers (x, y, z): 3 degrees of freedom. N free particles need 3N. Every constraint that can be written as an equation among the coordinates removes one degree of freedom, because one coordinate can then be calculated from the rest. Such constraints, expressible as equations between coordinates (and possibly time), are called **holonomic**; the rigid-body constraint is holonomic.

## The three-point argument: a free rigid body has 6 degrees of freedom

Do not try to count the constraints |rᵢ − rⱼ| = constant one by one: for N particles there are N(N−1)/2 of them, far more than 3N, because most are not independent. Count the coordinates that survive instead.

1. Choose any particle P₁ of the body. Its position needs 3 coordinates.
2. Choose a second particle P₂ not coinciding with P₁. Its distance from P₁ is fixed, so it must lie on a sphere centred on P₁. A point on a sphere needs 2 coordinates (like latitude and longitude). Running total: 5.
3. Choose a third particle P₃ not on the line P₁P₂. Its distances from both P₁ and P₂ are fixed, so it lies on the intersection of two spheres, which is a circle. A point on a circle needs 1 coordinate (an angle). Running total: 6.
4. Any further particle P₄ is at fixed distances from the three non-collinear points P₁, P₂, P₃. Three spheres meet in at most two points, mirror images of each other in the plane P₁P₂P₃, and a rigid motion cannot turn the body into its mirror image, so P₄ is fixed with no new coordinate.

Hence a rigid body has exactly **6 degrees of freedom**, however many particles it contains.

## Splitting the six: 3 translational + 3 rotational

The natural choice of the first point is the **centre of mass**; its three coordinates (X, Y, Z) describe where the body is (**translation**). The remaining three describe how the body is turned about its centre of mass (**orientation**, i.e. rotation). The standard three orientation coordinates are the **Euler angles** (φ, θ, ψ): starting from axes fixed in space, turn the body by φ about the space z-axis, then by θ about the new x-axis (the "line of nodes"), then by ψ about the body's own z-axis. Any orientation can be reached by such a sequence. You only need the idea today; the formulas belong to the top and gyroscope topic, where φ is the precession angle, θ the tilt (nutation angle) and ψ the spin angle.

## Reductions

| System | Degrees of freedom | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Free particle | 3 | x, y, z |
| N free particles | 3N | 3 each |
| Free rigid body | 6 | 3 (position of one point) + 2 (second point on a sphere) + 1 (third point on a circle) |
| Rigid body with one point fixed (top on its tip, gyroscope in gimbals) | 3 | position known; only the three Euler angles remain |
| Rigid body with a fixed axis (door, fan, flywheel) | 1 | one angle θ about the axis |
| Rigid linear molecule (N₂, O₂, CO₂) | 5 | 3 for the centre of mass + 2 for the direction of the line; turning about the line itself changes nothing |
| Rigid non-linear molecule (H₂O, NH₃) | 6 | three non-collinear atoms, so the full count applies |

The linear case deserves a second look. If all particles lie on one line, step 3 of the argument never happens: there is no third point off the line. So the count stops at 5. Physically, rotating the line about itself moves no mass, so it is not a change of configuration. This is exactly why a diatomic gas has 5 degrees of freedom per molecule in kinetic theory (3 translational + 2 rotational, ignoring vibration), while a monatomic gas has 3.

## Trap to notice now

"Degrees of freedom of a rigid body" (6) and "degrees of freedom of a gas molecule" in thermodynamics (3, 5, 6, 7 or more depending on structure and on whether vibration is counted) use the same words for related but differently applied counts. In a rigid-body question, count configuration coordinates; in a kinetic-theory question, count the ways the molecule can store energy.

### 4. Chasles' theorem and Euler's theorem on rotation about a fixed point

*Objective:* State Chasles' theorem and Euler's rotation theorem precisely, prove Euler's theorem completely by the spherical construction, and understand the matrix proof through the eigenvalue +1.

## Statements

**Euler's theorem (rotation theorem):** the most general displacement of a rigid body with one point O fixed is equivalent to a single rotation about some axis passing through O.

**Chasles' theorem:** the most general displacement of a rigid body is a translation plus a rotation. More precisely, choose any point P of the body; the displacement equals a translation that carries P to its final position P′, followed by a rotation about an axis through P′. The translation depends on which point you choose; the rotation (axis direction and angle) does not.

Chasles follows from Euler. Translate the whole body by the vector from P to P′ (every particle gets the same displacement, so this is a pure translation). After this step P is already in its final place, so the rest of the displacement leaves P′ fixed; by Euler's theorem it is a rotation about an axis through P′. In mechanics P is usually the centre of mass, which is why the motion of a rigid body is split into "motion of the centre of mass" plus "rotation about the centre of mass".

## Geometric proof of Euler's theorem (complete)

**Setup.** Because O is fixed and the body is rigid, every point at unit distance from O stays at unit distance. So the displacement maps the unit sphere centred on O onto itself and preserves the great-circle distance between any two points on it (great-circle distance is fixed by the straight-line chord, which is fixed by rigidity). Pick two points A and B on this sphere, not coincident and not at opposite ends of a diameter, and let the displacement carry them to A′ and B′. Rigidity gives arc AB = arc A′B′.

**Step 1 (construct a candidate fixed point).** If A′ = A or B′ = B, that point is already fixed; go to Step 3. Otherwise draw the great circle C_A made of all points of the sphere equidistant from A and A′ (the perpendicular bisector of arc AA′); it is the intersection of the sphere with the plane Π_A through O that bisects AA′ perpendicularly. Draw C_B likewise for B and B′. Choose B so that C_B ≠ C_A. This is always possible. Suppose that every point B that the displacement moves had C_B = C_A. Then each such B′ would be the mirror image of B in the plane Π_A, so the displacement would act on the sphere as the reflection in Π_A (and any point it leaves unmoved would be a fixed point, ending the proof at once). A reflection reverses handedness, but the displacement of a rigid body is a continuous motion from the identity and preserves handedness, so it cannot be a reflection. Hence some B has C_B ≠ C_A. Two distinct great circles always intersect, in two diametrically opposite points. Call one of them P. (A generic choice of B also avoids the degenerate case in which P lies on the great circle through A and B.)

**Step 2 (P is fixed).** By construction PA = PA′ and PB = PB′, and AB = A′B′. So the spherical triangles PAB and PA′B′ have three pairs of equal sides and are congruent, but they could be congruent with the same handedness or as mirror images. We show that the handedness is the same. If triangles PAB and PA′B′ had opposite handedness, the reflection in the plane of C_A (which swaps A and A′ and fixes O and P) would carry B to B′. Reason: that reflection maps triangle PAB to a triangle PA′B* of opposite handedness to PAB, with PB* = PB and A′B* = AB; the only two points at these distances from P and A′ are mirror images of each other in the plane OPA′, and the opposite handedness selects B′, so B* = B′. That makes the plane of C_A the perpendicular-bisector plane of BB′, so C_A = C_B, contrary to the choice in Step 1. Hence the two triangles have the same handedness. Now follow P under the actual displacement: it goes to some point P″ with P″A′ = PA and P″B′ = PB, so triangle P″A′B′ is the image of PAB under the displacement and has the same handedness as PAB. Triangle PA′B′ also has the same handedness as PAB. Both P and P″ are therefore on the same side of the great circle through A′ and B′ and at the same distances from A′ and B′; there is only one such point, so P″ = P. Hence P is a fixed point.

**Step 3 (a fixed line means a rotation).** The displacement fixes both O and P, hence every point of the line OP (each is determined by its distances from O and P). Every other particle Q keeps its perpendicular distance from the line OP and stays in its plane perpendicular to that line, so it moves on a circle about the line. All particles must turn through the same angle, otherwise distances between them would change. A motion in which every particle turns through the same angle about one fixed line is, by definition, a rotation about that line. Because the displacement preserves handedness it is not the reflection in a plane containing OP; a distance- and handedness-preserving map fixing the line OP is a rotation about it. Therefore the displacement is a rotation about the axis OP through the fixed point. ∎

## Matrix proof (sketch, completed in Practice derivation D1)

Fix axes at O. The orientation change is a linear map **r** → R**r** with R a 3×3 matrix. Rigidity means lengths are preserved, so RᵀR = 1 (R is orthogonal; for square matrices this also gives R Rᵀ = 1, which the chain below uses); continuity from the identity means det R = +1, which the chain also uses. Then

$$ det(R − 1) = det(R − R Rᵀ) = det R · det(1 − Rᵀ) = det((1 − R)ᵀ) = det(1 − R) = (−1)³ det(R − 1) = −det(R − 1) $$

so det(R − 1) = 0. Hence there is a non-zero vector **n** with R**n** = **n**: an eigenvector with eigenvalue +1, i.e. a direction the displacement leaves unchanged. That direction is the axis, and the displacement is a rotation about it. The rotation angle φ follows from the trace: Tr R = 1 + 2 cos φ.

## What the theorem gives mechanics

At any instant the motion of a body about a fixed point is a rotation about some **instantaneous axis** through that point, with some angular speed. That is what allows a single vector **ω** (direction = instantaneous axis, magnitude = angular speed) to describe the whole motion, which Module 5 develops. Keep the three "Euler" names apart: Euler's **theorem** is this statement about displacements; Euler **angles** are the three orientation coordinates of Module 3; Euler's **equations** are the equations of motion in the body frame, previewed in Module 8.

### 5. Angular velocity as a vector: v = ω × r, composition, and why finite rotations do not add

*Objective:* Define the angular velocity vector, derive v = ω × r for rotation about a fixed axis or a fixed point, show that infinitesimal rotations commute while finite rotations do not, and hence justify vector addition of angular velocities.

## The angular velocity vector

For rotation about a fixed axis all particles sweep the same angle dθ in the same time dt, so one number ω = dθ/dt describes the body. We make it a vector **ω** by giving it the direction of the axis, with the sense fixed by the right-hand rule: curl the right-hand fingers in the direction of turning and the thumb points along **ω**. Nothing moves along **ω**; the vector is a conventional way to record the plane of rotation (perpendicular to **ω**) and the sense. By Euler's theorem the same description works instant by instant for rotation about a fixed point: **ω** then points along the instantaneous axis and may change direction with time.

## Derivation of v = ω × r

**Setup and assumptions.** Origin O on the axis (or at the fixed point). A particle P of the body has position vector **r** making angle φ with **ω**. In time dt the body turns through dθ = ω dt about the unit vector **n** = **ω**/ω.

1. P moves on a circle whose centre C lies on the axis; the radius is the perpendicular distance r⊥ = r sin φ.
2. The arc moved in dt is ds = r⊥ dθ = r sin φ ω dt.
3. The displacement d**r** is tangent to that circle, hence perpendicular to both **n** and **r**, in the sense of turning: exactly the direction of **n × r**. Its magnitude is |**n × r**| dθ = r sin φ dθ, matching step 2. So d**r** = dθ (**n × r**).
4. Divide by dt:

$$ v = dr/dt = (dθ/dt) n × r = ω × r $$

**Check.** Dimensions: [ω][r] = T⁻¹ L = L T⁻¹, a velocity. Limiting cases: a particle on the axis has **r** ∥ **ω**, so **v** = 0 (the axis is at rest); a particle in the plane through O perpendicular to the axis has φ = 90° and v = ωr, the familiar circular-motion result. Splitting **r** = **OC** + **CP** gives **ω × OC** = 0 (parallel vectors) and **v** = **ω × CP**, which shows that only the perpendicular distance matters.

## Infinitesimal rotations commute; finite rotations do not

An infinitesimal rotation by dθ about **n** sends **r** to **r** + dθ (**n × r**). Apply two of them in succession, about **n**₁ by dθ₁ and about **n**₂ by dθ₂:

$$ r → r + dθ₁ (n₁ × r) + dθ₂ (n₂ × r) + (terms of order dθ₁ dθ₂) $$

To first order the result is the same in either sequence; the sum dθ₁**n**₁ + dθ₂**n**₂ behaves like a single vector. Dividing by dt, **angular velocities add as vectors**: if a body turns with **ω**₁ relative to a frame that itself turns with **ω**₂ relative to the laboratory, every particle has **v** = **ω**₁ × **r** + **ω**₂ × **r** = (**ω**₁ + **ω**₂) × **r**, so the body's angular velocity relative to the laboratory is **ω** = **ω**₁ + **ω**₂. This is the **composition (parallelogram law) of angular velocities**, and it is why a spinning top's spin and precession can simply be added.

Finite rotations are different. Take the vector (1, 0, 0). A 90° rotation about x leaves it as (1, 0, 0); a following 90° rotation about y (which sends **i** → −**k**) gives (0, 0, −1). Reverse the order: the 90° rotation about y first gives (0, 0, −1); the 90° rotation about x (which sends −**k** → **j**) then gives (0, 1, 0). The results (0, 0, −1) and (0, 1, 0) differ, so finite rotations do **not** commute and cannot be represented by vectors that add. You can see the same thing by turning a book 90° about a vertical and then about a horizontal edge, and then repeating in the opposite order. Only the limit of very small angles produces commuting, vector-like behaviour; angular velocity lives in that limit, which is why it is a legitimate vector while "finite angular displacement" is not.

## Angular acceleration

**α** = d**ω**/dt is also a vector. For a fixed axis the direction of **ω** is constant and α reduces to dω/dt; for a fixed point **α** may point anywhere, because **ω** can change direction as well as magnitude. Mixing these two cases is a frequent source of error in later topics (precession is exactly a case where |**ω**| is constant but **ω** changes direction).

### 6. Angular momentum, the inertia tensor and the equation of motion for rotation

*Objective:* Derive L = Σ m r × (ω × r), extract the components of the inertia tensor, explain why L is generally not parallel to ω, obtain the axial result L = Iω, and establish τ = dL/dt and τ = Iα for a fixed axis.

## Angular momentum of one particle and of the body

For a particle of mass m at **r** with momentum **p** = m**v**, the **angular momentum about O** is **l** = **r × p**. For a rigid body turning about O with angular velocity **ω**, use **v** = **ω × r** from Module 5 and add over all particles:

$$ L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ × (ω × rᵢ) $$

Expand the triple product with the BAC−CAB identity, **a × (b × c)** = **b**(**a · c**) − **c**(**a · b**), taking **a** = **r**, **b** = **ω**, **c** = **r**:

$$ L = Σ mᵢ [ rᵢ² ω − (rᵢ · ω) rᵢ ] $$

Read this carefully. The first term is along **ω**. The second term is along **r**ᵢ, which is generally not along **ω**. So **L** is **not in general parallel to ω**. Unlike the particle case **p** = m**v**, the rotational relation is not "a scalar times **ω**".

## Components: the inertia tensor

Write **r**ᵢ = (xᵢ, yᵢ, zᵢ), **ω** = (ωₓ, ω_y, ω_z), rᵢ² = xᵢ² + yᵢ² + zᵢ². The x-component of the bracket is rᵢ² ωₓ − (xᵢωₓ + yᵢω_y + zᵢω_z) xᵢ = (yᵢ² + zᵢ²) ωₓ − xᵢyᵢ ω_y − xᵢzᵢ ω_z. Hence

$$ Lₓ = Iₓₓ ωₓ + Iₓ_y ω_y + Iₓ_z ω_z,  with  Iₓₓ = Σ m (y² + z²),  Iₓ_y = −Σ m x y,  Iₓ_z = −Σ m x z $$

and similarly for L_y and L_z. The nine numbers I_jk form a symmetric 3×3 matrix, the **inertia tensor** I, and the relation is **L** = I **ω**. The diagonal elements Iₓₓ, I_yy, I_zz are the **moments of inertia** about the three axes (each is Σ m × (perpendicular distance from that axis)²). The off-diagonal elements are the **products of inertia**; they are what tilt **L** away from **ω**. For a continuous body each sum becomes an integral over dm.

Because I is real and symmetric, there always exist three mutually perpendicular axes through O, the **principal axes**, along which all products of inertia vanish. The moments about them, I₁, I₂, I₃, are the **principal moments of inertia**, found as the roots of the cubic det(I − λ1) = 0. If **ω** lies along a principal axis then **L** = I₁**ω** is parallel to **ω**; otherwise it is not. Any axis of symmetry of a body is a principal axis (for each mass element at (x, y, z) there is a partner that cancels its product of inertia). This is why a sphere, a cube about its centre, or a disc about its own axle have **L** ∥ **ω**, while a rod spun about an axis inclined to its length does not (Numerical N3).

## The axial component: L = Iω about a fixed axis

Take the z-axis along **ω**. The z-component of **L** = Σ m [r² **ω** − (**r · ω**) **r**] is Σ m [r² ω − (r ω cos φ)(r cos φ)] = ω Σ m r² sin² φ = ω Σ m r⊥². Define the **moment of inertia about the axis**

$$ I = Σ m r⊥²  (= ∫ r⊥² dm for a continuous body) $$

Then the component of angular momentum along the axis is L_axis = I ω. The perpendicular components of **L** are produced by the products of inertia and vanish when the axis is a principal axis (for example a symmetry axis through the centre of mass); then the full vector relation **L** = I **ω** holds. Also the rotational kinetic energy is K = ½ Σ m v² = ½ **ω · L**, which reduces to ½ I ω² about a fixed axis.

## Equation of motion

Differentiate **L** = Σ **r**ᵢ × **p**ᵢ. The term **v**ᵢ × **p**ᵢ vanishes (parallel vectors), leaving d**L**/dt = Σ **r**ᵢ × **F**ᵢ. Internal forces come in equal and opposite collinear pairs (Newton's third law) whose torques cancel, so only external torques survive:

$$ τ_ext = dL/dt $$

This is the rotational analogue of **F** = d**p**/dt and holds about a point fixed in an inertial frame or about the centre of mass even when it accelerates. For a **fixed axis** take components along it: τ_axis = d(Iω)/dt = I dω/dt = I α, since I is constant for a rigid body about a fixed axis. The component of torque perpendicular to the axis does not turn the body; it is balanced by the bearings. If no external torque acts, **L** is conserved; that is how a skater speeds up by pulling in her arms (I falls, ω rises, Iω fixed).

**Dimensional check.** [I] = M L², SI unit kg·m². [L] = M L² T⁻¹, SI unit kg·m²/s (the same as J·s, the unit of Planck's constant). [τ] = M L² T⁻², the same dimensions as energy, but torque is a vector and is written in N·m, never in joules.

### 7. Moments of inertia: the parallel-axis and perpendicular-axis theorems, and the rod, ring, disc and sphere

*Objective:* Derive both theorems with their conditions, derive the moments of inertia of a thin rod, a ring, a disc and a solid sphere step by step, and check each result by dimensions and limiting cases.

## Moment of inertia

I = Σ m r⊥² (or ∫ r⊥² dm) measures a body's resistance to changes in rotation about a given axis, exactly as mass measures resistance to changes in translation. It depends on the mass **and** on how the mass is distributed about the axis; move the axis and I changes. Writing I = M K² defines the **radius of gyration** K, the distance at which the whole mass could be concentrated to give the same I. Dimensions M L², unit kg·m².

## Parallel-axis theorem (Steiner)

**Statement.** I about any axis = I about the parallel axis through the centre of mass + M h², where h is the distance between the two axes.

**Derivation.** Put the origin at the centre of mass C and the z-axis along the axis through C. Let the parallel axis pass through the point **h** = (hₓ, h_y, 0) in the xy-plane. For a mass element at **ρ** = (x, y, z), the squared perpendicular distance from the C-axis is x² + y², and from the other axis it is (x − hₓ)² + (y − h_y)². Then

$$ I = Σ m [(x − hₓ)² + (y − h_y)²] = Σ m (x² + y²) − 2hₓ Σ m x − 2h_y Σ m y + (hₓ² + h_y²) Σ m $$

The first term is I_cm. The middle terms contain Σ m x and Σ m y, which are M times the coordinates of the centre of mass and therefore zero because the origin **is** the centre of mass. The last term is M h². Hence

$$ I = I_cm + M h² $$

**Condition.** One of the two axes must pass through the centre of mass. It is wrong to apply the formula between two arbitrary parallel axes; go through the centre of mass in two steps instead. Note I ≥ I_cm: of all parallel axes, the one through the centre of mass has the least moment of inertia.

## Perpendicular-axis theorem (laminae only)

**Statement.** For a plane lamina lying in the xy-plane, I_z = Iₓ + I_y, where the three axes meet at a point of the lamina and z is perpendicular to it.

**Derivation.** Every element has z = 0, so its distance from the x-axis is |y| and from the y-axis is |x|:

$$ Iₓ + I_y = Σ m y² + Σ m x² = Σ m (x² + y²) = Σ m r² = I_z $$

**Why it fails for three-dimensional bodies.** With z ≠ 0, Iₓ = Σ m (y² + z²) and I_y = Σ m (x² + z²), so Iₓ + I_y = I_z + 2 Σ m z² ≠ I_z. For a 3-D body the correct identity is Iₓ + I_y + I_z = 2 Σ m r², used below for the spherical shell.

## Four standard bodies

**Thin uniform rod, length ℓ, mass M, axis through the centre perpendicular to the rod.** Mass per unit length λ = M/ℓ. An element dx at distance x has dm = λ dx and dI = x² λ dx.

$$ I_c = ∫_{−ℓ/2}^{ℓ/2} λ x² dx = λ [x³/3]_{−ℓ/2}^{ℓ/2} = λ (2/3)(ℓ/2)³ = λ ℓ³/12 = M ℓ²/12 $$

About one end: either integrate from 0 to ℓ (I = λℓ³/3 = Mℓ²/3) or use the parallel-axis theorem with h = ℓ/2: Mℓ²/12 + Mℓ²/4 = Mℓ²/3. Both agree.

**Thin ring, radius R, axis through the centre perpendicular to its plane.** Every element is at distance R, so I = Σ m R² = M R². About a diameter: by symmetry the two perpendicular diameters have equal I_d, and the perpendicular-axis theorem gives 2 I_d = M R², so I_d = M R²/2.

**Uniform disc, radius R, axis through the centre perpendicular to its plane.** Mass per unit area σ = M/(πR²). Take a ring of radius x and width dx: dm = σ · 2πx dx, dI = x² dm.

$$ I = ∫_0^R 2πσ x³ dx = 2πσ R⁴/4 = (πσR²) R²/2 = M R²/2 $$

About a diameter, by the perpendicular-axis theorem, I_d = M R²/4.

**Solid sphere, radius R, about a diameter.** Density ρ = M/(4πR³/3). Slice into discs perpendicular to the axis: at height z the disc has radius √(R² − z²), thickness dz, mass dm = ρπ(R² − z²) dz, and (from the disc result) dI = ½ dm (R² − z²).

$$ I = (ρπ/2) ∫_{−R}^{R} (R² − z²)² dz = ρπ ∫_0^R (R⁴ − 2R²z² + z⁴) dz = ρπ R⁵ (1 − 2/3 + 1/5) = (8/15) ρπ R⁵ $$

Substitute ρπ = 3M/(4R³): I = (8/15)(3M/4) R² = (2/5) M R². About a tangent, by the parallel-axis theorem: (2/5 + 1) M R² = (7/5) M R².

**Thin spherical shell** (bonus, by the 3-D identity): all mass at distance R from the centre, so Iₓ + I_y + I_z = 2 M R²; by symmetry the three are equal, so I = (2/3) M R².

## Checks

Dimensions: every result is (number) × M × (length)², correct. Limiting cases: the disc (MR²/2) has smaller I than a ring of the same mass and radius (MR²) because its mass sits closer to the axis; a rectangular plate of width b → 0 reduces to the rod; the radius of gyration of the sphere is K = √(2/5) R ≈ 0.63 R, less than R as it must be since no mass lies farther than R from the axis.

### 8. How the examiner tests this topic, the forward links, and the 2025 question

*Objective:* Recognise the question types set on this clause, avoid the standard traps, see how Euler's equations and precession grow out of τ = dL/dt, and plan an approach to the verified 2025 Paper I question.

## What gets asked

Questions on this clause come in four shapes. (1) **Definition and counting**: define a rigid body, show it has six degrees of freedom, count for a linear molecule. (2) **State and prove**: Euler's theorem; the parallel-axis or perpendicular-axis theorem; L = Iω about an axis. (3) **Derive a moment of inertia** for a named body, often followed by a parallel-axis extension (sphere about a tangent, disc about a rim axis). (4) **Problems on angular momentum and rotation about a fixed point**, which lead into the inertia tensor, principal axes and Euler's equations.

Marks follow structure: a clear statement of assumptions and axes, every algebraic step, a labelled sketch, and a final check. An answer with the right result and no steps scores poorly.

## Traps the examiner relies on

- Confusing **Euler's theorem** (any displacement about a fixed point is a rotation) with **Euler angles** (three orientation coordinates) and **Euler's equations** (equations of motion in the body frame). Decide which "Euler" is meant before writing.
- Applying the **perpendicular-axis theorem to a three-dimensional body** (sphere, cylinder, cube). It holds only for laminae.
- Using the **parallel-axis theorem between two axes neither of which passes through the centre of mass**.
- Counting **degrees of freedom** as "3 per atom" for a rigid molecule, or giving a linear molecule 6 instead of 5.
- Writing **L** = I**ω** with a scalar I for rotation about an arbitrary axis; in general I is a tensor and **L** is not parallel to **ω**.
- Treating a finite angular displacement as a vector.
- Giving torque the unit joule, or omitting the dimensional check.

## Forward links (preview only)

Since **L** = I**ω** with I constant only in axes fixed in the body, write τ = d**L**/dt in the rotating body frame. The derivative in an inertial frame equals the derivative in the body frame plus **ω × L**. Choosing the principal axes gives **Euler's equations**:

$$ I₁ dω₁/dt − (I₂ − I₃) ω₂ ω₃ = τ₁,  I₂ dω₂/dt − (I₃ − I₁) ω₃ ω₁ = τ₂,  I₃ dω₃/dt − (I₁ − I₂) ω₁ ω₂ = τ₃ $$

With no torque, ½(I₁ω₁² + I₂ω₂² + I₃ω₃²) and L² = I₁²ω₁² + I₂²ω₂² + I₃²ω₃² are both conserved. **Precession** is the other consequence of τ = d**L**/dt: a torque perpendicular to **L** changes its direction and not its size, so the axis turns at a rate Ω = τ/L. These are the next lessons; everything in them rests on Modules 5 and 6.

## The verified 2025 question (Paper I, Q2(a), 20 marks)

A body moves about a fixed point under no force; the principal moments at that point are 3A, 5A and 6A; initially ω₁ = n, ω₂ = 0, ω₃ = n; find the components for large t.

**Approach, not an official key.** Write the torque-free Euler equations with I₁ = 3A, I₂ = 5A, I₃ = 6A: 3 dω₁/dt = −ω₂ω₃, 5 dω₂/dt = 3 ω₃ω₁, 3 dω₃/dt = −ω₁ω₂. Form the two conserved quantities from the initial data: 3ω₁² + 5ω₂² + 6ω₃² = 9n² (twice the kinetic energy divided by A) and 9ω₁² + 25ω₂² + 36ω₃² = 45n² (L²/A²). Eliminating gives ω₁² = ω₃² = n² − (5/9)ω₂² at all times; since ω₁ and ω₃ start equal and obey symmetric equations they stay equal. Substituting into the ω₂ equation gives dω₂/dt = (1/3)(9n²/5 − ω₂²), whose solution with ω₂(0) = 0 is ω₂ = (3n/√5) tanh(nt/√5). As t → ∞, ω₂ → 3n/√5 and ω₁ = ω₃ → 0. Check: 5 × 9n²/5 = 9n² and 25 × 9n²/5 = 45n², both conserved quantities are respected. Physically, L²/(2T) = 5A = I₂, so the initial state lies exactly on the separatrix of the intermediate axis, and the motion asymptotically settles into rotation about the intermediate axis. The traps: using the wrong sign pattern in Euler's equations, forgetting that two conserved quantities exist, and expecting an oscillating answer instead of a limiting one.

## How a 10-mark definition-and-counting answer is built (about 150 words)

1. **Definition** (2 lines): a rigid body is a system of particles with |rᵢ − rⱼ| = constant for every pair; real bodies approximate this when deformations are negligible.
2. **Counting argument** (6 to 8 lines): first point 3 coordinates; second point on a sphere, 2; third non-collinear point on a circle, 1; every other point then fixed; total 6 = 3 translational (centre of mass) + 3 rotational (Euler angles).
3. **Reductions** (3 lines): one point fixed, 3; fixed axis, 1; linear molecule, 5, because no third point lies off the line.
4. **One-line check**: the count is independent of the number of particles.

## How a 15-mark "state and prove" answer is built (about 250 words)

1. Statement in one sentence with the condition ("with one point fixed").
2. Setup with a labelled sketch: the unit sphere about O, points A, B and images A′, B′.
3. Construction: perpendicular-bisector great circles C_A and C_B (B chosen so that C_B ≠ C_A, otherwise the displacement would be a reflection), their intersection P.
4. Proof that P is fixed: triangles PAB and PA′B′ are congruent (SSS). If their handedness were opposite, the reflection in the plane of C_A would carry B to B′, making C_A = C_B, contrary to the choice; so the handedness is the same, and since the actual displacement preserves handedness, the image of P is P.
5. Conclusion: fixed line OP, hence a rotation about OP.
6. Remark: equivalently det(R − 1) = 0 for a proper orthogonal R, giving an eigenvector with eigenvalue +1.
7. Corollary (Chasles): translation of any point plus rotation about an axis through it.

For a parallel-axis extension, state the theorem and the condition (through the centre of mass) before using it.

### Notes: Definitions

- Rigid body: a system of particles in which the distance between every pair of particles remains constant, |rᵢ − rⱼ| = constant; a model valid when deformations are negligible.
- Degrees of freedom: the number of independent coordinates needed to specify the configuration of a system completely.
- Holonomic constraint: a restriction expressible as an equation among the coordinates (and possibly time); each independent one removes one degree of freedom.
- Angular velocity vector ω: magnitude dθ/dt, direction along the instantaneous axis of rotation by the right-hand rule.
- Moment of inertia about an axis: I = Σ m r⊥² = ∫ r⊥² dm; radius of gyration K defined by I = MK².
- Inertia tensor: the symmetric 3×3 array I_jk with I_xx = Σ m (y² + z²) and I_xy = −Σ m x y, such that L = I ω.
- Principal axes: the three mutually perpendicular axes through a point along which all products of inertia vanish; the corresponding moments I₁, I₂, I₃ are the principal moments.
- Euler angles (φ, θ, ψ): three successive rotation angles (about space z, about the line of nodes, about body z) that specify the orientation of a rigid body.

### Notes: Theorems stated exactly

- Euler's theorem: the most general displacement of a rigid body with one point fixed is a rotation about some axis through that point.
- Chasles' theorem: the most general displacement of a rigid body is a translation of any chosen point plus a rotation about an axis through that point; the rotation is independent of the point chosen.
- Parallel-axis theorem: I = I_cm + M h², valid when one axis passes through the centre of mass and the axes are parallel at separation h.
- Perpendicular-axis theorem: for a plane lamina in the xy-plane, I_z = I_x + I_y with the three axes concurrent; not valid for three-dimensional bodies.
- Three-dimensional identity: I_x + I_y + I_z = 2 Σ m r² for any body about three perpendicular axes through a point.
- Equation of motion for rotation: τ_ext = dL/dt about a fixed point or the centre of mass; about a fixed axis τ = I α.

### Notes: Key equations

- v = ω × r (velocity of a particle of a body rotating about a fixed axis or fixed point, origin on the axis or at the point).
- Composition: ω = ω₁ + ω₂ when a body turns with ω₁ relative to a frame turning with ω₂.
- L = Σ m r × (ω × r) = Σ m [r² ω − (r·ω) r] = I ω (tensor relation).
- L_axis = I ω and K_rot = ½ ω·L = ½ I ω² about a fixed axis.
- det(R − 1) = 0 for a proper orthogonal matrix R; Tr R = 1 + 2 cos φ gives the rotation angle.
- Torque-free Euler equations: I₁ dω₁/dt = (I₂ − I₃) ω₂ ω₃ and cyclic permutations (preview for the next topic).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: parallel-axis theorem

- Origin at centre of mass C; z-axis along the axis through C; parallel axis through h = (h_x, h_y, 0).
- Squared distance of element (x, y, z) from the parallel axis: (x − h_x)² + (y − h_y)².
- I = Σ m (x² + y²) − 2h_x Σ m x − 2h_y Σ m y + h² Σ m.
- Σ m x = Σ m y = 0 because the origin is the centre of mass; Σ m = M.
- Result I = I_cm + M h²; hence I_cm is the least moment among parallel axes.

### Notes: Standard moments of inertia (uniform bodies)

- Each entry is of the form (number) × M × (length)²; the radius of gyration is the square root of the number times the length.

### Notes: Degrees of freedom at a glance

- Free particle: 3. N free particles: 3N.
- Free rigid body: 6 = 3 (centre of mass) + 3 (orientation).
- Rigid body with one point fixed: 3 (Euler angles). Rigid body with a fixed axis: 1.
- Rigid linear molecule (N₂, O₂, CO₂): 5. Rigid non-linear molecule (H₂O): 6.
- Dumbbell hinged at one end so the hinge cannot move: 2 (direction of the rod only).

### Notes: Easily confused

- Euler's theorem (a statement about displacements about a fixed point) versus Euler angles (orientation coordinates) versus Euler's equations (equations of motion in body axes).
- Perpendicular-axis theorem (laminae only) versus the three-dimensional identity I_x + I_y + I_z = 2Σmr².
- Parallel-axis theorem requires one axis through the centre of mass; between two arbitrary parallel axes it must be applied twice via the centre of mass.
- Angular velocity (a true vector because infinitesimal rotations commute) versus finite angular displacement (not a vector; finite rotations do not commute).
- Torque and energy share dimensions M L² T⁻² but torque is a vector written in N·m, energy a scalar in J; angular momentum shares the unit J·s with Planck's constant.
- L = Iω with scalar I is a component relation about a fixed axis, or a full vector relation only about a principal axis; in general L = I ω with I a tensor.

### Notes: Exceptions and conditions

- v = ω × r requires the origin to lie on the axis of rotation (or at the fixed point); for a general origin add the velocity of the reference point.
- τ = dL/dt holds about a point fixed in an inertial frame or about the centre of mass, not about an arbitrary accelerating point.
- τ = Iα assumes a rigid body (I constant) and a fixed axis; if mass redistributes, use τ = d(Iω)/dt.
- Euler's theorem concerns displacements (orientation changes); it says nothing about translation, which Chasles' theorem adds.
- A linear rigid body has 5 degrees of freedom, not 6, because rotation about its own line moves no mass.

### Notes: Dimensions and units

- ω: T⁻¹, rad/s (radian dimensionless). α: T⁻², rad/s².
- I: M L², kg·m². L: M L² T⁻¹, kg·m²/s = J·s. τ: M L² T⁻², N·m.
- Rotational KE ½Iω²: M L² T⁻², joule. Conversion: N rpm = 2πN/60 rad/s.

### Notes: Procedure for any moment-of-inertia derivation

- State the body, its uniformity, the axis and the coordinate system; draw and label the element.
- Write the mass of the element dm in terms of the density (per length, area or volume) and the element's size.
- Write dI = (perpendicular distance)² dm, using a previously derived result for the element if it is itself extended (disc slices of a sphere).
- Integrate over limits that cover the whole body; simplify using the total mass.
- Apply the parallel-axis or perpendicular-axis theorem for related axes, checking their conditions.
- Finish with a dimensional check and a limiting case.

  - - **q:** State the condition under which Chasles' rotation part is independent of the chosen base point.
    - **a:** It always is: changing the base point changes only the translation; the rotation axis direction and angle are the same.
  - - **q:** Why does a particle on the axis of rotation have zero velocity according to v = ω × r?
    - **a:** Its position vector from the origin on the axis is parallel to ω, and the cross product of parallel vectors vanishes.
  - - **q:** Give one body and axis for which L is parallel to ω and one for which it is not.

    - **task:** Prove Euler's theorem algebraically: show that every proper orthogonal 3×3 matrix R (RᵀR = 1, det R = +1) has an eigenvalue +1, and interpret the eigenvector as the rotation axis. Obtain the rotation angle from the trace.
    - **steps:**
      - Setup: axes fixed at the fixed point O. A rigid displacement about O is linear and preserves lengths, so r → Rr with RᵀR = 1. Continuity from the identity (det 1 = +1, and det is continuous and equals ±1 for orthogonal matrices) gives det R = +1.
      - Write 1 = RRᵀ (for square matrices RᵀR = 1 implies RRᵀ = 1), so R − 1 = R − RRᵀ = R(1 − Rᵀ).
      - Take determinants: det(R − 1) = det R · det(1 − Rᵀ) = 1 · det(1 − Rᵀ), using det R = +1 (this is where the Module 4 chain uses det R = 1).
      - Use det(Aᵀ) = det A: det(1 − Rᵀ) = det((1 − R)ᵀ) = det(1 − R).
      - Use det(−A) = (−1)³ det A for 3×3: det(1 − R) = det(−(R − 1)) = −det(R − 1).
      - Combine: det(R − 1) = −det(R − 1), hence det(R − 1) = 0.
      - Therefore (R − 1)n = 0 has a non-zero solution n: Rn = n. The displacement leaves the direction n unchanged, so every point on the line through O along n is fixed; the displacement is a rotation about that line Since Rn = n and R preserves dot products, R maps the plane perpendicular to n into itself; restricted to it R is a 2×2 orthogonal matrix with determinant det R = +1, i.e. a plane rotation by φ.
      - Angle: in axes with z along n, R has the form of a plane rotation by φ in the xy-plane, so Tr R = 1 + 2cos φ; since the trace is basis-independent, cos φ = (Tr R − 1)/2 in any axes.
      - Interpretation: the other two eigenvalues are e^(iφ) and e^(−iφ) (product +1 with the eigenvalue 1, consistent with det R = +1); only the real eigenvalue +1 gives a physical fixed direction.
    - **checks:**
      - Limiting case φ = 0: R = 1, Tr R = 3 = 1 + 2cos 0, every direction is fixed (identity). Limiting case φ = π: Tr R = −1.
      - Condition check: with det R = −1 the step det(R − 1) = det R · det(1 − Rᵀ) gives det(R − 1) = +det(R − 1), an identity that is trivially satisfied and forces nothing; e.g. inversion r → −r has no fixed direction, and a reflection fixes a plane but is not a rotation. Thus det R = +1 is essential.

    - **task:** Derive the parallel-axis theorem I = I_cm + Mh² in vector form, stating the condition on the axes.
    - **steps:**
      - Setup and assumptions: rigid body of total mass M; axis 1 through the centre of mass C with unit direction n; axis 2 parallel to axis 1 at perpendicular separation h. Origin at C. Position of a mass element: ρ.

      - Let h be the perpendicular vector from axis 1 to axis 2 (h ⊥ n, |h| = h). The perpendicular distance of the element from axis 2 is |ρ_⊥ − h|.
      - I = Σ m |ρ_⊥ − h|² = Σ m (|ρ_⊥|² − 2 ρ_⊥·h + h²).

      - Result: I = I_cm + M h².
      - Physical interpretation: shifting the axis away from the centre of mass always increases I by the amount the whole mass would contribute if concentrated at the centre of mass.

      - Apply a × (b × c) = b(a·c) − c(a·b) with a = c = rᵢ, b = ω: L = Σ mᵢ [rᵢ² ω − (rᵢ·ω) rᵢ].
      - x-component: L_x = Σ mᵢ [(xᵢ² + yᵢ² + zᵢ²) ω_x − (xᵢω_x + yᵢω_y + zᵢω_z) xᵢ] = ω_x Σ mᵢ (yᵢ² + zᵢ²) − ω_y Σ mᵢ xᵢyᵢ − ω_z Σ mᵢ xᵢzᵢ.
      - Define I_xx = Σ m (y² + z²), I_xy = −Σ m x y, I_xz = −Σ m x z; then L_x = I_xx ω_x + I_xy ω_y + I_xz ω_z. Cyclic permutation gives L_y and L_z.
      - Matrix form L = I ω with I the 3×3 array of I_jk. Symmetry: I_xy = −Σ m x y = I_yx, and likewise for the other pairs, so I = Iᵀ.
      - Diagonal elements are moments of inertia about the axes (Σ m × squared perpendicular distance); off-diagonal elements are products of inertia.
      - L ∥ ω requires I ω = λ ω, i.e. ω an eigenvector of I; the eigenvectors of a real symmetric matrix are three perpendicular principal axes. Along them products of inertia vanish and L = I₁ω₁ e₁ + I₂ω₂ e₂ + I₃ω₃ e₃.
      - Interpretation: the second term −Σ m (r·ω) r is the part of L not along ω; its component perpendicular to ω vanishes when ω is along a principal axis (e.g. a symmetry axis).
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions: [I_jk] = M L², [L] = M L² T⁻¹.
      - Limiting case: a single particle at r perpendicular to ω gives L = m r² ω, parallel to ω (then r·ω = 0).
      - Consistency: the component of L along ω is ω Σ m r_⊥² = Iω, matching the fixed-axis result.
    - **traps:**
      - Sign of the products of inertia (negative of the sum).
      - Writing I_xx = Σ m x² instead of Σ m (y² + z²).

    - **task:** Derive the moment of inertia of a uniform solid sphere of mass M and radius R about a diameter by slicing it into discs, and then obtain the result for a thin spherical shell using the identity I_x + I_y + I_z = 2Σmr².
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: uniform density ρ = M/(4πR³/3) = 3M/(4πR³); axis is the z-axis through the centre.

      - Moment of inertia of a disc about its own axis is ½ (mass)(radius)², so dI = ½ dm a² = ½ ρπ (R² − z²)² dz.
      - Integrate from z = −R to R; by symmetry twice the integral from 0 to R: I = ρπ ∫₀^R (R⁴ − 2R²z² + z⁴) dz.
      - Evaluate: ∫₀^R R⁴ dz = R⁵; ∫₀^R 2R²z² dz = 2R⁵/3; ∫₀^R z⁴ dz = R⁵/5. Sum: R⁵(1 − 2/3 + 1/5) = R⁵ (15 − 10 + 3)/15 = 8R⁵/15.
      - So I = ρπ · 8R⁵/15. Substitute ρπ = 3M/(4R³): I = (3M/4R³)(8R⁵/15) = (24/60) M R² = (2/5) M R².

      - Dimensions: M L² in both cases.

      - Parallel-axis extension: about a tangent, solid sphere 7MR²/5 and shell 5MR²/3.

      - Using ring elements with the wrong perpendicular distance.
      - Forgetting the factor ½ from the disc result.

  - - **id:** PH-015-D5
    - **task:** Derive v = ω × r for a particle of a rigid body rotating about a fixed point, and show that two angular velocities combine by vector addition.
    - **steps:**
      - Setup: fixed point O as origin; at the instant considered the body rotates about the unit vector n through O with angular speed ω = dθ/dt (such an instantaneous axis exists by Euler's theorem). Particle P at r, angle φ between n and r.
      - P moves on a circle centred on the axis with radius r_⊥ = r sin φ; in dt it turns through dθ, so the arc is ds = r sin φ dθ.
      - The displacement dr is tangent to this circle: perpendicular to n and to r, in the sense of rotation, i.e. along n × r. Its magnitude equals |n × r| dθ = r sin φ dθ. Hence dr = dθ (n × r).
      - Divide by dt: v = dr/dt = (dθ/dt)(n × r) = ω × r with ω = ω n.
      - Composition: suppose a frame S′ rotates with ω₂ relative to the laboratory and the body rotates with ω₁ relative to S′, both about O. A particle's velocity relative to S′ is ω₁ × r; the velocity of the point of S′ coinciding with it is ω₂ × r; by Galilean addition v = ω₁ × r + ω₂ × r = (ω₁ + ω₂) × r.
      - Comparing with v = ω × r, the body's angular velocity relative to the laboratory is ω = ω₁ + ω₂: angular velocities add as vectors.
      - Justification of the vector character: an infinitesimal rotation acts as r → r + dθ (n × r); two in succession give r + dθ₁(n₁ × r) + dθ₂(n₂ × r) to first order regardless of order, so infinitesimal rotations commute and compose by adding dθ n. Finite rotations do not (90° about x then y versus y then x give different results), so finite angular displacements are not vectors.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions: [ω × r] = T⁻¹ L = L T⁻¹.
      - Limiting cases: r ∥ n gives v = 0 (points on the axis are at rest); φ = 90° gives v = ω r, the circular-motion formula.
      - Sign check: for ω along +z and r along +x, v = ω × r is along +y, i.e. anticlockwise seen from +z, consistent with the right-hand rule.
    - **traps:**
      - Choosing an origin not on the axis without adding the origin's velocity.
      - Treating finite rotations as additive vectors.
