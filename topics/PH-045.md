### Bridge: Reciprocals with signs

The thin-lens formula is written with reciprocals, 1/v − 1/u = 1/f, and every quantity carries a sign. Two habits prevent most arithmetic errors.

**Habit 1: add the reciprocals first, invert last.** For u = −50 cm and f = +20 cm, 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 0.05 − 0.02 = 0.03 cm⁻¹, so v = 1/0.03 = +33.3 cm. Never invert each term separately and add the answers.

**Habit 2: keep one unit.** If f is in cm, keep u and v in cm. The power of a lens is 1/f with f in metres and is measured in dioptres (D); 1/(0.25 m) = 4 D. A 25 cm lens and a 4 D lens are the same lens.

**Sign check at the end.** A positive v means the image is on the right of the lens (on the side where light leaves), a negative v means it is on the left. If a result for a real object gives an image on the wrong side of a converging lens at a large object distance, recheck the sign of u.

### Bridge: Similar triangles and small angles

Two facts carry the whole geometry of this lesson.

**Small angles.** For an angle θ in radians, tan θ ≈ sin θ ≈ θ when θ is small. At θ = 0.1 rad (5.7 degrees) tan θ = 0.1003, an error of 0.3 percent; at θ = 0.3 rad the error is about 3 percent. A **paraxial** ray makes such a small angle with the axis and meets a surface at such a small height that this replacement is allowed. Slopes of rays can then be written simply as θ = (height change)/(horizontal distance).

**Similar triangles.** Two right triangles with equal angles have proportional sides. If a ray from the tip of an object of height h₁ passes through a point on the axis a distance a away, and a second triangle with the same angle has base b and height H, then h₁/a = H/b. In module 5 we will draw two such triangles on either side of a lens and equate the ratios.

**Direction matters.** In this lesson heights above the axis are positive and heights below are negative; a ray that rises to the right has positive slope. A ratio of two signed lengths can therefore be negative, and that negative sign carries physical meaning (an inverted image has negative magnification).

### 2. Foundation: the words, the Cartesian sign convention and the one-surface formula

*Objective:* Define the lens vocabulary, fix one sign convention for the whole lesson and recall the single-surface formula that the lens derivation uses twice.

## The words

- **Principal axis:** the line through the centres of curvature of the two surfaces. **Vertex:** the point where a surface meets the axis. **Radius of curvature R:** the distance from a surface to its centre of curvature.
- **Thin lens:** a lens whose thickness is negligible next to the radii and next to the object and image distances (Brij Lal PDF p. 93). Every lens has some thickness, so "thin" is an approximation, not a type of glass.
- **Paraxial ray:** a ray close to the axis and nearly parallel to it, so that angles can be replaced by their tangents (bridge B2).
- **Real image / virtual image:** a real image is where refracted rays actually meet (it can be caught on a screen); a virtual image is where they only appear to come from.
- **Conjugate points:** an object point and its image point; light can be reversed along the path (Brij Lal PDF p. 90).
- **Principal focus:** the point to which rays parallel to the axis converge (convex lens) or from which they appear to diverge (concave lens). **Focal plane:** the plane through a focus perpendicular to the axis.

## The sign convention used throughout (Cartesian)

Draw light travelling from **left to right**. Put the origin at the vertex of a surface (or at a thin lens).

- Distances measured to the **right** of the origin are positive, to the left negative. So a real object, which lies to the left, has **u < 0**; a real image to the right has **v > 0**.
- Heights above the axis are positive, below negative.
- A surface has **R > 0** if its centre of curvature lies to the **right** of its vertex. A biconvex lens therefore has R₁ > 0 (first surface) and R₂ < 0 (second surface).
- A ray that rises to the right has positive slope.

Brij Lal's Chapter 4 states the angle rule the other way round (PDF p. 93); Chapter 7 and this lesson make a rising ray positive. Day 18 lesson PH-044 flags the same point. No formula in this lesson depends on the angle rule except the angular magnification, where a ratio of slopes is used.

## The one-surface formula

A surface of radius R separates medium 1 (index μ₁, on the left) from medium 2 (index μ₂, on the right). For paraxial rays from an axial object at u the image is at v, where

$$ μ₂/v − μ₁/u = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R $$

This was derived in Day 17 lesson PH-043 (section "refraction at a spherical surface from the optical path", Brij Lal Eq. 2.18) and appears as Eq. 4.1 in Chapter 4. The right side, P_s = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R, is the **power of the surface**. *Dimension check:* each term is (dimensionless)/(length). *Numbers:* air to glass (μ₂ = 1.5), R = +10 cm, object at infinity gives 1.5/v = 0.05 cm⁻¹, so v = 30 cm.

## Why the lens is a double use of this formula

A lens is two such surfaces back to back. The first surface forms an image I′ of the object; I′ becomes the object for the second surface, whether or not the light reaches it (a *virtual object* if I′ lies beyond the second surface). Module 3 uses exactly this chain.

**Beginner trap:** forgetting that u is negative for a real object. Putting u = +50 cm into 1/v − 1/u = 1/f gives the wrong answer; the real-is-positive convention has a different formula, 1/u + 1/v = 1/f with u = +50. Do not mix the two.

### 3. Core: the thin lens formula from refraction at two spherical surfaces

*Objective:* Derive 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) from two applications of the one-surface formula, naming every assumption and sign.

## Set-up and assumptions

A lens of refractive index μ is in air on both sides. Its surfaces have vertices V₁ and V₂ and radii R₁ and R₂ in the Cartesian convention of module 2. A point object O is on the axis at u (negative) from the lens. Assumptions: (i) the rays are paraxial; (ii) the lens is **thin**, so the vertices V₁ and V₂ may be treated as one point and distances measured from either surface are the same; (iii) the medium on both sides is air (μ₁ = 1).

## Derivation

**Step 1: first surface.** The medium on its left is air and on its right is glass. Let it form an image I′ at distance v′ from the lens. The one-surface formula with μ₁ = 1, μ₂ = μ, R = R₁ gives

$$ μ/v′ − 1/u = (μ − 1)/R₁   (E1) $$

I′ is the place where the image *would* form if the second surface were absent. It may lie inside the glass, or beyond it; the algebra does not care.

**Step 2: second surface.** Now glass (μ₁ = μ) is on the left and air (μ₂ = 1) on the right, with R = R₂. The object for this surface is I′ at v′. Because the lens is thin, v′ is measured from the same point as before (this is where assumption (ii) is used; for a thick lens the object distance would be v′ − t, with t the thickness). The final image is at v:

$$ 1/v − μ/v′ = (1 − μ)/R₂   (E2) $$

**Step 3: add.** Add E1 and E2. The term μ/v′ appears with opposite signs and cancels:

$$ 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)/R₁ + (1 − μ)/R₂ $$

**Step 4: factor.** Since (1 − μ)/R₂ = −(μ − 1)/R₂,

$$ 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)   (thin lens formula) $$

This is Brij Lal Eq. 4.5. The intermediate image I′ has disappeared: only the end result matters, which is why a lens can be treated as a single device.

## Interpretation and checks

The right-hand side depends only on the lens (μ, R₁, R₂), so it is a property of the lens: its **power** P = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) is the sum of the two surface powers P₁ + P₂ with P₁ = (μ − 1)/R₁ and P₂ = (1 − μ)/R₂.

- *Dimensions:* every term is 1/length.
- *Flat plate:* R₁, R₂ → ∞ gives 1/v = 1/u, so v = u: the image is where the object is, no focusing.
- *No index step:* μ = 1 gives zero power.
- *Reversal:* turn the lens round; R₁ → −R₂ and R₂ → −R₁ so the bracket is unchanged.

## Numbers (recomputed)

μ = 1.5, R₁ = +20 cm, R₂ = −20 cm, object at u = −60 cm. E1: 1.5/v′ = 0.5/20 − 1/60 = 0.025 − 0.016667 = 0.008333, so v′ = +180 cm (beyond the lens: a virtual object for surface 2). E2: 1/v = 1.5/180 + (−0.5)/(−20) = 0.008333 + 0.025 = 0.033333, so v = +30 cm. The direct formula gives 1/v = 1/(−60) + 0.5(1/20 + 1/20) = −0.016667 + 0.05 = 0.033333, the same.

**Practice target (not an official allotment):** 12 minutes for a 10-mark derivation. A hand-drawn figure with O, V₁, V₂, C₁, C₂, I′ and I, with the signs marked, earns the structure marks.

**Beginner trap:** writing E2 with the glass on the right. Surface 2 has glass on its *left*, so μ₁ = μ and μ₂ = 1.

### 4. Core: lens-maker's formula, focal points, power and lenses in contact

*Objective:* Obtain the focal length from the lens-maker's formula, show f₁ = −f₂ in air, handle a lens in another medium and derive power addition for thin lenses in contact.

## Focal points from the lens formula

From 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), let the object go to infinity (1/u → 0). The image forms at the **second focal point** F₂, v = f₂:

$$ 1/f₂ = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)   (lens-maker's formula, Brij Lal Eq. 4.6) $$

Now find the **first focal point** F₁: the object position u = f₁ for which the image is at infinity (1/v → 0). Then −1/f₁ = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), so

$$ f₁ = −f₂ ,   and the two focal points lie at equal distances on opposite sides of the lens. $$

Chapter 4 prints "f₁ = f₂" (Eq. 4.10) in the text layer; the surrounding discussion (f₂ positive, f₁ negative for a converging lens) and Chapter 5 Eq. 5.12 give f₁ = −f₂, so the minus sign is probably lost in extraction; check on the page image. Writing f for f₂, the Gauss form is

$$ 1/v − 1/u = 1/f $$

A converging (convex) lens has f > 0 and F₂ on its right; a diverging lens has f < 0.

## What the formula tells the lens-maker

A short focal length needs small radii and a high index. Turning the lens round leaves f unchanged. Power P = 1/f with f in metres is in **dioptres** (D); P > 0 converging, P < 0 diverging. Example: the 2017 lens (μ = 1.5, R = ±50 cm) has P = 0.5 × (2/0.5 m) = 2 D, f = 50 cm.

## A lens in another medium

If the same medium of index μ_m surrounds the lens, the formula holds with μ replaced by the **relative index** μ/μ_m (Brij Lal PDF pp. 95 to 96). Brij Lal's Example 4.2 (glass lens of f = 24 cm in air placed in water, μ = 1.33) uses μ/μ_m = 1.125 and gets 96 cm. The ratio is 1.5/1.33 = 1.1278, and the exact result is f_w = 24 × 0.5/0.1278 = **93.9 cm** (recomputed in Python); the book's answer is a rounding slip of about 2 percent. A lens of index greater than the surroundings converges; if μ_m > μ the sign of the bracket reverses and the lens diverges (index 1.6 liquid: f = −192 cm).

## Deviation and lenses in contact

A ray parallel to the axis at height h passes through F₂, so it is turned towards the axis by an angle δ with tan δ = h/f; in the paraxial limit **δ = h/f** (Brij Lal Eq. 4.24, which shows it is the same for every object position). With the Cartesian slope convention the slope changes by −h/f.

Put two thin lenses of focal lengths f₁′ and f₂′ in contact (negligible separation). A ray at height h meets both at the same height, so the deviations add:

$$ h/f = h/f₁′ + h/f₂′   so   1/f = 1/f₁′ + 1/f₂′ ,   P = P₁ + P₂ $$

(Brij Lal Eq. 4.26). Example: +15 cm and −40 cm: 1/f = 0.06667 − 0.025 = 0.04167 cm⁻¹, f = **24 cm**, P = 6.67 − 2.5 = 4.17 D. Brij Lal's Example 5.7 (a glass meniscus with oil in the hollow) uses the same addition: 1/40 + 3/100 = 11/200, f = 18.2 cm. This is the d = 0 case of the separated-lens formula of Day 20 lesson PH-046.

**Beginner trap:** adding focal lengths instead of powers.

### 5. Core: Newton's form and the three magnifications

*Objective:* Derive x₁x₂ = f₁f₂ and m = f/x₁ from similar triangles, deduce the Gauss form from it, and obtain the lateral, longitudinal and angular magnifications with signs.

## Set-up

A thin lens in air with F₁ at −f and F₂ at +f (f > 0, convex). An object arrow of height h₁ stands at u (< 0). Newton measures from the foci: **x₁ = distance from F₁ to the object = u + f** (negative if the object is to the left of F₁), and **x₂ = distance from F₂ to the image = v − f**.

## Derivation (two rays from the tip of the arrow)

**Ray a, through F₁.** The line through the tip (u, h₁) and F₁ (−f, 0) has slope h₁/(u + f) = h₁/x₁. At the lens (x = 0) its height is slope × f:

$$ y = h₁ f/x₁ $$

After the lens it travels parallel to the axis at this height, so the image tip has height h₂ = h₁f/x₁ and

$$ m = h₂/h₁ = f/x₁   (N1) $$

**Ray b, parallel to the axis.** It reaches the lens at height h₁ and goes through F₂ (f, 0). At horizontal distance v from the lens its height is h₁(1 − v/f) = −h₁ x₂/f, since 1 − v/f = −(v − f)/f. So

$$ m = −x₂/f   (N2) $$

**Equate N1 and N2:** f/x₁ = −x₂/f, hence

$$ x₁ x₂ = −f² = f₁ f₂   (Newton's formula) $$

because f₁ = −f and f₂ = +f. Brij Lal prints x₁x₂ = f² (Eq. 4.12), the version with magnitudes; with both x's signed from their own focus the product is negative for a real object and a real image. The general form x₁x₂ = f₁f₂ (Eq. 5.7) holds for any coaxial system.

**Gauss from Newton.** Put x₁ = u + f and x₂ = v − f: (u + f)(v − f) = −f², so uv − uf + vf = 0. Divide by uvf: 1/f − 1/v + 1/u = 0, i.e. 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Also m = f/(u + f) = v/u.

## The three magnifications

- **Lateral:** m = h₂/h₁ = v/u = f/x₁. Negative means inverted.
- **Longitudinal:** m_L = dv/du. From x₂ = −f²/x₁, dx₂/dx₁ = f²/x₁² = m². So **m_L = +m²**: a small displacement of the object along the axis moves the image the same way, stretched by m². Brij Lal Eq. 4.17 prints −m², but his Eq. 5.23 gives (μ₂/μ₁)m², which is +m² in air; the minus belongs to a convention that counts x₂ the other way (check on the page image).
- **Angular:** γ = θ₂/θ₁ for conjugate rays through the axial object and image points. The ray from O at slope θ₁ reaches the lens at height y = −uθ₁ and is turned by −y/f (module 4): θ₂ = θ₁(1 + u/f). Since 1 + u/f = u/v (Gauss form), **γ = u/v = 1/m**. So mγ = 1 in air (Brij Lal Eq. 5.26); in general mγ = μ₁/μ₂.

## Numbers (recomputed)

f = +20 cm, u = −50 cm. x₁ = −30 cm; x₂ = −400/(−30) = +13.33 cm; v = 33.33 cm (Gauss: 1/v = 0.05 − 0.02 = 0.03). m = f/x₁ = −0.667 and −x₂/f = −0.667 and v/u = −0.667. m_L = m² = 0.444 (a central finite difference of v(u) gives 0.4444). γ = −1.5 = 1/m.

*Limits and checks.* u → −∞: x₁ → −∞, x₂ → 0, image at F₂, m → 0. Object at F₁ (x₁ = 0): x₂ → ∞, image at infinity. Object *at the lens* (u = 0): m = f/(0 + f) = +1 and v = 0: the lens plane images itself with unit magnification; module 6 uses this. Dimensions: x₁x₂ and f² are length²; m, m_L, γ are dimensionless.

**Practice target (not an official allotment):** 12 minutes for the derivation with a figure.

### 6. Core: the thin lens as an optical system, principal planes and the six cardinal points

*Objective:* Show that a thin lens has both principal planes on the lens, define the six cardinal points of a general coaxial system and give the thick-lens positions of the principal planes.

## A thin lens: both principal planes are the lens

In module 5 we found that an object placed at the lens plane (u = 0) has m = f/(u + f) = +1 and v = 0: the image is in the same plane, the same height, the same way up. A pair of conjugate planes with lateral magnification +1 is, by definition, the pair of **principal planes** (unit planes). For the thin lens the two planes coincide with the lens. So the focal lengths measured from the principal planes are the familiar ones, f₂ = +f and f₁ = −f, and the Gauss formula "from the lens" is the Gauss formula "from the principal planes".

## The six cardinal points of a general coaxial system

Gauss (1841) showed that any number of coaxial lenses acts like a single unit described by six points on the axis (Brij Lal PDF p. 109). The planes through them, perpendicular to the axis, are the **cardinal planes**.

- **Focal points F₁, F₂.** Rays parallel to the axis in the image space come from, or go to, F₂ (second focal point); a source at F₁ gives a parallel beam leaving the system.
- **Principal points H₁, H₂** (planes: unit planes). Imagine the whole refraction happening at one stroke at the principal plane: the incident parallel ray extended forward and the emergent ray extended backward meet on H₂ (Brij Lal PDF pp. 109 to 110). The two planes are conjugate with m = +1.
- **Nodal points N₁, N₂** (module 7).

The **focal lengths** are f₁ = H₁→F₁ and f₂ = H₂→F₂, measured from the principal points. In general f₂/f₁ = −n₂/n₁ (Brij Lal Eq. 5.17), so f₁ = −f₂ in the same medium.

Two working formulas: **Newton** x₁x₂ = f₁f₂ with x measured from F₁, F₂ (valid for any system, thin or thick) and **Gauss** 1/v − 1/u = 1/f with u and v measured from H₁ and H₂ (same medium both sides). Day 18 lesson PH-044, section "principal planes, focal points and nodal points from the matrix elements", proves the Gauss form by multiplying matrices: a translation, a thin lens of power 1/F at the planes and a translation.

Properties (Brij Lal PDF p. 110): the planes are intrinsic to the system, do not move with the object, and are only constructions; they may lie outside the glass. Brij Lal's remark that "between H₁ and H₂ all rays are parallel to the axis" is true of the *construction* rays, not of real rays; a reader should take it as a drawing convention (check).

## Thick lens: where the planes are

A thick lens (index μ, thickness t, air on both sides) has power and plane positions (from the matrix product of PH-044 modules 6 and 7; re-derived symbolically for this lesson):

$$ 1/F = (μ − 1)[ 1/R₁ − 1/R₂ + (μ − 1)t/(μR₁R₂) ] $$

$$ α = −F(μ − 1)t/(μR₂) ,    β = −F(μ − 1)t/(μR₁) $$

where α is the distance V₁→H₁ and β is V₂→H₂ (Cartesian). Take R₁ = +4 cm, R₂ = −4 cm, μ = 1.5, t = 1 cm: 1/R₁ − 1/R₂ = 0.5 cm⁻¹ and (μ − 1)t/(μR₁R₂) = 0.5/(1.5 × (−16)) = −0.020833, so 1/F = 0.5 × 0.479167 = 0.239583 cm⁻¹, **F = 4.174 cm**; α = −4.174 × 0.5 × 1/(1.5 × (−4)) = **+0.348 cm** (H₁ is inside the glass, to the right of V₁) and β = **−0.348 cm** (H₂ is to the left of V₂, also inside). When t → 0 both α and β → 0 and F → the thin-lens value: the planes merge into the lens.

**Beginner trap:** measuring u and v from the vertices of a thick lens in the Gauss formula. It needs H₁ and H₂ (or use Newton from the foci).

### 7. Core: nodal points and nodal planes, and when they coincide with the principal planes

*Objective:* Define the nodal points, locate them at H₁N₁ = H₂N₂ = (n₂ − n₁)/P from the principal points, and state and prove the condition under which nodal and principal planes coincide.

## Definition

The **nodal points** N₁ and N₂ are the pair of axial conjugate points with **angular magnification +1**: a ray aimed at N₁ with slope θ leaves the system as if from N₂ with the same slope θ, parallel to the incident ray (Brij Lal PDF p. 111). The planes through N₁, N₂ perpendicular to the axis are the **nodal planes**. For a thin lens in air both nodal points are at the lens centre, which is why a ray through the centre goes straight on: at u = 0 module 5 gives γ = 1 + u/f = 1.

Care with wording: nodal points are where the *direction* of a ray is unchanged. Brij Lal's phrase "planes where refraction does not take place" (PDF p. 111) must not be read to mean that the nodal planes have unit lateral magnification; the unit-magnification planes are the principal planes.

## Where they are: a derivation

**Result used.** Between the principal planes the system acts as one thin refraction. If a ray crosses the first principal plane at height y with slope θ₁ and leaves the second at the same height with slope θ₂, then with P the power of the system

$$ n₂θ₂ = n₁θ₁ − P y $$

(n₁, n₂ the indices of the first and last media; f₁ = −n₁/P, f₂ = n₂/P). A ray parallel to the axis (θ₁ = 0) goes to F₂ at distance f₂, so θ₂ = −y/f₂, which gives the term −Py; the coefficient 1 of n₁θ₁ follows from m = +1 at the principal planes and the Lagrange relation n₁hθ₁ = n₂hθ₂ (Brij Lal Eq. 5.16). PH-044 modules 7 and 9 obtain the same law from the matrix.

**Step 1.** For the nodal ray θ₂ = θ₁ = θ, so n₂θ = n₁θ − Py, i.e. **y = −(n₂ − n₁)θ/P**.

**Step 2: N₁.** The incident ray travels in a straight line to N₁ on the axis, at distance x₁ from H₁ (positive to the right). It crosses the H₁ plane at height y, so y + θx₁ = 0, hence

$$ H₁N₁ = x₁ = −y/θ = (n₂ − n₁)/P = f₁ + f₂ $$

**Step 3: N₂.** The ray leaves H₂ at the same height y with slope θ and meets the axis after x₂ = −y/θ, so

$$ H₂N₂ = (n₂ − n₁)/P = f₁ + f₂ $$

This agrees with Brij Lal PDF p. 113 (P₁N₁ = P₂N₂ = f₁ + f₂). Because both points are displaced from their principal points by the same amount in the same direction, **N₁N₂ = H₁H₂** (Brij Lal Eq. 5.2). Equivalently F₁N₁ = f₂ and N₂F₂ = −f₁.

## The condition

If **n₁ = n₂** (same medium on both sides, for example air), f₂ = −f₁ and the displacement is zero: **N₁ = H₁ and N₂ = H₂**, so the nodal planes coincide with the principal planes (Brij Lal PDF pp. 112 to 113, Eq. 5.3; PDF p. 170, Eqs. 7.65 and 7.66). If n₁ ≠ n₂ they are displaced by (n₂ − n₁)/P.

## Checks (recomputed)

- *Single surface* (the Day 18 lesson PH-044 nodal-point section already runs this check from the matrix; here it confirms the geometric route), air to glass 1.5, R = +25 cm: P = 0.02 cm⁻¹, H₁ = H₂ = vertex, displacement = 0.5/0.02 = 25 cm, so both nodal points are at the centre of curvature, where a ray along the radius meets the surface normally and is not bent. ✓
- *Conjugacy.* Object at N₁: x₁ = f₂, so x₂ = f₁f₂/f₂ = f₁, giving the image at F₂ + f₁ = N₂. ✓
- *Dimensions.* (n₂ − n₁)/P is a length.

**Practice target (not an official allotment):** 12 minutes for the location derivation.

**Beginner trap:** assuming nodal and principal points coincide always. They coincide only when the end media are the same.

### 8. Master: the nodal slide and why the image stands still

*Objective:* Explain the principle of the nodal slide, derive that the image of a distant object is displaced by −aφ (magnitude |a|φ) when the system is rotated by φ about a pivot at signed distance a from N₂, and describe how the method locates the nodal points.

## The idea

A **nodal slide** is a carriage that holds a lens system, can slide along an optical bench and can be turned about a vertical axis (Brij Lal PDF pp. 117 to 119). Its purpose is to find the nodal point N₂ (and then N₁, and the focal length) of a lens system. The principle quoted by the book: with a parallel beam falling on a converging system and a screen at the second focal plane, a small rotation of the system about a vertical axis through the second nodal point leaves the image where it was.

## Derivation: image displacement for a pivot at distance a from N₂

*Assumptions:* system in air (so N = H), small rotation angle φ in radians, parallel incident beam fixed in the laboratory, screen on the second focal plane. Let z be the axial coordinate along the system, measured from the pivot O; N₂ is at z = −a (a > 0 if the pivot lies on the screen side of N₂).

1. A rigid rotation by φ moves a point on the axis at coordinate z sideways by zφ (first order in φ). So N₂ moves to lateral position y = −aφ.
2. Relative to the rotated system the beam arrives at angle −φ, but in the laboratory it is still horizontal. A ray aimed at the first nodal point leaves N₂ **parallel to the incident direction** (nodal property), so in the laboratory it leaves N₂ horizontally at height −aφ.
3. All rays of the parallel beam meet at the image point; the nodal ray is one of them, and it reaches the second focal plane at a distance f from N₂. To first order in φ, the tilt of that plane does not change the height at which the horizontal ray meets it. So the image point sits at height −aφ.
4. In the unrotated position the image was at height 0. The **displacement of the image is −aφ**, with magnitude |a|φ.

*Conclusions.* The displacement is zero if and only if a = 0, i.e. the pivot is at N₂. For a pivot on one side of N₂ the image moves one way and for a pivot on the other side it moves the opposite way, so the observer slides the carriage until the sideways motion changes sign and vanishes (Brij Lal PDF p. 119). 

*Number (recomputed):* pivot 3 cm from N₂, φ = 0.01 rad (0.57 degrees): shift = 3 × 0.01 = 0.03 cm = 0.3 mm. A pivot error of 1 mm gives only 0.001 cm = 0.01 mm of image motion at this angle, which is hard to see, so in practice the rotation is made larger and the image is watched through an eyepiece; what is located is the reversal of direction.

## What the null measures

When the slide is set at the null, the pivot is at N₂ and the screen is at F₂, so the pivot-to-screen distance is N₂F₂ = −f₁ (a positive length, since f₁ < 0), which Brij Lal calls the **first focal length**, quoted as a magnitude (PDF p. 119; with the same medium on both sides f₁ and f₂ have equal magnitude, and N₂ = H₂). Turning the carriage by 180 degrees and repeating the null finds the other nodal point. In air the nodal points are also the principal points, so the method gives the principal points and the focal length of a combination without knowing its construction.

*Dimension check:* a·φ has the dimension of length (φ is dimensionless).

**Practice target (not an official allotment):** 12 minutes for the derivation with a figure of the rotated system.

**Beginner trap:** saying the pivot at the lens centre always gives a stationary image. Only a pivot through N₂ does, and N₂ is inside or outside the glass according to the system.

### 9. Master: different media on the two sides, the general thin lens and the optical centre

*Objective:* Derive the thin-lens formula for different media on the two sides, locate the nodal points of such a lens and see why 'the ray through the centre goes straight' belongs to the same-medium case.

## A thin lens with index μ₁ on its left, μ₂ on its right

Let the lens have index μ_L. Add the two surface equations of module 3 with the correct media:

$$ μ_L/v′ − μ₁/u = (μ_L − μ₁)/R₁ ,    μ₂/v − μ_L/v′ = (μ₂ − μ_L)/R₂ $$

The terms μ_L/v′ cancel on adding:

$$ μ₂/v − μ₁/u = P ,    P = (μ_L − μ₁)/R₁ + (μ₂ − μ_L)/R₂ $$

With μ₁ = μ₂ = 1 this is the thin-lens formula of module 3. The focal lengths follow as before: u → −∞ gives f₂ = μ₂/P, and v → ∞ gives f₁ = −μ₁/P. So f₂/f₁ = −μ₂/μ₁, the relation of Brij Lal Eq. 5.17. Lateral magnification is m = μ₁v/(μ₂u) (the product of the two surface magnifications).

## Numbers (recomputed)

μ_L = 1.5, μ₁ = 1 (air), μ₂ = 1.33 (water), R₁ = +20 cm, R₂ = −20 cm. P = 0.5/20 + (1.33 − 1.5)/(−20) = 0.025 + 0.0085 = **0.0335 cm⁻¹**. Then f₂ = 1.33/0.0335 = **39.70 cm** and f₁ = −1/0.0335 = **−29.85 cm**. For an object at u = −50 cm: 1.33/v = 0.0335 − 0.02 = 0.0135, so v = 98.52 cm, the same as chaining the two surfaces (v′ = 1.5/(0.025 − 0.02) = 300 cm, then 1.33/v = 1.5/300 + 0.0085). Check with Newton: x₁ = u − f₁ = −20.15 cm, x₂ = v − f₂ = 58.82 cm, x₁x₂ = −1185.1 = f₁f₂ ✓, and m = −f₁/x₁ = −1.481 = μ₁v/(μ₂u) ✓.

## Nodal points of this lens, and the ray through the centre

Both principal planes are still on the lens, but module 7 gives nodal points at (μ₂ − μ₁)/P = 0.33/0.0335 = **9.85 cm** to the right of the lens, for both N₁ and N₂ (F₁N₁ = 39.70 = f₂ ✓). A ray aimed at the centre of the lens crosses it at height 0, where the refraction law n₂θ₂ = n₁θ₁ − Py gives θ₂ = (μ₁/μ₂)θ₁ ≠ θ₁: it is bent like a ray crossing a plane boundary. The ray that goes through undeviated is the one aimed at N₁. A numerical trace confirms it: aimed with slope 0.01 at N₁, it leaves with slope 0.01. The familiar rule "the ray through the optical centre is not deviated" (Brij Lal PDF pp. 89, 91) is the same-medium special case.

## The optical centre of a lens

Brij Lal defines the **optical centre** as the axial point for which a ray through it emerges parallel to the incident ray. Its position follows from geometry: choose the points A on surface 1 and B on surface 2 where the tangents are parallel, that is the radii C₁A and C₂B are parallel. A ray along AB crosses the glass like a ray through a parallel plate, emerging parallel to itself. Triangles OC₁A and OC₂B are similar, so the axial point O where AB meets the axis divides C₁C₂ in the ratio R₁ : |R₂|. In Cartesian signs

$$ V₁O = t R₁/(R₁ − R₂) $$

For t = 1 cm and R = ±4 cm this is 0.5 cm, the middle of the lens. For a thin lens t → 0, so O is on the lens. The optical centre is a property of the glass alone, while the nodal points depend on the surrounding media too.

**Beginner trap:** using the thin-lens formula 1/v − 1/u = 1/f with a single f when the media on the two sides differ. Use μ₂/v − μ₁/u = P and f₁ ≠ −f₂.

### Notes: Definitions to know

- Thin lens: thickness negligible against the radii and against the object and image distances; then the two vertices are treated as one point.
- Paraxial ray: a ray close to the axis and nearly parallel to it, so that sin θ and tan θ may be replaced by θ.
- Principal focus F₂ (second focal point): the image point of an axial object at infinity. F₁ (first focal point): the axial object point whose image is at infinity.
- Principal (unit) planes: a pair of conjugate planes with lateral magnification +1; the whole refraction can be imagined at these planes. Their axial points are H₁ and H₂ (Brij Lal calls them P₁ and P₂).
- Nodal points: a pair of conjugate axial points with angular magnification +1; a ray aimed at N₁ leaves as if from N₂ with the same direction. Nodal planes are the planes through them perpendicular to the axis.
- Cardinal points: the six points F₁, F₂, H₁, H₂, N₁, N₂ which fix the imaging properties of a coaxial system.
- Power of a lens: P = 1/f with f in metres, in dioptres; sum of the two surface powers for a thin lens.
- Optical centre: the axial point such that a ray through it emerges parallel to its incident direction.

### Notes: Equations to know (Cartesian convention)

- One surface: μ₂/v − μ₁/u = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R.
- Thin lens in air: 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) = 1/f; lens-maker's formula 1/f = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂); f₁ = −f₂ = −f.
- Thin lens, different media: μ₂/v − μ₁/u = P, P = (μ_L − μ₁)/R₁ + (μ₂ − μ_L)/R₂; f₁ = −μ₁/P, f₂ = μ₂/P.
- Lens in a medium of index μ_m: use μ/μ_m in place of μ.
- Contact: P = P₁ + P₂, 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂. Deviation of a thin lens: δ = h/f.
- Newton: x₁x₂ = f₁f₂ (= −f² in air), x₁ from F₁ and x₂ from F₂; m = f/x₁ = −x₂/f (air).
- Gauss from the principal planes: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f (air).
- Magnifications in air: m = v/u, m_L = dv/du = m², γ = 1/m; in general mγ = μ₁/μ₂ and m_L = (μ₂/μ₁)m².
- Nodal points: H₁N₁ = H₂N₂ = (n₂ − n₁)/P = f₁ + f₂; N₁N₂ = H₁H₂; coincide with H₁, H₂ iff n₁ = n₂.
- Thick lens in air: 1/F = (μ − 1)[1/R₁ − 1/R₂ + (μ − 1)t/(μR₁R₂)]; α (V₁→H₁) = −F(μ − 1)t/(μR₂); β (V₂→H₂) = −F(μ − 1)t/(μR₁).
- Nodal slide: image displacement = −aφ (magnitude |a|φ) for a pivot at distance a from N₂ and rotation φ (small).

### Notes: Derivation skeletons

- D1 thin lens: assumptions (paraxial, thin, air) → E1 μ/v′ − 1/u = (μ − 1)/R₁ → E2 1/v − μ/v′ = (1 − μ)/R₂ → add → 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) → u → −∞ gives the lens-maker's formula.
- D2 Newton: ray through F₁ gives m = f/x₁; ray parallel to the axis gives m = −x₂/f; equate → x₁x₂ = −f² = f₁f₂; substitute x₁ = u + f, x₂ = v − f → Gauss form; differentiate for m_L = m²; ray-slope argument for γ = 1/m.
- D3 nodal points: n₂θ₂ = n₁θ₁ − Py across the principal planes → θ₂ = θ₁ gives y = −(n₂ − n₁)θ/P → ray straight to N₁ gives H₁N₁ = (n₂ − n₁)/P; same for N₂ → N₁N₂ = H₁H₂ → equal media gives zero displacement.
- D4 nodal slide: rotation moves N₂ sideways by −aφ → nodal ray leaves N₂ parallel to the beam → image shifts by −aφ (magnitude |a|φ) → zero only for a = 0.
- D5 least object-image distance: u = −(d − x), v = x; 1/x + 1/(d − x) = 1/f → x² − dx + fd = 0 → real roots need d² ≥ 4fd, i.e. d ≥ 4f.

### Notes: Limiting cases and checks

- Flat plate: R₁, R₂ → ∞ gives P = 0 and v = u.
- μ = μ_m (lens index equal to its surroundings): relative index 1, P = 0; a glass rod in an index-matched liquid vanishes optically.
- Thin limit of a thick lens: t → 0 gives α, β → 0, 1/F → (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂).
- Same media (n₁ = n₂): nodal points equal principal points and f₁ = −f₂. Single refracting surface: both nodal points at the centre of curvature.
- Object at the lens: u = 0 gives v = 0, m = +1, γ = +1: the lens plane is both a principal plane and a nodal plane for a thin lens in air.
- Dimensions: f, u, v, x₁, x₂, a are lengths; m, m_L, γ, μ are dimensionless; P is 1/length (dioptre = m⁻¹).

### Notes: Least object-image distance and the displacement method (Brij Lal Eqs. 4.19 to 4.23, recomputed)

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Principal planes: unit lateral magnification, refraction imagined there. Nodal planes: unit angular magnification, a ray aimed at N₁ leaves N₂ parallel. Different properties; the planes coincide only when the end media are the same.
- Focal length is measured from the principal point, not from the vertex of a thick lens. Back focal distance (V₂ to F₂) is F + β, not F.
- Optical centre (a property of the glass) is not the same as the nodal point (a property of the system and its surroundings); they agree for a thin lens in air.
- Newton's x's are measured from the foci; Gauss's u, v from the principal planes. Mnemonic: Newton from the foci, Gauss from the glass planes.
- Power adds for lenses in contact; focal lengths do not.
- A lens that converges in air can diverge in a denser liquid.

### Memory skeleton

- Cartesian convention: light left to right, distances from the lens; real object u < 0, biconvex R₂ < 0
- Two surfaces: μ/v′ − 1/u = (μ − 1)/R₁ and 1/v − μ/v′ = (1 − μ)/R₂; add, the intermediate image cancels
- Thin lens 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) = 1/f; u → −∞ gives the lens-maker's formula; powers add in contact
- Newton x₁x₂ = f₁f₂ = −f² from the foci; m = v/u, m_L = m², γ = 1/m
- Thin lens as a system: both principal planes at the lens, f₁ = −f₂; six cardinal points
- Nodal points: unit angular magnification, H₁N₁ = H₂N₂ = f₁ + f₂; equal end media make them the principal points
- Nodal slide: the pivot at N₂ keeps the image of a distant object still

### Last-week summary

- μ₂/v − μ₁/u = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R twice; μ/v′ cancels; 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) = 1/f; biconvex R₂ < 0; in a liquid use μ/μ_m.
- Contact: powers add, P = P₁ + P₂. Newton x₁x₂ = f₁f₂ = −f² (from the foci); m = v/u = f/x₁, m_L = +m², γ = 1/m in air.
- Principal planes m = +1, nodal planes γ = +1; H₁N₁ = H₂N₂ = f₁ + f₂, N₁N₂ = H₁H₂; equal end media: nodal points = principal points.
- Thin lens in air: H₁ = H₂ = N₁ = N₂ = lens centre. Single surface: principal points at the vertex, nodal points at the centre of curvature.
- Nodal slide: rotate about N₂ and a distant object's image stays still. A real image of a real object needs object-image distance d ≥ 4f.
