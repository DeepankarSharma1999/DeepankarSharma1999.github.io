## GS-272: Environmental laws and key institutions

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Environment · Ecology fundamentals

*Goal:* Explain Environmental laws and key institutions and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### The legal framework

India's environmental laws grew sector by sector. The Wildlife Act of 1972 protects scheduled species and backs protected areas; its 1991 amendment stiffened penalties and covered specified plants. The Water Act of 1974 and the Water Cess Act of 1977 target water pollution. The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, now the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam and amended in 1988 and 2023, makes central approval compulsory before forest is de-reserved, diverted to non-forest use, leased, or clear-felled of natural trees for replanting. The Air Act followed in 1981 and the Environment (Protection) Act in 1986, described by MoEFCC as umbrella legislation. Later came the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 and the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010. Forest policy dates from 1952, revised in 1988.

### Key institutions

The National Committee for Environmental Planning and Coordination (1972) paved the way for the Ministry of Environment and Forests in 1984. The Central Pollution Control Board started on 23 September 1974 under the Water Act, took its present name in 1988, gained air-pollution duties in 1981 and widened its work under the 1986 Act; with state boards it monitors water quality at 507 stations. The Biological Diversity Act, passed to give effect to the Convention on Biological Diversity that India ratified in 1994, works through three tiers: the National Biodiversity Authority, set up at Chennai on 1 October 2003, State Biodiversity Boards and local Biodiversity Management Committees. The National Green Tribunal, created on 18 October 2010, sits at New Delhi with zonal benches at Pune, Kolkata, Bhopal and Chennai.

### Why enforcement lags and what helps

NCERT's verdict is blunt: the Water Act and Environment Protection Act were not effectively implemented, as shown by 251 polluting industries along rivers and lakes in 1997, and the Water Cess Act made marginal impact. Groundwater suffers from heavy metals, fluoride and nitrates. Institutions try to close the gap: the NGT exists for quick disposal of environmental and forest cases, including relief and compensation for damage, and may have up to twenty judicial and twenty expert members. Forest diversion is permitted only when unavoidable and with compensatory afforestation; in 2025, approvals for 12,649.57 ha carried afforestation over 13,768.35 ha. NCERT stresses public awareness and community participation, so answers should weigh law, institutions, enforcement capacity and people together.
