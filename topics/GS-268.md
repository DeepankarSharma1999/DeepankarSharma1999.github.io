## GS-268: Invasive alien species

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Environment · Ecology fundamentals

*Goal:* Explain Invasive alien species and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why some aliens turn invasive

Species outside their native range are called exotic or alien. The Class 11 geography text defines exotic species as those introduced into a system where they are not natural inhabitants, and notes many cases of extensive damage to native communities. Biology NCERT explains the mechanism: an introduced species becomes invasive and spreads rapidly because the invaded land lacks its natural predators. Some such invaders then cause decline or even extinction of indigenous species. Stable, species-rich communities are expected to be more resistant to such invasions.

### Case studies to remember

Global examples: Nile perch in Lake Victoria eliminated over 200 cichlid species; prickly pear cactus overran Australian rangeland in the 1920s; goats on the Galapagos outcompeted the Abingdon tortoise to extinction within a decade through superior browsing. Indian examples from NCERT: the weeds Parthenium (carrot grass), Lantana and water hyacinth (Eichhornia) damage native ecosystems, and the illegally introduced African catfish Clarias gariepinus, brought for aquaculture, threatens indigenous catfishes. These names recur in statement-based questions.

### Control through ecological principles

Australia controlled prickly pear only after introducing a cactus-feeding moth from the cactus's native habitat. This is biological control: using a predator's ability to regulate prey, the same principle applied to agricultural pest control. Predators keep prey from reaching densities that destabilise ecosystems. Predators also maintain species diversity by easing competition among prey: when Pisaster starfish were removed from an intertidal plot, more than ten invertebrate species vanished within a year.
