### Bridge: The height of a circular arc above its tangent line

Rest a ball of radius R on a table. At the touching point the table and the ball are in contact. Move a distance r sideways along the table and look straight up: the ball's surface is now at some height t above the table. That height is the gap thickness in a Newton's ring set-up.

**Exact relation.** Draw the radius from the centre of the ball to the surface point above r. The centre is a height R above the table. The right triangle has hypotenuse R, one side r (sideways) and the other side R - t (the vertical distance from the centre down to the point). Pythagoras gives R² = r² + (R - t)², so r² = 2Rt - t² and t = R - √(R² - r²).

**Approximation.** When the sideways distance r is small compared with R, t is tiny compared with R, so the term t² is negligible beside 2Rt. Then r² ≈ 2Rt, that is t ≈ r²/(2R). Size check with real numbers: R = 1 m and r = 1 cm give t = 5.00 × 10⁻⁵ m (exact value 5.000125 × 10⁻⁵ m), a difference of 0.0025 per cent. Doubling r makes the gap four times larger, because t grows as r².

**Reading the formula.** The gap grows with the square of the distance, so the gap is only half a wavelength of visible light (0.3 µm) at r = 0.77 mm for R = 1 m. This is why a small contact region shows many interference rings.

### Bridge: Reading a straight-line graph: slope and intercept

A relation y = s x + c plots as a straight line. The number s is the **slope**: how much y increases when x increases by 1. The number c is the **intercept**: the value of y where the line meets the y-axis at x = 0.

**How to find the slope from data.** Pick two points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) far apart on the line. Then s = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1). Using points far apart reduces the effect of small reading errors. If a line is drawn by hand through many points, take the two points from the line, not from individual data points.

**A shift in x does not change the slope.** If you relabel x as x + k (you started counting rings from a different number), then y = s(x + k) + c' = s x + (s k + c'). The line shifts but its slope is still s. In Newton's rings, the ring number m that you assign to the first visible ring may differ from its true order; the slope of D² against m is unaffected.

**Units.** If y is a diameter squared in mm² and x counts rings, the slope is in mm² per ring. Convert to m² per ring before dividing by 4R in metres.

**Worked mini-example.** Points (m, D²) = (5, 9.38) and (20, 44.72) in mm²: slope = (44.72 - 9.38)/(20 - 5) = 35.34/15 = 2.356 mm² per ring.

### 1. Orientation: what Newton's rings are and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Name the parts of the ring arrangement, state in one sentence why rings appear, and list the four verified questions and where each is solved.

## The picture first

Place a lens with one flat face and one gently curved face (a **plano-convex lens**) with its curved face down on a flat sheet of glass. Look straight down in one colour of light. You see a dark spot surrounded by alternate bright and dark circles, which crowd closer together as you go outwards. These are **Newton's rings**. The arrangement is described in Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam, A Textbook of Optics (PDF pp. 368 to 369).

## Why rings appear, in one paragraph

Between the curved face and the plate there is a thin film of air whose thickness t is zero at the contact point and grows outwards. A ray going down is split at the glass-to-air boundary: one part reflects at once, the other crosses the air film and reflects from the plate. The two parts come from the same ray (**division of amplitude**), so they are coherent and interfere. Wherever the thickness gives a path difference of a whole number of wavelengths, the reflected light is bright; wherever it gives a half-integer, it is dark. Points of equal thickness lie on circles around the contact point, so the fringes are circles: **fringes of equal thickness**.

## Vocabulary you need

- **Radius of curvature R:** the radius of the sphere of which the curved face is a part; for the experiment R is of the order of 1 m (PDF p. 372).
- **Order m:** the counting number of a ring, m = 0 at the centre, 1 for the first ring and so on.
- **Monochromatic:** one wavelength only; white light gives a few coloured rings.
- **Reflected light** means the rings are viewed from the side from which the light comes; **transmitted light** means viewed from below.

### 2. Foundation: the air film as a curved wedge and its geometry

*Objective:* Derive the thickness t of the air film at distance r from the contact point exactly and in the small-gap approximation, and state how big the neglected term is.

## Setting up coordinates

Take the flat plate as the horizontal plane. Let O be the contact point, taken as origin, and let r be the horizontal distance from O to a point P on the plate (the axis of the arrangement is the vertical line through O). Let t(r) be the vertical gap between the plate and the curved surface directly above P. The lens surface is part of a sphere of radius R whose centre C lies on the vertical axis at height R above O. Light travels vertically downward and is observed from above. These conventions are used in every derivation below.

## The exact relation

The surface point above P is at distance R from C. Its vertical distance from C is R - t, its horizontal distance from the axis is r. Pythagoras (Brij Lal Fig. 15.17 and Eq. 15.38, PDF p. 371) gives

$$ R² = r² + (R − t)²   so   r² = 2Rt − t²   (exact) $$

Solving for t: t = R − √(R² − r²). Expanding the root for r much smaller than R gives t = r²/(2R) × [1 + r²/(4R²) + ...]. The first term is the one used in all textbooks.

## The approximation and its size

Because t is far smaller than R, t² is negligible beside 2Rt, so

$$ r² ≈ 2Rt   or   t ≈ r²/(2R)   (Brij Lal Eq. 15.39) $$

Size of the error. For R = 1 m and r = 1 cm, the first correction factor r²/(4R²) is 2.5 × 10⁻⁵, so the approximation is good to 0.0025 per cent. At the ring where t = mλ/2 (a dark ring) the neglected term t² compared with 2Rt equals t/(2R) = mλ/(4R): for the 10th ring with λ = 589 nm and R = 1 m this is 1.5 × 10⁻⁶, and for the 200th ring it is 2.9 × 10⁻⁵. The ring formulas are therefore accurate to better than one part in 10⁴ for every ring that can be seen. This is why textbooks never keep the t² term.

## Why a set of circles is guaranteed

The gap t depends only on r, not on the direction. Every point at the same r therefore has the same gap, so the locus of any chosen gap is a circle about O. This is the geometric content of the phrase 'fringes of equal thickness' (PDF pp. 370 to 372).

## The film is a curved wedge

For a wedge (PH-050 module 5) the gap grows linearly with distance, so fringes are equally spaced straight lines. Here t grows as r², so the gap changes slowly near O and quickly farther out. Consecutive fringes need the gap to change by λ/2 each time, so near the centre a large r-interval is needed for each fringe and far from the centre a small one: the rings crowd outwards (the number of rings within radius r is about r²/(λR); for r = 1 cm, R = 0.80 m, λ = 600 nm this is 208).

## Interference between which beams

Two reflected beams matter: ray 1 reflects at the lower curved face of the lens (glass to air) and ray 2 at the plate (air to glass). Both come from the same incident ray, so they are coherent. Brij Lal Chapter 14 (PDF pp. 340 to 342) makes the general point: two independent sources can never be coherent, but two beams derived from one source by a device such as a thin film can be. Reflections from the upper face of the lens and the lower face of the plate produce only a roughly uniform background.

**Dimension check:** r² and Rt both have dimension length², as the equation requires.

### 3. Core derivation 1: dark and bright ring radii and diameters

*Objective:* Derive r_m squared equals m lambda R for dark rings, the bright-ring version, the diameters and the spacing law, and explain the dark centre.

## Assumptions

Monochromatic light of wavelength λ falls normally on the air film, viewed in reflection from above; the film is air (index 1) and the glass has index greater than 1; r is measured from O; t is the gap from module 2.

## Step 1: the path difference and the phase jump

Ray 1 reflects at glass to air (lower index beyond): no phase jump. Ray 2 reflects at air to glass (higher index beyond): a jump of π, equal to half a wavelength (PH-050 bridge B2). Ray 2 also travels an extra path 2t down and up the film (normal incidence, index 1). The effective path difference is

$$ Δ = 2t − λ/2   (Brij Lal Eq. 15.35) $$

Writing +λ/2 instead differs by a whole λ and gives the same pattern.

## Step 2: conditions

Bright: Δ = kλ, so 2t = (2k + 1)λ/2, k = 0, 1, 2, ... Dark: Δ is an odd multiple of λ/2, namely (2k − 1)λ/2, so **2t = kλ**, k = 0, 1, 2, ... (Eqs. 15.36 and 15.37, PDF pp. 369 to 370). The book writes the dark condition as Δ = (2m + 1)λ/2 and then 2t = mλ; the algebra needs the integer renamed (m + 1 = k), which also admits k = 0, the centre. The result is the same, and the same renaming is noted in PH-050. Below, m means this k.

## Step 3: the dark centre

At O, t = 0 and Δ = −λ/2, so the two beams are exactly out of phase and the centre is dark; this is the m = 0 dark ring (PDF p. 372).

## Step 4: substitute the geometry

Using t = r²/(2R) from module 2. For a dark ring: 2 × r_m²/(2R) = mλ, so

$$ r_m² = m λ R,   r_m = √(m λ R),   D_m² = 4 m λ R   (Eqs. 15.40, 15.41) $$

For a bright ring: r²/R = (2m + 1)λ/2, so r² = (2m + 1)λR/2, **D² = 2(2m + 1)λR**, with m = 0 for the first bright ring. If the n-th bright ring is counted n = 1, 2, 3, ... the formula is D_n² = 2(2n − 1)λR; always check how the question counts.

## Step 5: what the formulas say

- Diameters grow as √m, so D_m : D_1 = √m (1, 1.41, 1.73, 2, ...). The spacing between neighbours falls: the radial gap between ring m and m + 1 is about √(λR)/(2√m).
- D ∝ √λ: red rings are bigger than blue; D ∝ √R: a flatter lens (larger R) gives larger rings.
- Dark and bright rings alternate: the first bright ring (t = λ/4) lies inside the first dark ring (t = λ/2).

## Numbers (R = 0.80 m, λ = 600 nm, all recomputed)

| ring | dark D (mm) | bright D (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.386 | 0.980 |
| 2 | 1.960 | 1.697 |
| 3 | 2.400 | 2.191 |
| 4 | 2.771 | 2.592 |
| 5 | 3.098 | 2.939 |
| 10 | 4.382 | not needed |

D_2/D_1 = 1.414 and D_4/D_1 = 2.000. The radial step from dark ring 10 to 11 is 0.1069 mm (formula √(λR)/(2√10.5) gives 0.1069 mm), but from 1 to 2 it is 0.2870 mm.

## Transmitted light

In transmitted light the pattern is complementary: bright rings at 2t = mλ (so r_m² = mλR describes the bright rings) and a bright centre (PDF p. 374, Eqs. 15.51 and 15.52). For an ideal film with no absorption, energy conservation makes the two patterns complementary.

*Limit check:* R → ∞ (flat lens face) gives no rings, because t = 0 everywhere. *Dimension:* D² has dimension length².

### 4. Core derivation 2: measuring the wavelength and the radius of curvature

*Objective:* Derive lambda = (D_(m+p)^2 - D_m^2)/(4pR), explain why the difference removes the contact error, and describe the graph method.

## The experiment (Brij Lal PDF pp. 372 to 373)

A plano-convex lens of large radius of curvature (about 1 m) lies on a plate, lit by a sodium lamp through a glass plate inclined at 45° that turns the beam to fall normally. A travelling microscope is focused on the rings. The cross-wire is set tangent to a dark ring on the right side of the centre and to the same ring on the left; the difference of the two scale readings is the ring's diameter D. The book takes readings for rings from about the 5th to the 19th, squares the diameters, and plots D_m² against m.

## Derivation 2: wavelength from two rings

From module 3, D_m² = 4mλR and D_(m+p)² = 4(m + p)λR. Subtract:

$$ D²_(m+p) − D²_m = 4pλR   so   λ = (D²_(m+p) − D²_m) / (4pR)   (Eq. 15.44) $$

The slope of the graph of D_m² against m is 4λR (Eq. 15.45). If R is known (spherometer) the slope gives λ; if λ is known the same slope gives R = slope/(4λ). A diameter is measured and not a radius because the centre of the pattern is not sharp, whereas the left-right difference of a ring is precise.

## Why the difference and not a single ring

Dust or the elastic flattening of the glass at the contact means the lens does not touch the plate exactly at a point, so the gap is t = r²/(2R) + ε with an unknown small ε. The dark condition 2t = mλ becomes r²/R + 2ε = mλ, so

$$ D_m² = 4 m λ R − 8 R ε $$

The graph is a line with the same slope 4λR, shifted down by 8Rε. The difference of two squares removes ε. A second effect is that the true order of the first ring seen may not be 1; relabelling m → m + k changes only the intercept (bridge B2). Therefore only the number p of rings between the two chosen rings matters, never their absolute order.

## Numbers (R = 1.00 m, λ = 589 nm, ε = 0.3 µm, recomputed)

| m | D² (mm²) |
|---|---|
| 5 | 9.38 |
| 10 | 21.16 |
| 15 | 32.94 |
| 20 | 44.72 |

Slope = (44.72 − 9.38)/15 = 2.356 mm² per ring = 4λR ✓. Using the pair m = 5 and 20 (p = 15): λ = 35.34 × 10⁻⁶ m² / (4 × 15 × 1.00 m) = 5.89 × 10⁻⁷ m. The intercept is −8Rε = −2.4 mm².

## Two book problems solved here (answers not given in the extract)

- Problem 6 (PDF p. 401): λ = 590 nm, D₁₀ = 0.5 × 10⁻² m: R = D²/(4mλ) = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵/(4 × 10 × 5.9 × 10⁻⁷) = 1.059 m. This single-ring form assumes ε = 0.
- Problem 13 (PDF p. 401): D₃ = 0.2 cm, D₂₃ = 0.6 cm, R = 92 cm, so p = 20 and λ = (0.36 − 0.04) cm² / (4 × 20 × 92 cm) = 4.35 × 10⁻⁵ cm = 4348 Å.

## Practical points

- Use rings from about the 5th to the 20th, away from the distorted centre and still countable (the range the book uses; the reason is standard practice, verify).
- The book's unit habit: keep D in cm and R in cm, then λ comes out in cm; convert at the end.

*Dimension check:* (length²)/(length) = length ✓.

### 5. Core derivation 3: a liquid in the gap (the 2026 question)

*Objective:* Derive the radius of the n-th dark ring when a liquid of index mu fills the gap, explain how the pattern changes, and use the result to measure mu.

## Assumptions

Lens and plate are glass of index n_g. The gap holds a transparent liquid of index μ, and μ is less than n_g (water or oil on crown glass). Light is normal, viewed in reflection; t = r²/(2R) as in module 2.

## Step 1: path difference in the liquid

Optical path is index times geometric path (PH-043). The extra geometric path of ray 2 is 2t, so the extra optical path is 2μt (Brij Lal PDF p. 373).

## Step 2: phase jumps

Ray 1 reflects at lens glass to liquid: going to a lower index, no jump. Ray 2 reflects at liquid to plate glass: going to a higher index, jump π (half a wavelength). This is the same pattern of jumps as for air, so

$$ Δ = 2μt − λ/2 ,   dark: 2μt = mλ   (m = 0, 1, 2, ...) $$

The centre (t = 0) is still dark.

## Step 3: the ring radius

Put t = r²/(2R): μ r²/R = mλ, so

$$ r_m² = m λ R / μ ,   D_m² = 4 m λ R / μ   (Eq. 15.46) $$

## Step 4: how the pattern changes

Compared with air, D_m is smaller by the factor 1/√μ. Reason: the wavelength in the liquid is λ/μ, so a given gap holds μ times more half-waves and the m-th dark ring is met at a smaller gap. The radial spacing falls by the same factor, and the number of rings inside a given circle grows in proportion to μ.

## Step 5: the effect of a larger refractive index

Since D_m ∝ μ^(−1/2), a larger μ shrinks the pattern further and crowds the rings. Numbers for R = 1.00 m, λ = 589 nm (recomputed): D₁₀ = 4.854 mm in air, 4.209 mm for μ = 1.33, 3.963 mm for μ = 1.50; the ratios to air are 0.867 and 0.816.

**Visibility.** Each interface now reflects less. For normal incidence the reflectance is ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))² (standard; verify, see PH-050 module 8). Air to glass 1.50 gives 4.0 per cent; water 1.33 to glass 1.50 gives 0.36 per cent, about 11 times less. The two reflected beams stay about equally strong, so contrast stays good, but the rings fade against stray reflections; at μ = n_g nothing reflects and they vanish. Beyond n_g the reflectance rises again (the π jump moves to the lens face), the centre stays dark and D ∝ μ^(−1/2) continues.

**Bright or dark centre.** If μ is bigger than both n_lens and n_plate, or smaller than both, exactly one reflection carries the jump and the centre is dark. If μ lies between n_lens and n_plate, both reflections or neither carry the jump, the net jump is zero and the centre is bright; this answers Brij Lal's question 13 (PDF p. 400; PH-050 module 7).

## Step 6: measuring μ

For the same ring in air and in the liquid, D²_air / D²_liq = μ (Eq. 15.50). To remove a contact error use rings p apart:

$$ μ = (D²_(m+p) − D²_m)_air / (D²_(m+p) − D²_m)_liq $$

Worked example (original): R = 1.20 m, λ = 589 nm: the 10th dark ring in air has D = 5.317 mm. With a liquid of μ = 1.33 it has D = 4.611 mm. Check: (5.317/4.611)² = 1.330 ✓. Book Example 15.8 (μ = 1.215) is in PH-050 masterNotes.

**Answer skeleton (2026, 10 marks; practice target about 12 minutes, not an official allotment):** state the new path 2μt; the jump pattern and dark centre; derive r_m² = mλR/μ; describe the shrinkage by 1/√μ; and discuss larger μ (smaller, more crowded rings; fainter, vanishing at μ = n_g; bright centre if μ is between the two glass indices).

### 6. Core derivation 4: rings between two curved surfaces (the 2020 question)

*Objective:* Derive the gap and the ring radii when two spherical surfaces touch, for the convex-convex and convex-concave cases, and define the equivalent radius.

## Definition first

**Newton's rings** are the concentric bright and dark circles of equal thickness seen in monochromatic light when a thin film of variable thickness, with zero thickness at the point of contact, lies between a curved surface and another surface (PDF pp. 368 to 369). The commonest film is air between a convex lens and a flat plate. Here both surfaces are curved.

## Set-up and sign convention

Two spherical surfaces touch at O. Take the common tangent plane at O as horizontal. The upper surface is the convex lens face of radius R₁, curving upward away from O (its centre is above O). At horizontal distance r it lies a height r²/(2R₁) above the tangent plane (module 2).

**Case A, lower surface convex (a second convex lens lying with its curved face up, radius R₂).** It curves downward away from O, so at distance r it lies r²/(2R₂) below the tangent plane. The gap is the sum:

$$ t = r²/(2R₁) + r²/(2R₂) $$

**Case B, lower surface concave (a spherical cup, radius R₂).** It curves upward, at height r²/(2R₂) above the tangent plane. The gap is the difference:

$$ t = r²/(2R₁) − r²/(2R₂),   needs R₂ > R₁ $$

If R₂ were smaller than R₁ the lens would touch the cup along a ring, not at O. If R₂ = R₁ the surfaces fit and the gap is zero everywhere.

## Dark rings in reflected light

The phase jumps are the same as for air (module 3), so the dark condition is 2t = mλ, m = 0, 1, 2, ...:

$$ r_m² (1/R₁ ± 1/R₂) = m λ   (Eqs. 15.54, 15.56; + for convex, − for concave) $$

so r_m² = mλR_eq, where the **equivalent radius** is

$$ R_eq = R₁R₂ / (R₂ ± R₁) $$

with + for the convex lower surface and − for the concave one. Bright rings: r²(1/R₁ ± 1/R₂) = (2m + 1)λ/2 (Eqs. 15.55 and 15.57). A dark centre follows in both cases.

## Checks

- R₂ → ∞ (a plane): R_eq = R₁ and r_m² = mλR₁, the usual law ✓.
- Convex-convex: R_eq is smaller than either radius, so the rings are smaller and crowd faster.
- Convex-concave: R_eq is larger than R₁, so the rings are larger; as R₂ approaches R₁, R_eq grows without limit and the rings spread to infinity.

## Numbers (R₁ = 1.00 m, R₂ = 2.00 m, λ = 589 nm, recomputed)

| ring m | plane plate D (mm) | convex-convex D (mm) | convex-concave D (mm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.535 | 1.253 | 2.171 |
| 2 | 2.171 | 1.772 | 3.070 |
| 5 | 3.432 | 2.802 | 4.854 |
| 10 | 4.854 | 3.963 | 6.864 |

The equivalent radii are 1.000 m (plane), 0.667 m (convex-convex) and 2.000 m (convex-concave). The convex-concave D for ring 1 equals the plane-plate D for ring 2 because R_eq is exactly twice R₁.

## A mismatch test

For a lens R₁ = 1.000 m on a cup R₂ = 1.001 m, 1/R₁ − 1/R₂ = 9.99 × 10⁻⁴ m⁻¹ and R_eq is about 1001 m. The number of rings inside a radius a is N = a²(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)/λ; for a = 1 cm and λ = 589 nm, N = 0.17: a mismatch of 1 mm in radius moves the gap at the edge by only 0.17 of a fringe. This is why surface fit is tested by counting fringes against a master surface (PDF p. 393; module 9).

### 7. Core: the numericals - coincident rings (2019) and bright-ring diameters (2022)

*Objective:* Solve the 2019 and 2022 numericals with every step, and state the reading of the stem used and how much the answer depends on it.

## 2019, part (ii): coincident dark rings

Stem: two wavelengths λ₁ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m and λ₂ = 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ m; the m-th dark ring of one coincides with the (m + 1)-th dark ring of the other; R = 90 cm; find the diameter of the m-th dark ring. Part (i): see PH-050 module 4.

**Which ring is which.** Dark rings obey r² = mλR, so for the same order the longer wavelength gives the larger ring. For two rings to share a radius, the longer wavelength must have the lower order. So the m-th ring of λ₁ coincides with the (m + 1)-th ring of λ₂.

1. Same radius: mλ₁R = (m + 1)λ₂R.
2. m(λ₁ − λ₂) = λ₂, so m = λ₂/(λ₁ − λ₂) = 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ / 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ = 59.
3. r² = mλ₁R = 59 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.90 = 3.186 × 10⁻⁵ m².
4. D = 2r = 2 × √(3.186 × 10⁻⁵) = 1.129 × 10⁻² m = **1.13 cm**.
5. Check with the other wavelength: (m + 1)λ₂R = 60 × 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.90 = 3.186 × 10⁻⁵ m² ✓.

General result (Brij Lal question 15, PDF p. 400): r² = λ₁λ₂R/(λ₁ − λ₂). Here 6.0e-7 × 5.9e-7 × 0.90 / 1.0e-8 = 3.186 × 10⁻⁵ ✓. *Limit:* λ₁ → λ₂ makes m infinite. Coincidences recur at (59k, 60k), D = 1.60 cm for k = 2, but those orders differ by 2, 3, ...; only k = 1 satisfies the stem (m and m + 1), so m = 59 is unique.

## 2022: bright rings with two diameters

Stem: R = 100 cm; the 4th and 15th bright rings have D₄ = 0.314 cm and D₁₅ = 0.574 cm. Find D₂₄, D₃₆ and λ.

**Method (differences, so the contact error cancels).** For bright rings D_n² = 2(2n − 1)λR + (contact term). For two rings the contact term cancels in the difference. The order jump is 15 − 4 = 11, so the difference of the factors 2(2n − 1) is 2 × 2 × 11 = 44, i.e. D₁₅² − D₄² = 4 × 11 × λR:

1. D₁₅² − D₄² = 0.329476 − 0.098596 = 0.230880 cm².
2. λ = 0.230880 / (44 × 100) cm = 5.247 × 10⁻⁵ cm = **5247 Å** (524.7 nm).
3. D₂₄² = D₄² + 4 × 20 × λR = 0.098596 + 0.41978 = 0.51838 cm², **D₂₄ = 0.720 cm**.
4. D₃₆² = D₄² + 4 × 32 × λR = 0.098596 + 0.67165 = 0.77025 cm², **D₃₆ = 0.878 cm**. (Starting from D₁₅ gives the same: 0.720 and 0.878 cm.)

**Why not use each ring alone.** If the zero-contact-error law held, each ring would give the same λ. Using D_n² = 2(2n − 1)λR: D₄ gives 7.04 × 10⁻⁵ cm, D₁₅ gives 5.68 × 10⁻⁵ cm; using D_n² = 4nλR: 6.16 × 10⁻⁵ and 5.49 × 10⁻⁵ cm. They disagree, so the printed data contain an offset (positive D² offset, ε ≈ −0.3 µm: flattening at the contact, not dust) and the difference method is the consistent one.

**Other readings, for comparison.** Taking the λ above and the zero-offset law gives D₂₄, D₃₆ = 0.710, 0.869 cm (law 4nλR) or 0.702, 0.863 cm (law 2(2n − 1)λR). The three linear readings differ by at most 2.5 per cent; scaling one ring by a ratio (D₂₄ = D₄√(47/7) = 0.814 cm) ignores the offset and gives 0.73 to 0.81 cm, so avoid it. The lesson solves the linear reading; no examiner key is asserted. State the reading in the answer.

### 8. Master: visibility, oblique light, a lens that does not touch, white light

*Objective:* Explain, with numbers, why reflected rings have better contrast than transmitted rings, what oblique incidence and a lifted lens do to the rings, and what white light shows.

## Reflected versus transmitted contrast

Visibility is V = 2√(I₁I₂)/(I₁ + I₂) for two interfering beams of intensities I₁ and I₂ (PH-051 bridge B2). Take the air-glass reflectance R = 0.04 (module 5).

- Reflected light: beam 1 has I₁ = R = 0.04; beam 2 has I₂ = (1 − R)²R = 0.0369. They are almost equal: V = 0.999. The extreme intensities are 0.154 and 0.00006 of the incident intensity: near-black dark rings.
- Transmitted light: the direct beam has I₁ = (1 − R)² = 0.922 and the twice-reflected beam I₂ = (1 − R)²R² = 0.00147. They are very unequal: V = 2R/(1 + R²) = 0.080. The transmitted rings are faint, a few per cent modulation on a bright background.

This is why reflected rings are the ones used (a standard result; the book states only that the patterns are complementary, PDF p. 374). The numbers assume ideal surfaces and ignore the other glass faces.

## Oblique incidence

If light enters the film at angle r (inside the film), the dark condition is 2t cos r = mλ, so t = mλ/(2 cos r) and every ring diameter squared is multiplied by 1/cos r. At r = 10° the factor is 1.015 (1.5 per cent in D²); at 20°, 1.064; at 30°, 1.155. The rings are then no longer simple fringes of equal thickness alone and the wavelength measurement is biased, which is why a parallel beam falling normally is used (the 45° glass plate sends the beam down along the axis, PDF p. 369).

## A lens that does not touch

Suppose the lens is lifted a small height h above the plate (or sits on a dust speck). The gap is t = h + r²/(2R). The dark condition 2t = mλ becomes 2h + r²/R = mλ, so

$$ r_m² = R (m λ − 2h) $$

Consequences: (a) the diameters are no longer proportional to √m, but D² is still a straight line in m of slope 4λR, now with intercept −8Rh, matching the contact-error result of module 4 with ε = h. (b) The ring of a given order m has a smaller radius for larger h. (c) At the centre the order is 2h/λ: the centre is dark when 2h = kλ and bright when 2h = (k + ½)λ, so it alternates every λ/4 of lift, and a full dark-to-dark cycle (one ring passing through the centre) needs λ/2. For h = λ/4 the centre is bright and the dark rings follow r_m² = Rλ(m − ½), the series that was the bright series before. Every λ/2 of lift swallows one ring. (Standard derivation; the book's equation 15.84 for the Fizeau expansion test is the same idea in module 9.)

## White light

Each wavelength has its own ring system with D ∝ √λ. At the centre every colour is dark, so the centre is black. Near the centre the violet and red dark rings differ only slightly in position, so coloured rings are seen for a few orders; farther out the many colours overlap and the field fades to a nearly uniform pale tint (standard; verify for the number of orders). Monochromatic light is preferred for measurement because exact coincidences of order can then be counted (the book uses sodium light, PDF p. 372).

## Why an extended source is allowed

The two reflected beams from the same incident ray leave the film almost parallel to each other when the gap is small, so the fringes remain sharp for a broad source if the observer focuses on the film with a microscope (standard; verify). The film must be thin compared with the coherence length of the source, a limit developed in PH-050 and PH-051 module 7.

### 9. Master: uses of Newton's rings - expansion, flatness and lens testing

*Objective:* Explain, with a worked count, how Newton's rings measure small displacements and test surfaces, and relate these uses to the ring law.

## Counting rings is counting half-wavelengths

At the centre of the pattern the order is m_c = 2x/λ, where x is the gap between the lens vertex and the plate (module 8). If x changes by λ/2, one ring crosses any fixed point. A count of N rings therefore means a change in x of

$$ Δx = N λ / 2 $$

For 100 rings in 589 nm light, Δx = 29.45 µm; for 250 rings, 73.6 µm.

## Thermal expansion (Fizeau; Brij Lal PDF p. 392)

A crystal stands on a metal disc held by three screws; a lens rests on the screws' ends with a thin air film between lens and crystal (the book's Fig. 15.37). Light falls on the lens-crystal combination and the rings are read with a travelling microscope. When the crystal is heated its top rises, the air film becomes thinner and the rings expand. The rings that pass the cross-wire are counted. The ring law with a gap x gives, for the m-th dark ring of radius r_m (module 8, derivation by 2(x + r_m²/2R) = mλ),

$$ x = m λ / 2 − r_m² / (2R) $$

Brij Lal Eq. 15.84 is 2μ(x + r_m²/2R) = mλ, μ the air-film index (reconstructed from the broken text layer; verify); μ = 1 gives the form above. Two readings of x, before and after heating, give the expansion; dividing by the crystal height and the temperature rise gives the expansion coefficient (standard step; verify).

## Testing flatness (PDF p. 392)

An optical flat laid at a small angle on a component forms an air wedge. For a smooth, flat surface the bands are straight and equidistant. If the bands curve toward the contact edge the surface is concave, if away it is convex (the book's statement). One band of departure from straightness corresponds to a gap change of λ/2; with 589 nm light, one band is 0.295 µm, and a departure of one fifth of a band is 59 nm (recomputed). A band of curvature equal to its own spacing is a full λ/2 error.

## Testing a lens surface (PDF p. 393)

A lens is laid on a master optical flat (fused quartz, faces flat to better than a wavelength). A well-ground lens gives true circular rings; irregular or elliptical rings show where to grind and polish further. The master surface can also be a test cup (module 6): rings count the mismatch of the two radii of curvature.

## Other measurements

- The radius R of a lens from a known wavelength (module 4).
- The refractive index of a liquid (module 5); the book names this arrangement a refractive index detector in the figure caption on PDF p. 373.
- The thickness of a thin spacer is a wedge application (PH-050 module 5), not a ring one.

## Which kind of fringe is it?

Newton's rings are fringes of equal thickness; the Michelson circular fringes of PH-051 are fringes of equal inclination, although both are circles. The difference is explained in masterNotes. The 2019 question part (i) asks exactly this contrast (PH-050 module 4).

**Dimension and limit checks:** Δx has dimension length. With N = 0 there is no displacement.

### Notes: Definitions

- Newton's rings: concentric bright and dark circles of equal thickness seen when a lens of large radius of curvature rests on a plane plate (or another curved surface) and is lit by monochromatic light.
- Radius of curvature R: radius of the sphere of which the lens face is a part; about 1 m in the standard experiment (Brij Lal PDF p. 372).
- Order m: counting number of a ring; m = 0 is the centre; dark rings obey 2t = m lambda in reflected light with an air film.
- Fringes of equal thickness: each fringe marks one film thickness; Newton's rings and wedge fringes belong here; fringes of equal inclination (Haidinger, Michelson circles) mark one angle in a parallel film.
- Contact error epsilon: a small extra gap at the centre (dust, flattening) that shifts D squared by the constant 8 R epsilon.
- Equivalent radius R_eq: R1 R2 / (R2 plus or minus R1), the radius of a single lens on a plane that would give the same rings as the pair.

### Notes: Equations to know

- Gap: t = R - sqrt(R^2 - r^2) (exact); t = r^2/(2R) (approximation, error about r^2/(4R^2) of t).
- Path difference (air, normal incidence, reflected light): delta = 2t - lambda/2; dark 2t = m lambda; bright 2t = (2m + 1) lambda/2.
- Dark rings: r_m^2 = m lambda R, D_m^2 = 4 m lambda R. Bright rings: D^2 = 2(2m + 1) lambda R with m = 0 for the first (or 2(2n - 1) lambda R for the n-th, n = 1, 2, ...).
- Transmitted light: bright rings r_m^2 = m lambda R, dark rings r_m^2 = (2m + 1) lambda R/2; bright centre.
- Wavelength: lambda = (D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2)/(4 p R); graph slope = 4 lambda R.
- Liquid: D_m^2 = 4 m lambda R / mu; mu = D_air^2 / D_liq^2 (same ring), or ratio of differences for rings p apart.
- Two curved surfaces: r_m^2 (1/R1 plus or minus 1/R2) = m lambda, plus for convex-convex, minus for convex-concave (R2 > R1).
- Coincident dark rings, m-th of lambda1 with (m + 1)-th of lambda2: m = lambda2/(lambda1 - lambda2); r^2 = lambda1 lambda2 R/(lambda1 - lambda2).
- Lifted lens (gap h at the centre): r_m^2 = R(m lambda - 2h). Count of rings crossing a point: N = 2 delta x / lambda.

### Notes: Derivation skeletons (for revision)

- Dark and bright rings: draw lens on plate; gap t(r): R^2 = r^2 + (R - t)^2 so r^2 = 2Rt; path 2t with a pi jump at the plate; dark 2t = m lambda so r_m^2 = m lambda R; bright 2t = (2m + 1) lambda/2.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Order m versus ring number: m = 0 is the dark centre; the 'first dark ring' is m = 1. For bright rings the first has m = 0 in 2(2m + 1) lambda R but n = 1 in 2(2n - 1) lambda R. Read the question.
- Radius versus diameter: the book formulas for D contain the factor 4 (dark) or 2(2m + 1) (bright); using r with these gives an answer off by a factor 2.
- Equal thickness versus equal inclination: both give circles, but Newton's rings come from a curved film seen at normal incidence; Michelson circles come from a parallel film seen over many angles.
- Dark centre depends on the phase jumps: a liquid with an index between the lens and the plate indices gives a bright centre.
- Raising the lens is not the same as a larger R: raising shifts the intercept; changing R changes the slope.
- Reflected and transmitted rings are complementary in position but not in contrast.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- Approximation r^2 = 2Rt fails only for gaps comparable with R; irrelevant for visible rings (error under 3e-5 for the 200th ring).
- R2 < R1 for a concave lower surface: the lens rests on the rim of the cup and the rings are not centred at one contact point; excluded.
- Contact error epsilon changes the intercept, not the slope; using a single ring then misestimates lambda or R.
- A liquid whose index equals the glass index removes the reflections and the rings fade; the rings stay visible only while the interface reflects.
- Oblique incidence multiplies D squared by 1/cos r; large angles also distort the rings from circles when the source is extended.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-OSX3-FILMPHASE (verified):** Light undergoes a phase change of 180 degrees (pi radians) on reflection at an interface beyond which the refractive index is higher; no phase change takes place on reflection from a medium of lower refractive index. The phase change is equivalent to lambda/2 in path length.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 3.4 'Interference in Thin Films'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/3-4-interference-in-thin-films)
- **F-OSX3-NEWTONRING (verified):** A lens can be compared with a master surface as it is being ground. Each successive ring of a given colour indicates an increase of only half a wavelength in the distance between the lens and the blank, so great precision can be obtained; once the lens is perfect, no rings appear. Hooke discovered the rings; Newton described them in detail.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 3.4 'Interference in Thin Films'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/3-4-interference-in-thin-films)

### Memory skeleton

- Set-up: convex lens (R about 1 m) on a plate, sodium light at normal incidence, travelling microscope
- Gap geometry: R² = r² + (R − t)², so t ≈ r²/(2R)
- Path 2t − λ/2 (π jump at the plate only): dark 2t = mλ, bright 2t = (2m + 1)λ/2, dark centre
- Rings: D_m² = 4mλR (dark), D² = 2(2m + 1)λR (bright); D ∝ √m, rings crowd outward
- Measurement: λ = (D²_(m+p) − D²_m)/(4pR); contact error ε shifts only the intercept
- Liquid in the gap: 2μt = mλ, r_m² = mλR/μ, rings shrink by 1/√μ
- Two curved surfaces: sum or difference of sags, R_eq = R₁R₂/(R₂ ± R₁); coincident rings of two wavelengths

### Last-week summary

- t = r²/(2R); Δ = 2t − λ/2; dark 2t = mλ (m = 0 is the dark centre), bright 2t = (2m + 1)λ/2; transmitted rings complementary, bright centre.
- Dark D_m² = 4mλR; bright D² = 2(2m + 1)λR with m = 0 first (2(2n − 1)λR if n = 1 first); first bright ring inside first dark.
- λ = (D²_(m+p) − D²_m)/(4pR), graph slope 4λR; contact gap ε gives D_m² = 4mλR − 8Rε, cancelled by the difference.
- Liquid μ < n_glass: r_m² = mλR/μ, μ = D²_air/D²_liq. Two surfaces: r_m²(1/R₁ ± 1/R₂) = mλ, + convex-convex, − convex-concave (R₂ > R₁).
- Coincidence: m-th of λ₁ (longer) on (m + 1)-th of λ₂: m = λ₂/(λ₁ − λ₂); 2019: m = 59, D = 1.13 cm.
