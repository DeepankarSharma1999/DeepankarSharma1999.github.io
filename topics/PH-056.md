### Bridge: Adding N equal phasors: the geometric series

A **phasor** is an arrow of length equal to an amplitude and direction equal to a phase. Two waves of the same frequency add as arrows placed head to tail. Write a unit phasor of phase φ as the complex number e^(iφ) = cos φ + i sin φ.

**The sum.** N equal unit phasors, each turned by 2β from the one before, are 1, e^(i2β), e^(i4β), ..., e^(i2(N−1)β). This is a geometric series with ratio q = e^(i2β). Its sum is (1 − q^N)/(1 − q). Factor e^(iNβ) out of the top and e^(iβ) out of the bottom:

(1 − e^(i2Nβ))/(1 − e^(i2β)) = e^(i(N−1)β) × (e^(−iNβ) − e^(iNβ))/(e^(−iβ) − e^(iβ)) = e^(i(N−1)β) × sin(Nβ)/sin(β),

because e^(iφ) − e^(−iφ) = 2i sin φ. The factor e^(i(N−1)β) is only a phase; the length of the chord is |sin(Nβ)/sin(β)|.

**Checks.** For N = 1 the length is 1. For N = 2 it is sin 2β/sin β = 2 cos β, the two-slit result of PH-055. When β is a multiple of π every arrow points the same way and the length is N (limit of sin(Nβ)/sin β as β tends to nπ).

**Why it matters.** In a grating each slit sends a phasor of the same length, turned by the phase 2β = 2π d sin θ/λ from its neighbour, so the grating amplitude is the single-slit amplitude times sin(Nβ)/sin(β).

### Bridge: Small changes from a relation: differentiating d sin(theta) = n lambda

Suppose two quantities are linked by a relation, for example d sin θ = n λ, where d and n are fixed. If λ changes by a small amount dλ, θ must change by a small amount dθ so that the relation still holds. Differentiate both sides with respect to the changing quantities:

d cos θ dθ = n dλ, so dθ/dλ = n/(d cos θ).

The derivative of sin θ with respect to θ is cos θ (θ in radians). The rule 'differentiate both sides' works for any relation.

**Using it.** The angular shift per unit change of wavelength is dθ/dλ. For d = 1.0 µm, n = 1, θ = 36.1°: cos θ = 0.808, so dθ/dλ = 1/(1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.808) = 1.24 × 10⁶ rad per metre = 1.24 × 10⁻³ rad per nm. A wavelength difference of 0.6 nm gives dθ = 7.4 × 10⁻⁴ rad.

**Size of the approximation.** The formula is exact only as dλ tends to zero. For dλ = 0.6 nm the exact angle difference is 7.427 × 10⁻⁴ rad against 7.425 × 10⁻⁴ rad from the derivative (the cosine taken at the first line), a difference of 0.03 per cent.

**Two uses in this lesson.** The same step gives the width of a principal maximum: a change of path of λ/N per slit gives dθ = λ/(N d cos θ). And it gives the dispersion.

### 1. Orientation: what a grating is and the two different 'powers'

*Objective:* State what a diffraction grating does, name its quantities, and separate dispersion from resolving power before any derivation.

## The picture first

A **plane transmission grating** is a plate with a very large number N of equal, parallel, narrow transparent slits, separated by opaque strips. Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam (A Textbook of Optics, PDF pp. 457 to 458) describe how it is made: lines are ruled with a diamond on glass, or on a silvered surface to make a **reflection grating**; practical gratings are replicas moulded in collodion from a ruled original; a grating for the visible has about 10,000 lines per cm. Send white light onto it and you see a white central image and, on each side, coloured spectra in which violet is nearer the centre than red.

## Vocabulary

- **Grating element (grating constant) d = a + b:** the distance from one slit to the next, where a is the slit width and b the opaque width. If a grating has N′ lines per cm then d = 1/N′ cm. Example: 15,000 lines per inch gives d = 2.54 cm/15,000 = 1.693 × 10⁻⁴ cm = 1693 nm.
- **Order n:** the integer in d sin θ = n λ; n = 0 is the central image; n = ±1, ±2, ... are the first, second, ... orders on the two sides.
- **Principal maximum:** one of the sharp bright lines of the pattern, one for each wavelength in each order.
- **Dispersion:** how far apart in angle two wavelengths are sent (a property of the angles).
- **Resolving power:** the smallest wavelength difference dλ that the grating can separate, expressed as λ/dλ (a property of the sharpness).

## The key point

These two properties are not the same. A prism or a grating can spread two lines far apart but still leave each so broad that they overlap; or it can send them close together but with very narrow lines. The grating's dispersion depends on d (the line spacing); its resolving power depends on N (the total number of lines). Everything in this lesson is built to show that.

### 2. Foundation: the grating equation from the path difference

*Objective:* Derive d sin(theta) = n lambda from the path difference between neighbouring slits and use it to find angles, the highest order and the colour order of a spectrum.

## Set-up and sign convention

Let the grating lie in a plane with the slits perpendicular to the page. Light of wavelength λ falls on it normally (plane wave, wave fronts parallel to the grating). Angles θ are measured from the normal to the grating, positive on either side; the orders ±n are symmetric about the normal. A lens collects the light diffracted at angle θ and brings it to a point P of its focal plane (Fraunhofer conditions, PH-054 module 7). Brij Lal's Fig. 18.13 (PDF p. 459) shows the same arrangement.

## Step 1: the path difference between neighbouring slits

Take corresponding points A and C of two neighbouring slits, a distance d = a + b apart along the grating. Light leaving both at angle θ differs in path by AC sin θ = d sin θ (PDF p. 459).

## Step 2: the condition for reinforcement

If this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, the wavelets from A and C are in phase; the same holds for every pair of neighbouring slits, so all N slits reinforce:

$$ d sin θ_n = n λ ,   n = 0, ±1, ±2, ...   (Brij Lal Eq. 18.42) $$

This is the **grating equation**. Different wavelengths satisfy it at different angles, so each wavelength has its own line in each order. In a given order the number of lines equals the number of wavelengths in the source (PDF p. 459).

## Step 3: what the equation tells you

- Order n = 0 has θ = 0 for every wavelength: the central image is white for white light.
- Longer λ gives a larger sin θ: red is deviated more than violet, the opposite of a prism (module 8).
- sin θ cannot exceed 1, so the highest order is n < d/λ. If d < λ there is no diffracted order at all (PDF p. 462).
- Halving d (twice as many lines per cm) doubles sin θ for a given order.

## Numbers (15,000 lines per inch, d = 1693.3 nm; recomputed)

For λ = 600 nm: sin θ₁ = 0.3543, θ₁ = 20.75°; sin θ₂ = 0.7087, θ₂ = 45.13°; the third order would need sin θ = 1.063, impossible, so there are two orders on each side (d/λ = 2.82). The book gives θ₁ = 20°45′ and θ₂ = 45°7′ for these data (PDF pp. 464 to 465), which agree (20.75° = 20°45′; 45.13° = 45°8′).

Brij Lal's Example 18.6 (PDF p. 475): 18,000 lines per inch, λ = 450 nm: d = 1411 nm, d/λ = 3.14, so the highest order is 3.

## Worked pattern: 6000 lines per cm, λ = 546 nm

d = 1667 nm; d/λ = 3.05. Normal incidence allows orders up to 3 on each side: sin θ₁ = 0.3276 (19.1°), sin θ₂ = 0.6552 (40.9°), sin θ₃ = 0.9828 (79.4°). The third order is nearly grazing, so it is faint in practice (check; standard).

## Why a grating needs many slits

Two slits make broad fringes (PH-055). Many slits, equally spaced, make the same positions of maxima but very narrow lines; the next module shows why. A grating therefore gives a clear spectrum, whereas two slits give a blur.

*Dimension check:* d sin θ and n λ are both lengths. *Limit:* n = 0 gives θ = 0 for all colours.

### 3. Core derivation 1: the N-slit intensity by adding N phasors

*Objective:* Derive I = I0 (sin alpha / alpha)^2 (sin N beta / sin beta)^2, find the principal maxima and count the secondary minima and maxima.

## Assumptions

N identical slits of width a and separation d = a + b, a plane wave at normal incidence, Fraunhofer conditions (PH-054, PH-055). Slit j (j = 0, 1, ..., N − 1) has its centre at x = j d. Angles from the normal, positive upward. Define, as in Brij Lal (PDF pp. 456 to 457),

$$ α = π a sin θ / λ ,   β = π d sin θ / λ $$

(β is called γ in PH-055).

## Step 1: one slit

By PH-054 each slit sends in direction θ a vibration of amplitude A₀ sin α/α. This factor is the same for every slit, because the slits are identical and the wave is plane.

## Step 2: the phase of slit j

Slit j is j d further along than slit 0, so its light differs in path by j d sin θ and in phase by 2π j d sin θ/λ = 2jβ. Thus slit j contributes the complex amplitude A₀ (sin α/α) e^(i2jβ).

## Step 3: add the N contributions

$$ A = A₀ (sin α/α) Σ e^(i2jβ) = A₀ (sin α/α) e^(i(N−1)β) sin(Nβ)/sin(β) $$

using the geometric series of bridge B1. The factor e^(i(N−1)β) is a phase only; the intensity is the squared length:

$$ I = I₀ (sin α/α)² (sin Nβ/sin β)²   (Brij Lal Eq. 18.40) $$

where I₀ is the intensity of a single slit in the forward direction. The first factor is the single-slit envelope; the second, the interference factor of N slits.

## Step 4: principal maxima

The interference factor has sin Nβ = 0 and sin β = 0 together when β = nπ, and the ratio tends to ±N. There the factor equals N², so

$$ I_max = N² I₀ (sin α/α)²   at   β = nπ ,   i.e.   d sin θ = n λ $$

The principal maxima are the grating equation of module 2 (Eq. 18.42), and they are N² times as intense as one slit (Eq. 18.41).

## Step 5: minima and secondary maxima

Zeros occur at Nβ = kπ with sin β not zero: k = 1, 2, ..., N − 1 between β = 0 and π, and so on. Between two adjacent principal maxima there are therefore N − 1 zeros and, between those zeros, N − 2 weak **secondary maxima** (PDF p. 457). In terms of the path difference the first zero beside a principal maximum corresponds to an extra path difference of λ/N per slit (PDF p. 460).

## Numbers for N = 5 (relative to the principal maximum, recomputed)

There are 3 secondary maxima between β = 0 and π, at Nβ/π = 1.45, 2.50, 3.55, with relative intensities 0.0625, 0.0400, 0.0625. For N large the first secondary maximum approaches 4.7 per cent of the principal maximum (N = 3000 gives 4.72 per cent, at Nβ/π = 1.431); they are too weak and too close to the principal line to be seen, so the field between principal maxima looks dark (PDF p. 461).

## Checks

- N = 2: sin²(2β)/sin²β = 4 cos²β, the double-slit result of PH-055 ✓.
- β = 0: both numerator and denominator vanish; the limit is N, the central maximum.
- The total light: the peak height grows as N² while the width falls as 1/N (module 4), so the energy in each principal maximum grows as N, as it should for N slits.
- A numerical check (brute-force integration over N = 5 slits of width 0.4 µm at spacing 1.0 µm and 0.5 µm light) matched the formula to better than 0.2 per cent at the five angles tried.

### 4. Core derivation 2: the width of a principal maximum and what N does

*Objective:* Derive the angular half-width of a principal maximum, show how it depends on N, the order and the angle, and correct the book's statement about higher orders.

## Assumptions and convention

Normal incidence; the n-th principal maximum at θₙ with d sin θₙ = n λ; the half-width δθ is the angle from the peak to the first zero beside it.

## Derivation (Brij Lal PDF pp. 460 to 461, Eqs. 18.43 to 18.45)

1. The first zero after the n-th principal maximum occurs when the path difference between neighbouring slits has increased by λ/N, so that the N slits together span an extra λ and cancel in pairs (module 3, Step 5):

$$ d sin(θₙ + δθ) = n λ + λ/N $$

2. Expand for small δθ: d sin θₙ cos(δθ) + d cos θₙ sin(δθ) ≈ d sin θₙ + d cos θₙ δθ. Subtract d sin θₙ = n λ:

$$ d cos θₙ δθ = λ/N ,   so   δθ = λ/(N d cos θₙ) $$

3. Using d = n λ/sin θₙ: δθ = sin θₙ/(n N cos θₙ) = tan θₙ/(n N) = 1/(n N cot θₙ), which is the book's Eq. 18.45.

The full angular width of the peak (zero to zero) is 2δθ.

## What it says

- δθ is proportional to 1/N: more lines, sharper lines. With N = 3000, d = 1.0 µm, λ = 589.3 nm, first order (θ₁ = 36.1°) δθ = 2.43 × 10⁻⁴ rad = 50 arcsec.
- At the same N, λ and d, δθ increases with θ: lines at large angle are broader, since the path of the whole grating, N d cos θ, shrinks as θ grows. Red lines (larger θ) are broader than violet lines (smaller θ), so lines are sharper toward the violet than toward the red, as the book says (PDF p. 461).
- δθ does not depend on the slit width a (the envelope only changes intensities).

## Correction: the order dependence

The book states that n cot θₙ is larger for higher orders, so the half-width is smaller for higher orders. **Checked and wrong in general.** Since n cot θₙ = n cos θₙ/sin θₙ = d cos θₙ/λ, and cos θₙ decreases as the order (and angle) increases, n cot θₙ decreases and δθ increases with the order at fixed N and d. Numbers for 15,000 lines per inch and λ = 600 nm: n = 1: θ = 20.75°, n cot θ = 2.639, tan θ/n = 0.379; n = 2: θ = 45.13°, n cot θ = 1.991, tan θ/n = 0.502. So the second-order line is 1.33 times broader in angle than the first-order line, though its dispersion is greater (module 5). It is the resolving power nN, not the half-width, that improves with order (module 6): the lines are broader but more widely separated, by more.

## Energy and the sharpness

Peak height N² and width proportional to 1/N means the energy in each principal maximum grows in proportion to N, as expected for N slits each adding a fixed amount. The book's question 26 (PDF p. 477), 'why gratings with a larger number of lines are preferred', is answered here: narrower lines (1/N), taller peaks (N²) and, in module 6, higher resolving power.

## A comparison with two slits

The half-width formula gives δθ equal to 1/N of the fringe spacing λ/(d cos θ). For N = 2 it is half the spacing, the broad cos² band of PH-055. For N = 3000 it is 1/3000 of the spacing: the lines are thin.

*Dimension check:* λ/(N d cos θ) is dimensionless (radians) ✓. *Limit:* N → ∞ gives δθ → 0, ideal sharp lines.

### 5. Core derivation 3: angular and linear dispersion

*Objective:* Derive d(theta)/d(lambda) = n/(d cos theta), the linear dispersion f n/(d cos theta), and explain why the spectrum is called a normal spectrum.

## Definition and caution on names

The angular **dispersion** of a grating is the change of angle per unit change of wavelength, dθ/dλ. Brij Lal calls it the 'dispersive power of a grating' (PDF p. 465, Eq. 18.56). That name differs from the 'dispersive power' of a prism material (the quantity (μ_violet − μ_red)/(μ − 1)), so this lesson says 'dispersion' for the grating.

## Derivation (Brij Lal PDF p. 465)

1. Start with the grating equation for the n-th order, d sin θ = n λ.
2. Differentiate both sides, keeping d and n fixed (bridge B2):

$$ d cos θ dθ = n dλ   so   dθ/dλ = n/(d cos θ) = n N′/cos θ $$

where N′ = 1/d is the number of lines per unit length.
3. If the spectrum is received on a screen at the focal plane of a lens of focal length f, the distance between two lines is dx = f dθ, so the **linear dispersion** is

$$ dx/dλ = f n/(d cos θ) = f n N′/cos θ   (Eq. 18.57) $$

## What it says

- Dispersion is proportional to the order n: the second-order spectrum is spread twice as widely as the first at nearly the same angle (strictly, divided by cos θ).
- It is proportional to N′, the number of lines **per unit length**, not to the total N: a finely ruled grating spreads the colours more.
- It is smallest at θ = 0 (cos θ = 1) and grows as the angle increases. If θ is small, cos θ is about 1 and dθ ∝ dλ: equal wavelength steps give equal angle steps, so the spectrum is evenly spread, called a **normal spectrum** (PDF pp. 465 and 466).

## Numbers (recomputed)

For d = 1693.3 nm and λ = 600 nm: n = 1: dθ/dλ = 6.32 × 10⁻⁴ rad/nm (0.0362° per nm); n = 2 (θ = 45.13°): 1.67 × 10⁻³ rad/nm (0.0959° per nm), which is 2.65 times the first-order value (the factor 2 from n and 1.33 from cos θ). With f = 1.5 m in the first order the linear dispersion is 0.947 mm per nm.

For d = 1.0 µm at 589 nm, first order: dθ/dλ = 1.237 × 10⁻³ rad/nm; the 0.6 nm separation of the sodium D lines then gives 7.4 × 10⁻⁴ rad (module 9).

## Dispersion is not resolution

Two lines separated by Δθ can still merge if each line has a half-width bigger than about Δθ. High dispersion requires small d; sharp lines require large N (module 4). Whether two lines are seen apart depends on both: the ratio of the dispersion to the line width gives the resolving power (module 6).

## Grating versus prism, the first three differences (PDF p. 465)

A grating gives many orders, a prism only one spectrum. The grating angle is tied directly to λ by d sin θ = n λ with no dependence on the material, so λ can be measured from angles and d alone. In a grating violet is deviated less than red, in a prism more. The full comparison with eight points is in masterNotes.

*Dimension check:* n/(d cos θ) has dimension 1/length, giving radians per unit wavelength ✓. *Limit:* d → ∞ (no ruling) gives zero dispersion.

### 6. Core derivation 4: the resolving power of a grating, R = nN

*Objective:* Derive the resolving power nN with the Rayleigh criterion in two ways, solve the sodium-doublet numerical and state what R does and does not depend on.

## The criterion (Brij Lal PDF pp. 483 to 484)

Lord Rayleigh proposed that two nearby lines are just resolved when the central maximum of one coincides with the first minimum of the other. The resolving power is defined as R = λ/dλ, where dλ is the smallest wavelength difference just resolved at wavelength λ (PDF p. 491).

## Derivation 1: by the extra path difference (the book's method, Eqs. 19.24 to 19.27)

1. The n-th maximum of λ: d sin θₙ = n λ.
2. The n-th maximum of λ + dλ, at angle θₙ + dθ: d sin(θₙ + dθ) = n(λ + dλ).
3. The first zero after the n-th maximum of λ lies where the path difference per slit has grown by λ/N: d sin(θₙ + dθ) = n λ + λ/N.
4. Equate the right-hand sides of steps 2 and 3: n(λ + dλ) = n λ + λ/N, so n dλ = λ/N.

$$ R = λ/dλ = n N   (Eq. 19.27) $$

## Derivation 2: dispersion divided by width

The two lines are just resolved when their angular separation equals the half-width: (dθ/dλ) dλ = δθ. Using modules 4 and 5: n dλ/(d cos θ) = λ/(N d cos θ), so dλ = λ/(n N) and R = n N, in agreement. The cos θ and d cancel.

## What R depends on

- R = nN: proportional to the order and to the **total number of lines illuminated**. It is independent of the line spacing d and of the material. Writing N = W/d (W the ruled width), R = n W/d. Therefore a coarse grating of large width can have the same R as a fine grating of small width. But R = nN = W(sin θ + sin i)/λ ≤ 2W/λ: the ruled width W, not d, caps R (standard; verify).
- Dispersion depends on d (module 5), resolution on N: a fine grating of few lines spreads light widely but gives broad lines.
- In the second order R is doubled for the same N; the book notes that the lines are then twice as far apart (PDF p. 492).

## The doublet numerical: sodium D lines (2018, original working)

N = 3000 lines over 3 mm, so d = 3 mm/3000 = 1.000 µm. λ₁ = 5890 Å, λ₂ = 5896 Å (mean 5893 Å, dλ = 6 Å).

1. Resolving power needed: λ/dλ = 5893/6 = 982.2, about 982.
2. Resolving power available in the first order: R = nN = 1 × 3000 = 3000.
3. 3000 is larger than 982, so the lines are resolved (a factor 3.05 to spare).
4. Cross-check by angles: separation Δθ = dλ/(d cos θ) = 6e-10/(1.0e-6 × 0.8081) = 7.42 × 10⁻⁴ rad, and the half-width δθ = 2.43 × 10⁻⁴ rad; the ratio 3.05 equals 3000/982 ✓.
5. Minimum number of lines for this doublet: N = 982 in the first order (491 in the second).

The same problem with 2500 lines over 5 mm (Brij Lal Example 19.6, PDF p. 495) gives 982 needed against 2500 available.

## How good is the Rayleigh criterion for a grating?

Near a principal maximum the intensity is (sin Nβ/(N sin β))². Two equal lines at the Rayleigh separation have a dip midway of 1 − 8/π² = 18.9 per cent of the peak (recomputed numerically for N = 3000: sum of the two profiles at the midpoint is 0.8106). The book says about 20 per cent (PDF p. 484), which agrees.

*Dimension check:* R is dimensionless ✓. *Limit:* N = 2 gives R = 2n, so a double slit cannot separate close lines; N → ∞ gives R → ∞.

### 7. Core: oblique incidence, the highest order, missing orders and overlapping orders

*Objective:* Write the grating equation for oblique incidence with a stated sign convention, find the highest order, and decide which orders are absent or overlap.

## Oblique incidence: sign convention

Take the grating in the plane z = 0, x across the slits, light travelling toward +z. Angles are measured from the normal (the z-axis), positive toward +x: i for the incident beam, θ for the diffracted beam. Between neighbouring slits the incident wave arrives with extra path d sin i and the diffracted wave from the second slit is shorter in path by d sin θ, so reinforcement requires

$$ d (sin θ − sin i) = n λ ,   n = 0, ±1, ±2, ...   (same-side convention) $$

The zero order is the undeviated beam at θ = i. Brij Lal gives the two cases separately: path difference d (sin θ + sin i) for a beam diffracted on one side of the normal and d (sin θ − sin i) for the other (Eqs. 18.46 to 18.48, PDF pp. 461 to 462). The signed form above contains both, with the sign of θ carrying the side.

## Highest order

Since |sin θ| ≤ 1, with the same-side convention the largest order on the side toward +x is n = d(1 − sin i)/λ, and the largest on the other side is |n| = d(1 + sin i)/λ.

**Brij Lal Example 18.5 (PDF p. 475, recomputed).** 6000 lines per cm, λ = 546 nm, i = 30°. d = 1667 nm. Far side: d(1 + 0.5)/λ = 4.58, so orders up to 4; near side: d(1 − 0.5)/λ = 1.53, so order 1. Beams exist at θ = −54.1°, −28.9°, −8.9°, +9.9°, +30° (n = 0), +55.9°. The highest order is **4**. The book answers 3: it puts θₙ = i (the minimum-deviation condition) and gets n = 2 d sin i/λ = 3.05. That is the largest order that can be observed *at minimum deviation* (the deviation θ + i of the book's form is least when θ = i, giving 2 d sin(D/2) = n λ, Eq. 18.50; for n = 3, D = 58.9°), not the highest order overall (checked; recorded as a source slip).

## Missing orders

If a slit of width a sends zero light in a direction where a principal maximum would be, that order is absent. The envelope zeros satisfy a sin θ = p λ and the maxima d sin θ = n λ; if both hold at one angle, dividing gives (Eq. 18.53)

$$ n = p d / a ,   p = 1, 2, 3, ... $$

Cases (tested by listing orders 1 to 12): d/a = 2 (a = b): orders 2, 4, 6, ... absent. d/a = 3/2 (a = 2b): orders 3, 6, 9, ... absent. d/a = 5/2: orders 5, 10, ... absent (orders 2 and 4 present). See PH-055 module 6 for the double-slit rule.

## Overlapping orders

Lines of different wavelengths coincide when n₁λ₁ = n₂λ₂. The book's example (PDF p. 463): 7000 Å in order 3, 5250 Å in order 4 and 4200 Å in order 5 all give d sin θ = 21,000 Å. For white light (400 to 700 nm) the first order (d sin θ from 400 to 700) and the second (800 to 1400) do not overlap, so the book finds no overlap in the visible, true for orders 1 and 2 only. The third order spans 1200 to 2100 and overlaps the second: third-order 400 to 467 nm falls on second-order 600 to 700 nm (recomputed). The range free of overlap in order n is λ to λ + λ/n (first order from 400 nm: up to 800 nm). With the book's visible range to 720 nm, third-order 400 to 480 nm meets second-order 600 to 720 nm. Filters absorb the unwanted lines (PDF p. 463).

### 8. Master: using a grating - measuring wavelengths, accuracy, and grating versus prism

*Objective:* Describe the spectrometer measurement of a wavelength with a grating, estimate its accuracy, and compare gratings with prisms and with the other resolving instruments.

## Measuring a wavelength (Brij Lal PDF pp. 463 to 465)

A spectrometer is adjusted for parallel light (Schuster's method). The telescope is set in line with the collimator, then turned through 90°. The grating, mounted vertically on the table, is turned until the slit image is seen reflected from the grating surface in the telescope; at this setting the light meets the grating at 45°. The table is then turned a further 45° so that the grating surface is normal to the incident beam. The telescope is turned to the first-order image on each side of the centre and the angle between the two settings is read; half of it is θ₁ (the second order gives θ₂). Then

$$ λ = d sin θ₁   and   λ = d sin θ₂ / 2 $$

with d the reciprocal of the lines per cm (for 15,000 lines per inch, d = 2.54 cm/15,000), and the mean of the two values is taken. For λ = 6000 Å the book finds θ₁ = 20°45′ and θ₂ = 45°7′.

## Why this is accurate

The book contrasts it with interference methods that need small distances (PDF p. 465): here only angles are measured. The error in λ from an angle error δθ is δλ/λ = cot θ δθ (differentiate λ = d sin θ / n). For δθ = 1 arcminute = 2.9 × 10⁻⁴ rad: at θ = 20.75° the error is 7.7 × 10⁻⁴ (0.077 per cent); at 45.13° it is 2.9 × 10⁻⁴ (0.029 per cent). The higher order and larger angle give the better value (recomputed).

## Grating versus prism

The book lists eight points (PDF pp. 465 to 467); the comparison table is in masterNotes. The decisive ones: many orders versus one spectrum; wavelength read directly from d and θ without the material's refractive index; violet less deviated than red (prism: more); a grating spectrum is spread evenly (normal), while a prism's is stretched at the violet end and compressed at the red end because the refractive index changes more rapidly at the violet end; resolving power nN (grating) against t dμ/dλ (prism, t the base length), so a grating resolves far more; and the weaker lines of a grating because most light goes into the central order (so intensity is lower than with a prism).

## Other resolving instruments, side by side

| Instrument | Resolving power | What sets it |
|---|---|---|
| Plane grating | n N | order and total lines |
| Fabry-Perot (PH-053) | m × finesse | gap and reflectivity |
| Prism | t dμ/dλ | base length and dispersion of glass |
| Telescope (PH-057) | D/(1.22 λ) (for angle) | aperture diameter |

The first three resolve wavelengths (R = λ/dλ, dimensionless). The telescope resolves directions: its resolving power is the reciprocal of the minimum angle, in rad⁻¹.

## Reflection and concave gratings

A reflection grating has the lines ruled on a silvered surface (PDF p. 458). A **concave reflection grating** focuses by itself and so needs no lenses; this removes the chromatic aberration of the lenses (PDF p. 467). Its theory is built on the Rowland circle. That theory and the echelon grating (very high resolving power) are in the extract but are not taught here (completeness table).

## Blazing

A weakness noted in the book is that most light goes into the central order, so the spectral orders are faint. Making a grating concentrate light into one order is called blazing; the blazing theory is not in the extract and is not taught here (standard knowledge, verify).

### Notes: Definitions

- Plane transmission grating: a plate with N equal parallel slits of width a separated by opaque spacings b; grating element d = a + b = 1 / (lines per unit length).
- Order n: the integer in d sin(theta) = n lambda; n = 0 is the undeviated central image.
- Principal maximum: a sharp bright line at d sin(theta) = n lambda with intensity N^2 times that of one slit.
- Secondary maxima and minima: N - 1 zeros and N - 2 weak maxima between adjacent principal maxima.
- Angular dispersion: d(theta)/d(lambda) = n / (d cos theta); linear dispersion on a screen at distance f is f times that.
- Resolving power: R = lambda / d(lambda) for a just-resolved pair by Rayleigh's criterion; for a grating R = n N.
- Normal spectrum: a spectrum in which the angle is proportional to the wavelength (small angles).

### Notes: Equations to know

- I = I0 (sin alpha / alpha)^2 (sin N beta / sin beta)^2, alpha = pi a sin(theta) / lambda, beta = pi d sin(theta) / lambda.
- Principal maxima: d sin(theta) = n lambda (n = 0, +-1, ...), intensity N^2 I0 (sin alpha / alpha)^2; N - 1 zeros and N - 2 secondary maxima between.
- Half-width of a principal maximum: d(theta) = lambda / (N d cos theta_n) = tan(theta_n) / (n N).
- Angular dispersion n / (d cos theta); linear dispersion f n / (d cos theta).
- Resolving power R = lambda / d(lambda) = n N; minimum N for a doublet N = lambda / (n d(lambda)).
- Oblique incidence (signed, same-side): d (sin theta - sin i) = n lambda; book form d (sin theta + sin i) = n lambda; minimum deviation 2 d sin(D/2) = n lambda.
- Highest order: n < d / lambda (normal); n < d (1 + sin i) / lambda on the far side (oblique).
- Missing orders: n = p d / a (p = 1, 2, ...). Overlap: n1 lambda1 = n2 lambda2.
- Telescope (circular aperture): theta_min = 1.22 lambda / D; resolving power 1 / theta_min (PH-057).

### Notes: Derivation skeletons (for revision)

- N-slit intensity: slit j phase 2 j beta; sum of geometric series gives sin(N beta)/sin(beta) times the single-slit amplitude; square; principal maxima at beta = n pi with value N^2.
- Half-width: d sin(theta + dtheta) = n lambda + lambda/N; d cos(theta) dtheta = lambda/N.

- Resolving power: n(lambda + dlambda) = n lambda + lambda/N gives n dlambda = lambda/N, R = n N; or dispersion times dlambda equals half-width.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Dispersion (angle per wavelength, depends on d) versus resolving power (lambda over dlambda, depends on N and n).
- Lines per cm (sets d, sets dispersion) versus total lines N (sets resolving power).
- Grating element d versus slit width a: d fixes line positions, a fixes the envelope and missing orders.
- Sharp principal maxima (N slits) versus broad fringes (2 slits): the half-width is 1/N of the order spacing.
- Resolving power of a grating (dimensionless, wavelengths) versus of a telescope (reciprocal angle, directions).
- Angular separation of two lines versus the half-width of each: the pair is resolved when the first is at least the second.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- Orders with n >= d / lambda do not exist; for d < lambda there is only the central beam.
- Missing orders: any order n = p d / a falls on an envelope zero; if n exceeds d / lambda it is irrelevant.
- Overlapping orders: spectra of orders 2 and 3 overlap in the visible; filters or a prism separate them.
- Rayleigh's criterion is a convention; modern detectors can resolve a little closer, and noise can prevent resolving at the limit.
- Near grazing exit (sin(theta) close to 1) the lines broaden (cos(theta) small) and the order is faint.
- The N-slit derivation assumes equal slits, plane waves and the far field; a finite beam width limits the effective N.

    - **task:** Derive the intensity distribution of a plane transmission grating of N slits, find the principal maxima and count the secondary minima and maxima. Practice target about 15 minutes for 10 to 15 marks (not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: N identical slits of width a and separation d = a + b, plane wave at normal incidence, far field with a lens; angles from the normal; alpha = pi a sin(theta)/lambda and beta = pi d sin(theta)/lambda.
      - Each slit sends the single-slit amplitude A0 sin(alpha)/alpha in the direction theta (single-slit result of PH-054).
      - Slit j is displaced by j d, so its wavelet is advanced in phase by 2 j beta relative to slit 0; the contributions are A0 (sin alpha / alpha) e^(i 2 j beta), j = 0, ..., N - 1.
      - Sum the geometric series: sum of e^(i 2 j beta) = e^(i (N - 1) beta) sin(N beta) / sin(beta); the intensity is the square of the magnitude: I = I0 (sin alpha/alpha)^2 (sin N beta / sin beta)^2.
      - Principal maxima: beta = n pi gives sin(N beta)/sin(beta) = +-N, so I = N^2 I0 (sin alpha/alpha)^2, with d sin(theta) = n lambda.
      - Zeros at N beta = k pi (k not a multiple of N): N - 1 zeros and N - 2 secondary maxima between adjacent principal maxima. Check N = 2: 4 cos^2 beta.
    - **checks:**
      - N = 2 gives the double slit 4 cos^2 beta.
      - Limit beta tending to 0: the ratio tends to N.
      - Numerical check by direct integration over N = 5 slits agreed to 0.2 per cent.
    - **traps:**
      - Using N instead of N - 1 for the number of minima.
      - Leaving out the envelope factor.

    - **task:** Derive the angular half-width of a principal maximum and the angular dispersion of a grating, and compare their dependence on N, n and d. Practice target about 12 minutes for 10 marks.
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: normal incidence, n-th principal maximum at d sin(theta_n) = n lambda; the first zero beside it occurs for an extra path difference of lambda / N per slit.
      - Write d sin(theta_n + dtheta) = n lambda + lambda / N and expand for small dtheta: d sin(theta_n) + d cos(theta_n) dtheta = n lambda + lambda / N.
      - Subtract d sin(theta_n) = n lambda: d cos(theta_n) dtheta = lambda / N, so half-width dtheta = lambda / (N d cos theta_n) = tan(theta_n)/(n N).
      - For dispersion differentiate d sin(theta) = n lambda with d and n fixed: d cos(theta) dtheta = n dlambda, so dtheta/dlambda = n / (d cos theta).
      - Compare: the half-width goes as 1/N and (tan theta)/n; the dispersion goes as n and 1/d. The half-width grows with order; the dispersion grows faster.

      - Claiming the half-width falls with order.
      - Using total N in the dispersion.
  - - **id:** PH-056-D3
    - **task:** Obtain the resolving power of a plane transmission grating by the Rayleigh criterion, and show that it is independent of the line spacing. Practice target about 12 minutes for 10 marks.
    - **steps:**
      - Rayleigh: lambda and lambda + dlambda are just resolved when the n-th maximum of lambda + dlambda falls on the first zero beside the n-th maximum of lambda.
      - n-th maximum of lambda + dlambda: d sin(theta + dtheta) = n (lambda + dlambda). First zero of lambda: d sin(theta + dtheta) = n lambda + lambda / N.
      - Equate: n lambda + n dlambda = n lambda + lambda / N, so n dlambda = lambda / N.
      - R = lambda / dlambda = n N.

      - Using lines per cm for N.
      - Quoting the telescope formula.

  - **a:** Add N phasors (geometric series): I = I₀(sin α/α)²(sin Nβ/sin β)². Maxima N²× one slit at β = nπ; between: N−1 minima, N−2 secondary maxima.
- - **id:** PH-056-C3
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** step
  - **exam:** - mains
  - **q:** Half-width of a principal maximum: the step, the result, and how it changes with order?
  - **a:** Next zero where path grows by λ/N: d cos θ dθ = λ/N, so dθ = λ/(N d cos θ). Grows with order (book wrong).
- - **id:** PH-056-C4
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** step
  - **exam:** - mains
  - **q:** Angular dispersion of a grating: the step, the result, and which grating quantity sets it?
  - **a:** Differentiate d sin θ = nλ: dθ/dλ = n/(d cos θ). Set by the order and line spacing d, not the total lines.
- - **id:** PH-056-C5
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** step
  - **exam:**
    - mains
    - prelims
  - **q:** Resolving power of a grating: the Rayleigh step and the result?
  - **a:** λ+dλ's n-th maximum sits on λ's next zero: n(λ+dλ) = nλ + λ/N, so R = λ/dλ = nN.
- - **id:** PH-056-C6
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** number
  - **exam:**
    - mains
    - prelims
  - **q:** Sodium D lines (5890, 5896 Å), 3000 lines over 3 mm (2018): R needed, R available in first order, verdict?
  - **a:** Needed λ/dλ = 5893/6 ≈ 982; available nN = 3000; resolved. Minimum N is 982 (first order), 491 (second).
- - **id:** PH-056-C7
  - **tier:** 1

  - **q:** Oblique incidence at angle i: the book's grating equation and the highest order (Example 18.5 trap)?
  - **a:** d(sin θ + sin i) = nλ; sin θ ≤ 1 gives n below d(1 + sin i)/λ, not the minimum-deviation limit 2d sin i/λ.

- - **id:** X-GRATINGDISPERSION-GRATINGRP
  - **a:**
    - **topicId:** PH-056
    - **text:** Angular dispersion n/(d cos θ): angle per unit wavelength; set by lines per unit length (d).
