## GS-214: Electoral reforms and campaign finance

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Institutions, law and politics

*Goal:* Explain Electoral reforms and campaign finance and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Reform through law, courts and the Commission

NCERT stresses that no electoral system is flawless, so democracies keep refining theirs. Proposals from the Election Commission, parties and scholars include moving to proportional representation, reserving a third of seats for women, state funding of elections, barring candidates facing criminal cases, banning caste and religious appeals and a law to regulate parties; none commands consensus. Several reforms came without fresh legislation. The Supreme Court obliged candidates to file affidavits on assets and pending criminal cases, a duty now also written into section 33A, while section 125A punishes false or concealed affidavit details. NCERT cautions that nobody verifies these declarations. The Election Commission made parties hold organisational elections and file tax returns. NCERT also counts alert citizens and watchdog groups as levers beyond the law.

### Campaign finance rules in statute

The RPA 1951 regulates money at two levels. For parties, section 29B (as printed in the older edition cited) allows voluntary contributions from people and companies other than Government companies but forbids foreign sources, and section 29C makes the treasurer report each contribution above ₹20,000 to the Election Commission before the income-tax return is due, on pain of losing tax relief. For candidates, sections 77–78 require a separate expense account within a prescribed ceiling, lodged with the district election officer within 30 days of the result; unjustified failure can bring three-year disqualification under section 10A. Company donations fall under section 182 of the Companies Act, 2013, which, as the Supreme Court recounts, barred Government companies and new firms, demanded a board resolution and capped giving at 7.5% of average net profits.

### Electoral bonds and the 2024 judgment

The Finance Act, 2017 rewired this framework. It added a proviso so that donations through electoral bonds escaped the section 29C report, deleted the corporate cap in section 182, let companies publish only a total instead of party-wise figures, and spared parties from recording bond donors under the Income-tax Act. In Association for Democratic Reforms v Union of India, decided on 15 February 2024, the Supreme Court held the Electoral Bond Scheme, the 29C proviso, amended section 182(3) and amended section 13A(b) unconstitutional under Article 19(1)(a), and the removal of the corporate cap arbitrary under Article 14. It halted bond issuance and directed SBI to hand purchase and encashment details to the Election Commission by 6 March 2024 for publication by 13 March. Justice Sanjiv Khanna agreed through separate reasoning.

### Worked example

Mains frame (money power): problem (NCERT: money and muscle power) → existing law (RPA s.29B source limits, s.29C reports above ₹20,000, ss.77–78 expense accounts, s.10A disqualification, s.125A false affidavits; Companies Act s.182) → 2017 changes and the ADR (2024) ruling on voters' right to information (Art 19(1)(a)) and the corporate cap (Art 14) → judicial/ECI measures (affidavits, organisational elections, tax returns) → pending ideas (state funding, party regulation) → balance transparency with donor concerns → conclusion.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which RPA section requires parties to report donations, and above what amount?
  - **a:** Section 29C; contributions above ₹20,000 in a financial year.
- - **q:** What did the Supreme Court mandate for candidates?
  - **a:** Affidavits disclosing property and pending criminal cases.
- - **q:** Penalty for failing to lodge an election-expense account without good reason?
  - **a:** Disqualification for three years by the Election Commission under section 10A.
- - **q:** Name two NCERT-listed reform proposals.
  - **a:** PR system; state funding of elections (also one-third women, bar on criminal candidates).
- - **q:** Which provisions did ADR v Union of India (2024) strike down under Article 19(1)(a)?
  - **a:** The Electoral Bond Scheme, the proviso to RPA s.29C(1), amended Companies Act s.182(3) and amended Income-tax Act s.13A(b).
- - **q:** What cap on company donations did section 182 contain before 2017?
  - **a:** 7.5% of average net profits of the three preceding financial years.

### Common trap

Affidavit disclosure of assets and criminal cases is not a ban on candidates with criminal records; NCERT notes there is no system to verify the information and the effect on criminalisation is uncertain. Separately, do not describe electoral bonds as a current funding channel: the Supreme Court held the scheme unconstitutional on 15 February 2024 and ordered issuance to stop.
