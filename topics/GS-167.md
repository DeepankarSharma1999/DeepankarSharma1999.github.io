### Bridge: Types of majority with numeric examples

## Three counting bases

Every vote in a House can be counted against three different bases, and confusing them is the commonest error in this topic.

- **Members present and voting**: those physically present who cast a vote for or against. Members who are present but abstain, or who are absent, are not counted.
- **Total membership**: the full number of seats in the House, including vacant seats and absent members. Laxmikanth says it means the total number of members comprising the House irrespective of vacancies or absentees.
- **Effective strength**: the members actually holding seats at that moment (total membership minus vacancies). This is not the same as total membership; some constitutional provisions use this base [verify the label in Laxmikanth's Parliament chapter, which is not in today's extract].

## Three tests built on those bases

1. **Simple majority** (ordinary law): more than half of the members **present and voting**. NCERT's example: if 247 members vote, 124 votes are enough, since half of 247 is 123.5.
2. **Majority of the total membership**: more than half of the **total number of seats**, whoever is present. In a House of 545, that is 273 (half is 272.5).
3. **Two-thirds of present and voting**: at least two-thirds of those who actually vote. If 300 vote, two-thirds is exactly 200; if 400 vote, 266.67, so 267 votes are needed because votes are whole numbers.

A **special majority for an amendment** needs tests 2 and 3 together. Worked numbers come in module 4. For now, remember that 'more than half' means rounding **up** to the next whole number, and 'two-thirds' means at least two-thirds, also rounded up.

### Bridge: What ratification means, and constituent versus legislative power

## Ratification

To **ratify** is to give formal approval to something already passed elsewhere. When the Constitution says an amendment must be ratified by the State legislatures, it means that after both Houses of Parliament pass the Bill, a sufficient number of State legislatures must each pass a resolution approving it before the Bill goes to the President. The States do not draft or change the Bill; they only approve it or fail to approve it. Think of a housing society committee passing a rule that also needs the approval of half the member flats before it takes effect.

## Two kinds of law-making power

**Legislative power** is the power to make ordinary laws (about taxes, schools, crimes) within the limits set by the Constitution. **Constituent power** is the power to make or change the Constitution itself, the rulebook that creates and limits all the other powers. NCERT's key point is that the Constitution is placed above ordinary law. Article 368 uses the phrase 'constituent power' for Parliament's amending role.

## A one-line way to keep them apart

A law that breaks the Constitution can be struck down by a court. An amendment changes the thing against which laws are tested. This is why the question 'is an amendment a law?' became the centre of a long fight between Parliament and the Supreme Court (module 6). When you see 'Article 13' in this lesson, read it as the Article that protects Fundamental Rights from ordinary laws.

### 2. The anatomy of Article 368 and constituent power

*Objective:* Describe what Article 368 says, how its clauses developed, and why constituent power differs from legislative power.

## What Article 368 says

Article 368, in Part XX, deals with Parliament's power to amend the Constitution and its procedure. NCERT prints the key words: Parliament may, in exercise of its **constituent power**, amend by way of **addition, variation or repeal** any provision of the Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down in the Article. All three verbs matter: PYQ 2024 Q66 tests exactly the three modes of changing a provision.

Laxmikanth adds the limit that matters most: Parliament cannot amend provisions that form the **basic structure**, as the Supreme Court ruled in Kesavananda Bharati (1973).

## How the clauses grew [standard knowledge; verify clause numbering against the official text]

The Article as it stands has several clauses, built up over time:

- **Clause (1)** states the power in so many words: Parliament may amend by addition, variation or repeal. This was inserted by the 24th Amendment (1971); the Article's heading was also widened to speak of the power as well as the procedure.
- **Clause (2)** lays down the procedure: initiation by a Bill in either House, the special majority, the proviso listing the federal provisions that also need ratification by the States, and the President's assent. After the 24th Amendment the President 'shall give' assent.
- **Clause (3)** says Article 13 does not apply to an amendment made under Article 368 (the 24th Amendment).
- **Clauses (4) and (5)** were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976): no amendment could be questioned in any court, and there was no limitation on Parliament's constituent power. The Supreme Court held these invalid in Minerva Mills (1980) because they removed judicial review and enlarged a limited power into an unlimited one. After a judgment, the printed text may still show the clauses with a note that they were struck down; check the official text.

## Constituent power versus legislative power

NCERT says the Constitution was deliberately placed above ordinary law. **Legislative power** makes ordinary laws within the Constitution. **Constituent power** changes the Constitution itself. In a country with a written Constitution the two are different in kind, which is why an amendment passed by special majority is not just 'a bigger law'. Laxmikanth's note on Shankari Prasad (module 6) shows the argument: if an amendment were an ordinary law, Article 13 would make it void for breaching a Fundamental Right; if it is an exercise of constituent power, it stands on a different footing.

## Where Parliament's power comes from

Before the 24th Amendment, the question whether Article 368 contained the power or only laid down the procedure was open (module 6). The 24th Amendment settled the text: Article 368 now both gives the power and sets the procedure. The only other body that has any share is the State legislatures, and only through ratification. No outside body, such as a constitutional convention, and no referendum is involved (NCERT, Laxmikanth).

### 3. The procedure stage by stage, and where it differs from an ordinary Bill

*Objective:* Walk through each stage with the exact conditions, and contrast each with the ordinary legislative process.

## Stage 1: introduction

An amendment can be initiated only by a Bill in either House of Parliament, and not in the State legislatures. A minister or a private member may introduce it, and the **prior permission of the President is not needed**. Contrast [standard knowledge]: certain ordinary Bills, such as Money Bills and Bills on State boundaries, do need a recommendation; the amendment Bill does not. Laxmikanth notes one exception to State inaction: a State legislature may pass a resolution asking Parliament to create or abolish its legislative council, but Parliament may ignore it.

## Stage 2: the special majority, in each House

The Bill must be passed in each House by a majority of the total membership of that House and by two-thirds of the members present and voting. Laxmikanth quotes Subhash Kashyap that, strictly, the special majority is required at the third reading, but the rules of the Houses extend the requirement to all effective stages as an abundance of caution. Vacancies and absentees do not reduce 'total membership'.

## Stage 3: separate passage, no joint sitting

Each House passes the Bill separately. If the Houses disagree, **there is no joint sitting**. In the ordinary legislative process a joint sitting is available for a deadlock on an ordinary Bill, which makes the amendment route stricter on this point: the Rajya Sabha has an effective veto, equal in power to the Lok Sabha (PYQ 2020 Q2).

## Stage 4: ratification, only for federal provisions

If the Bill seeks to change a federal provision listed in Article 368(2) (module 5), legislatures of **half the States** must approve it, by a **simple majority** of members present and voting in each State legislature. The Constitution sets no time limit and is silent on whether a State can withdraw approval after giving it (Laxmikanth's criticisms). Once half have approved, silence from the rest does not matter. Under the Article's text the ratification must be done before the Bill is presented to the President [standard knowledge; PYQ 2025 Q58 uses this wording].

## Stage 5: assent, compulsory

The Bill goes to the President for assent. Since the 24th Amendment (1971) the President must assent: he can neither withhold assent nor return the Bill for reconsideration. Contrast: for an ordinary Bill the President may withhold assent or return it, other than a Money Bill, which cannot be returned [standard knowledge on ordinary Bills; see GS-179].

## Stage 6: effect

After assent the Bill becomes a Constitutional Amendment Act and the Constitution stands amended as the Act says.

## Two things that are not in the procedure

There is no role for a referendum, and no outside body. NCERT says only elected representatives take the final decision; sovereignty of elected representatives is the basis of the procedure, and the Rajya Sabha's equal say adds a second check.

### 4. Voting arithmetic with worked examples

*Objective:* Compute the minimum votes needed for an amendment Bill given total membership and the number voting.

## The two tests together

Let T be the total membership and V the members present and voting. The Bill needs Y votes in favour where both hold:

$$ Test 1: Y > T / 2 (at least floor(T/2) + 1)     Test 2: Y ≥ (2/3) V (rounded up) $$

The minimum is the larger of the two numbers. Check each case by hand.

## Worked examples for T = 545 (NCERT's House)

| V (voting) | Test 1 | Test 2 | Votes needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 300 | 273 | 200 | 273 |
| 400 | 273 | 267 (two-thirds of 400 is 266.67) | 273 |
| 450 | 273 | 300 | 300 |
| 480 | 273 | 320 | 320 |
| 545 | 273 | 364 (363.33 rounded up) | 364 |

Each row repeats the method: find 273, find two-thirds of V, take the larger. NCERT's own illustration is the first two rows: with 300 present, 273 is needed; and its question about 400 voting has the answer 273, because two-thirds of 400 is only 267. The crossover is near V = 409: at V = 409 two-thirds is 272.67, rounded up to 273, equal to Test 1; at V = 410 it is 273.33, rounded up to 274. Up to 409 voting, 273 is the binding figure; from 410 up, two-thirds binds.

## Worked examples for T = 250 (the Constitution's maximum for the Rajya Sabha)

Test 1 gives 126 (half is 125). With 150 voting, two-thirds is 100, so 126 are needed. With 200 voting, two-thirds is 133.33, rounded up to 134. With all 250 voting, two-thirds is 166.67, rounded up to 167. The crossover is at V = 190, where two-thirds (126.67) first exceeds 126.

## Failing cases to study

- **T = 545; 250 for, 100 against, 195 absent.** V = 350, Test 2 needs 234 and Test 1 needs 273. 250 passes Test 2 (71 per cent of those voting) but fails Test 1, so the Bill fails.
- **T = 250; 120 for, 60 against, 70 absent.** V = 180, two-thirds is exactly 120, so Test 2 passes; Test 1 needs 126, so the Bill fails.
- **Abstentions:** T = 250, 250 present, 30 abstain, V = 220, two-thirds is 146.67, so 147 votes are needed. Abstainers lower V, which helps Test 2; but Test 1 still needs 126.

## Vacancies and current strength

Total membership counts vacant seats. With 545 seats and 20 vacancies, Test 1 is still 273, not the majority of the 525 who sit (which is 263). If you meant the majority of the 525, you would be counting effective strength, a different test used elsewhere (verify). For present-day numbers: Lok Sabha elected strength 543 gives Test 1 = 272; Rajya Sabha numbers vary with nominations and vacancies (verify before using real numbers). For an ordinary Bill in NCERT's example, 247 present and voting need only 124.

### 5. The three modes and a classifier you can use on any provision

*Objective:* Classify a given provision into the correct mode and explain the logic behind each list.

## The three modes

Laxmikanth and NCERT agree that the Constitution can be changed in three ways: by simple majority of Parliament (outside Article 368), by special majority, and by special majority with ratification by half the States. Module 3 of GS-172 gave the lists; here is the classifier.

## Mode A: simple majority, outside Article 368

Some provisions can be changed by Parliament by an ordinary-law procedure (simple majority), and the Constitution itself says such a change 'shall not be deemed to be an amendment ... for the purposes of article 368'. The clearest textual cases are Articles 4(2) (new States and boundaries under Articles 2 and 3), 169(3) (legislative councils), 239A(2), Fifth Schedule paragraph 7(2) and Sixth Schedule paragraph 21(2). NCERT's examples are Articles 2 and 3 on new States and boundaries. Laxmikanth's list includes: admission, formation and boundaries of States; legislative councils; Second Schedule emoluments; quorum; salaries and allowances of MPs; rules of procedure; privileges; use of English; number of puisne judges of the Supreme Court; extra jurisdiction of the Supreme Court; official language; citizenship; elections to Parliament and State legislatures; delimitation; Union territories; and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.

## Mode B: special majority only

Laxmikanth places here Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and all provisions not covered by the other two modes. It is the **residual** category, so a provision not named in A or C is a B case.

## Mode C: special majority plus half the States

These are the provisions related to the federal structure: election of the President and its manner; the extent of the executive power of the Union and the States; the Supreme Court and High Courts; distribution of legislative powers between the Union and the States; the GST Council (added by the 101st Amendment); any of the Seventh Schedule lists; representation of States in Parliament; and Article 368 itself. In Article 368(2) these appear as Articles 54, 55, 73, 162, 241 and 279A, Chapter IV of Part V (the Union judiciary), Chapter V of Part VI (High Courts in the States), Chapter I of Part XI (legislative relations), the Seventh Schedule lists, representation of States in Parliament, and Article 368 itself [Article numbers are standard knowledge; verify against the official text].

## The classifier (ask in this order)

1. Is it on the named simple-majority list? These are the provisions where the Constitution itself says the change 'shall not be deemed to be an amendment ... for the purposes of article 368' (Articles 4(2), 169(3), 239A(2), Fifth Schedule paragraph 7(2), Sixth Schedule paragraph 21(2)), plus Laxmikanth's other listed items. If so, Mode A.
2. Is it one of the eight federal items (Articles 54, 55, 73, 162, 241, 279A, the court chapters, Part XI Chapter I, the Seventh Schedule lists, representation of States in Parliament, Article 368)? If so, Mode C.
3. Otherwise, Mode B.

**Warning: the phrase 'by law' alone never decides the mode.** Article 241 says 'Parliament may by law constitute a High Court for a Union territory', yet Article 241 is on the Article 368(2) ratification list, so changing that Article is Mode C. The provisos to Articles 73 and 162 also mention a 'law made by Parliament', and those Articles are federal too (PYQ 2025 Q58). 'By law' lets Parliament legislate under the provision; it says nothing about how the provision's own text is amended.

## Logic and tricky cases

Mode A covers matters Parliament could not sensibly bind itself on. Mode C protects the Union-State balance and the top institutions. PYQ 2025 Q58 shows the trap: the Union List (Seventh Schedule) and the extent of a State's executive power are federal; the Governor's conditions of office are not named in the list. NCERT's exercise asks you to classify 'Provision regarding Election Commission'. Using Laxmikanth's lists it is on neither the federal list nor the simple-majority list, so it falls in the residual special-majority category (Mode B). Elections to the legislatures are a Mode A matter, but the provisions on the Election Commission are not. Remember that technical content does not change the mode: where the provision sits decides it.

### 6. Power or procedure? Article 13, the 24th and 42nd Amendments, and the judgments

*Objective:* Explain how the Court and Parliament changed the meaning of Article 368 between 1951 and 1981.

## The question

Is an amendment under Article 368 'law' within Article 13? Article 13 voids laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights. If an amendment is such a law, Parliament cannot touch the rights; if not, it can. The cases below answer it in turn (the full storyline is in GS-173; here the focus is on what each did to Article 368).

## The sequence (Laxmikanth, chapter 11)

1. **Shankari Prasad (1951)**: the First Amendment was challenged. The Court held the amending power includes power over Fundamental Rights; 'law' in Article 13 means ordinary law and not constitutional amendment acts. Parliament may abridge rights by amendment.
2. **Golak Nath (1967)**: reversed this. The 17th Amendment was challenged; Fundamental Rights had a 'transcendental and immutable' position; an amendment is a law within Article 13 and is void if it violates a right. [Standard knowledge, verify: the majority treated Article 368 as laying down only the procedure, with the power coming from the ordinary law-making Articles.]
3. **24th Amendment (1971)**: Parliament answered by amending Articles 13 and 368. It declared that Parliament may abridge or take away any Fundamental Right under Article 368 and that such an act is not a 'law' under Article 13 (the new Article 13(4) and Article 368(3) [verify clause numbers]). It also made assent compulsory and put the power into the text of Article 368(1), ending the 'only procedure' argument.
4. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**: overruled Golak Nath and upheld the 24th Amendment: Parliament may abridge any Fundamental Right, but cannot alter the **basic structure**.
5. **Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975)**: applied the doctrine to strike down a provision of the 39th Amendment that kept the Prime Minister's and Speaker's election disputes outside the courts.
6. **42nd Amendment (1976)**: Parliament added clauses (4) and (5): no limit on constituent power, and no challenge in any court on any ground.
7. **Minerva Mills (1980)**: invalidated that provision because it removed judicial review, a basic feature; a limited amending power cannot be enlarged into an absolute one. Laxmikanth quotes the passage; it is the line for PYQ 2019 Q12.
8. **Waman Rao (1981)**: the doctrine applies to amendments enacted after 24 April 1973.

## What the 43rd and 44th amendments did to Article 368

The brief for this topic mentions the 43rd and 44th changes. Laxmikanth and NCERT say that after the 1977 election the 43rd and 44th Amendments (1977-78) cancelled most of the changes made by the 38th, 39th and 42nd Amendments, restoring the constitutional balance. The extracts do not say that either one rewrote Article 368 itself. The correction of Article 368 came from the Court in Minerva Mills, not by deletion through an amendment [verify against the official text and amendment history]. Treat 'the 43rd and 44th changed Article 368' as an unsupported claim until verified. One related fact (verify): the 44th Amendment Bill as introduced proposed a referendum for some Article 368 changes, and the Rajya Sabha dropped that proposal.

## The takeaway

Article 368 now contains both a power and a procedure, with an unwritten limit (basic structure) and with judicial review as the guard. NCERT's three effects of Kesavananda: limits the power; allows amendment of all parts within the limit; makes the judiciary the final authority on what violates the basic structure.

### 7. Judicial review, criticism and defence, and amendment versus ordinary law

*Objective:* Distinguish procedural from substantive review of amendments and weigh the criticisms of the procedure.

## Two kinds of limit

**Procedural limits** concern how an amendment is made: special majority, separate voting, ratification where required, assent. **Substantive limits** concern what an amendment contains: the basic structure. The Court can strike down an amendment for breaching either. [Standard knowledge, verify: in Kihoto Hollohon (decided 1992, reported 1993) the Court struck down paragraph 7 of the Tenth Schedule, which barred court review of anti-defection decisions, because it curtailed the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts, matters on the ratification list, without ratification by the States; Laxmikanth lists the case for free and fair elections and democracy.] Its value for you is the principle: an amendment that skips a required step is not valid, even if Parliament passed it by a special majority.

## Amendment versus ordinary law

| Feature | Amendment under Article 368 | Ordinary law |
|---|---|---|
| Power exercised | Constituent power | Legislative power |
| Majority | Total-membership majority plus two-thirds of present and voting | Simple majority of present and voting |
| Joint sitting on disagreement | No | Yes, for an ordinary Bill |
| President | Must assent | May withhold or return (except Money Bills) |
| States | Half must ratify for federal provisions | No role |
| Article 13 | Not a 'law' (Article 13(4), after the 24th Amendment) | A 'law'; void if it violates a Fundamental Right |
| Court's test | Procedure and basic structure | Constitutional provisions |

## Laxmikanth's criticisms of the procedure

No special body such as a Constitutional Convention; States cannot initiate except for councils; Parliament alone changes most of the Constitution, and the States' share is half, compared with three-fourths of the States in the USA; no time limit for States and no rule on withdrawal; no joint sitting for deadlock; similarity to the ordinary legislative process apart from the special majority; and a sketchy procedure that invites court challenge.

## The defence

Laxmikanth concludes the process is simple and has met changing needs, neither so flexible that rulers change it at will nor so rigid that it cannot adapt, and quotes K. C. Wheare that it strikes a good balance and that the variety of procedures is wise but rare. Nehru told the Assembly there is no permanence in a Constitution and that a rigid one stops the nation's growth. Ambedkar said the Assembly did not give the Constitution a seal of finality and provided a facile procedure. NCERT adds that the basic structure doctrine strengthens the balance: rigid for core features, flexible for the rest.

## For the 2025 Mains question

The statement that the Constitution gives amending power to ordinary legislative institutions with a few procedural hurdles invites a two-part answer: procedural limits (special majority, no joint sitting, ratification, assent) and substantive limits (basic structure, Minerva Mills, Waman Rao).

## Prelims traps on majority definitions

- **Majority of the total membership** is based on all seats, including vacancies and absentees (Laxmikanth's definition of 'total membership'). It is not based on members present.
- **Two-thirds of those present and voting** is based on people who vote; abstainers are not counted.
- **Simple majority** is based on present and voting and is used for ordinary law and for Mode A changes.
- **Special majority** for amendments is the combination of the two tests, not either alone.
- **Effective majority** [verify the exact label and examples in Laxmikanth's Parliament chapter, which is not in today's extract]: a majority of the members actually in the House at that time, excluding vacancies. It is a different base from total membership and appears in some removal resolutions. Do not use it for Article 368.
- **Other special majorities exist** for other provisions (for example impeachment of the President needs two-thirds of the total membership of each House, per Laxmikanth's President chapter, which is not in today's extract; verify). Never carry the Article 368 formula to another Article without checking the wording.

## Other traps

- 'The President's prior recommendation is needed': it is not.
- 'The President may return the Bill': not after 1971.
- 'Half of the States must approve every amendment': only federal provisions.
- 'Three-fourths of the States' is the American figure; India needs half.
- 'Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights': it can, subject to basic structure.
- 'State legislatures can initiate amendments': they cannot, except by asking for a council.

### Notes: Key definitions

- Article 368 (Part XX): Parliament's power to amend the Constitution by addition, variation or repeal, and the procedure for doing so.
- Constituent power: the power to make or change the Constitution itself; distinct from ordinary legislative power.
- Simple majority: more than half of the members present and voting; used for ordinary laws and for Mode A changes outside Article 368.
- Special majority (amendment): a majority of the total membership of the House and a majority of two-thirds of the members present and voting.
- Total membership: the total number of members comprising the House, irrespective of vacancies or absentees (Laxmikanth).
- Ratification: approval by resolution of the State legislatures of an amendment already passed by Parliament, before the Bill goes to the President.
- Basic structure: the features of the Constitution that Parliament cannot alter under Article 368 (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
- Amendment: a change by addition, variation or repeal made by the constitutional procedure and passed as a Constitutional Amendment Act.

### Notes: Article 368 procedure in eight steps (Laxmikanth)

- 1. Initiation only by a Bill in either House of Parliament, not in State legislatures.
- 2. Minister or private member may introduce; no prior permission of the President.
- 3. Special majority in each House: majority of total membership and two-thirds of present and voting.
- 4. Each House passes separately; no joint sitting.
- 5. If a federal provision is touched: ratification by legislatures of half the States by simple majority.
- 6. Bill presented to the President for assent.
- 7. President must assent (24th Amendment, 1971); cannot withhold or return.
- 8. Becomes a Constitutional Amendment Act; the Constitution stands amended.

- 2016: 101st Amendment adds the GST Council to the ratification list (Laxmikanth, note 3a).

### Notes: Judgments linked to Article 368

- Shankari Prasad v Union of India (1951): amending power includes Fundamental Rights; 'law' in Article 13 means ordinary law.
- Golak Nath v State of Punjab (1967): amendment is a law under Article 13; Fundamental Rights 'transcendental and immutable'.
- Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973): Golak Nath overruled; 24th Amendment valid; basic structure limits the amending power.
- Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975): basic structure applied to the 39th Amendment's election-dispute provision.
- Minerva Mills v Union of India (1980): limited amending power is itself basic; Parliament cannot enlarge it into an absolute power; judicial review basic.
- Waman Rao v Union of India (1981): basic structure applies to amendments after 24 April 1973.
- Kihoto Hollohon v Zachilhu (decided 1992, reported 1993) [verify]: a provision needing State ratification was held invalid because ratification had not been obtained.

### Notes: Amendments affecting Article 368 itself

- 24th Amendment (1971): amended Articles 13 and 368; Article 13 not applicable; assent obligatory; power placed in Article 368.
- 42nd Amendment (1976): clauses (4) and (5) on no court challenge and no limit; held invalid in Minerva Mills.
- 101st Amendment (2016): Article 279A GST Council added to the federal list requiring ratification.
- 43rd and 44th Amendments: cancelled most changes of the 38th, 39th and 42nd; not shown to have rewritten Article 368 in the extracts.

### Notes: Criticisms of the amendment procedure and replies (Laxmikanth)

- No special body (convention or assembly) -> constituent power in Parliament -> reply: elected representatives decide (NCERT).
- States cannot initiate -> centre-favouring design -> reply: States share by ratifying federal provisions.
- Parliament alone amends most provisions -> ruling-party dominance -> reply: special majority forces opposition consent (NCERT).
- No time limit for States and no rule on withdrawal -> uncertainty -> reply: half suffices; silence irrelevant.
- No joint sitting -> deadlock possible -> reply: protects the Constitution from a bare majority.
- Process similar to ordinary law -> blurs constituent and legislative power -> reply: basic structure and review restore the distinction.
- Sketchy provisions -> litigation -> reply: courts supply the gaps.

### Notes: Concepts commonly confused

- Total membership versus effective strength versus present and voting.
- Special majority for amendments versus the other special majorities elsewhere in the Constitution.
- Simple-majority changes outside Article 368 versus amendments under Article 368.
- Ratification by half the States (federal provisions) versus three-fourths (America).
- Constituent power versus legislative power.
- Amendment of a Fundamental Right (allowed within basic structure) versus ordinary law violating it (void under Article 13).
- Procedure being 'sketchy' versus procedure being absent.
- Basic structure as a fixed list versus an evolving doctrine.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- The State legislatures may initiate one thing only: a resolution asking Parliament to create or abolish a legislative council; Parliament need not act.
- Amendments in Mode A are not 'amendments' for Article 368.
- Governor's conditions of office are not on the ratification list; the extent of a State's executive power is.
- Provisions on the Election Commission fall in the residual special-majority category.
- The basic structure doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973 (Waman Rao).
- Presidential assent is compulsory only for constitutional amendment Bills; ordinary Bills allow withholding or returning.

### Core concepts

- Article 368 gives Parliament the constituent power to amend by addition, variation or repeal, and sets the procedure.
- The special majority combines a majority of total membership with two-thirds of present and voting, in each House separately.
- There are three modes: simple majority outside Article 368, special majority, and special majority plus ratification by half the States.
- The basic structure doctrine limits the amending power; the judiciary decides.

### Practice

**Q.** In which Part and Article is the amending power found?

**A.** Part XX, Article 368.

**Q.** Where can an amendment Bill be introduced?

**A.** Only in either House of Parliament, not in State legislatures.

**Q.** What majority is needed for ratification by a State legislature?

**A.** A simple majority of members present and voting, in at least half of the States.

**Q.** Is a joint sitting available on an amendment Bill?

**A.** No.

**Q.** Why does the Constitution require both a majority of the total membership and two-thirds of those voting?

**A.** The first prevents passage by a thin attendance; the second ensures broad support among those who vote; together they force consensus beyond the ruling party (NCERT).

**Q.** Why are the Seventh Schedule lists on the ratification list?

**A.** They distribute powers between the Union and the States, so changing them without the States would let the Centre rewrite the federal balance.

**Q.** Why was the question of whether an amendment is a 'law' so important?

**A.** If it is a law under Article 13, it is void when it abridges a Fundamental Right, which limits Parliament; if not, Parliament can amend rights.

**Q.** What did Minerva Mills add to Kesavananda?

**A.** It held that the limited amending power itself is basic and cannot be turned into an absolute power, and struck down the 42nd Amendment's clauses.

**Q.** T = 545; 380 vote, 270 in favour. Does the Bill pass?

**A.** Test 1 needs 273 and Test 2 needs two-thirds of 380, which is 253.33, rounded up to 254; the larger is 273, and 270 is less, so it fails.
