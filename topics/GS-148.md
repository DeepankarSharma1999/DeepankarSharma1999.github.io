## GS-148: Demographic dividend and ageing

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Demographic dividend and ageing and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### How the dividend arises

The dependency ratio compares people too young or too old to work with those of working age, generally taken as 15 to 64. When fertility falls, the child share shrinks and a large working-age cohort supports relatively few dependants, which can become a source of growth; this is the demographic dividend. India's under-15 share dropped from 42% in 1971 to 29% in 2011, and NCERT notes that in 2020 the average Indian was 29, much younger than people in China, the United States, Western Europe or Japan. The age-based dependency ratio was 79 in 1970 and 64 by 2005, with a projected low of 48 in 2025 and a rise to 50 by 2050 as the old-age share grows. The window is therefore temporary.

### Why the dividend is not automatic

NCERT insists that age structure alone guarantees nothing. If new labour-force entrants are poorly educated their productivity stays low, and if they cannot find work they become dependants rather than earners. The real measure should be non-workers relative to workers, which depends on unemployment and underemployment, explaining why some countries exploit the window and others miss it. The chapter cites evidence of weak job creation: employment growth in the 15–30 age group, around 2.4 per cent a year in 1987–94 for rural and urban men, dropped in 1994–2004 to 0.7 per cent (rural men) and 0.3 per cent (urban men). It warns that market forces alone may not deliver the policies needed. Regional variation also matters: Kerala's age structure resembles developed countries while Uttar Pradesh remains young.

### The ageing side of the transition

MoSPI's Elderly in India 2021, using the Technical Group's 2011–2036 projections, estimates about 138 million people aged 60 and above in 2021, 71 million of them women. The elderly share climbed from 5.6% in 1961 to 10.1% in 2021 and may reach 13.1% by 2031; elderly numbers grew about 36% in each of the last two decades against slower general population growth. Ageing is uneven: Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Himachal Pradesh lead, while Bihar and Uttar Pradesh have the smallest elderly shares. The old-age dependency ratio, measured as persons 60+ per 100 aged 15–59, is projected to rise to 20.1 by 2031, reaching 34.3 in Kerala. With about 70% of the aged dependent on others in 2017–18, ageing raises questions of pensions, health care and women's security.
