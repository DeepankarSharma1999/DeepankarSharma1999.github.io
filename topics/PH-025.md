## PH-025: Bernoulli equation

Physics Paper I · Physics · Continuous media

### Bridge: Words you need: streamline, steady, incompressible, ideal fluid

**Streamline.** A curve whose tangent at every point is the direction of the fluid's velocity there (NCERT 9.3). In steady flow a fluid particle follows a streamline and two streamlines never cross, because a crossing would give a particle two possible velocities at one point.

**Steady flow.** The velocity at any given point does not change with time. It may differ from point to point; steady does not mean uniform. Steady flow occurs at low speed; beyond a critical speed it becomes turbulent (NCERT 9.3).

**Tube of flow.** A bundle of streamlines forming a pipe-like surface; fluid cannot cross its sides, so it enters and leaves only through the ends (Mathur 15.5).

**Incompressible.** The density ρ stays constant. Liquids are nearly incompressible; gases are not, but moving slowly compared with the speed of sound they behave nearly incompressibly (standard; verify).

**Ideal (non-viscous) fluid.** An incompressible fluid with no internal friction. Real fluids have viscosity (PH-023 module 3), so every use of Bernoulli's equation is an approximation. Write ‘ideal’ in your assumptions every time.

### Bridge: Pressure, units and energy per unit volume

**Pressure.** P = F/A, force normal to a surface per unit area; it is a scalar. Unit: pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻². Atmospheric pressure is about 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa (NCERT summary).

**Pressure is energy per volume.** 1 N m⁻² = 1 N m / m³ = 1 J m⁻³. So P, ½ρv² and ρgh can be added: each is an energy per cubic metre.

**Sizes to feel.** Water at 1 m/s has ½ρv² = 500 Pa; at 10 m/s, 50 000 Pa. A water column of 1 m gives ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 1 = 9800 Pa. Atmospheric pressure equals about 10.3 m of water (1.01 × 10⁵/9800 = 10.34 m).

**Gauge and absolute.** Gauge pressure is P − Pa (NCERT points to ponder). In Bernoulli's equation a constant added to P on both sides cancels, so gauge pressures work if both points use the same reference.

**Hydrostatic reminder.** In a fluid at rest P = Pa + ρgh_depth (NCERT 9.2), so pressure rises with depth.

### Bridge: The minimum calculus: derivative along a path and v dv

**Derivative as a rate.** dv/dt is how fast the speed changes with time (an acceleration); dv/ds is how fast it changes with distance along the path.

**Chain rule.** If a particle moves along s with speed v = ds/dt, then a = dv/dt = (dv/ds)(ds/dt) = v dv/ds. Also d(½v²)/ds = ½ · 2v · dv/ds = v dv/ds, which is why ½v² appears.

**Partial and total derivatives.** In a flowing fluid the speed depends on both position s and time t, v(s, t). The partial ∂v/∂t holds position fixed; ∂v/∂s holds time fixed. A particle's acceleration is ∂v/∂t + v ∂v/∂s. In steady flow ∂v/∂t = 0, so only ordinary derivatives of s remain.

**Zero derivative means constant.** If d(F)/ds = 0 for all s, then F is the same at every point of the streamline. Bernoulli's equation is exactly this statement for F = P + ½ρv² + ρgh.

**Integrating v dv.** ∫v dv = ½v² (add a constant). In Torricelli's draining problem, ∫dh/√h = 2√h.

P is pressure, ρ density, v speed, h height, g gravitational acceleration. It is the work-energy theorem written for a flowing fluid (NCERT 9.4; Mathur 15.8). ### 2. Foundation: the equation of continuity

*Objective:* Derive A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ from conservation of mass and use it for speeds in pipes and tanks.

**Recap.** PH-023 module 2 defines a streamline (a curve whose tangent is the velocity direction; two streamlines never cross in steady flow). Here is the short derivation that Bernoulli's proof reuses (NCERT 9.3; Mathur 15.6).

**Setup.** Take a tube of flow: a bundle of streamlines whose sides act as walls, because fluid cannot cross a streamline (Mathur 15.5). It has cross-section A₁ at section 1 and A₂ at section 2, thin enough that the speed is uniform over each section: v₁ and v₂, with densities ρ₁ and ρ₂.

**Steps.**

1. In a short time Δt the fluid crossing section 1 forms a slab of length v₁Δt, volume A₁v₁Δt and mass ρ₁A₁v₁Δt. At section 2 the mass is ρ₂A₂v₂Δt.
2. Steady flow means the mass between sections 1 and 2 does not change with time. Nothing crosses the sides and there is no source or sink, so mass in equals mass out:

$$ ρ₁ A₁ v₁ = ρ₂ A₂ v₂ $$

3. For an incompressible fluid ρ₁ = ρ₂, so

$$ A₁ v₁ = A₂ v₂ = Q $$

Q is the volume flow rate, unit m³ s⁻¹.

**Meaning.** Where the tube narrows the fluid speeds up (v ∝ 1/A). Closely spaced streamlines mean high speed.

**Worked number.** A pipe of diameter 10 cm narrows to 5 cm. The area ratio is (10/5)² = 4, so the speed rises fourfold. If v₁ = 1.0 m/s then v₂ = 4.0 m/s, and Q = 1.0 × π(0.05)² = 7.85 × 10⁻³ m³ s⁻¹ (check: 4.0 × π(0.025)² gives the same). Trap: using the diameter ratio 2 instead of the area ratio 4.

**Checks.** Units: m² × m s⁻¹ = m³ s⁻¹ ✓. Limit: A₂ = A₁ gives v₂ = v₁ ✓. For a gas, ρ changes with pressure, so only ρAv is constant and Bernoulli's incompressible form needs care (module 8).

**Large tank, small hole.** With A_tank ≫ a_hole the surface speed is v_surface = (a/A)v_hole ≈ 0. That is why the surface speed is set to zero in Torricelli's law (module 6).

### 3. Core: Bernoulli's equation by work and energy (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant for a tube of flow with every step, sign convention, assumption and check.

**Aim.** Derive the equation by the work-energy theorem (NCERT 9.4, Fig. 9.9). The step about the unchanged middle part is added here for completeness.

**Coordinates and signs.** Distance along the flow is positive in the flow direction. Height h is measured upward from any datum. A force pushing the fluid forward does positive work; a force pushing it backward does negative work.

**Assumptions.** Steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow in a tube of flow; sections 1 and 2 thin enough for uniform v and P across each.

**Steps.**

1. Take the fluid that at time t lies between sections 1 and 2. At t + Δt its rear face has moved v₁Δt from section 1 and its front face v₂Δt from section 2 (see the diagram).
2. The middle part is common to both positions. The flow is steady, so every point of it has the same speed and height as before and its energy is unchanged. The change of energy of the whole fluid is therefore the energy of slab 2 minus the energy of slab 1.
3. By continuity both slabs have the volume ΔV = A₁v₁Δt = A₂v₂Δt and the mass Δm = ρΔV.
4. Work by pressure. Behind section 1 the fluid pushes forward with P₁A₁ through v₁Δt: W₁ = +P₁ΔV. Ahead of section 2 the fluid pushes backward with P₂A₂ through v₂Δt: W₂ = −P₂ΔV. Forces on the tube sides are perpendicular to the motion and do no work. With no viscosity there is no friction work.
5. Potential energy gained: ΔU = Δm g (h₂ − h₁) = ρΔV g (h₂ − h₁). Kinetic energy gained: ΔK = ½Δm (v₂² − v₁²) = ½ρΔV (v₂² − v₁²).
6. Work-energy theorem, work by pressure = ΔK + ΔU:

$$ (P₁ − P₂) ΔV = ½ρΔV (v₂² − v₁²) + ρΔV g (h₂ − h₁) $$

7. Divide by ΔV (not zero):

$$ P₁ − P₂ = ½ρ (v₂² − v₁²) + ρ g (h₂ − h₁) $$

8. Put the 1-terms on the left and the 2-terms on the right:

$$ P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂ $$

Points 1 and 2 are any two points on the streamline, so P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along it.

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* ρv² = ML⁻³ · L²T⁻² = ML⁻¹T⁻², the dimension of pressure; ρgh and P are pressures; each term is also energy per volume, J m⁻³ ✓. *Static limit (v = 0):* P₁ − P₂ = ρg(h₂ − h₁), the hydrostatic result (NCERT). *Horizontal uniform pipe:* v₁ = v₂ and h₁ = h₂ give P₁ = P₂ for an ideal fluid; a real viscous pipe shows a pressure drop (module 8). *Number:* horizontal water speeding up from 2 to 6 m/s needs P₁ − P₂ = ½ × 1000 × (36 − 4) = 16 000 Pa.

**Timed target (practice target, not an official allotment).** 10 marks about 12 minutes: assumptions, diagram, steps 1 to 8, one check.

### 4. Core: Bernoulli's equation from Euler's equation (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive the same result from Newton's second law on a thin fluid element and see why the unsteady case differs.

**Why a second derivation.** The work-energy proof uses a finite slab. Euler's equation applies Newton's second law to a tiny element on a streamline, and it is Mathur's route (15.8 and 15.9). It also shows what breaks when the flow is unsteady. Calculus needed is in bridge module B3.

**Assumptions.** Non-viscous fluid; ρ constant; element thin so its sides are parallel to the streamline. The coordinate s runs along the streamline in the flow direction, z is height, t is time.

**Steps.**

1. The element has cross-section dA, length ds and mass dm = ρ dA ds.
2. Pressure along s: the rear face gets p dA forward, the front face gets (p + (∂p/∂s) ds) dA backward. Net force: −(∂p/∂s) ds dA. Pressures on the sides are perpendicular to s and add nothing along s.
3. Gravity: the weight dm g is downward; its component along s is −dm g (∂z/∂s). (Mathur writes dm g cos θ with cos θ = −∂h/∂l.)
4. Acceleration of a particle along s: a = ∂v/∂t + v ∂v/∂s. The speed changes because time passes and because the particle moves to a place where the speed differs.
5. Newton's second law, then divide by dA ds:

$$ ρ (∂v/∂t + v ∂v/∂s) = − ∂p/∂s − ρ g ∂z/∂s $$

This is Euler's equation along a streamline (Mathur 15.9), valid for steady and unsteady flow.
6. Steady flow: ∂v/∂t = 0 and the partial derivatives become ordinary: ρ v dv/ds + dp/ds + ρ g dz/ds = 0.
7. For constant ρ, v dv/ds = d(½v²)/ds, so

$$ d/ds ( ½ρv² + p + ρgz ) = 0 $$

8. A quantity whose derivative along s is zero is constant: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant ✓ (same as module 3).

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* each term of Euler's equation is force per volume, kg m⁻² s⁻² = N m⁻³ ✓. *Static limit:* v = 0 gives dp/dz = −ρg ✓. *Unsteady flow:* the ∂v/∂t term survives and, integrated along s, adds a non-zero term, so the Bernoulli sum is not constant (standard; verify). *Compressible gas:* step 7 becomes ∫dp/ρ + ½v² + gz = constant (Mathur 15.8); for adiabatic efflux Mathur quotes a form with γ/(γ − 1) times p/ρ (PDF p. 802; the OCR is rough, verify).

**Comparison.** Work-energy: easy to picture, needs the slab and continuity. Euler: local, and it explains the cause: a pressure gradient accelerates the fluid (module 5).

**Timed target.** 10 marks about 12 minutes (practice target, not an official allotment).

### 5. Core: meaning, forms, limiting cases and the Venturi graph

*Objective:* Write the equation per unit volume, mass and weight, name its terms correctly, explain cause and effect, and sketch pressure against position.

**Three equivalent forms** (along one streamline).

| Per | Equation | Unit | Terms called |
|---|---|---|---|
| unit volume | P + ½ρv² + ρgh = C | Pa = J m⁻³ | pressure, kinetic, potential energy per volume |
| unit mass | P/ρ + ½v² + gh = C | J kg⁻¹ | pressure energy P/ρ (Mathur 15.7) |
| unit weight | P/(ρg) + v²/(2g) + h = C | m | pressure head, velocity head, elevation head (Mathur 15.8) |

**Pressure energy.** Mathur 15.7 pushes a small mass dm into a tube against pressure P through length dl: work P·A·dl, and dm = ρA dl, so the work per unit mass is P/ρ. It is flow work done by the surrounding fluid, not energy stored by compression.

**Naming trap.** Mathur calls P + ρgh the static pressure and ½ρv² the dynamic pressure. Many modern texts call P alone the static pressure and P + ½ρv² the total (stagnation) pressure. State which meaning you use.

**Cause and effect.** Do not write “fast fluid causes low pressure”. Entering a constriction, the fluid must accelerate, and acceleration needs a net forward force; it exists because the pressure behind is higher than ahead (NCERT 9.4). Euler's equation (module 4, step 5) says the same: acceleration ∝ −∂p/∂s.

**Limiting cases.** v = 0 everywhere: P + ρgh constant (hydrostatics). Horizontal flow: P + ½ρv² constant, so high speed means low pressure. Open jet in air: P = Pa. Gauge versus absolute pressure (NCERT Exercise 9.12): replacing P by P − Pa on both sides subtracts the same constant, so either works if used consistently on both sides.

**Graph: pressure against position in a Venturi** (ideal flow; Q = 50 L/s, inlet diameter 20 cm, P₁ = 150 kPa, diagram below). Pressure falls through the converging part, is lowest at the throat (131.0 kPa) and rises through the diverging part back to 150 kPa in the ideal case. The speed curve is the mirror image. In real flow viscosity leaves the exit pressure below the inlet value, a permanent loss (module 8). The viscous pipe of PH-024 is different: its pressure falls along a straight line.

**Numbers behind the graph.** Throat speed = Q/A = 0.05/(π × 0.05²) = 6.366 m/s; inlet speed = 0.05/(π × 0.1²) = 1.592 m/s. P_throat = 150 000 − ½ × 1000 × (6.366² − 1.592²) = 131 003 Pa ≈ 131.0 kPa. Dimension check: ρv² is a pressure ✓.

### 6. Core: Torricelli's law, jet range, draining time and the siphon

*Objective:* Derive the efflux speed, the range 2√(y(H−y)), the draining time and the siphon's crest pressure, with their limits.

**Torricelli's law.** Open tank, liquid depth h above a small side hole (NCERT 9.4.1; Mathur 15.11). Take a streamline from the free surface (point 2) to the jet at the hole (point 1); datum at the hole. Both points are at atmospheric pressure Pa. Continuity gives v_surface = (a/A)v₁ ≈ 0 when a ≪ A (module 2).

1. P₂ + ½ρ·0 + ρgh = P₁ + ½ρv₁² + 0, with P₁ = P₂ = Pa.
2. So ρgh = ½ρv₁², and

$$ v₁ = √(2gh) $$

the speed of a body falling freely through h (energy conservation, not coincidence). Exactly, v₁ = √(2gh/(1 − (a/A)²)); for a/A = 0.1 the correction is 0.5%. If the surface carries gauge pressure ΔP, v₁ = √(2ΔP/ρ + 2gh) (NCERT 9.14). Numbers: h = 1.0 m gives 4.43 m/s; with ΔP = 50 kPa of water, v₁ = √(2 × 50 + 2 × 9.8) = 10.94 m/s.

**Range of the jet.** The tank is full to height H and the hole is a depth y below the surface, so it is H − y above the ground. The jet leaves horizontally.

1. Fall time: H − y = ½gt², so t = √(2(H − y)/g).
2. Range x = v₁t = √(2gy) × √(2(H − y)/g):

$$ x = 2 √( y (H − y) ) $$

3. Maximum: d/dy [y(H − y)] = H − 2y = 0, so y = H/2 and x_max = H. Holes at depths y and H − y give equal ranges. For H = 2.0 m: y = 0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 1.50, 1.75 m give x = 1.32, 1.73, 2.00, 1.73, 1.32 m.

Real orifices (Mathur): the velocity coefficient is 0.95 to 0.99, and the jet contracts to a vena contracta (coefficient of contraction about 0.624 for a circular sharp-edged orifice; Mathur quotes π/(π + 2) = 0.611 as the theoretical value, verify), so with C_D = C_v C_c the discharge is about 0.62 a√(2gh) (Mathur).

**Draining time.** A dh/dt = −a√(2gh). Separate: dh/√h = −(a/A)√(2g) dt. Integrate from H to 0: 2√H = (a/A)√(2g) t, so

$$ t = (A/a) √(2H/g) $$

For A/a = 100 and H = 2 m: t = 63.9 s (ideal). Contrast PH-024: viscous Q ∝ h gives an exponential fall, while Torricelli's Q ∝ √h empties the tank in finite time.

**Siphon.** Tube full of liquid and of uniform bore, crest h above the reservoir surface, outlet d below it.

1. Surface to outlet (both at Pa): ½ρv² = ρgd, so v = √(2gd), independent of h.
2. Surface to crest: Pa = P_c + ½ρv² + ρgh, with ½ρv² = ρgd:

$$ P_c = Pa − ρ g (h + d) $$

Example: h = 1.5 m, d = 0.5 m, v = 3.13 m/s and P_c = 101 325 − 1000 × 9.8 × 2.0 = 81 725 Pa (gauge −19.6 kPa).

**Limits.** (i) d > 0, or nothing flows. (ii) P_c must stay above the vapour pressure, so h + d < (Pa − P_v)/(ρg) ≈ 10.1 m for water (taking P_v ≈ 2.3 kPa at 20 °C; verify), less in practice because dissolved air comes out earlier. (iii) The tube must be primed and free of air.

### 7. Core: Venturi meter, Pitot tube and atomiser

*Objective:* Derive the Venturi flow rate (the 2022 question), the Pitot speed and the atomiser threshold, and avoid the density traps.

**Venturi meter (derivation).** Horizontal pipe: wide section 1 (area A₁), throat 2 (area A₂), ideal liquid of density ρ (Mathur 15.10.1).

1. Continuity: v₂ = (A₁/A₂) v₁.
2. Bernoulli with equal heights: P₁ − P₂ = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) = ½ρv₁² [(A₁/A₂)² − 1].
3. Solve: v₁ = A₂ √( 2(P₁ − P₂) / (ρ (A₁² − A₂²)) ).
4. Flow rate:

$$ Q = A₁ v₁ = A₁ A₂ √( 2(P₁ − P₂) / (ρ (A₁² − A₂²)) ) $$

5. Pressure difference from the gauge: two vertical tubes of the same liquid give P₁ − P₂ = ρgh, so Q = A₁A₂ √(2gh/(A₁² − A₂²)) (Mathur's form). A U-tube holding a denser liquid ρ_m below the pipe liquid gives P₁ − P₂ = (ρ_m − ρ)gh, because equal pipe-liquid columns above the two levels cancel.

*Checks.* Dimensions: ΔP/(ρA²) has units (kg m⁻¹ s⁻²)/(kg m⁻³ · m⁴) = m⁻² s⁻², its root is m⁻¹ s⁻¹, and A₁A₂ (m⁴) times that gives m³ s⁻¹ ✓. Limit A₂ ≪ A₁: Q → A₂√(2ΔP/ρ), the efflux form ✓. Number: d₁ = 20 cm, d₂ = 10 cm, mercury-water U-tube h = 4.0 cm: ΔP = (13 600 − 1000)(9.8)(0.040) = 4939 Pa; A₁ = 0.03142 m², A₂ = 0.007854 m²; Q = 0.02549 m³ s⁻¹ = 25.5 L/s (v₁ = 0.812 m/s, v₂ = 3.25 m/s). Real meters: Mathur gives a coefficient of discharge about 0.97. Mathur's remark that the throat pressure must not become “negative (less than atmospheric)” needs care: a sub-atmospheric throat is normal; the real limit is the lower pressure at which dissolved air comes out and, lower still, the vapour pressure (cavitation), the mechanism Mathur himself names.

**Pitot tube (derivation).** Point B at the nose facing the flow is a stagnation point (v = 0). Point A on the same streamline upstream has the free-stream pressure P_A and speed v. Horizontal: P_A + ½ρv² = P_B, so v = √(2(P_B − P_A)/ρ). A manometer liquid of density ρ_l reads P_B − P_A = ρ_l g h. Water in water: v = √(2gh); h = 5 cm gives 0.990 m/s and, in a 20 cm diameter main, Q = 0.990 × π × 0.1² = 31.1 L/s (Mathur 15.3 recomputed). Air with a water manometer, h = 2.0 cm: ΔP = 196 Pa, v = √(2 × 196/1.2) = 18.1 m/s. Trap: ρ in the formula belongs to the flowing fluid and ρ_l to the manometer liquid; using water's density for air gives 0.63 m/s. For gases near high speed (Mach number above about 0.3) incompressibility fails (standard; verify).

**Atomiser (Mathur 15.10.5).** Air of density ρ_a blows across the top of a vertical tube whose bottom dips into a liquid of density ρ_l, the top being H above the liquid level. Textbook idealisation (Mathur 15.10.5): treat the air as coming from still air at Pa and speeding up to v over the tube top, so P_top = Pa − ½ρ_a v² (height change negligible in air). A real free jet leaves its nozzle at about atmospheric pressure, and the suction also depends on jet geometry and entrainment, so this threshold is an estimate (standard; verify). The liquid rises when Pa − P_top > ρ_l g H:

$$ v_min = √( 2 ρ_l g H / ρ_a ) $$

Water, H = 10 cm, ρ_a = 1.2 kg m⁻³: v_min = 40.4 m/s. The Bunsen burner and carburettor use the same idea (Mathur 15.10.4, 15.10.6).

### 8. Master: when Bernoulli fails, energy loss, the PH-024 contrast and aerofoil lift

*Objective:* Say which assumption each failure breaks, contrast Bernoulli with Poiseuille, include a loss term, and explain lift without the equal-transit fallacy.

**Assumption by assumption.**

| Broken | What happens | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Viscosity | P + ½ρv² + ρgh falls along the flow; P₂ is lower than the equation predicts | NCERT 9.4, point 6 |
| Turbulent or unsteady flow | velocity and pressure fluctuate, no constant | NCERT 9.4, Exercise 9.11 |
| Compressibility | density changes; use the ∫dp/ρ form | Mathur 15.8, 15.11 |
| Pump or turbine | energy is added or removed: add a work term (standard; verify) | standard |
| Different streamlines | the constant may differ between streamlines unless the flow is irrotational (standard; verify) | standard |

**Key contrast, PH-025 versus PH-024.**

| | Bernoulli | Poiseuille (PH-024) |
|---|---|---|
| Fluid | ideal, no viscosity | viscous, coefficient η |
| Uniform horizontal tube | no pressure drop | ΔP = 8ηLQ/(πa⁴) |
| Energy | exchanged among P, ½ρv², ρgh | lost as heat |
| Dominant effect | inertia | viscous drag |

The Reynolds number Re = ρvD/η (PH-023 module 7) compares them: low Re in narrow tubes means viscosity rules (Poiseuille); high Re in wide pipes or open flow means Bernoulli works along streamlines outside thin wall layers (standard; verify).

**Energy loss.** With e_L the energy dissipated per unit mass between points 1 and 2 (standard form; verify):

$$ P₁/ρ + ½v₁² + gh₁ = P₂/ρ + ½v₂² + gh₂ + e_L $$

In a uniform horizontal pipe v₁ = v₂ and h₁ = h₂, so e_L = ΔP/ρ. That is the 2019 question: 5/800 = 6.25 × 10⁻³ J kg⁻¹ is heat, not kinetic energy. PH-024 module 5 shows where the two ideas meet: liquid entering a tube from a wide vessel spends part of P as kinetic energy ρQ²/(π²r⁴) before viscosity takes over.

**Lift and the equal-transit fallacy (standard knowledge; verify).** NCERT 9.4.2 explains lift by streamlines crowding above the wing, so the air there is faster and the pressure lower, and Mathur 15.10.3 says the same. Neither says why the speeds differ. A popular story says the air above travels farther and must rejoin the air below at the trailing edge at the same time. That is wrong: nothing forces the two parcels to meet, and in measurements and calculations the upper parcel arrives first. Bernoulli's equation links pressure to speed; it does not predict the speed difference, which depends on the wing's shape and its angle to the flow (NCERT mentions the wing's orientation). A balanced view: the wing turns air downward, and the pressure difference and the momentum change are two descriptions of one force (verify). The spinning ball (NCERT, Magnus effect) follows the same logic. NCERT Example 9.7, recomputed: ΔP = 3.3 × 10⁵ × 9.8/500 = 6.47 kPa, and the upper speed is only about 8% above the lower.

### 9. Exam: the three verified questions solved

*Objective:* Answer the 2019, 2022 and 2023 verified questions with assumptions, diagram, steps and a check, as analysis only.

Practice targets, not official allotments: 10 marks about 12 minutes, 15 marks about 18 minutes. Analysis only; no official key is asserted. Paper structure per the project brief (Q1 and Q5 compulsory, several 10-mark short parts); verify against the paper instructions.

**2019 Q1(b)(ii), 10 marks.** Horizontal uniform pipe; pressure falls by 5 N m⁻² over 1 km; oil ρ = 800 kg m⁻³; find the change of kinetic energy per kg. PH-023 module 8 solved it; the Bernoulli view adds the following.

1. Continuity with uniform A and incompressible oil: v is the same at both points, so the change of ½v² is strictly zero.
2. The only number the data give is ΔP/ρ = 5/800 = 6.25 × 10⁻³ J kg⁻¹. ### Notes: Definitions and symbols

- Streamline: curve whose tangent is the velocity direction; no crossing in steady flow (NCERT 9.3).
- Steady flow: velocity at each point constant in time. Incompressible: ρ constant. Ideal fluid: incompressible and non-viscous.
- Q = Av is the volume flow rate (m³ s⁻¹); mass flow rate is ρAv.
- Pressure energy P (per volume) or P/ρ (per mass); kinetic ½ρv²; potential ρgh.
- Heads: pressure head P/(ρg), velocity head v²/(2g), elevation head h; sum is the total head (Mathur 15.8).
- Stagnation point: where the fluid is brought to rest (nose of a Pitot tube).

### Notes: Key equations

- Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (incompressible); ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂ (general).
- Bernoulli: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline (steady, incompressible, non-viscous).
- Euler along s: ρ(∂v/∂t + v ∂v/∂s) = −∂p/∂s − ρg ∂z/∂s.
- Torricelli: v = √(2gh); with surface gauge pressure ΔP: v = √(2ΔP/ρ + 2gh). Range x = 2√(y(H − y)); draining time t = (A/a)√(2H/g).
- Venturi: Q = A₁A₂√(2ΔP/(ρ(A₁² − A₂²))), ΔP = ρgh or (ρ_m − ρ)gh. Pitot: v = √(2ΔP/ρ). Siphon crest: P_c = Pa − ρg(h + d).
- Atomiser threshold: v_min = √(2ρ_l g H/ρ_a). Loss form: P₁/ρ + ½v₁² + gh₁ = P₂/ρ + ½v₂² + gh₂ + e_L.

### Notes: Work-energy derivation in eight lines

- 1. Follow the fluid between sections 1 and 2 for Δt; slab 1 leaves, slab 2 arrives, the middle part is unchanged (steady).
- 2. Slab volume ΔV = A₁v₁Δt = A₂v₂Δt, mass ρΔV.
- 3. Work by pressure: +P₁ΔV at the rear, −P₂ΔV at the front; side forces do no work; no friction.
- 4. ΔK = ½ρΔV(v₂² − v₁²); ΔU = ρΔV g(h₂ − h₁).
- 5. Work = ΔK + ΔU: (P₁ − P₂)ΔV = ½ρΔV(v₂² − v₁²) + ρΔV g(h₂ − h₁).
- 6. Divide by ΔV.
- 7. Rearrange: P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂.
- 8. Check dimensions (Pa = J m⁻³), static limit and horizontal limit.

### Notes: Euler route in five lines

- 1. Element dA × ds, mass ρ dA ds.
- 2. Forces along s: −(∂p/∂s) dA ds and −ρ g (∂z/∂s) dA ds.
- 3. Acceleration ∂v/∂t + v ∂v/∂s.
- 4. Newton: ρ(∂v/∂t + v ∂v/∂s) = −∂p/∂s − ρg ∂z/∂s.
- 5. Steady, constant ρ: d/ds(½ρv² + p + ρgz) = 0, so the sum is constant.

### Notes: When Bernoulli's equation does not hold

- Viscous flow: the sum falls along the flow (PH-024 pipe; NCERT 9.4 and point 6).
- Turbulent or unsteady flow: no constant (NCERT 9.4; Exercise 9.11).
- Compressible flow at high speed: use the ∫dp/ρ or adiabatic form (Mathur 15.8, 15.11).
- Pump or turbine between the points; points on different streamlines in rotational flow (standard; verify).
- Points not on the same streamline, or a tube not full (siphon, Venturi).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Static pressure: Mathur uses P + ρgh; many texts use P alone. State your meaning.
- Fast flow does not ‘cause’ low pressure; a pressure gradient causes acceleration.
- Pitot: ρ in the formula is the flowing fluid's; the manometer liquid's density gives ΔP.
- Equal transit time is not the reason for lift (standard; verify).
- Pressure drop in a uniform viscous pipe is not a kinetic-energy change.

### Core concepts

- Bernoulli's equation is energy conservation for steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow along a streamline.
- Continuity (conservation of mass) links speed to area and is used with Bernoulli in almost every problem.
- Pressure, kinetic and potential energy per volume trade among themselves; viscosity drains the total as heat.
