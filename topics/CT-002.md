### Bridge: Logic vocabulary: premise, conclusion, inference, assumption

These four words appear in question stems and in every explanation in this lesson. Learn them first.

- **Premise**: a reason or piece of evidence the author gives. 'Complaints fell by half in wards with active committees' is a premise.
- **Conclusion**: the claim the premises are meant to support. 'So decentralisation works where residents take part' is a conclusion. It can come first, last or in the middle, and signal words such as 'therefore', 'thus', 'so' and 'hence' often introduce it.
- **Inference**: a new statement that you, the reader, draw from the passage. It is not written, and it must follow from what is written.
- **Assumption**: an unstated belief the author needs for the premises to support the conclusion.

Picture a bridge. The premises are one bank, the conclusion is the other bank, the assumption is a hidden plank in the middle, and your inference is a step you take along the bridge. If you pull out the plank (deny the assumption), the bridge falls. If you step along it (draw an inference), you must not step off the bridge.

A tiny example: 'The match was cancelled because of rain. So the pitch must have been unplayable.' The premise is rain; the conclusion is that the pitch was unplayable; the assumption is that rain made the pitch unplayable rather than, say, causing a power cut. Note that all four words describe the *role* a sentence plays, not its subject matter: the same sentence can be a premise in one passage and a conclusion in another.

### Bridge: Qualifiers: all, some, most, only, never

A qualifier says how many or how often. A single qualifier can reverse the answer, so read each one slowly.

| Word | Meaning in CSAT reading | What does not follow |
|---|---|---|
| All / every | No exceptions | Nothing about members of other groups |
| Some | At least one, possibly all | Do not conclude 'not all' unless stated |
| Most | More than half | Not 'all', and not 'nearly all' |
| Few | A small number | Not 'none' |
| Only | Nothing else qualifies | 'Only A are B' means every B is an A, not that every A is a B |
| Never / no | Zero cases | Nothing about related groups |
| Often, tends to | Common but with exceptions | Not 'always' |

**All and its reverse.** 'All doctors in the panel are graduates' does not give 'All graduates are in the panel'. The reverse of a statement (the converse) is a new claim and needs its own support.

**Only if.** 'A student gets the award only if her attendance is at least 75 per cent' means attendance is a necessary condition: no attendance of 75 per cent, no award. It does *not* mean 75 per cent attendance guarantees the award.

**Most and group sizes.** 'Most of the people who fail are careless' is a statement about the failing group. It says nothing about most of all people.

When a CSAT option contains one of these words, do not accept or reject it on instinct. Return to the passage and ask: does the passage support exactly this quantity?

### 1. From main idea to inference: what these questions ask

*Objective:* Say in one sentence what an inference question asks, and recognise the five stem families used in the verified papers.

## From main idea to inference

In CT-001 you learned to state what a passage says as a whole. This lesson asks a different question. An **inference** is a statement that the passage does not write down but does support: it is what you can safely say *after* reading, using only what is on the page. An inference question gives four statements and asks which one the passage supports best.

Begin with the smallest possible example.

> All the shops in the lane close on Monday. Ravi's shop is in the lane.

What follows? Ravi's shop closes on Monday. Nobody wrote that sentence, yet it cannot be false while the two given sentences are true. That is an inference in its purest form. What does *not* follow: that Ravi is a good shopkeeper, that Monday is a holiday, or that shops outside the lane stay open. Some of these may be true in real life, but the passage does not make them true.

## What the questions look like

This lesson is built on the 102 official CSAT comprehension questions mapped to this topic from the 2017 to 2026 papers (from 2 questions in 2025 to 16 in 2022; several questions share one passage). The stem wording varies, but it falls into five families:

- **Inference stems**: 'the most rational inference' (2017), 'most logical, rational and critical inference' (2017), 'most logical, scientific and rational inference' (2019), 'the most critical inference' (2021).
- **Implication stems**: 'What does the above passage imply?' (2017), 'most definitively implied' (2019), 'Which one of the following is implied by the passage?' (2022).
- **Corollary and message stems**: 'best reflects the corollary' (2021), 'practical suggestion implied' and 'practical message conveyed' (2024).
- **Author stems**: 'The author suggests that' (2017), 'The passage seems to suggest that' (2017), 'According to the author' (2022).
- **Statement-check stems**: a numbered list of statements with 'Which of the following statements is/are correct?' (or 'not correct') and a code such as '1 and 3 only'. Eleven of the twelve 2026 items use numbered statements with codes; Q7 (Kalagram) is a single-answer 'not correct' item.

## The one rule that governs everything

The passage is a **closed world**. Your knowledge of India, science or economics helps you understand the words; it is never evidence. An option can be true in the real world and still be the wrong answer, because the question is not 'what is true?' but 'what does this passage support?'

Where CT-001 asked for the whole passage in a sentence, an inference can come from one sentence alone. The two skills meet in the tests you will learn in module 2.

### 2. Inference, assumption, conclusion, corollary, implication

*Objective:* Separate five easily confused terms and apply the contradiction test (inference) and the denial test (assumption) consistently with CT-001.

## One passage, five different things

Use this short argument throughout.

> Pollution in the city fell by a fifth during the two weeks when the odd-even scheme was in force. The scheme should therefore be made permanent.

| Term | Where it sits | In the example |
|---|---|---|
| Premise | Stated, in support | Pollution fell by a fifth during the two weeks |
| Conclusion | Stated, the claim | The scheme should be made permanent |
| Assumption | Unstated, *before* the argument | The fall was caused by the scheme, not by (say) unusually windy weather |
| Inference | Unstated, *after* the passage | Pollution during the two weeks was lower than before the scheme began |
| Corollary / implication | Unstated, a consequence | If the scheme is made permanent, the restriction will outlast the two-week trial |

An **implied conclusion** is simply a conclusion the author never writes. If the passage had stopped after the first sentence, a reader asking 'so what?' would arrive at the second sentence. Questions on this topic often leave the conclusion off and ask you to supply it.

## Two tests, and they are different tests

The tests below are the same as in CT-001; keep them apart.

- **Contradiction test (for an inference)**: deny the option. If denying it contradicts the passage itself, the option is an inference. Deny 'pollution during the two weeks was lower than before' and you contradict 'fell by a fifth'. It is an inference.
- **Denial test (for an assumption)**: deny the option. If denying it makes the author's argument collapse, the option is an assumption. Deny 'the fall was caused by the scheme' and the reason for making the scheme permanent vanishes. It is an assumption.

Now see why they are not interchangeable. Deny 'The scheme will be popular': the passage is untouched and the argument stands, so it is neither. Deny 'Odd-even schemes reduce pollution in every city': the passage still stands, so it is not an inference; the argument survives too, so it is not an assumption. It is merely something that *could* be true.

## Must be true versus could be true

Most wrong options are **could-be-true** statements: consistent with the passage, but not forced by it. The right option is **must be true** (or, when the passage is hedged, *probably true*, as module 3 explains). If no option passes the strict contradiction test, choose the option whose denial most strongly conflicts with the passage (probably true). Keep this consistent with CT-001: for an inference, deny the option and see whether you contradict the passage; for an assumption, deny it and see whether the argument collapses. Agreeing with the passage is not enough; the option must be supported by it.

## Implication, corollary and the overlap

In ordinary speech these words blur. In CSAT stems, treat them this way.

- **Inference**: a statement that follows from the passage.
- **Implication**: what the passage suggests or commits the author to; it may include a practical consequence ('practical implication' or 'practical message', as in the 2024 stems). It is a little wider than a bare inference, but it still has to follow from the passage's own reasoning.
- **Corollary**: the strictest kind, a direct and necessary consequence. In the 2021 corollary question, the passage says monetary and fiscal policy cannot work without each other's support; the corollary concerns what that means for the institutions that run them, not new policy ideas.

All three are answered by the same test: choose the option the passage supports most directly and most safely.

### 3. Degrees of strength: certainly true to certainly false

*Objective:* Place any statement on the five-degree scale against a passage and choose the highest degree available.

## Not yes or no, but five degrees

Everyday talk treats 'follows' as yes or no. The examiner is subtler. Against any passage, a statement sits on a scale.

| Degree | Meaning | Usually looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Certainly true | Stated, or forced by the passage; denying it contradicts the passage | A restatement, or a necessary consequence |
| Probably true | Strongly supported but with a small gap | Well supported but not forced (a small gap); hedged words often make a claim certainly true, so judge support, not wording |
| Indeterminate | The passage is silent, or points both ways | A new cause, remedy, forecast or comparison |
| Probably false | The passage makes it unlikely | Needs most of the passage to be wrong |
| Certainly false | The passage contradicts it | Reverses a statement or a number |

## Work one passage through the scale

> Of the 200 students at a coaching centre, 140 passed the screening test last year and 60 failed. The centre reports that every student who passed had attended at least 80 per cent of the classes, and its teachers say that most of the students who failed had irregular attendance.

1. 'Most students at the centre passed.' **Certainly true**: 140 of 200 is 70 per cent.
2. 'No student who attended fewer than 80 per cent of the classes passed.' **Certainly true**: it is the same fact turned around.
3. 'Fewer than half the students passed.' **Certainly false**: 70 per cent passed.
4. 'Attendance has no bearing on the result.' **Probably false**: every passer attended well, and teachers link failure with irregular attendance. It is not proved false, so not 'certainly'.
5. 'Every student who attended at least 80 per cent passed.' **Indeterminate**: this is the *converse*. The passage describes the passers only; some regular attenders may have failed.
6. 'The classes caused the students to pass.' **Indeterminate**: attendance may simply go with motivation.

Statements 2 and 5 look alike and are not. 'Every passer attended well' does not become 'every good attender passed'. Learning to turn a statement around and ask 'is that the same claim?' is worth many marks.

## Choose the highest degree available

- If a certainly-true option exists, it is the answer.
- If none does, the answer is the *probably-true* option: the one strongly supported by the passage though not forced by it. If no option passes the strict contradiction test, choose the option whose denial most strongly conflicts with the passage (probably true).
- Hedged wording ('some', 'may', 'at least') claims little, so it often makes a claim *certainly* true; it does not make a claim probably true by itself. Separate the wording from the degree of support: always ask how far the passage actually supports the claim.
- Indeterminate, probably-false and false options are the usual distractors.

## Words that move a statement up or down

Strong words, which raise the claim: 'all', 'every', 'always', 'never', 'only', 'solely', 'must', 'cannot', 'impossible', 'entirely'. Soft words, which lower it: 'some', 'may', 'can', 'sometimes', 'often', 'tends to', 'at least', 'probably'. Do not reject an option merely because it contains a strong word; check whether the passage actually supports that strength, as CT-001 also taught. A strong word is a prompt to check the passage, not an automatic verdict.

The qualifiers are explained from scratch in bridge module CT-002-B2.

### 4. Stem by stem: what each question demands

*Objective:* State, for each stem family, the exact standard an option must meet, including statement-check questions with codes.

## Matching the stem to the standard

The same passage can be followed by different stems, and the stem sets how strict you must be.

| Stem | What to do |
|---|---|
| 'most logical and rational inference' | Pick the option that survives the contradiction test and adds nothing the passage lacks |
| 'implied', 'implication' | As above, but the option may restate the passage's wider point; stay at the passage's level of generality |
| 'corollary' | The strictest: a direct, necessary consequence; hedged wording is fine, new ideas are not |
| 'practical message / suggestion' | A recommendation is allowed, but only the one the passage's own reasoning points to |
| 'The author suggests / seems to suggest' | Stay with the author's view; do not add your own. 'Seems' signals a mild standard |
| 'emphatically conveyed' (2024) | The passage must *stress* it, not merely allow it |
| 'is/are correct', 'not correct', 'cannot be inferred' | Judge every statement separately, then use the codes |

## Statement-check questions with codes

Many papers give numbered statements and a code such as '1 and 3 only', and eleven of the twelve 2026 items in this set use this format with codes; Q7 (Kalagram) is a single-answer 'not correct' item with four statements. A reliable routine:

1. Judge each numbered statement on its own against the passage. Use the five degrees: only 'certainly true' and 'probably true' count as correct.
2. Mark each statement T (supported), F (not supported) or ? (unsure).
3. Match the code. Begin with the statements you are sure of; each sure statement removes options. A statement you are sure is correct removes every code that omits it; one you are sure is incorrect removes every code that contains it.
4. Resolve any remaining '?' only if the codes still leave a choice.

For a **'not correct'** stem, reverse the question: you are hunting the statements the passage does *not* support, and the passage's silence counts against a statement. For a **'cannot be inferred'** stem, three options are inferences and one is not; test all four.

## A recommendation is not an inference

Suppose a passage reports that a city lost a fifth of its tree cover. 'The city should plant more trees' is a recommendation. It follows only if the passage itself argues for restoring cover. In a plain inference stem, the option beginning 'should' is almost always a leap from description to prescription. In a 'practical suggestion' stem, the same option may be right *if* the passage's logic leads there. In the 2024 'practical message' questions, for example, the options that stay closest to the passage are the ones tied to its own finding (a method that turns carbon dioxide into stone; the data collected by connected home devices) rather than a new programme the passage never mentions.

## Read the stem before the passage

The stem tells you what to look for: a list of facts, a cause, a consequence or the author's attitude. Reading it first costs five seconds and saves a second reading of the passage.

### 5. Trap types in inference options

*Objective:* Name eight trap types, recognise each in an official wording, and use the PASS check before choosing.

## Eight ways an option goes wrong

1. **Beyond the passage (outside knowledge).** The option may be true in life but nothing in the passage supports it. In the 2017 labour-and-youth passage, an option about India being 'poised' for double-digit growth 'very soon' is a prediction the passage never makes.
2. **Too strong.** 'Always', 'only', 'cannot', 'no hope at all' turn a modest claim into an absolute. In the 2017 resource-conflict passage the option 'always politically motivated' fails because the passage says conflicts are *cloaked* in political or identity language.
3. **Converse and necessary-versus-helpful.** From 'benefits depend on the quality of the working-age population' (2022) it does not follow that any one thing is an 'essential condition' for growth. A factor that helps is not a factor that is required.
4. **The recommendation leap.** A descriptive passage followed by 'should' (2019 invasive-species passage: 'should be used to increase the biodiversity').
5. **Cause from sequence or correlation.** The passage gives two things happening together or in order and the option says one *caused* the other, or was 'solely responsible' (2024 food-waste statement).
6. **Scope shift.** The option stretches from a part to the whole, from a group to everyone, or from 'most of those who fail' to 'most people'. The 2022 money-and-opinions passage and the 2026 Rai Sahib passage both test this.
7. **True but not supported.** The option is a correct fact that the passage never states. Ask: which sentence supports it? If you cannot point to one, it fails.
8. **Misread numbers or direction.** A figure is quoted but the comparison reversed ('14 million jobs while the labour force grew by 15 million' does not mean jobs grew faster).

## The PASS check

Before you commit to an option, run four checks. Each begins with a letter of the word PASS.

- **P**assage-only: can I point to the sentence that supports it?
- **A**bsolutes: does it contain 'all', 'only', 'always', 'never' and does the passage back that strength?
- **S**cope: does it talk about the same people, place and time as the passage?
- **S**trength: is it claiming more than 'probably' when the passage says only 'may' or 'often'?

## Which trap is most dangerous?

For beginners the most expensive is number 7, **true but unsupported**, because it feels like knowledge rather than error. The second is number 4, the recommendation leap, because passages on environment, health and governance make 'should' options sound sensible. A third is the half-true option which begins with something stated and ends with something added: always read the end of the option, not only the start.

Keep a one-line error log entry for each wrong answer: 'PYQ, trap number, the word that fooled me'. Patterns appear within a week.

### 6. The step method for inference questions

*Objective:* Apply a seven-step method to a new passage, using prediction and the contradiction test, without reading the options too early.

## Seven steps

1. **Read the stem.** Identify the family (module 1) and the standard (module 4).
2. **Read the passage once, marking claims and qualifiers.** Circle 'some', 'most', 'all', 'only', 'may', 'often'. Number any figures.
3. **Say in your own words what is certainly true.** Two or three short statements.
4. **Predict.** Before looking at the options, write what a safe inference would sound like.
5. **Test each option** with the contradiction test: deny it; does the passage break? Mark each C (certainly), P (probably), I (indeterminate) or F (false).
6. **Eliminate by trap type** (module 5). Write the trap number beside each eliminated option.
7. **Choose the highest degree.** If two options tie, choose the one that claims less. Record any doubt in the error log.

## Demonstration on a new passage

> A village panchayat has installed solar street lights in 40 of its 60 lanes. Since then, the number of night-time thefts reported in the village has fallen, though the panchayat has also begun night patrols by volunteers.

Question: which is the most logical inference?

(a) Solar lights alone have reduced thefts. (b) The lights have been installed in some but not all of the lanes. (c) Volunteer patrols are more effective than lights. (d) Every lane in the village is now safer.

- Step 1: a plain inference stem. Step 2: circle '40 of its 60', 'since then', 'also'. Step 3: certainly true: 40 is less than 60; thefts reported have fallen; two changes happened together.
- Step 4: a safe inference will mention '40 of 60' or the two changes.
- Step 5: (a) 'alone': the patrols are a rival explanation, so denying (a) contradicts nothing: I (and too strong). (b) denying it gives 'lights are in all lanes or none', which contradicts '40 of 60': C. (c) a comparison the passage never makes: I. (d) 'every lane': the 20 lanes with no lights are not covered, and fewer *reported* thefts is not safer in each lane: I.
- Steps 6 and 7: eliminate (a) trap 5, (c) trap 1, (d) traps 2 and 6. Choose (b).

## Keeping the method short

On exam day the seven steps compress into about a minute for a short passage: stem, one reading with circles, a predicted sentence, and a quick denial of each option. Do not skip the prediction. Candidates who read the options first tend to be pulled towards the most attractive wording, which is exactly what the examiner designs for.

If you cannot predict anything, the passage is probably a statement-check type: judge each numbered statement separately instead.

### 7. Worked analyses: three official questions

*Objective:* Follow the method on three verified questions, naming the trap behind each rejected option; analysis only, no official key asserted.

Method note: these are analyses, not an answer key. The project has not verified UPSC's official key, so each analysis ends by saying which option the reasoning *points to*. Only short phrases of the official wording are quoted. Each question is in the PYQ bank under the id shown.

## Analysis 1: 2017, Q1 (upsc-2017-prelims-csat-gs-ii-q1)

**Passage.** Climate change will cause or amplify events that reduce resources; competition over them leads to political or violent conflict; such conflicts are rarely overt and appear in the guise of identity or ideology. **Stem:** 'What does the above passage imply?'

- 'Resource-based conflicts are always politically motivated.' Trap 2: 'always', and the passage says conflicts are *cloaked* as political.
- 'There are no political solutions...' Trap 1: the passage is silent on solutions.
- 'Environmental issues contribute to resource stresses and political conflict.' Follows the passage's chain from climate change to resource stress to conflict. 'Environmental issues' is slightly wider than 'climate change', but 'contribute' is soft.
- 'Political conflict based on identity or ideology cannot be resolved.' Traps 1 and 2.

The analysis points to the third option.

## Analysis 2: 2019, Q7 (upsc-2019-prelims-csat-gs-ii-q7)

**Passage.** A bat's wing, a human hand, a mole's digging limb, a horse's leg and a porpoise's paddle share one structural pattern, although each does a different job. **Stem:** 'most logical, scientific and rational inference'.

- The two options that call this 'biodiversity' use a concept the passage never discusses (trap 1).
- 'Coincidence in evolution' undermines the passage's whole point, which is that a *shared pattern* underlies different functions; it makes the pattern meaningless.
- 'Shared evolutionary history' is the one explanation that makes the repeated pattern meaningful.

The word 'scientific' in the stem permits a basic scientific reading; it does not permit a new topic. The analysis points to the option about shared evolutionary history.

## Analysis 3: 2019, Q43 (upsc-2019-prelims-csat-gs-ii-q43)

**Passage.** Most invasive species are neither very successful nor very harmful; new arrivals almost always increase diversity; invaders colonise disturbed habitats where little else lives. **Stem:** 'most logical and rational inference'.

- 'Invasive species should be used to rehabilitate desert areas...' Traps 1 and 4: deserts are not mentioned and 'should' is a recommendation.
- 'Laws against the introduction of foreign plants are unnecessary.' Trap 2 and trap 6: the passage says *most* invaders are harmless, not all, and says nothing of laws.
- 'Foreign plants should be used to increase the biodiversity...' Trap 4 again.
- 'Sometimes, the campaigns against foreign plants are pointless.' The hedge 'sometimes' claims only a small part of what the passage supports: if most invaders are neither successful nor harmful, then campaigns against *some* of them are pointless. Denying it ('no campaign against a foreign plant is ever pointless') conflicts with the passage, so it passes the contradiction test; the hedge makes it safe, but it is the support that makes it right.

The analysis points to the 'sometimes' option. Lesson: the weakest well-supported claim usually beats the stronger, more interesting one.

## Analysis 4: 2021, Q44, the corollary (upsc-2021-prelims-csat-gs-ii-q44)

**Passage.** In India the aim of macroeconomic policy is people's economic welfare, and neither monetary nor fiscal policy 'can independently work without active support of another'. **Stem:** 'best reflects the corollary'.

A bridge fact is needed: monetary policy is run by the central bank, fiscal policy by the government. Given that, the passage's direct consequence is that the central bank's policy cannot work without the government's support. 'The central bank cannot work independently of the Government' says exactly that. The options on closely regulating financial markets, on market economy versus socialist policy, and on financial-sector reforms add ideas the passage never raises (trap 1). The analysis points to the central-bank option. It is a corollary because it follows necessarily; no new idea enters.

## Analysis 5: 2022, Q61, the quantifier trap (upsc-2022-prelims-csat-gs-ii-q61)

**Passage.** The *majority of people who fail to accumulate money* are easily influenced by others' opinions. **Stem:** 'Which one of the following is implied by the passage?'

- 'Most of the people do not accumulate money for their needs.' Scope shift (trap 6): the passage speaks of the majority *of those who fail*, not of the majority of all people.
- 'Most of the people never fail...' Trap 1: nothing in the passage says how many people succeed.
- 'There is no need to accumulate money.' Contrary to the passage's concern.
- 'There are people who fail to accumulate money for their needs.' The passage cannot talk about 'people who fail' unless some exist.

The analysis points to the last option, which is the minimal implication. Notice how a passage that sounds like advice about money asks only for a logical presupposition.

## Analysis 6: 2026, Q55, 'not correct' with codes (upsc-2026-prelims-csat-gs-ii-q55)

**Passage.** Hori reflects that Rai Sahib refused good offers to lease a bit of common land because it was reserved for grazing, and 'held to the old values'. **Stem:** which statements are *not* correct?

1. 'The landholdings ... were currently not being used for farming.' The passage speaks of *this bit of land*; extending it to Rai Sahib's landholdings is a scope shift.
2. 'Temperamentally, Rai Sahib was as greedy as other landlords.' Contradicted by the contrast with 'selfish Zamindars'.
3. 'It cannot be ascertained that Rai Sahib could have made some money by leasing...' Contradicted: farmers 'offered a good price'.
4. 'It may be asserted that Rai Sahib valued his tenants.' Supported by the last sentence.

Now use the codes. Statement 4 is surely correct, so any code that lists it as 'not correct' fails. Statements 2 and 3 are surely not correct, so any code missing either fails. That leaves the code '1, 2 and 3' even if statement 1 were left unresolved. The analysis points to that code. Statement 1 is the delicate one and is marked for reviewer confirmation; the codes show how a hard statement can be settled by the statements around it.

### 9. Time, risk and the qualifying line

*Objective:* Compute net marks under negative marking and decide, with the 25 per cent break-even rule, when attempting an inference item is worth it.

## The arithmetic you must own

Each CSAT question carries 2.5 marks (as recorded for every verified question used here), and a wrong answer costs one-third of that: 2.5 divided by 3 is 0.833 marks. The qualifying bar is 33 per cent of the paper's 200 marks, which is 66 marks. One-third of 200 would be 66.67, so a safe target is **at least 67 net marks**. (CT-001 uses the 66.67 figure; aim for 67 and both are covered.)

Net marks = 2.5 x (correct) minus 0.833 x (wrong).

| Correct | Wrong | Calculation | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 | 0 | 27 x 2.5 | 67.5 |
| 30 | 3 | 75 - 2.5 | 72.5 |
| 28 | 6 | 70 - 5 | 65 (below 66) |
| 29 | 6 | 72.5 - 5 | 67.5 |

Three wrong answers cancel one right answer. This is the quickest rule to carry into the hall.

## When is a guess worth it?

Expected value (EV) of an attempt is 2.5 x p minus 0.833 x (1 - p), where p is your honest chance of being right. EV is zero when p = 0.25, so the break-even point is a **25 per cent honest chance**, whatever the number of options left.

- Blind guess among four options: p = 1/4, EV = 0.625 - 0.625 = **0**.
- One option safely eliminated: p = 1/3, EV = 0.833 - 0.556 = **+0.278**.
- Two options safely eliminated: p = 1/2, EV = 1.25 - 0.417 = **+0.833**.

The word 'safely' is the catch. If you believe you eliminated two options but the right answer is among your discards one time in three, your real p is lower than you think. For inference items, eliminate only by a named trap (module 5), not by a feeling.

## Time

Two hours for 80 questions is 90 seconds a question. A short inference passage should take about two minutes: ten seconds on the stem, forty on the passage, seventy on the options. Cap yourself at **two and a half minutes**. If you cannot name a C or P option by then, mark it and move on.

## Passes through the paper

- **First pass**: answer every inference item you can settle by the method. Attempt a guess only if at least one option is eliminated by a named trap and you honestly rate your chance above 25 per cent.
- **Second pass**: return to marked items; decide with the 25 per cent rule.

Because CSAT is qualifying, its marks do not add to your rank: the aim is a safe margin, not maximum marks. Comprehension items, which are your most reliable marks once the method is routine, are the best use of the time, but this lesson does not predict how many items you will answer correctly.

### Notes: Terms in one place

- Premise: a reason or piece of evidence the passage states.
- Conclusion: the claim the premises are meant to support; an implied conclusion is one the author never writes.
- Assumption: an unstated belief the argument needs; test: deny it and the argument collapses.
- Inference: an unstated statement the passage supports; test: deny it and you contradict the passage.
- Implication: what the passage suggests or commits the author to; may include a practical consequence that the passage's own reasoning supports.
- Corollary: a direct and necessary consequence; the strictest kind of inference.
- Converse: the reverse of a statement ('all A are B' becomes 'all B are A'); it needs its own support.

### Notes: Eight trap types and the PASS check

- 1 Beyond the passage: true in life, unsupported on the page.
- 2 Too strong: all, only, always, never, cannot.

- 5 Cause from sequence or correlation: 'solely', 'caused'.
- 6 Scope shift: part to whole, group to everyone, 'most of those who fail' to 'most people'.
- 7 True but unsupported: correct fact, no sentence behind it.
- 8 Misread numbers or direction: comparison reversed.

  - Taking 'depends on' for 'essential'
  - Skipping the qualifiers while reading

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** State the contradiction test for an inference in one sentence.
    - **a:** Deny the option; if the denial contradicts the passage, the option is an inference.
  - - **q:** State the denial test for an assumption in one sentence.
    - **a:** Deny the option; if the author's argument then collapses, the option is an assumption.
  - - **q:** Give one example of a recommendation leap.
    - **a:** A passage reports a falling tree cover; the option says 'the city should plant more trees' although the passage never argues for restoring cover.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is 'all A are B' not the same as 'all B are A'?
    - **a:** The first says nothing about B things that are not A. For example, all doctors in a panel being graduates does not make every graduate a panelist.
  - - **q:** Why do three wrong answers cancel exactly one right answer?
    - **a:** A right answer earns 2.5 and a wrong one costs 0.833, which is one-third of 2.5; three wrong answers cost 3 x 0.833 = 2.5.
  - - **q:** Why is a hedged option often the best on an inference item?
    - **a:** Because the passage usually gives limited evidence, a hedged option claims little and the passage often still forces it (certainly true), while stronger claims go beyond the evidence. Hedged wording is not enough by itself: the option must be supported, and when none passes the strict contradiction test choose the one whose denial most strongly conflicts with the passage.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** Passage: 'Of 80 trainees, 56 found jobs within six months. All who found jobs had completed the final module.' Is 'Every trainee who completed the final module found a job' an inference?
    - **a:** No. It is the converse. The passage describes those who found jobs; some who completed the module may not have found jobs. It is indeterminate.
  - - **q:** A candidate has eliminated one option on a clear trap and thinks the chance of the remaining three is equal. What is the expected value of guessing?
    - **a:** p = 1/3. EV = (1/3) x 2.5 - (2/3) x 0.833 = 0.833 - 0.556 = +0.278. A small positive value; it is worth taking only if the elimination is safe.

### Memory skeleton

- Stem first: name the family (inference, implication, corollary, message, author, statement-check) and its standard
- Read the passage once; circle qualifiers and number figures
- Say what is certainly true; predict one safe inference before the options
- Deny each option: passage contradicted? Label C, P, I or F
- Eliminate by trap type and the four checks: passage-only, absolutes, scope, strength
- Choose the highest degree; if tied, the option that claims less

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, write the seven steps, the five degrees of strength, the eight trap types, and the inference test beside the assumption test.

### Last-week summary

- Inference: deny it and the passage is contradicted. Assumption: deny it and the argument collapses. Never swap the tests.
- Five degrees: certainly true, probably true, indeterminate, probably false, certainly false; choose the highest, reject indeterminate.
- Traps: beyond the passage, too strong, converse, recommendation leap, cause from sequence, scope shift, true but unsupported, misread numbers.
- Qualifiers: 'all A are B' is not 'all B are A'; 'only A are B' means every B is an A; 'only if' is necessary, not sufficient.
- Statement-check: judge each statement alone (T, F, ?), then use sure ones to remove codes.
