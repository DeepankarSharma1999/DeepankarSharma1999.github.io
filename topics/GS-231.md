## GS-231: Budget: receipts, expenditure, deficit types

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Budget: receipts, expenditure, deficit types and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Structure of the budget

Article 112 obliges the government to place before Parliament an Annual Financial Statement for each financial year running from April to March. Because spending decisions have effects beyond one year, the budget has two parts. The revenue account holds current-year flows: revenue receipts from taxes and non-tax sources (interest, dividends, fees, grants) that create no repayment obligation, and revenue expenditure on running government, interest and grants to states, even grants used for assets. The capital account holds receipts that create liabilities or reduce assets — borrowing, loan recoveries, disinvestment — and capital expenditure that builds assets or cuts liabilities, such as land, machinery, equity investment and loans to states and PSUs.

### Three deficits and what each tells you

Revenue deficit is the excess of revenue expenditure over revenue receipts; a positive value means the government is dissaving and borrowing to meet consumption needs, which can squeeze capital or welfare spending later. Fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus revenue receipts and non-debt capital receipts such as loan recoveries and PSU sale proceeds; it equals what must be borrowed at home, from the RBI or abroad, and it contains the revenue deficit. Primary deficit subtracts net interest payments from the fiscal deficit, isolating today's imbalance from the cost of past debt. A large revenue-deficit share in the fiscal deficit signals that borrowing is funding consumption rather than investment.

### FRBM targets and the current path

Under the FRBM Act, 2003 and its 2004 rules, the budget carries a macroeconomic framework statement and a medium-term fiscal policy cum strategy statement. The 2026-27 statements record the statutory goals — a 3% of GDP fiscal deficit by 31 March 2021, and debt of 60% (general government) and 40% (Centre) of GDP by 31 March 2025 — and explain deviations. Consolidation since 2021-22 brought the deficit from 6.7% of GDP to 4.4% in RE 2025-26, with 4.3% projected for BE 2026-27. The government now uses the debt glide path as its fiscal anchor; central government debt is put at 56.1% of GDP (RE 2025-26) and 55.6% (BE 2026-27).

### Worked example

RE 2025-26 (FRBM statement): total expenditure ₹49.65 lakh crore; revenue receipts ₹33.42 lakh crore; non-debt capital receipts ₹0.64 lakh crore. Fiscal deficit = 49.65 − (33.42 + 0.64) = ₹15.59 lakh crore (the document reports ₹15.58 lakh crore; the gap is rounding). Revenue expenditure ₹38.69 lakh crore gives revenue deficit = 38.69 − 33.42 = ₹5.27 lakh crore.

### Recall

- - **q:** Is fiscal deficit total expenditure minus all receipts including borrowings?
  - **a:** No — borrowings are excluded; FD = total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts).
- - **q:** How is primary deficit obtained?
  - **a:** Fiscal deficit minus net interest payments.
- - **q:** What fiscal deficit is projected for BE 2026-27?
  - **a:** 4.3% of GDP (FRBM statements 2026-27).

### Common trap

Defining fiscal deficit as expenditure minus all receipts including borrowings — that would always be zero; borrowings are excluded because the fiscal deficit measures the borrowing need.
