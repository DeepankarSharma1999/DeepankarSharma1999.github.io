### Bridge: Words used for the body of an essay

The **body** is everything between the opening and the conclusion. It is built from **paragraphs**, and in this lesson a paragraph is a block of about 110 to 165 words that defends exactly one idea. That idea is the paragraph's **claim**: one sentence that someone could disagree with. The **reason** says why the claim is true. The **example** is a concrete case, scene or mechanism that shows the reason at work. The **link** is the closing sentence that ties the paragraph back to the thesis and prepares the next one. **Progression** means that the paragraphs are arranged so that each one adds something the earlier ones did not and prepares what comes next, instead of standing side by side. A **pattern** is a named way of ordering paragraphs, such as small to large or problem to cause to response. A **counter-view** is the best argument against your own thesis, and a **concession** is the part of it that you admit is fair. A **dimension** is one lens on a topic (social, economic, ethical and so on, WR-001), and a **label** such as 'economic' is not a claim until it says something that can be disputed.

### 1. What the body does and how big it is

*Objective:* State the three jobs of the body and work out, by arithmetic, how many words and paragraphs it has.

## The middle is most of the essay

The image-verified Essay records this project holds print the instruction to write two essays, one topic from each of Sections A and B, each essay carrying 125 marks (250 in all), with a word limit of 1,000 to 1,200; the three-hour duration is from the registry and WR-001 only (verify on the printed paper). WR-003 gave the opening 80 to 120 words. For the conclusion this lesson uses the same 80 to 120 words as a *working assumption* until the later lesson on conclusions (WR-008). Then:

- Smallest body: a 1,000-word essay with 120 + 120 at the ends leaves 1,000 - 240 = 760 words.
- Largest body: a 1,200-word essay with 80 + 80 at the ends leaves 1,200 - 160 = 1,040 words.
- Share: 760 / 1,000 = 76 per cent and 1,040 / 1,200 = 86.7 per cent, so the body is about **76 to 87 per cent** of the essay.

## Three jobs

1. **Prove.** Defend the thesis of WR-002: each paragraph supplies one part of the 'because'.
2. **Move.** Carry the reader from the opening's claim to the point where its boundary is reached. If paragraph 4 could swap with paragraph 2 at no loss, nothing is moving (module 4).
3. **Test.** Face the strongest objection and show how it narrows the claim instead of breaking it (module 5).

## Structure is not content

WR-002's grid gave *ingredients*: dimensions, examples, objections. Structure is the *recipe*: what each paragraph claims and why this one follows that one. Good ingredients with no recipe read as a list.

## Neighbouring lessons

The body is not a tour of dimensions or a pile of facts. Later lessons go deeper: dimensions (WR-005), evidence and counterarguments (WR-006), transitions (WR-007), the conclusion (WR-008).

No UPSC marking scheme or model essay has been found by this project; the numbers are arithmetic on assumed lengths and the three jobs are a teaching judgement.

### 2. Anatomy of a paragraph: claim, reason, example, link

*Objective:* Write a paragraph of 110 to 165 words with one claim, a reason, an example and a link, and say what each part is for.

## One paragraph, one claim

A body paragraph defends **one claim**; if you cannot say it in one sentence, it is two paragraphs or none. This lesson uses a four-part build called CREL: **C**laim, **R**eason, **E**xample, **L**ink (a Mission 90 construct, not an official scheme).

| Part | Job | Words |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | One disputable sentence that serves the topic's key word or qualifier | 20 to 30 |
| Reason | Why the claim is true ('because', 'since') | 35 to 50 |
| Example | A concrete case, scene or mechanism that shows the reason at work | 40 to 60 |
| Link | Ties the paragraph to the thesis and prepares the next claim | 15 to 25 |

The totals are 20 + 35 + 40 + 15 = 110 and 30 + 50 + 60 + 25 = 165: a CREL paragraph is **110 to 165 words**. Under about 90 words usually means no reason; over about 180, two claims.

## A worked paragraph (original, labelled as an example)

Assumed thesis: 'Preparation in calm times pays because urgency multiplies cost; yet it fails when routine outlives the risk it was built for.' (prompt: 'The time to repair the roof is when the sun is shining', official Essay 2022 Q5).

> **Claim.** Repair done while the sun is shining is cheaper than repair done in a storm, and the difference lies in time, not in skill. **Reason.** In good weather the work can be planned, the tiles bought at leisure and the job finished in daylight; in a storm every hour is urgent, helpers are few and the damage spreads while the repair waits. **Example.** Picture a village school whose roof is patched in the dry months by teachers and parents over one weekend. The same roof, ignored until the first heavy rain, may soak the books, close the school for days and then need a contractor called in a hurry (an invented case, used only as an example). **Link.** Waiting, then, does not save the cost of repair; it only raises it, which is why calm is the cheapest time to prepare.

Counted by python, the parts have 24, 37, 54 and 23 words: 138 in all. The reason names a mechanism, the example is an invented scene with no statistic, and the link points back to the thesis.

## Rules that keep a paragraph working

1. **Claim first**, so the reader knows the destination and the claims-only test of module 3 works.
2. **The example must prove the claim**, not merely illustrate the topic.
3. **Reason before example:** without a reason it is an anecdote; without an example, a slogan.
4. **No second claim.** A new 'but' or 'also' idea belongs in the next paragraph.
5. **Link, do not summarise.**

### 3. From thesis and grid to a paragraph map

*Objective:* Turn a thesis and a dimension grid into a map of five to seven one-sentence claims, and test it by reading the claims alone.

## The thesis is the spine

The thesis of WR-002 has three parts: claim, because, boundary. The *because* needs supporting paragraphs, the *boundary* needs a paragraph showing where the claim stops, and the strongest objection needs a place of its own.

## The seven steps
1. **Write the thesis** in one sentence (WR-002 module 4).
2. **Take three to five dimensions** from the grid (WR-002 module 2; dimensions as in WR-001) with the strongest examples.
3. **Turn each dimension into a claim.** 'Economic' is a label; 'Urgency makes the same repair dearer' is a claim someone can dispute.
4. **Add a counter-view paragraph** (module 5).
5. **Add a boundary paragraph** showing where the thesis stops holding; merge it with the counter-view if the objection is the boundary.
6. **Order the paragraphs** (modules 4 and 5).
7. **Read the claims alone.** Write the claim sentences in a column and read only that column. It should read as a short argument; if it reads as a list or repeats itself, repair the map before writing the body.

Count: three to five dimensions + one counter-view + one boundary = **five to seven** paragraphs, which fits module 1's arithmetic.

## Example map (original)

Assumed thesis: 'Preparation in calm times pays because urgency multiplies cost; yet it fails when routine outlives the risk it was built for.'

| # | Claim | Reason | Example idea | Link |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Urgency makes the same repair dearer. | Time pressure removes choice | School roof | So waiting does not save |
| 2 | Calm lets people share the work. | Volunteers come when unhurried | Ward clearing drains | And it spreads the cost |
| 3 | States meet the same arithmetic on a larger scale. | Budgets are planned, relief is not | Maintenance versus relief (type of case; verify any real one) | But some risks never arrive |
| 4 | Some preparation is wasted. | Resources are limited | Stock that expires | Yet the answer is proportion |
| 5 | Preparation must be reviewed, or routine outlives the risk. | Risks change | Drill never updated | Back to the thesis |

Column 2 read alone is an argument with a turn at row 4.

Map faults to avoid: labels instead of claims, two claims in one row, and no row that answers the boundary. Time (practice target): three minutes after the 12-minute plan of WR-002; if it takes longer, the thesis is probably vague (WR-002 module 5).

### 4. Six progression patterns and the swap test

*Objective:* Name six ways to order body paragraphs, say when each suits, and use the swap test to check that the order really progresses.

## Progression versus a pile

A body progresses when each paragraph needs the one before it; it is a pile when paragraphs could be shuffled. The six patterns are **teaching constructs** and can be combined; the master notes table gives the risk and repair of each.

1. **Scale (small to large).** Individual, community, state, world. Suits linkage topics, such as 'Poverty anywhere is a threat to prosperity everywhere' (official Essay 2018 Q3). Each level must add a new mechanism.
2. **Time.** Past, present, future. Suits topics about change, such as 'The years teach much which the days never know' (Essay 2025 Q6). End each period with what it shows about the thesis.
3. **Problem, cause, response.** What is wrong, why, what can be done, what limits it. Suits policy topics, such as 'Management of Indian border disputes - a complex task' (Essay 2018 Q4). Tie each response to a cause.
4. **Claim, objection, reply.** Thesis, strongest objection, reply, narrowed claim. Suits debatable aphorisms, such as 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone' (Essay 2025 Q5; module 7).
5. **Condition.** When it holds, when it fails, what decides. Suits an 'agree with conditions' thesis (WR-002 stance 2), such as 'The cost of being wrong is less than the cost of doing nothing' (Essay 2024 Q8).
6. **Contrast, then resolve.** Two ideas in turn, then their relation. Suits paired topics, such as 'A society that has more justice is a society that needs less charity' (Essay 2023 Q7). The last paragraph must relate the pair.

## The swap test

Swap two paragraphs that are not neighbours and ask: *does anything break?* If the argument reads equally well, the order was arbitrary. If the second claim now depends on something not yet established, the order was doing real work. Typical dependencies:

- **Mechanism before extension:** show how a thing works before applying it.
- **Concession before boundary:** the objection must come before the paragraph that narrows the claim.
- **Cause before response:** a response means nothing before the cause it answers.

Strength order (a teaching judgement): open with a strong, easily proved paragraph, put the weakest dimension in the middle, and end with the paragraph that best prepares the conclusion.

WR-002's grid gives dimensions; a pattern says in which order to use them (scale uses its levels, time its When column).

### 5. Choosing a pattern and placing the counter-view

*Objective:* Choose a pattern from the stance and the topic type, and place a fair counter-view paragraph so that it narrows the thesis.

## Choosing: a 20-second test

Ask what stance your WR-002 thesis takes (WR-002 module 4) and what type of topic it is (WR-001 module 2). A teaching suggestion, not an official rule:

| Stance or topic type | Try first | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Agree with conditions | Condition | The conditions are the paragraphs |
| Agree | Scale, time or problem-cause-response | Support is built by widening or sequencing |
| Disagree | Claim, objection, reply (reversed: state the claim you reject, show why it fails, put yours in its place) | The rejected claim is the objection |
| Reframe, or paired concepts | Contrast, then resolve | Two frames or ideas must be related |
| Policy or issue topic | Problem, cause, response | Causes and responses are the content |
| 'Inherently ...' or question-form ('Has NAM lost its relevance ...?', Essay 2017 Section A Q4) | Condition or time; qualifier named in thesis, counter-view and last claim | Nature versus use; then versus now |

If two patterns tie, choose the one with more ready examples (WR-002 module 4).

## Placing the counter-view

A counter-view paragraph makes three moves, in order.

1. **State it fairly,** as its holder would, in its strongest form. A straw man fails the rubric.
2. **Answer it with a reason.** 'But' alone is not an answer; 'the objection assumes the source has stopped, which is the case the thesis excludes' is.
3. **Narrow your claim.** Show how the objection marks the boundary instead of overturning the thesis.

Position it after two or three supporting paragraphs and before the boundary or test paragraph: first, it takes trust you have not earned; last, it leaves the essay on a doubt. Depth belongs to WR-006.

## Keep the qualifier

Each claim must serve the topic's operative word ('best', 'inherently', 'less than', 'least'): either use it or show how the paragraph bears on it. At least the thesis, the counter-view paragraph and the last paragraph should name it. WR-001 and WR-003 showed how a dropped qualifier changes the topic. For 'Shelving hard decisions is the least ethical course' (official Essay 2026 Q7), a body that proves only that shelving is *unethical* has dropped 'least': it must compare shelving with other failures or say why it ranks lowest.

## Before writing

Never let paragraph 5 contradict paragraph 2: either the boundary explains both (say so in the link) or one goes. Write the pattern name above the map, mark the counter-view row, mark where the qualifier is named, ask of every other claim 'how does this help prove it?' and run the swap test.

### 6. Links between paragraphs

*Objective:* Write links that state the true relation between two paragraphs, and avoid ladder words with no relation behind them.

## A link is a claim about a relation

At the joint between two paragraphs the reader asks: *what is the relation?* A good link answers it.

| Relation | Typical words |
|---|---|
| Cause | because, since |
| Result | so, therefore, as a result |
| Contrast | but, yet, however, whereas |
| Condition | unless, only if, provided that |
| New step or scale | beyond this, at a larger scale |
| Example | for instance, consider |

*Moreover* and *furthermore* are weakest: they promise an addition but not its kind.

## Three tools

1. **Relation link.** One connector in the last sentence of paragraph *n* or the first of *n+1*. From the roof map of module 3: '...so waiting does not save. *Beyond the household*, states meet the same arithmetic.'
2. **Echo link.** Repeat the last key word in the next opening: '...the cost of urgency. *Urgency* is not only a household matter.'
3. **Question link.** The last sentence raises a question the next paragraph answers; use it sparingly.

## What to avoid

- **The ladder:** 'Firstly ... Secondly ...' with no relation behind the numbers. It marks order, not progress.
- **A false connector:** 'However' with no contrast, 'Thus' with no conclusion. It asserts a relation that is not there, which is worse than none.
- **Announcements:** 'Now we shall discuss the economic aspect' repeats a label and adds no claim.

## The delete test

Delete the connector. If the relation still shows from the content, the connector is optional. If not, the *content* does not connect, and no connector will repair it: reorder or change a claim.

## The claims-chain read

Read the claim sentences with their connectors: 'A; so B; but C; yet D; therefore E'. Any place where you cannot say 'so', 'but', 'because' or 'unless' is a weak joint. Transitions have their own later lesson (WR-007).

### 7. A worked body: 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone'

*Objective:* Read a complete original body as a map first and then as text, and see how thesis, pattern, counter-view and links work together.

## The prompt and the plan

Prompt: official Essay 2025 Q5 (record upsc-2025-essay-q5), also used in the WR-003 sample openings. The body below is **original**, a model of *structure* and not an official answer; its scenes are invented and it has no statistics.

**Thesis (original):** 'Letting a disturbance settle is often the quickest cure, because most disturbances fade when nothing feeds them; but restraint is wisdom only while the source of the mud has stopped, and it becomes neglect when the source has not.'

**Pattern:** claim, objection, reply, combined with condition. 'Best' is named in the thesis and paragraphs 4 and 6; the other claims serve it by showing when leaving alone works, with the boundary 'while the source has stopped'.

## The paragraph map

| # | Claim (condensed) | Job | Words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Muddy water clears when left alone because its particles sink once nothing moves them. | Mechanism | 127 |
| 2 | Public tempers behave the same way: they fade when they are not fed. | Social extension | 150 |
| 3 | Institutions that stir with hasty rules add a second problem to the first. | Institutional extension | 147 |
| 4 | The objection that some water never clears narrows the claim: stop the source, then leave it alone. | Objection, reply, boundary | 153 |
| 5 | Two questions decide when to wait: has the source stopped, and does delay harm anyone? | Condition test | 163 |
| 6 | Restraint is best when it is done knowingly and can be explained. | Ethical duty | 130 |

The six paragraphs total 870 words (counted by python), inside the 760 to 1,040 budget of module 1; the longest (163) is under twice the shortest (127). The full text is in the master notes.

## What to notice

1. **Order.** Swap paragraphs 1 and 3 and 'a hand in the pot' arrives before the pot: the swap test at work.
2. **The counter-view sits fourth,** after three supporting paragraphs, in the three moves of module 5: stated fairly (a continuing injustice or disease), answered (the proverb is about muddy water, not a hand still in it), and used to narrow the claim (stop the source first).
3. **The last paragraph adds a dimension,** ethics, not a repeat.
4. **Links.** Each paragraph ends on a relation: extension (1 to 2), contrast (2 to 3), result (3 to 4), application (4 to 5) and duty (5 to 6); paragraph 6 opens with 'also', honest because it adds a new dimension.

## Weaknesses to find yourself

The example in paragraph 3 is generic, 'a careful officer' is an assertion, the example in paragraph 5 is one invented line, and an economic claim is absent. Take the method, not the text.

### 8. Eight body faults and how to repair them

*Objective:* Recognise eight common faults in a body, say how to detect each, and repair it.

## How to use this list

Run the faults as a checklist on your map and again on the written body. The numbers are used in the error log and the practice tasks. The list is derived from this lesson's structure, not from an examiner. A table is in the master notes.

1. **List essay.** Each paragraph is a dimension label with facts and no claim. *Detect:* the claims-chain read gives labels. *Repair:* a claim with a verb and a 'because'.
2. **Repetition.** Two paragraphs make the same claim in different words. *Detect:* two claims answer the same 'so what'. *Repair:* merge, or replace the weaker with a new step.
3. **Drift.** A paragraph is about the topic's theme, not its claim. *Detect:* you cannot say how the paragraph helps prove the topic's key word or qualifier (ask: how does this help prove 'best'?). *Repair:* rewrite so the claim uses the key word or shows its bearing, or delete.
4. **Example dump.** Several names or cases with no reasoning. *Detect:* no reason sentence. *Repair:* keep one and explain how it proves the claim; flag uncertain facts *verify* when practising.
5. **One-sided body.** No counter-view, or one in the last line only. *Detect:* no objection row. *Repair:* add the three-move paragraph of module 5.
6. **Flip.** One paragraph contradicts another or the thesis. *Detect:* a 'but' with no boundary behind it. *Repair:* state the boundary that explains both, or delete one.
7. **Orphan.** A paragraph that could sit anywhere. *Detect:* the swap test changes nothing. *Repair:* reorder, or add the dependency the order needs.
8. **Lopsided.** One paragraph over twice another, or several under 60 words. *Detect:* word count by paragraph. *Repair:* split the bulky one; give the thin one a reason and an example.

## Error log

Faults travel in groups, so repair the map first. Tally faults by number across drills; the commonest is the next drill.

### 9. Rubric, timed routine and practice

*Objective:* Score a body on an eight-item rubric, run a timed planning and writing routine, and use the error log to choose the next drill.

## The eight-item rubric (a self-assessment tool made for this lesson, not an official scale)

Score each item 0, 1 or 2; the total is out of 16 (the full table is in the master notes block 'Self-check rubric'). The items are: claims chain, spine (thesis and qualifier), paragraph anatomy, order, evidence, counter-view, links and proportion. Record the *lowest* item: it is the next drill.

## Timed routine (practice target, not an official allotment)

Three hours for two essays gives about 180 / 2 = 90 minutes each (verify on the paper; the split is the candidate's choice). For a 1,100-word essay with 100-word ends and a 900-word body, at 15 to 20 words a minute (WR-003; time yourself):

| Step | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Decode, brainstorm, thesis (WR-002 drill) | 12 |
| Paragraph map and swap test | 3 |
| Opening of 100 words (WR-003) | 8 |
| Body: 900 / 20 = 45 to 900 / 15 = 60 | 45 to 60 |
| Conclusion of 100 words: 100 / 20 = 5 to 100 / 15 = 6.7 | 5 to 7 |
| Read through, delete test, rubric | 4 |
| **Total** | 77 to about 94 |

At 20 words a minute this fits within 90; at 15 it runs about 4 minutes over. Either write a shorter body of about 800 words (still above the 760 floor) or raise your speed. Do not skip the map: three minutes of mapping saves rewriting.

## The three-day cycle

Day 1: four prompts, plan and map only (15 minutes each). Day 2: write one full body, timed. Day 3: score it and rewrite one paragraph. After seven days or more: rebuild one earlier map from memory; compare with the worked body of module 7 only after writing yours.

## Warnings

Completing drills is not mastery; honest scoring, repair of the lowest item and later retesting are the evidence. Do not memorise a body (fault 3).

### Notes: Definitions

- Body: everything between the opening (WR-003) and the conclusion (WR-008); about 760 to 1,040 words in a 1,000 to 1,200 word essay, on the working assumption of 80 to 120 word ends.
- Paragraph: a block of about 110 to 165 words that defends one claim.
- Claim: one sentence a thoughtful person could dispute; it is the topic sentence of the paragraph and serves the topic's key word or qualifier (it uses the word or shows how the paragraph bears on it).
- Reason: the sentence or two that says why the claim is true.
- Example: a concrete case, scene or mechanism that shows the reason at work; it must prove the claim, not merely mention the topic.
- Link: the last sentence of a paragraph; it ties the paragraph to the thesis and prepares the next claim.
- CREL: Claim, Reason, Example, Link; the four-part paragraph build of this lesson (a Mission 90 construct).
- Progression: an ordering in which each paragraph needs the one before it, tested by the swap test.
- Swap test: exchange two non-adjacent paragraphs; if the argument still reads equally well, the order is arbitrary.
- Counter-view: the strongest argument against your own thesis; the concession is the part you admit is fair.
- Claims-chain read: reading only the claim sentences of the body in order, to see whether they form an argument.
- Dimension: one lens on a topic (WR-001); a dimension label becomes a claim only when it says something disputable.

### Notes: Worked body (original, labelled as an example): 'Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone'

- Official Essay 2025 Q5 (record upsc-2025-essay-q5). Original model of structure, not an official answer or an examiner expectation. Invented scenes; no statistics. Total 870 words in six paragraphs (counts: 127, 150, 147, 153, 163, 130).
- Thesis (original): Letting a disturbance settle is often the quickest cure, because most disturbances fade when nothing feeds them; but restraint is wisdom only while the source of the mud has stopped, and it becomes neglect when the source has not.
- Paragraph 1 (127 words). Muddy water clears when it is left alone because the cloud is made of small particles that sink by themselves once nothing moves them. Stirring only lifts them again. A pot of pond water left on a shelf overnight is clearer in the morning without a single act from anyone, while a hand put in to hurry it makes it cloudy for another hour. The lesson of the image is that some remedies work through time, and the right action is to protect that time from interference. In this sense leaving it alone is 'best': it is the cheapest method that the situation already offers. If stillness clears a pot, the same should hold wherever disturbance feeds on movement, and public tempers are the first such case.
- Paragraph 2 (150 words). Public tempers behave much as the pot does. A quarrel at a neighbourhood meeting, a rumour passed from phone to phone, a wave of anger after a loss: each is loudest at the start and fades when it is not fed. Replying to every rumour at once gives it more circulation than it had, and a meeting adjourned to the next day often returns with half its heat. Consider an invented case. Two families dispute a boundary wall, and a visiting crowd takes sides within the hour. A day's pause, with the crowd gone, lets the families speak plainly. Restraint here is not indifference. It is a decision to remove the audience that keeps the water moving, and it works because anger needs fuel and a pause withdraws it. Calm voices are heard only after the shouting has tired, and the bodies that answer to crowds feel the opposite pull.
- Paragraph 3 (147 words). Institutions are often tempted to stir. An administration under public pressure issues a rule in the heat of the moment; a week later the rule is amended, then withdrawn, and the people who had to follow it are left with confusion on top of the original problem. A hasty order is a hand in the pot, because it adds a second disturbance to the first. The better habit is to gather the facts quietly, name who will decide and by when, and let the first alarm pass before acting. Such waiting is not drift if it has a date. It lets a decision rest on information instead of noise, which is why a careful officer asks for time before giving a verdict on a fresh crisis. Yet a case for waiting that has met no objection is still untested, so the strongest objection must now be heard.
- Paragraph 4 (153 words). The strongest objection is that some water never clears because someone keeps stirring. Where an injustice continues, a disease is spreading or a wrong is repeated daily, patience settles nothing; it only lengthens the suffering of those who cannot wait, so that waiting becomes a cost laid on the victim. The objection is fair, and it narrows the claim that leaving alone is best instead of destroying it. The proverb speaks of water that is muddy, not of a hand that is still in the water. If the source of the disturbance continues, the first act is to stop the source, and leaving alone applies after that. A physician may advise rest for a mild fever, yet will not advise it for a wound that keeps bleeding. The objection therefore marks the boundary of the thesis without overturning it, and a boundary is useful only if it can be applied as a test.
- Paragraph 5 (163 words). A practical test follows from this boundary. Two questions decide whether to wait. Has the source of the disturbance stopped? Does the delay itself make things worse for someone? If the source has stopped and the delay harms no one, wait. If either condition fails, act, and act first on the source. Consider an invented case. A village tank is repaired and the leak stops, but the water stays brown; the headman waits two days and no one is harmed. Had the pipe still been leaking, the same waiting would have been neglect. The test also guards against a quieter error, which is to treat every case as a case for waiting because waiting is comfortable. By forcing two questions before inaction, it turns patience from a habit into a judgement, and a judgement can be explained to the people it affects. Yet a test is only as honest as the person who applies it, which brings in the duty that restraint carries.
- Paragraph 6 (130 words). Restraint also carries an ethical duty. Those who wait are choosing, and a choice must be owned. A leader who lets a matter settle should be able to say why, for how long and what fresh fact would change the decision. Without that account, patience slides into avoidance, and the weak, who cannot afford to wait, pay the bill. The same duty binds citizens: the person who holds back an angry post has acted, while the person who merely looks away has not. Leaving muddy water alone is therefore best when it is done knowingly, with the source stopped and the delay harmless, and when someone is willing to explain it. That is the line that separates wisdom from neglect, and it is the line on which this essay rests.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Dimension versus claim: 'economic' is a lens; 'urgency makes repair dearer' is a claim.
- Paragraph versus point: a paragraph has one claim with a reason and example; a point is a bare idea.
- Progression versus sequence: 'firstly, secondly' numbers paragraphs; progression means each needs the one before.
- Counter-view versus contradiction: a counter-view narrows the thesis through the boundary; a contradiction leaves the reader unsure what you hold.
- Link versus summary: a link names what the paragraph did for the thesis; a summary repeats what it said.
- Example versus illustration of the topic: an example proves the paragraph's claim; an illustration of the topic may prove nothing.
- Pattern versus template: a pattern is chosen to fit the thesis; a memorised template ignores the topic.

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - The body is about 760 to 1,040 words, 76 to 87 per cent of a 1,000 to 1,200 word essay (on 80 to 120 word ends).
  - One paragraph, one claim: claim, reason, example, link (CREL), 110 to 165 words.
  - Thesis, then grid, then claims, then order: the body is a map of five to seven one-sentence claims.
  - Six patterns: scale, time, problem-cause-response, claim-objection-reply, condition, contrast-then-resolve.
  - The swap test shows whether the order progresses; the claims-chain read shows whether the claims form an argument.
  - The counter-view is stated fairly, answered with a reason and used to narrow the claim; it sits after two or three supporting paragraphs.
  - A link names a true relation; a false connector is worse than none.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - Body arithmetic: 1,000 - 240 = 760 and 1,200 - 160 = 1,040; 760 / 1,000 = 76 per cent and 1,040 / 1,200 = 86.7 per cent.
  - CREL word targets: claim 20 to 30, reason 35 to 50, example 40 to 60, link 15 to 25; totals 110 to 165.
  - Five to seven paragraphs: three to five dimensions plus one counter-view plus one boundary.
  - Under about 90 words a paragraph probably lacks a reason; over about 180 it probably holds two claims.
  - Eight faults: list essay, repetition, drift, example dump, one-sided body, flip, orphan, lopsided.
  - Rubric: eight items, 0 to 2 each, out of 16; the lowest item is the next drill.
  - No UPSC marking scheme or model essay has been found by this project; all structural rules are teaching constructs.
- **criticalDistinctions:**
  - Dimension label versus claim.
  - Progression versus a pile or a numbered ladder.
  - Counter-view (narrows the thesis) versus contradiction (confuses it).
  - Link versus summary.
  - Example that proves the claim versus an illustration of the topic.
  - Pattern chosen for the thesis versus a memorised template.
- **formulae:**
  - Body words = essay words - opening words - conclusion words
  - Words per paragraph = body words / number of paragraphs
  - Writing time = words / (words per minute)
- **traps:**
  - Writing the body as one paragraph per dimension label with no claim.
  - Putting the counter-view first or last.
  - Dropping the topic's qualifier so that the body proves a weaker claim ('best', 'least', 'inherently', 'less than').
  - Using 'however', 'thus' or 'moreover' where the relation is not true.
  - Quoting a statistic or a named incident from memory to fill an example.
  - Memorising a body in advance.

### Answer Lab diagrams

#### CREL: the four parts of a body paragraph (draft)

Module 2: CREL is a Mission 90 teaching construct, not an official scheme. Reason comes before example, and the link points back to the thesis rather than summarising.

Four boxes stacked top to bottom joined by short arrows: Claim (20 to 30 words), Reason (35 to 50), Example (40 to 60) and Link (15 to 25). The total for one paragraph is 110 to 165 words; under about 90 words usually lacks a reason and over about 180 usually holds two claims.

1. Draw four boxes stacked, labelled C Claim, R Reason, E Example, L Link, with arrows between them.
2. Beside each, write its word range: 20-30, 35-50, 40-60, 15-25.
3. Write the one-line job: claim serves the key word; reason gives the mechanism; example proves the claim; link names the relation and prepares the next claim.
4. Add the total, 110 to 165 words, and the warning limits (about 90 and 180).

#### Body map: thesis, paragraph chain, counter-view, close (draft)

Module 5: three to five supports, then the counter-view after two or three of them, then the boundary or test paragraph. The link words are examples of relations, to be chosen to fit your actual claims.

A vertical chain of seven boxes: thesis, three supporting paragraphs, a counter-view paragraph, a boundary or last paragraph and the conclusion. Arrows carry link words: because, so, beyond this, but, only if, therefore. The operative word is named in the thesis, the counter-view and the last paragraph, and every support claim serves it. The counter-view comes after two or three supports and has three moves: state fairly, answer with a reason, narrow the claim.

1. Draw a tall column of boxes: thesis at the top, then Support 1, 2 and 3, a counter-view box, a boundary or last paragraph box and the conclusion.
2. Between boxes draw arrows and write the relation word on each: because, so, beyond this, but, only if, therefore.
3. Mark Q beside the thesis, the counter-view and the last paragraph, to show the operative word is named there.
4. Bracket the supports with the note that each claim uses the operative word or shows how it bears on it.
5. Beside the counter-view write its three moves: state fairly, answer with a reason, narrow the claim.

#### Claims-chain read and the delete test (draft)

Module 6: a link is a claim about a relation. If the relation disappears when the connector is deleted, no connector will repair the joint; the order or the claim must change.

A row of five claim boxes A to E joined by arrows labelled so, but, yet and so. Below is the delete test: delete the connector and ask whether the relation still shows. If yes, the connector is optional; if no, the content does not connect, so reorder or change a claim. A note warns against the ladder and false connectors.

1. Draw five small boxes in a row, Claim A to Claim E, joined by arrows.
2. Write a relation word on each arrow (so, but, yet, so) and note that a joint is weak where none of so, but, because or unless can be said.
3. Below, draw the question box: delete the connector, does the relation still show?
4. Branch yes to connector is optional and no to the content does not connect, so reorder or change a claim.
5. Add the two things to avoid: the ladder and a false connector.

### Memory skeleton

- The body is most of the essay (about 760 to 1,040 words on assumed 80-120-word ends); three jobs: prove, move, test
- One paragraph, one claim: CREL (claim, reason, example, link), 110 to 165 words
- Thesis to map: three to five dimensions turned into disputable claims, plus a counter-view and a boundary: five to seven paragraphs; read the claims alone
- Six progression patterns; the swap test shows whether the order does real work
- Choose the pattern by stance; counter-view in three moves after two or three supporting paragraphs; keep the qualifier
- Links name a true relation: relation, echo and question links; the delete test
- Eight body faults with repairs; eight-item rubric, the lowest item is the next drill
