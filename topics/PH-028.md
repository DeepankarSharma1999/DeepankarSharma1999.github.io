## PH-028: Lorentz transformations

Physics Paper I · Physics · Special relativity

### Bridge: Squares, binomial approximation and gamma as a number

This lesson is mostly algebra, so three tools must feel comfortable.

**1. Expanding squares.** (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b² and (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². The middle term 2ab is the 'cross term' that most often goes missing or loses a sign.

**2. The factor γ as a number.** Write β = v/c. Then γ = 1/√(1 − β²). For β = 0.6: 1 − β² = 0.64, √0.64 = 0.8, so γ = 1/0.8 = 1.25. Always compute 1 − β² first, then the square root, then the reciprocal. A useful identity: γ²(1 − β²) = 1, and therefore γ²β² = γ² − 1.

**3. Binomial approximation.** For a small number x (much less than 1), (1 − x)^(−1/2) ≈ 1 + x/2 and (1 − x)^(−1) ≈ 1 + x. Test it: x = 0.01 gives (0.99)^(−1/2) = 1.00504, while 1 + 0.005 = 1.005. With x = β², this says γ ≈ 1 + β²/2 when β is small. Do not use it when β is not small: at β = 0.8 the approximation gives 1.32 but γ = 1.667.

**4. Solving a quadratic.** If aβ² + bβ + c = 0 then β = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a). In relativity both roots may be mathematically valid, so check each against the physical requirement (here 0 ≤ β < 1 and the sign condition in the question).

### Bridge: Differentials, the chain rule and partial derivatives

**Differentials.** If x′ = γ(x − vt) with γ and v constant, a small change dx at a small change dt gives dx′ = γ(dx − v dt). This is just 'differentiate both sides'. Velocity is the ratio of two such small changes: u = dx/dt and u′ = dx′/dt′.

**Partial derivative.** For a quantity that depends on two variables, such as f(x, t), the partial derivative ∂f/∂x means: differentiate with respect to x while treating t as a constant. Example: f = x²t gives ∂f/∂x = 2xt and ∂f/∂t = x².

**Chain rule.** If f depends on x′ and t′, and x′ and t′ themselves depend on x and t, then

$$ ∂f/∂x = (∂x′/∂x)(∂f/∂x′) + (∂t′/∂x)(∂f/∂t′) $$

and similarly for ∂/∂t. Each factor in front is a partial derivative of the transformation, so with x′ = γ(x − vt) and t′ = γ(t − vx/c²): ∂x′/∂x = γ, ∂x′/∂t = −γv, ∂t′/∂x = −γv/c², ∂t′/∂t = γ. You will use exactly these four numbers in derivation PH-028-D6.

**Second derivative.** Apply the operator twice; keep the order and watch the signs. Vector background (components and dot product) is in PH-001, bridge PH-001-B1.

### 1. Orientation: what the Lorentz transformation is and the conventions used

*Objective:* State what the Lorentz transformation does, define event, inertial frame, β and γ, and fix the frame conventions used in every module.

## What this topic is

Two observers who move at constant velocity relative to each other can describe the same happening, for example a lamp flashing. Each uses their own ruler and clock. The **Lorentz transformation** is the exact rule that converts the position and time recorded by one observer into the position and time recorded by the other. It replaces the Galilean rule of PH-001 (module 2), which is only an approximation valid when speeds are far below the speed of light.

## Words to know first

- **Event**: something that happens at one point and one instant, described by four numbers (x, y, z, t). A lamp flash is an event; a moving train is a chain of events.
- **Inertial frame**: a frame in which a force-free body keeps constant velocity (PH-001, module 2). Rotating or accelerating frames are excluded (PH-002 treats those).
- **Observer**: a person or instrument at rest in a frame, with synchronised clocks throughout that frame.
- **c**: the speed of light in vacuum, taken as 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in numericals (the more exact value is 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹).
- **β and γ**: β = v/c is a pure number from 0 up to (not including) 1, and γ = 1/√(1 − β²) is a pure number that is at least 1.

## Conventions used in every module

Frame S is the laboratory. Frame S′ moves relative to S with constant velocity v along the +x direction. The corresponding axes are parallel, the axes are right-handed (x̂ × ŷ = ẑ, so with x to the right and y upward, z points out of the page towards you), and the origins coincide at t = t′ = 0. Primed symbols are always measured in S′. The sources do not state the handedness; it is the standard choice and is stated here so that cross products and signs are unambiguous.

## Where this lesson sits

The syllabus clause names the Michelson–Morley experiment, the Lorentz transformations, length contraction, time dilation, velocity addition, aberration, Doppler effect, mass–energy and four-vectors. This lesson covers the transformation with its inverse, limiting cases, the invariant interval, relativity of simultaneity and, as an early preview, velocity addition (home topic PH-030, Day 11). Time dilation and length contraction are PH-029 (Day 10); aberration is with PH-030 (Day 11, velocity addition and aberration) and the Doppler effect is PH-031 (Day 12); the Michelson–Morley experiment has its own topic PH-027, which the roadmap schedules later, so here it appears only as motivation (module 3).

### 2. Foundation: the Galilean rule meets light

*Objective:* Show with algebra that the Galilean transformation cannot keep the speed of light the same in two inertial frames.

## What PH-001 already gave you

PH-001 (module 2) derived the Galilean transformation x′ = x − vt, y′ = y, z′ = z, t′ = t, the velocity rule u′ = u − v, the invariance of acceleration and the Galilean invariance of F = ma. None of it is repeated here. The new question is what hides inside it. Upadhyaya (§6.2) names two hidden assumptions: time is the same for every observer (t′ = t, so 'simultaneous' would mean the same to everyone), and the distance between two points is the same for every observer.

## Light enters

Maxwell's theory (1864, per Mathur) shows that light is an electromagnetic wave whose vacuum speed c comes out of the equations themselves, the same in all directions. Earlier waves such as sound need a material medium, so a medium called the **luminiferous ether** was imagined. If it existed it would define one special frame at rest in the ether, in which light travels at c. Observers moving through it should measure a different speed.

Galilean prediction: light moves at c along +x in S. An observer in S′ moving at +v along x gets c′ = c − v; moving towards the light gives c + v. For v = 0.6c, c′ = 0.4c.

## The wavefront test

A flash leaves the common origin at t = t′ = 0. In S, at time t the light fills the sphere x² + y² + z² = c²t². If the speed of light is c in S′ as well, then in S′ the light must fill x′² + y′² + z′² = c²t′². Substitute the Galilean rule into the second sphere:

$$ (x − vt)² + y² + z² = c²t²  ⟹  x² − 2vxt + v²t² + y² + z² = c²t² $$

This does not match x² + y² + z² = c²t²: the extra terms −2vxt + v²t² do not vanish. (As extracted, Mathur's line reads 2xyt; this may be an extraction artefact, so verify against the page image. The cross term is 2xvt.) So with t′ = t and x′ = x − vt the two observers cannot both see a sphere centred on themselves moving at c.

## The fork

Either the Galilean rule is wrong at high speed, or the constancy of c is wrong. Experiment must decide. Module 3 describes the experiment and module 4 builds the replacement rule.

### 3. Foundation: the Michelson–Morley experiment (as the sources describe it) and the postulates

*Objective:* Reproduce the fringe-shift estimate, state what the null result implied, and write Einstein's two postulates precisely.

## The experiment

Light from a source meets a half-silvered glass plate that splits it into two beams at right angles. Each beam travels to a mirror a distance D away and back, and the beams are recombined to give interference fringes seen through a telescope. Mathur and Upadhyaya describe the same layout; effective arm length was increased to about 11 m by repeated reflections and the apparatus floated on mercury so it could be rotated.

Suppose the apparatus moves at speed u through a stationary ether, with arm 1 along the motion. Write β = u/c.

- Arm along the motion: out at c − u, back at c + u. Time t∥ = D/(c − u) + D/(c + u) = 2Dc/(c² − u²) = (2D/c)/(1 − β²).
- Arm across the motion: the beam is aimed slightly upstream so that its resultant is across, with speed √(c² − u²) each way. Time t⊥ = 2D/√(c² − u²) = (2D/c)/√(1 − β²).

For β ≪ 1, 1/(1 − β²) ≈ 1 + β² and 1/√(1 − β²) ≈ 1 + β²/2, so t∥ − t⊥ ≈ (2D/c)(β²/2) = Dβ²/c. The path difference is c(t∥ − t⊥) = Dβ². Turning the apparatus through 90° swaps the roles of the arms, so the change in path difference is 2Dβ², and the fringe shift is

$$ N = 2Dβ²/λ $$

Numbers: with Earth's orbital speed about 3 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹, β = 10⁻⁴ and β² = 10⁻⁸. For D = 11 m and λ = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m, N = 2 × 11 × 10⁻⁸ / 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ = 0.40. Mathur says the apparatus could measure about one-hundredth of a fringe. The shift observed was far smaller than expected (the source says almost negligible); no exact readings are given in these extracts, so none are quoted here. PH-027 is the dedicated topic.

## Reactions to the null result

Michelson suggested the Earth drags the ether; per Mathur, Lodge (1892) found at most about half a per cent of c could be dragged. FitzGerald and Lorentz (1892) proposed that a body shortens along its motion by √(1 − β²). Check that this cancels the shift: the arm along the motion becomes D√(1 − β²), so t∥ = (2D√(1 − β²)/c)/(1 − β²) = (2D/c)/√(1 − β²) = t⊥. The idea works but was introduced only for this purpose.

## Einstein's postulates (1905)

1. **Relativity principle**: the laws of physics have the same form in all inertial frames.
2. **Constancy of c**: the speed of light in vacuum is c in every inertial frame, independent of the motion of the source or observer.

Mathur says postulate 2 states the Michelson–Morley result; Upadhyaya says it follows from Maxwell's equations plus postulate 1. Historians differ on how much the experiment influenced Einstein, so in an answer say the experiment is consistent with postulate 2. Dates differ between the sources: Mathur gives 1887, Upadhyaya prints 1880 (verify; commonly cited are 1881 for the first version and 1887 with Morley).

### 4. Core: deriving the Lorentz transformation step by step

*Objective:* Derive x′ = γ(x − vt) and t′ = γ(t − vx/c²) from the two postulates, stating every assumption.

## What we look for

A rule taking (x, y, z, t) in S to (x′, y′, z′, t′) in S′ such that light has speed c in both and the rule becomes Galilean when v ≪ c. Conventions are those of module 1.

## Assumptions to state in an exam

1. S and S′ are inertial; S′ moves at +v along x.
2. Homogeneity of space and time (no special point or instant) requires a linear transformation in x and t; consistent with this, uniform motion in S stays uniform in S′. (Mathur argues that a linear form gives one interpretation only.)
3. y′ = y and z′ = z. Reason: take two identical rods along y, one at rest in each frame, passing each other. If the S′ rod looked shorter to S, postulate 1 would make the S rod look shorter to S′, a contradiction. Lengths perpendicular to the motion are unchanged.
4. Seen from S, the origin O′ (x′ = 0) is at x = vt. So x′ must vanish when x = vt: x′ = γ(x − vt), where γ depends only on v and tends to 1 as v → 0.
5. By postulate 1, the inverse rule has the same form with v → −v and the same γ: x = γ(x′ + vt′).

## Step 1: find t′

Put assumption 4 into 5:

$$ x = γ[γ(x − vt) + vt′] = γ²x − γ²vt + γvt′ $$

Isolate the t′ term: γvt′ = x − γ²x + γ²vt, so

$$ t′ = γt − (γ² − 1)x/(γv) $$

## Step 2: find γ with a light pulse

A pulse leaves the common origin at t = t′ = 0 along +x. In S, x = ct. By postulate 2, in S′, x′ = ct′. From assumption 4, x′ = γ(c − v)t. From step 1 with x = ct, t′ = γt − (γ² − 1)ct/(γv). Setting x′ = ct′:

$$ γ(c − v)t = cγt − c²(γ² − 1)t/(γv) $$

The term γct appears on both sides; cancel it and divide by t: −γv = −c²(γ² − 1)/(γv). Multiply by −γv: γ²v² = c²(γ² − 1). Then γ²(c² − v²) = c², so

$$ γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) $$

(positive root, so that γ → 1 as v → 0). A real γ needs v < c.

## Step 3: simplify t′

Since γ² − 1 = γ²β², (γ² − 1)/(γv) = γβ²/v = γv/c². Therefore t′ = γt − γvx/c² = γ(t − vx/c²).

## Result

$$ x′ = γ(x − vt),  y′ = y,  z′ = z,  t′ = γ(t − vx/c²) $$

Module 8 proves that the sphere x² + y² + z² = c²t² maps to x′² + y′² + z′² = c²t′² in all directions, which completes the check. Upadhyaya's alternative route, guessing t′ = γ′(t + fx) and matching coefficients, is derivation PH-028-D2.

### 5. Core: the inverse transformation, the size of γ and limiting cases

*Objective:* Verify the inverse transformation by algebra, read values of γ from a table, and explain the cases v ≪ c and v → c.

## The inverse transformation, verified

Module 4 assumed the inverse has the same form with v → −v. Check it by substitution (nothing is trusted):

$$ x′ + vt′ = γ(x − vt) + vγ(t − vx/c²) = γx(1 − v²/c²) = x/γ $$

so γ(x′ + vt′) = x. Similarly t′ + vx′/c² = γ(t − vx/c²) + γv(x − vt)/c² = γt(1 − v²/c²) = t/γ, so γ(t′ + vx′/c²) = t. The inverse transformation is

$$ x = γ(x′ + vt′),  y = y′,  z = z′,  t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²) $$

## How large is γ?

| β | γ = 1/√(1 − β²) | 1 + β²/2 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 1.0050 | 1.0050 |
| 0.5 | 1.1547 | 1.1250 |
| 0.6 | 1.2500 | 1.1800 |
| 0.8 | 1.6667 | 1.3200 |
| 0.9 | 2.2942 | 1.4050 |
| 0.99 | 7.0888 | 1.4901 |
| 0.999 | 22.366 | 1.4990 |

The approximation 1 + β²/2 is within 1 per cent of γ up to β ≈ 0.4 and 2.6 per cent off at β = 0.5 (Mathur says it is valid enough up to about 0.5). It fails badly at higher speed.

## Limiting case 1: v ≪ c

Then γ ≈ 1 + β²/2 → 1 and vx/c² is small, so x′ → x − vt and t′ → t: the Galilean rule is recovered. For Earth's orbital speed β = 10⁻⁴, γ − 1 = 5 × 10⁻⁹. One caution: the term vx/c² is small compared with t only while x ≪ c²t/v, so the Galilean form holds for ordinary distances and times, not for arbitrarily large separations.

## Limiting case 2: v → c

γ grows without limit, and for v ≥ c the factor √(1 − β²) is zero or imaginary: no real transformation exists. Within this derivation, no inertial frame can move at or beyond c relative to another.

## Checks to quote in an answer

- Dimensions: β and γ are pure numbers; vx/c² has units (m s⁻¹)(m)/(m² s⁻²) = s, matching t.
- Origin: x′ = 0 exactly when x = vt.
- Sign: the inverse has +v because S moves at −v as seen from S′.

### 6. Core: relativity of simultaneity

*Objective:* Prove that events simultaneous in S at different places are not simultaneous in S′, give the size and sign of the time gap, and state when the time order is frame-independent.

## Definition

Two events are simultaneous in S if t₁ = t₂ there. In Galilean physics (t′ = t) they would then be simultaneous in every frame.

## Derivation (the 2019 question, part i)

From t′ = γ(t − vx/c²), for two events

$$ t₂′ − t₁′ = γ[(t₂ − t₁) − v(x₂ − x₁)/c²] $$

If t₂ = t₁ the bracket is −vΔx/c², so

$$ Δt′ = −γvΔx/c² $$

This is nonzero whenever Δx ≠ 0 and v ≠ 0. Events at the same x stay simultaneous. As v → 0 (or c → ∞) the gap vanishes, the Galilean case.

## Size and sign

Take Δx = x₂ − x₁ = 600 m and v = 0.6c with γ = 1.25: Δt′ = −1.25 × 0.6 × 600 / 3 × 10⁸ = −1.5 μs. The negative sign means event 2, with the larger x, happens first in S′. This is plausible: S′ moves towards +x, towards event 2.

## Why it happens

A flash at the midpoint of a carriage reaches both ends together in the carriage frame. For an observer outside, with the carriage moving, the front wall runs away from the light and the rear wall runs towards it, yet the light still travels at c in both directions (postulate 2), so the rear is reached first. The disagreement about 'at the same time' is forced by constant c (Mathur §3.7.3).

## When the order of events can be reversed

For two events, Δt′ = γΔt(1 − vΔx/(c²Δt)).

- If |Δx| < c|Δt| (timelike separation), then |Δx/Δt| < c and v < c, so the bracket stays positive and the sign of Δt′ equals the sign of Δt in every frame. A cause cannot be reversed with its effect.
- If |Δx| > c|Δt| (spacelike), the bracket can be zero or negative: a frame with v = c²Δt/Δx exists (v < c exactly because |Δx| > c|Δt|) in which the events are simultaneous, and faster frames reverse the order.

Time dilation and length contraction use this same bookkeeping and are treated in PH-029 (Day 10; see PH-029); in particular a length is the separation of two ends measured simultaneously in the observer's frame, which is why simultaneity must come first.

### 7. Preview: velocity addition derived (home topic PH-030, Day 11)

*Objective:* Derive the relativistic velocity-addition formulas from the Lorentz transformation, check them against the Galilean limit and the invariance of c, and use them in numbers.

> Early preview: velocity addition belongs to PH-030 (Day 11); this lesson derives it now only because the 2024 composition question depends on it.

## Set-up

A particle has velocity components uₓ = dx/dt, u_y = dy/dt, u_z = dz/dt in S and u′ₓ = dx′/dt′ and so on in S′. S′ moves at +v along x. (Conventions of module 1.) The Galilean rule u′ₓ = uₓ − v is from PH-001; here the Lorentz rule is derived.

## Derivation

Differentiate x′ = γ(x − vt), y′ = y, z′ = z, t′ = γ(t − vx/c²) (γ and v are constants):

$$ dx′ = γ(dx − v dt),  dy′ = dy,  dz′ = dz,  dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²) $$

Divide dx′ by dt′ and divide top and bottom by dt:

$$ u′ₓ = dx′/dt′ = (dx/dt − v)/(1 − v(dx/dt)/c²) = (uₓ − v)/(1 − vuₓ/c²) $$

For the transverse components the denominator is the same dt′ but the numerator is unchanged:

$$ u′_y = u_y/[γ(1 − vuₓ/c²)],  u′_z = u_z/[γ(1 − vuₓ/c²)] $$

The inverse (replace v by −v and swap primes) is, for the useful case of motion along x,

$$ uₓ = (u′ₓ + v)/(1 + vu′ₓ/c²) $$

Here v is the velocity of S′ relative to S, u′ is measured in S′ and u in S.

## Checks

- **Galilean limit**: if v and u′ are much less than c, vu′/c² ≪ 1 and u ≈ u′ + v.
- **Light**: u′ₓ = c gives u = (c + v)/(1 + v/c) = c. And u′ₓ = −c gives (−c + v)/(1 − v/c) = −c. The speed c is the same in both frames. For light along y′ (u′ₓ = 0, u′_y = c): uₓ = v and u_y = c/γ, so the speed is √(v² + c²(1 − β²)) = c.
- **Never above c**: with a = u′/c, b = v/c, 1 − u²/c² = (1 − a²)(1 − b²)/(1 + ab)², which is positive whenever |a| < 1 and |b| < 1. Two sub-light speeds always combine to a sub-light speed.

## Numbers

A ship moves at 0.6c and fires a projectile forward at 0.5c relative to itself: u = (0.5 + 0.6)c/(1 + 0.3) = 0.846c, whereas the Galilean sum 1.1c is impossible. Fired backward (−0.5c): u = 0.1c/0.7 = 0.143c. Two ships approaching a moon at 0.8c and 0.7c: take S as one ship and S′ as the moon, v = −0.8c, u′ = −0.7c (Mathur Example 21): u = (−1.5c)/(1 + 0.56) = −0.9615c, so each ship sees the other approach at 0.9615c.

This module is an early preview, labelled as such: velocity addition is the home topic of PH-030 (Day 11), which also covers aberration, and the relativistic Doppler effect is PH-031 (Day 12). It appears here because the 2024 composition question needs it; PH-030 must link back to this module. Composing two boosts is derivation PH-028-D5.

### 8. Master: the invariant interval, light cones and the Minkowski diagram

*Objective:* Prove the invariance of the spacetime interval, classify intervals as timelike, lightlike or spacelike, and read a Minkowski diagram.

## The interval

For two events with separations Δt, Δx, Δy, Δz define

$$ Δs² = c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz² $$

Sign conventions differ between books: Mathur's §3.7.4 uses this form, while Upadhyaya §6.19 writes Δx² + Δy² + Δz² − c²Δt², the negative of it. Invariance holds either way; state which one you use. This lesson takes Δs² > 0 as timelike.

## Proof of invariance

Using Δx′ = γ(Δx − vΔt), Δt′ = γ(Δt − vΔx/c²), Δy′ = Δy, Δz′ = Δz:

$$ c²Δt′² − Δx′² = γ²[c²(Δt² − 2vΔxΔt/c² + v²Δx²/c⁴) − (Δx² − 2vΔxΔt + v²Δt²)] $$

The cross terms −2vΔxΔt and +2vΔxΔt cancel. Collecting what remains:

$$ c²Δt′² − Δx′² = γ²[c²Δt²(1 − v²/c²) − Δx²(1 − v²/c²)] = c²Δt² − Δx² $$

because γ²(1 − v²/c²) = 1. Adding the unchanged −Δy² − Δz² gives Δs′² = Δs². For the 2017 question, take the first event as the origin flash and the second as (x, y, z, t): x² + y² + z² − c²t² is the same in S and S′. Hence the wavefront equation x² + y² + z² = c²t² (value zero) holds in both frames, completing the check promised in module 4. Note that the quantity is invariant; the equation is only 'invariant in form' because its value is zero.

Time and space intervals separately are not invariant (modules 4 and 6); only the combination is.

## Classification

| Type | Condition | Frame property | Invariant meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timelike | Δs² > 0 (light has time to cross the gap) | frame exists with Δx′ = 0, v = Δx/Δt | proper time τ = Δs/c |
| Lightlike | Δs² = 0 | a light ray joins them | same in all frames |
| Spacelike | Δs² < 0 (light cannot cross the gap in time) | frame exists with Δt′ = 0, v = c²Δt/Δx | proper distance √(−Δs²) |

Check against Mathur Example 7: with lengths in light-seconds, events at (x = 0, t = 0) and (x = 5, t = 4 s) have Δs² = 16 − 25 = −9 (light-second)², spacelike; the frame with Δt′ = 0 has v = c²Δt/Δx = 0.8c. (Mathur's wording for part (ii)(a) says 'earlier' but sets Δt′ = +2, which is 'later'; check signs yourself.) Mathur Example 7 (p136) also computes S² = Δx² − c²Δt² = +9c², the opposite sign to this lesson's Δs²; invariance and the classification are unchanged.

## Minkowski diagram

Plot ct upward and x to the right. Light rays are the 45° lines. The worldline of the S′ origin (x = vt) is the ct′ axis, tilted towards the light line with tan(angle) = β. The line t′ = 0 is ct = βx, the x′ axis, tilted by the same angle on the other side. Light rays stay at 45° in S′ as well. Events inside the upper cone are in the absolute future; the left and right wedges are 'elsewhere'. Upadhyaya §6.19 writes the same geometry with an imaginary fourth coordinate x₄ = ict so that Δs² becomes an ordinary sum of squares; four-vector momentum is a later topic.

### 9. Exam: the seven past questions and the Optional answer format

## What the seven questions ask

- 2018 Q4(a), 15 marks: a rod at angle θ₀ to x′ in its rest frame, moving at v in the lab: find length and orientation (modules 4 and 6 plus PH-029).
- 2019 Q2(d), 15 marks: (i) simultaneity (module 6); (ii) mean life of a moving meson (PH-029).
- 2021 Q1(c), 10 marks: two explosions, 2.0 ms and 100 km apart; time gap in the frame where they are at the same place (module 8, numerical N4).
- 2023 Q1(c), 10 marks: consequences of the Lorentz transformations for length and time (modules 4 to 6, PH-029).
- 2024 Q4(c)(i), 15 marks: compose three inertial frames (module 7, derivation D5).
- 2025 Q2(c), 15 marks, verified text: "Show that the electromagnetic wave equation is invariant under Lorentz transformations." (derivation D6).

## Five-part answer format

1. **Set-up**: sketch S and S′, state the conventions (v along +x, parallel axes, right-handed, origins coincide at t = t′ = 0).
2. **Law used**: write the Lorentz transformation and its inverse, naming the postulates if you derive it.
3. **Working**: every algebra step; do not skip expanding squares or the cancelling cross terms.
4. **Result**: boxed, in symbols first, then numbers with units.
5. **Checks**: v ≪ c limit, dimensions, and (where relevant) c unchanged.

## Timing and structure

As a practice target, not an official allotment: about 12 minutes for 10 marks and about 18 minutes for 15 marks. Per the project brief, Paper I has Q1 and Q5 compulsory, each with several 10-mark short parts; verify against the paper instructions.

## Traps

- Using t′ = t, or forgetting the −vx/c² term.
- Wrong sign of v: if S′ moves at +v, x′ = γ(x − vt), but the inverse has +v.
- Confusing which frame holds the proper interval (events at the same place) before applying a formula from PH-029.

## Guided problem A: transform an event and check it

Event: x = 900 m, t = 4.0 μs. S′ moves at v = 0.6c along +x. Find x′, t′ and check with the interval.

*Thinking.* Which frame is which? The event is given in S; S′ is moving, so use the forward transformation. Compute γ first: 1 − 0.36 = 0.64, √0.64 = 0.8, γ = 1.25. Convert units before substituting: v = 0.6 × 3 × 10⁸ = 1.8 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, vt = 1.8 × 10⁸ × 4 × 10⁻⁶ = 720 m.

$$ x′ = 1.25(900 − 720) = 225 m,   t′ = 1.25(4.0 × 10⁻⁶ − 1.8 × 10⁸ × 900/(3 × 10⁸)²) $$

The second term is 0.6 × 900/3 × 10⁸ = 1.8 μs, so t′ = 1.25 × 2.2 μs = 2.75 μs. Check: Δs² = (ct)² − x² = (1200)² − (900)² = 630 000 m²; in S′: (ct′)² − x′² = (825)² − (225)² = 680 625 − 50 625 = 630 000 m². The Galilean answer would be x′ = 180 m and t′ = 4.0 μs, so you can see the size of the relativistic correction.

## Guided problem B: the 2021 explosions

Two explosions occur Δt = 2.0 ms apart and Δx = 100 km apart in observer 1's frame. Another observer sees both at the same place. Find the time gap for observer 2.

*Thinking.* 'Same place' means Δx′ = 0, so Δt′ is the proper time. The interval is invariant, so skip finding v: c²Δt′² = c²Δt² − Δx². Check separation type first: cΔt = 3 × 10⁸ × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 6.0 × 10⁵ m is greater than 1.0 × 10⁵ m, so it is timelike and the frame exists (v = Δx/Δt = 5 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ = 0.167c). Then

$$ Δt′ = √(Δt² − (Δx/c)²) = √((2.0 × 10⁻³)² − (3.33 × 10⁻⁴)²) = 1.97 × 10⁻³ s $$

This is a worked analysis of the question, not an official key. Check: Δt′ < Δt, and the Galilean answer would be 2.0 ms.

### Notes: Definitions

- Event: a point in space at an instant, specified by (x, y, z, t) in a given frame.
- Inertial frame: a frame in which a force-free body moves with constant velocity; any frame moving uniformly relative to one is also inertial (PH-001, module 2).
- Lorentz transformation: the linear rule that relates the coordinates of one event measured in two inertial frames in uniform relative motion, consistent with a constant speed of light.
- β = v/c and γ = 1/√(1 − β²); γ ≥ 1 and γ → 1 as v → 0.
- Spacetime interval between two events: Δs² = c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz² (sign convention stated; some books use the negative).
- Timelike, lightlike, spacelike: Δs² > 0, = 0, < 0 under the convention above.
- Simultaneous events: equal time coordinate in a given frame.

### Notes: Key equations (S′ moves at +v along x of S, parallel right-handed axes, origins coincide at t = t′ = 0)

- x′ = γ(x − vt), y′ = y, z′ = z, t′ = γ(t − vx/c²).
- Inverse: x = γ(x′ + vt′), y = y′, z = z′, t = γ(t′ + vx′/c²).
- Velocity addition: u′ₓ = (uₓ − v)/(1 − vuₓ/c²); u′_y = u_y/[γ(1 − vuₓ/c²)]; inverse uₓ = (u′ₓ + v)/(1 + vu′ₓ/c²).
- Simultaneity gap for events simultaneous in S: Δt′ = −γvΔx/c².
- Interval invariance: c²Δt′² − Δx′² − Δy′² − Δz′² = c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz².
- Composition of collinear boosts V then V′: W = (V + V′)/(1 + VV′/c²) and γ_W = γ_V γ_V′ (1 + VV′/c²).
- Wave operator invariance: ∇²φ − (1/c²)∂²φ/∂t² keeps its form under the transformation.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton (module 4 and PH-028-D1)

- Assumptions: inertial frames; linear transformation; y′ = y, z′ = z; x′ = γ(x − vt); inverse x = γ(x′ + vt′) with the same γ.
- Eliminate x′: x = γ²(x − vt) + γvt′ gives t′ = γt − (γ² − 1)x/(γv).
- Light pulse x = ct, x′ = ct′ gives γ²v² = c²(γ² − 1), so γ² = 1/(1 − v²/c²).
- Use γ² − 1 = γ²β² to get t′ = γ(t − vx/c²).
- Checks: v ≪ c gives the Galilean rule; x′ = 0 at x = vt; interval invariance (module 8).

### Notes: Galilean versus Lorentz transformation

- Compare the two rules row by row; the Lorentz rule reduces to the Galilean one for v ≪ c.

### Notes: Cause-and-effect chain

- Maxwell's theory fixes the speed of light in vacuum, so postulate 2 makes it the same for all inertial observers.
- A constant c cannot be reconciled with t′ = t, so the Galilean rule must change.
- The new rule mixes space and time (t′ depends on x), so simultaneity becomes frame-dependent.
- The same mixing leaves only the combination c²Δt² − Δx² unchanged, which defines the invariant interval.
- Because the transformation is linear and consistent with c, velocities combine by (u + v)/(1 + uv/c²) and never exceed c.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Invariant versus equal to zero: x² + y² + z² − c²t² has the same value in both frames; the wavefront equation is the case where that value is zero.
- Lorentz transformation versus time dilation and length contraction: the transformation is the rule; dilation and contraction (PH-029) are special consequences with specific conditions on which events are chosen.
- Primed versus unprimed: S′ is the moving frame; always say which frame measures each quantity.
- Forward versus inverse: x′ = γ(x − vt) has −v; the inverse has +v.
- Event versus object: an event is a point of spacetime; an object has a worldline made of events.
- Speed of a frame versus speed of a particle: v is the relative velocity of the frames; u and u′ are the particle's velocities.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- Lengths perpendicular to the motion do not change (y′ = y, z′ = z).
- Events at the same x (Δx = 0) that are simultaneous in S remain simultaneous in S′.
- For timelike separation the time order is the same in all frames; for spacelike separation a frame exists in which the order reverses.
- The Galilean limit needs both v ≪ c and x ≪ c²t/v for the term vx/c² to be negligible in t′.
- No real γ exists for v ≥ c; the derivation only covers v < c.

### Core concepts

- The Galilean rule predicts c ± v for light and fails the wavefront test; the Lorentz rule is built to keep c constant.
- Two postulates: relativity principle and constancy of c in every inertial frame.
- The transformation is linear, y′ = y and z′ = z, x′ = γ(x − vt), t′ = γ(t − vx/c²); the inverse has +v.
- Simultaneity is relative; Δt′ = −γvΔx/c² for events simultaneous in S.
- Velocity addition keeps all speeds below c and keeps c invariant.
- The interval c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz² is invariant and classifies event pairs.

**Q.** Why does the Galilean transformation fail for the sphere of light?

**A.** Substituting x′ = x − vt, t′ = t leaves extra terms −2vxt + v²t², so the wavefront is not a sphere in S′.

**Q.** Why can no inertial frame move at c relative to another within the Lorentz transformation?

**A.** γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) is infinite at v = c and not real beyond it.

**Q.** Why are y and z unchanged?

**A.** By the symmetry of the identical-rod argument, a transverse length that contracted for one observer would contract for the other, which is contradictory.
