## GS-093: Important crafts, textiles and regional schools

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Important crafts, textiles and regional schools and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Painting traditions of the people

NCERT stresses the thin line between people's art and craft: both join creativity, need and aesthetics. Mithila art fills every space with symbolic birds, fish, snakes, sun and moon, using bamboo twigs with cotton or straw and colours once drawn from minerals, flowers, lamp-black and turmeric. Warli Chowk paintings mark marriage, harvest and sowing around Palaghat, the fertility goddess. Gond painting of Mandla has become colourful depictions of animals, humans and plants. Pithoro wall paintings of the Rathva Bhils separate a heavenly register of riders from an earthly one. Scrolls appear as pata, pachedi and phad: Bengal patuas sing their vertical patas, while Rajasthan's bhopas perform phads painted by Joshi artists.

### Metal craft and regional weaves

Dhokra metal casting uses a clay core, saal resin threads for detail and layered clay moulds before molten metal replaces the resin; terracotta votives are made by potters across regions. Textiles carry equally strong regional identities. The Ministry of Textiles cites Pashmina shawls of Jammu and Kashmir, Kasaragod sarees of Kerala and Patan Patola of Gujarat, and its showcases feature Banarasi, Chanderi, Jamdani, Kota Doria, Maheshwari, Khun, Murshidabad, Tussar, Venkatagiri and Kuthampully weaves. GI tags awarded in 2023-24 went to Barabanki handloom products (Uttar Pradesh), Chedibutta sarees (Tamil Nadu), Jodhpur Bandhej (Rajasthan), Basohli Pashmina (Jammu and Kashmir), Rangwali Pichhoda (Uttarakhand) and the Tangail, Garad and Korial sarees of West Bengal. Handloom remains a rural, women-led household industry: the 2019-20 census found 35.22 lakh workers, over 72 per cent of them women.

### Markets, decline and protection

Before machine industry, Indian silk and cotton dominated world markets via Surat, Masulipatnam and Hooghly. After the 1760s the East India Company used gomasthas and advances to bind weavers, and from the early nineteenth century Manchester cloth flooded India: piece-goods fell from 33 per cent of exports in 1811-12 to 3 per cent by 1850-51. After Independence, NCERT notes, crafts were revived for commercial production and sold through State emporia. Today protection is legal and market-based: the Handlooms Act, 1985 reserves 11 articles for handlooms, the GI Act, 1999 guards regional names (106 handloom products registered by 2026), and the Handloom Mark and India Handloom Brand certify authenticity.
