## PH-063: Fibre optics, numerical aperture and attenuation

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Logarithms and the decibel

Fibre loss is quoted in **decibels (dB)** because power can fall by a factor of a million over a long link, and a logarithm turns a huge range into small numbers. If a power P_in goes in and P_out comes out, the loss is

$$ loss (dB) = 10 log10 (P_in / P_out) $$

The symbol log10 means 'the power of 10 that gives this number': log10(100) = 2 because 10 squared is 100; log10(1000) = 3; log10(1) = 0; log10(0.1) = -1. So a loss of 10 dB means P_out = P_in / 10; 20 dB means one hundredth; 30 dB means one thousandth. A loss of 3 dB means about one half (log10 2 = 0.301, so 10 times 0.301 = 3.01 dB). The great convenience: **losses in dB simply add** along a chain (fibre, splice, connector), while power fractions would have to be multiplied. To go back from dB to a power fraction use P_out / P_in = 10^(-loss/10). Example: 25 dB gives 10^(-2.5) = 0.00316. A **dBm** is a power expressed in decibels relative to 1 milliwatt: P(dBm) = 10 log10 (P / 1 mW); 1 mW is 0 dBm and 10 mW is 10 dBm. (Standard convention, not in the extract; verify with your course notes.)

### Bridge: Snell's law, total internal reflection and complementary angles

**Refractive index** n of a medium is the speed of light in vacuum divided by its speed in the medium, n = c/v; glass has n of about 1.5, air about 1.0003 (taken as 1). A medium with larger n is optically **denser**. **Snell's law** for a ray crossing from medium 1 to medium 2 is n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2), with both angles measured from the **normal** (the line at right angles to the boundary). Going from the denser to the rarer medium (n1 > n2) the ray bends away from the normal; as theta1 grows, theta2 reaches 90 degrees at the **critical angle** phi_c with sin(phi_c) = n2/n1. For any larger angle of incidence there is no refracted ray and the light is totally reflected: **total internal reflection (TIR)**, with the angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence (Brij Lal, PDF pp. 676 to 677). TIR needs two things: light travelling in the denser medium, and an angle of incidence above phi_c.

One identity is used repeatedly: for two angles that add to 90 degrees, **sin(90 degrees - x) = cos x**, and cos x = sqrt(1 - sin^2 x). Angles in the fibre are measured two ways: from the **axis** (the long centre line) or from the **normal** to the wall. A ray at angle theta from the axis meets the wall at angle of incidence phi = 90 degrees - theta, because the normal to the wall is perpendicular to the axis.

### 1. Orientation: a light pipe, its three layers and the three questions

*Objective:* Describe what an optical fibre is, name its three layers with sizes, and state the three questions (guiding, light collection, survival) that organise the topic.

## The picture first
An **optical fibre** is a hair-thin rod of glass (or clear plastic) that carries light from one end to the other, even round a gentle bend. The light stays inside because of **total internal reflection (TIR)**: each time a ray reaches the wall, it bounces back instead of escaping (Brij Lal, PDF p. 673). Telephone and internet links, and the thin cameras doctors pass inside the body, rest on this one idea. The book gives a human hair as about 70 µm across; 1 µm (micrometre) is one millionth of a metre.

## Three layers
A practical fibre has three coaxial regions, one inside the next (PDF pp. 673 to 674):
- **Core**: the inner, light-carrying region, index n₁. The book gives a diameter of about 8.5 µm to 62.5 µm.
- **Cladding**: glass around the core, outer diameter about 125 µm, with a slightly **lower** index n₂ < n₁.
- **Buffer coating (sheath)**: elastic plastic of 250 µm to 900 µm overall, protecting against abrasion and moisture. It takes no part in guiding.

Notice the scale: the core is thinner than the cladding, which is thinner than the coating.

## The system around it
A fibre alone does nothing. A basic link (PDF p. 675) has a **transmitter**, an LED or a laser diode small enough to match the core; the **fibre**; and a **receiver**, a semiconductor photodiode. The electrical signal is turned into light pulses by varying the current through the source, and at the far end the photodiode turns the pulses back into current.

## Three questions that organise this topic
1. **Guiding**: under what condition does a ray stay inside the core? (module 2)
2. **Light collection**: what cone of incoming rays is accepted, and how is that measured? This gives the **acceptance angle** and the **numerical aperture (NA)**. (modules 2 and 3)
3. **Survival**: how much power is left after a long run, and why is some lost? This is **attenuation**. (modules 4 and 5)

## What this lesson does not repeat
Pulse spreading (dispersion) is taught in Day 17 lesson PH-064; modes, the V-number and single-mode design in Day 18 lesson PH-065; the step-index versus parabolic-index comparison in Day 18 lesson PH-208. Whenever a result from those is needed it is quoted with a link.

### 2. Foundation: conditions for guiding, why a cladding, and the acceptance angle (derivation 1)

*Objective:* State the two guiding conditions, explain why the cladding is needed, and derive the acceptance angle and numerical aperture NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) with checks.

## Two conditions for guiding (PDF p. 678)
1. The core index n₁ must be slightly greater than the cladding index n₂.
2. At the core-cladding boundary the angle of incidence φ (from the normal to the boundary) must exceed the critical angle φ_c, where sin φ_c = n₂/n₁ (bridge B2).

A ray meeting the wall at less than φ_c refracts into the cladding and is lost.

## Why not a bare fibre? (PDF pp. 674 to 675)
A bare glass strand in air does guide light, but the book lists the problems the cladding removes:
- The surroundings must be the same along the whole length; dust, moisture or a touching object lets light leak out. A small part of the light penetrates a little beyond the surface (an *evanescent* field), so anything touching the surface can draw energy away.
- Scratches change the local normal, so rays are scattered out.
- Bare fibres packed in a bundle exchange light by *frustrated* total internal reflection across the tiny gaps; a cladding of sufficient thickness stops this crosstalk.
- A cladding **reduces the acceptance cone**, so fewer steep, slow rays are admitted and pulses spread less, allowing a higher bit rate.
- It also protects mechanically.

## Two easily confused angles
Let θ_r be the angle between a ray inside the core and the axis. The angle of incidence at the wall is φ = 90° − θ_r. TIR needs φ ≥ φ_c, that is θ_r ≤ θ_c = 90° − φ_c, so cos θ_c = sin φ_c = n₂/n₁.
- **Critical angle** φ_c: measured from the normal to the wall.
- **Critical propagation angle** θ_c: measured from the axis, the complement of φ_c (PDF p. 679).

## Derivation 1: acceptance angle and numerical aperture
*Assumptions:* straight step-index fibre; meridional rays (rays in a plane containing the axis); flat end face perpendicular to the axis; launching medium of index n₀ (air: n₀ = 1). *Conventions:* θ_i is the ray angle with the axis outside, θ_r inside the core, both positive; φ is measured from the wall normal.

1. Snell's law at the end face: n₀ sin θ_i = n₁ sin θ_r.
2. Geometry: θ_r = 90° − φ, so sin θ_r = cos φ.
3. Hence n₀ sin θ_i = n₁ cos φ.
4. Guiding needs φ ≥ φ_c, so cos φ ≤ cos φ_c. The largest allowed θ_i belongs to φ = φ_c: n₀ sin θ₀ = n₁ cos φ_c.
5. cos φ_c = √(1 − sin² φ_c) = √(1 − n₂²/n₁²) = √(n₁² − n₂²)/n₁.
6. Therefore n₀ sin θ₀ = √(n₁² − n₂²). For air:

$$ NA = sin θ₀ = √(n₁² − n₂²) $$

θ₀ is the **acceptance angle**; spinning the ray about the axis gives the **acceptance cone** of full angle 2θ₀ (PDF p. 680). The **numerical aperture** is defined as NA = sin θ₀ (PDF p. 681).

*Dimension check:* indices are pure numbers, so NA is dimensionless. *Limits:* n₂ → n₁ gives NA → 0 (nothing guided); if n₁² − n₂² ≥ 1 with air, every ray from air is accepted (θ₀ = 90°).

## In terms of Δ
The **fractional index change** is Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁ (PDF p. 680), about 0.01 in practice. Since n₁² − n₂² = (n₁ − n₂)(n₁ + n₂) = n₁Δ · n₁(2 − Δ), we get NA = n₁√(Δ(2 − Δ)) ≈ n₁√(2Δ) for small Δ (the book's form, Eq. 24.15).

## Numbers (recomputed with python)
- n₁ = 1.50, n₂ = 1.48 (the study-data application question): NA = √(2.25 − 2.1904) = 0.2441, θ₀ = 14.13°, θ_c = 9.37°.
- n₁ = 1.50, n₂ = 1.43: φ_c = 72.43°, θ_c = 17.57°, NA = 0.4529, θ₀ = 26.93°.
The same derivation appears in Day 17 lesson PH-064 (module 2); here it is the base for the new material in module 3.

### 3. Core: numerical aperture in use, other launch media, and skip distance (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Use NA for any launch medium, explain what NA does and does not depend on, and derive the skip distance and the number of reflections per metre.

## What NA depends on
NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) depends **only on the two refractive indices**, not on the core diameter (PDF p. 681). The book gives a range of about 0.13 to 0.50 for typical fibres. A large NA means light from a source is easier to couple in. The price is more allowed ray directions, hence more spread in arrival times: the delay between fastest and slowest ray grows as NA² (Day 17 lesson PH-064, module 4). That trade-off is why long-distance links use small-NA fibre (Day 18 lesson PH-065).

## A different launching medium
The derivation gives n₀ sin θ₀ = NA, so sin θ₀ = NA/n₀.
- NA = 0.20 launched from water (n₀ = 1.33): sin θ₀ = 0.1504, θ₀ = 8.65° = 8°39′ (the book's Problem 9).
- NA = 0.4529: in air θ₀ = 26.93°, in water 19.91°. The fibre has not changed; the cone has narrowed because light entering from a denser medium is already bent towards the axis.

## Meridional and skew rays
A **meridional ray** crosses the axis; a **skew ray** spirals round without crossing it (PDF pp. 683 to 684). The formula above is for meridional rays. The book notes that skew rays are a substantial share of guided rays and add to the light-gathering capacity, so the real accepted cone is somewhat wider than the meridional estimate. In examinations use the meridional result.

## Derivation 2: skip distance and number of reflections
The **skip distance** l_s is the distance along the axis between two successive reflections of a ray (PDF p. 681).

*Assumptions:* straight step-index fibre of core radius a, meridional ray, angle θ_r with the axis inside the core.
1. Between a reflection at the lower wall and the next at the upper wall, the ray rises across the full core diameter 2a.
2. In a right triangle with rise 2a and run l_s: tan θ_r = 2a / l_s.
3. So l_s = 2a / tan θ_r.
4. From the launch: sin θ_r = (n₀/n₁) sin θ_i, so cos θ_r = √(1 − (n₀ sin θ_i / n₁)²). Then

$$ l_s = 2a √(n₁² − n₀² sin² θ_i) / (n₀ sin θ_i) $$

5. Over a length L the number of reflections is N = L / l_s = L tan θ_r / (2a).

*Checks:* l_s is a length (a times a pure number). A ray along the axis (θ_r → 0) has l_s → ∞ and N = 0: it never touches the wall. A steeper ray reflects more often.

## Numbers (recomputed)
n₁ = 1.5, launched from air, a = 25 µm:

| θ_i | θ_r | l_s | N per metre |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10° | 6.65° | 429.0 µm | 2,331 |
| 30° | 19.47° | 141.4 µm | 7,071 |
| 60° | 35.26° | 70.7 µm | 14,142 |

The book's example (θ_i = 30°) prints l_s = 141 µm and 7,092 reflections per metre; 7,092 is simply 1/141 µm, so it is a rounding of 7,071, not an error.

## Why each reflection must be almost perfect
At 30°, 7,071 reflections occur in one metre. If each reflection lost only 0.01 % of the power, the power surviving one metre would be 0.9999^7071 = 0.493, a loss of 3.07 dB in a single metre; over a kilometre the light would be gone. Total internal reflection is lossless in principle (PDF p. 677), and that is why a fibre can carry light for kilometres (original illustration).

### 4. Core: attenuation, the exponential law and the decibel (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Define attenuation, convert between α in per km and α in dB/km, and compute output power, loss and link length from given data.

## What attenuation is
As light travels along a fibre its power falls. **Attenuation** is this loss of optical power (PDF p. 692). For a uniform fibre the fall is exponential, like radioactive decay:

$$ P_out = P_in e^(−αL) $$

Here P_in is the power launched, P_out the power at distance L, and α the **attenuation coefficient**, in km⁻¹ when L is in km (book Eq. 24.34). Taking the natural logarithm: α = (1/L) ln(P_in / P_out).

## The engineer's form: dB per km
The loss in decibels (bridge B1) per unit length is

$$ α_dB = (10 / L) log₁₀ (P_in / P_out)   [dB per km] $$

(book Eq. 24.36). This is what data sheets quote. Ideal fibre: P_out = P_in, α = 0.

## Derivation 3: converting α and α_dB
1. From the exponential law, P_in / P_out = e^(αL).
2. Take log₁₀: log₁₀(P_in / P_out) = αL log₁₀ e.
3. Multiply by 10/L: α_dB = 10 α log₁₀ e = 4.343 α.
4. Reverse: α = 0.2303 α_dB. Example: 0.5 dB/km is α = 0.1151 km⁻¹.
5. Surviving fraction for any length: P_out / P_in = 10^(−α_dB L / 10) = e^(−αL).

*Checks:* αL is dimensionless; doubling L doubles the dB loss; losses in dB add for sections in series. The result does not depend on P_in because the fibre is a linear system.

## The 2024 numerical (verified question 2024 Q3(b)(ii))
10 mW, 50 km, 0.5 dB/km.
1. Total loss = 0.5 dB/km × 50 km = 25 dB.
2. Fraction surviving = 10^(−2.5) = 3.162 × 10⁻³.
3. P_out = 10 mW × 3.162 × 10⁻³ = 3.16 × 10⁻² mW = **31.6 µW**.
4. Cross-check by the natural form: αL = 0.1151 × 50 = 5.756 and e^(−5.756) = 3.162 × 10⁻³ ✓. In dBm: 10 dBm − 25 dB = −15 dBm, and −15 dBm = 31.6 µW ✓.

## The book's worked examples, recomputed
- Example 24.12: 1 mW in, 0.3 mW out over 100 m: α_dB = (10/0.1) log₁₀(1/0.3) = 52.3 dB/km ✓.
- Example 24.13: 15 % of the power is lost in a fibre of length ½ km (L = ½ km, confirmed by the printed formula 10/0.5). The surviving fraction is 0.85, so α_dB = (10/0.5) log₁₀(1/0.85) = 1.41 dB/km. The book prints 16.48 dB/km, which is 20 log₁₀(1/0.15): it treated 15 % as the **output** fraction. A loss of 15 % means 85 % arrives. Always ask which fraction the question gives.
- Problem 20: 120 µW in, 3 µW out over 8 km: loss 10 log₁₀ 40 = 16.02 dB, 2.00 dB/km; for a 10 km link 20.03 dB, a power ratio of 100. The printed "numerical input/output ratio 100" belongs to the 10 km link; for the 8 km fibre the ratio is 40.

## How far can a signal go? (original illustration, assumed numbers)
Suppose 1 mW (0 dBm) is launched, the receiver needs at least −30 dBm, and 3 dB is kept as safety margin. The fibre may lose 30 − 3 = 27 dB. At 0.2 dB/km the length is 135 km; at 0.5 dB/km, 54 km; at 2.2 dB/km, 12.3 km. The loss values 0.2 and 2.2 dB/km are the book's window figures (module 5); the receiver level and margin are assumed for illustration (standard practice; verify).

### 5. Core: why light is lost: absorption, Rayleigh scattering, bending and the low-loss windows

*Objective:* Name the intrinsic and extrinsic loss mechanisms with their physical causes, explain the wavelength dependence of the loss, and say why 1300 nm and 1550 nm are used.

## Two families of loss (PDF p. 692)
The book divides attenuation into **intrinsic** loss, caused by the glass itself and the impurities in it, and **extrinsic** loss, caused by external forces such as bending. (Loss of *shape* of a pulse is a different thing, called distortion or dispersion, taught in Day 17 lesson PH-064.)

## Intrinsic 1: material absorption
Atoms and impurities in the glass absorb light and turn it into vibration or other energy (PDF p. 693). The worst culprit is the **hydroxyl ion (OH⁻)**, left over from water entering during manufacture or from humidity. Transition-metal ions (copper, nickel, chromium, vanadium, manganese) absorb in and near the visible. Even perfectly pure glass absorbs at the two ends of the spectrum: strongly in the ultraviolet (electronic absorption) and in the infrared (vibrational absorption). Better manufacture can cut the metal impurities almost to nothing, but OH cannot be removed completely. The book says absorption is lowest near 1.3 µm and (as printed) is 3 to 5 % of the attenuation.

## Intrinsic 2: Rayleigh scattering
Glass is frozen from a melt, so its density varies slightly from place to place on a microscopic scale. Density changes mean refractive-index changes, and each small variation scatters light in all directions (PDF p. 693). This **Rayleigh scattering** cannot be eliminated. Its loss varies as **1/λ⁴**, so it rises steeply at short wavelengths. The book says it is about 96 % of attenuation (as printed; verify) and sets a lower usable limit near 0.8 µm; the upper limit, near 1700 nm, comes from infrared absorption.

*A numerical feel for 1/λ⁴ (original, indicative):* from 850 nm to 1550 nm a pure 1/λ⁴ law cuts scattering by (1550/850)⁴ = 11.06 times. The book's table (next section) shows 2.2 dB/km near 850 nm and 0.2 dB/km near 1550 nm, a ratio of 11. At 1300 nm pure scattering would give 2.2/5.47 = 0.40 dB/km but the table lists 0.6, so absorption adds something there.

## The three windows (PDF p. 694)
A plot of loss against wavelength has a minimum; the bands of low loss are called **transmission windows**. The book lists three, matched to the sources and detectors available:

| Wavelength band | Approximate loss |
|---|---|
| 820 to 880 nm | 2.2 dB/km |
| 1200 to 1320 nm | 0.6 dB/km |
| 1550 to 1610 nm | 0.2 dB/km |

The 1550 to 1610 nm band is best for low loss. About 1300 nm is best for low intramodal dispersion, so each window has its use (the dispersion side is in lesson PH-064). Note the book's typical-loss remark of "about 4 dB/km" in its merits list (PDF p. 708) does not match this table; quote the table.

## Extrinsic loss: bending (PDF pp. 694 to 695)
- **Macrobend**: a large visible bend. At the bend the rays meet the wall at smaller angles of incidence than before; the book attributes the effect to strain altering the index and critical angle. Either way, TIR fails locally and light refracts out. Makers therefore specify a **minimum bend radius**.
- **Microbend**: a small, often invisible kink from pressure, temperature change, tension, crushing, or winding on a spool. Light scatters at the small bends and escapes into the cladding.

*A ray-model estimate of the minimum bend radius (the lesson's own; verify).* For a fibre of core radius a bent to centre-line radius R, a ray running parallel to the axis along the inner edge meets the outer wall at an angle of incidence φ with sin φ = (R − a)/(R + a). TIR requires sin φ ≥ n₂/n₁, which gives R ≥ a(n₁ + n₂)/(n₁ − n₂) ≈ 2a/Δ. For a = 25 µm, n₁ = 1.5, Δ = 0.01 this is about 5 mm. Treat 5 mm as a rough ray-picture bound only (derivation 4 in the practice section).

### 6. Core: fibre types and materials, recapped (step-index, graded-index, single-mode, plastic and PCS)

*Objective:* Classify fibres by profile, modes and material, quote typical sizes, and decide which type suits a short or a long link.

## Three ways to classify (PDF p. 685)
- **By refractive-index profile**: *step-index* (the core index is constant and drops abruptly at the cladding) or *graded-index (GRIN)* (the core index is highest on the axis and falls smoothly to the cladding value).
- **By modes**: *single-mode* (one allowed ray path) or *multimode* (many).
- **By material**: glass core and cladding, plastic core and cladding, or *plastic-clad silica (PCS)*.

In practice three types are used (PDF pp. 686 to 688):

| Type | Core | Cladding | Light source | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-mode step-index | 8 to 12 µm | about 125 µm | laser diode | smallest NA and Δ, hardest to couple into, least pulse spread |
| Multimode step-index | 50 to 100 µm | 150 to 250 µm | LED or laser | large NA, easy coupling, most spread |
| Graded-index (multimode) | about the same as step-index | about the same | LED or laser | parabolic profile (exponent 2) preferred; nearly equal path times |

The full treatment of modes, the V-number and graded-index ray paths is in Day 18 lesson PH-065; the comparison of step and parabolic spread is in lesson PH-208. Two results are enough here. The normalised frequency is V = (2πa/λ) NA, and a step-index fibre carries a single mode when V < 2.405 (PDF p. 691). A design check: with NA = 0.12 at λ = 1.3 µm the largest single-mode core radius is a = 2.405 λ / (2π NA) = 4.15 µm, a core diameter of 8.3 µm, which sits at the small end of the book's single-mode range (original check).

## Materials (PDF pp. 688 to 689)
**All-glass fibres.** The base is silica (SiO₂), index 1.458 at 850 nm. Doping with germania (GeO₂) or phosphorus pentoxide (P₂O₅) raises the index (core); doping with boria (B₂O₃) or fluorine lowers it (cladding). Examples: GeO₂·SiO₂ core with pure silica cladding, or silica core with B₂O₃·SiO₂ cladding. Losses are very low, so these serve long-distance links.

**All-plastic fibres.** Cores of perspex (PMMA, index 1.49) or polystyrene (1.59 in the text) with fluorocarbon or silicone-resin cladding. The large index step gives a large NA and a wide acceptance angle, and the fibre is cheap and flexible with a thick core (110 to 1400 µm), but it is temperature-sensitive (below about 80 °C) and lossy, so it is used in low-cost short links.

**PCS fibres.** A silica core with a polymer cladding (silicone resin, index 1.405, or Teflon-type FEP, index 1.338). Cheaper than glass, higher loss, short distances; the cladding choice applies to step-index only.

*Recomputed NAs:* silica 1.458 with silicone 1.405 gives NA = 0.390 (θ₀ = 22.9°); with FEP 1.338, NA = 0.579 (θ₀ = 35.4°). PMMA 1.49 with its copolymer cladding 1.40 (read from the garbled figure text of PDF p. 689; verify) gives NA = 0.510, in agreement with the book's 0.50. For polystyrene against 1.49 the book prints NA 0.60 and acceptance angles up to 77°; the indices (n₁ = 1.60 in the figure text, 1.59 in the running text) give NA = 0.583 or 0.555, θ₀ = 35.7° or 33.7° and a full cone of about 71° or 67°. The printed 0.60 and 77° look rounded upward; check the original before quoting them.

## Choosing a fibre
Short link, low cost, easy coupling: multimode step-index or plastic. High bit rate over many kilometres: single-mode with a laser near 1300 or 1550 nm (loss from module 5, dispersion from lesson PH-064). Intermediate: graded-index.

### 7. Master: the communication system, image bundles, sensors, merits and the two limits of a link

*Objective:* Describe a fibre communication link, coherent and incoherent bundles, one sensor, the merits over copper, and state which two effects limit a link.

## The communication link (PDF pp. 705 to 707)
A message (sound or picture) is turned into an electrical signal by a transducer, then used to **modulate** the light of an LED or semiconductor laser, which emits in the infrared at 850 nm, 1300 nm or 1550 nm. In **analog** modulation the light intensity follows the signal; in **digital** modulation the source flashes on and off, a pulse standing for 1 and no pulse for 0. Digital needs encoders, decoders and more bandwidth, but allows a greater distance for the same power, so it dominates. Along the way the signal is weakened (attenuation) and smeared (dispersion), so **repeaters** regenerate it at intervals. At the end an output coupler directs the light on to a photodiode and the electrical signal is amplified and decoded.

## Bundles and the endoscope (PDF pp. 704 to 705)
Many fibres bound together form a flexible bundle.
- **Incoherent bundle**: fibre ends are not in matching positions at the two ends, so no image passes; it simply conducts light for illumination.
- **Coherent bundle**: the fibre ends occupy the same relative positions at both ends, so a point-by-point image is carried (like pixels).

An **endoscope** (fibrescope) uses a coherent bundle to view inside the body, with a second bundle for illumination. The book says about 10,000 fibres fit a bundle 1 mm across and resolve details 70 µm apart; medical uses include bronchial, gastrointestinal and laparoscopic examination (PDF p. 705).

## Merits over copper (PDF pp. 707 to 708)
Silica is plentiful and the link is cheaper; fibres are small and light; they are insulators, so no sparks or short circuits; photons are neutral, so there is no pickup of electromagnetic or radio-frequency interference and no crosstalk, which also makes tapping hard; bandwidth is very wide; loss per kilometre is low, so repeaters can sit up to 100 km apart against about 2 km for copper. Disadvantage: installation and testing need special skills and costly equipment.

## One sensor in depth: the liquid-level detector (PDF p. 711)
A multimode fibre has a notch at one end and a chamfered tip. The fibre index is chosen **less than** the liquid's. While the tip is in air, light reaching the tip is totally reflected at the fibre-air boundary and returns to the detector. When the liquid rises and touches the tip, the index condition for TIR fails (the liquid is denser than the glass), light escapes into the liquid and the detector goes dark. This uses nothing but the module 2 condition: TIR needs the second medium to be rarer. The book's choice n_fibre < n_liquid guarantees that TIR fails in the liquid; it is sufficient, not necessary. A tip whose angle of incidence φ lies between arcsin(1/n_fibre) and arcsin(n_liquid/n_fibre) also works: glass 1.50 and water 1.33 at φ = 45° have critical angles 41.8° (air, TIR) and 62.5° (water, light escapes) (standard; verify).

Other sensors in the extract (PDF pp. 708 to 711): intensity-modulated temperature sensor (silicon layer, reflected intensity changes with temperature); phase-modulated temperature sensor (Mach-Zehnder arrangement, fringe shift); displacement sensor (reflected intensity versus gap); force sensor (microbend loss between corrugated blocks).

## Two limits of a link
A link is limited by **power** (attenuation, module 4: how far before the signal is too weak) and by **time spreading** (dispersion: how fast pulses can be sent before they overlap). The step-index figures of the 2023 question show the second: about 245 ns per km. Which limit bites first depends on the fibre: for multimode step-index it is usually dispersion; for single-mode at 1550 nm either can bind: attenuation for a narrow-line laser at a modest bit rate, chromatic dispersion at a high rate or with a broad source (the Day 17 lesson PH-064 module 6 table: D = 17 ps/(nm km), 1 nm width, 100 km limits the rate to about 0.29 Gb/s; verify).

## Traps
- Giving φ_c (72.43°) when the question says critical propagation angle (17.57°), or the reverse.
- Forgetting n₀ for a non-air launch, or quoting the full cone 2θ₀ as the acceptance angle (the book's Problems 8 and 11 do this).
- Mixing the sign of the dB: a loss of 25 dB means the output is smaller by 10^(2.5); do not multiply by 10^(2.5).
- Treating "15 % lost" as "15 % output".
- Quoting percentages of absorption and Rayleigh scattering as exact; say "about" and name the book.

### Notes: Definitions

- Optical fibre: a cylindrical dielectric waveguide (core, cladding, buffer coating) that guides light by total internal reflection; n₁ (core) is slightly larger than n₂ (cladding).
- Critical angle φ_c at the core-cladding boundary: sin φ_c = n₂/n₁, measured from the normal. Critical propagation angle θ_c = 90° − φ_c, measured from the axis, cos θ_c = n₂/n₁.
- Acceptance angle θ₀: the largest angle to the axis, outside the fibre, for which a meridional ray is guided; the acceptance cone has full angle 2θ₀. Numerical aperture NA = n₀ sin θ₀ = √(n₁² − n₂²).
- Fractional index change Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁, much less than 1 (about 0.01); NA ≈ n₁√(2Δ).
- Skip distance l_s: axial distance between successive reflections of a ray; l_s = 2a / tan θ_r.
- Attenuation: loss of optical power along the fibre; α in km⁻¹ (P_out = P_in e^(−αL)) or α_dB in dB/km.
- Meridional ray: crosses the axis; skew ray: spirals without crossing the axis.

### Notes: Equations to know

- sin φ_c = n₂/n₁; θ_c = 90° − φ_c; cos θ_c = n₂/n₁.
- n₀ sin θ₀ = √(n₁² − n₂²); in air NA = sin θ₀ = √(n₁² − n₂²) = n₁√(Δ(2 − Δ)) ≈ n₁√(2Δ).
- l_s = 2a / tan θ_r with sin θ_r = (n₀/n₁) sin θ_i; N = L/l_s = L tan θ_r / (2a).
- P_out = P_in e^(−αL); α_dB = (10/L) log₁₀(P_in/P_out); α_dB = 4.343 α; P_out/P_in = 10^(−α_dB L/10).
- Intermodal delay (from lesson PH-064): Δt = (L n₁/c)(n₁/n₂ − 1) = L n₁(n₁ − n₂)/(c n₂), the book's Example 24.11 form (244.8 ns for the 2023 numbers); the small-Δ form L n₁Δ/c gives 233 ns.
- V = (2πa/λ) NA; single mode for V < 2.405 (lesson PH-065).
- Rough ray-model bend limit (the lesson's own; verify): R ≥ a(n₁ + n₂)/(n₁ − n₂) ≈ 2a/Δ.

### Notes: Loss mechanisms: cause and remedy

- Material absorption: impurities (OH⁻, Cu, Ni, Cr, V, Mn) and the glass's own UV and IR edges convert light to other energy. Remedy: purer glass; OH⁻ cannot be fully removed; choose a wavelength where absorption is small (the book says near 1.3 µm, but the window table puts the lowest total loss at 1550 to 1610 nm; the OH⁻ absorption peak lies near 1.38 µm, standard, verify).
- Rayleigh scattering: microscopic density and index variations frozen into the glass scatter light, loss ∝ 1/λ⁴. Remedy: none in the glass; use longer wavelength (1550 nm).
- Macrobend: large bend, TIR fails locally. Remedy: respect the minimum bend radius.
- Microbend: small kinks from pressure, temperature, winding. Remedy: protective buffer and cable design, avoid crushing.
- Windows: 820 to 880 nm (2.2 dB/km), 1200 to 1320 nm (0.6 dB/km), 1550 to 1610 nm (0.2 dB/km), as printed.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- φ_c (from the normal, 72.43° here) versus θ_c (from the axis, 17.57°): complements.
- θ_c (inside the core) versus θ₀ (outside, in the launching medium): related by sin θ₀ = n₁ sin θ_c for n₀ = 1.
- Acceptance angle θ₀ versus acceptance cone angle 2θ₀.
- Attenuation (power falls, pulse shape unchanged) versus dispersion (pulse spreads in time).
- α in km⁻¹ (natural) versus α in dB/km (decibel): factor 4.343.
- Loss percentage versus surviving percentage.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: acceptance angle and NA

- Assume: straight step-index fibre, meridional ray, plane end face, launch index n₀.
- Snell at the end: n₀ sin θ_i = n₁ sin θ_r; geometry: sin θ_r = cos φ.
- Limit ray: φ = φ_c with sin φ_c = n₂/n₁, so cos φ_c = √(n₁² − n₂²)/n₁.
- Result: n₀ sin θ₀ = √(n₁² − n₂²); NA = sin θ₀ in air.
- Checks: dimensionless; n₂ → n₁ gives 0; approximate form n₁√(2Δ).

### Core concepts

- Guiding needs n₁ > n₂ and incidence at the wall above φ_c.
- NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) is a property of the two indices only.
- Attenuation is exponential in length, additive in dB.
- Loss mechanisms: absorption, Rayleigh scattering (1/λ⁴), macro- and microbending.

### Practice

**Q.** Name the three regions of a practical fibre and say which has the lowest index.

**A.** Core, cladding, buffer coating; the cladding has the lower index of the two guiding regions (n₂ < n₁); the coating is for protection.

**Q.** What happens to a ray that meets the core-cladding boundary at less than the critical angle?

**A.** It refracts into the cladding and is lost.

**Q.** What is a decibel loss of 3 dB in power terms?

**A.** About half the power remains (10^(−0.3) = 0.501).

**Q.** Write NA for a fibre in terms of n₁ and n₂, for air launch.

**A.** NA = √(n₁² − n₂²).

**Q.** Why does a larger NA make coupling easier but pulse spreading worse?

**A.** A larger NA accepts a wider cone, so more light from a source enters; but the steepest accepted rays travel longer zig-zag paths and arrive later, so the delay between fastest and slowest ray grows as NA² (lesson PH-064).

**Q.** Why is TIR at the wall lossless in principle, yet a long fibre still loses power?

**A.** TIR returns all the power at each reflection, but the glass absorbs and scatters light along the path, and bends and microbends spoil the TIR condition locally; these cause the attenuation.

**Q.** Why does a fibre bent very tightly lose light?

**A.** At the bend rays meet the outer wall at smaller angles of incidence; once the angle drops below φ_c, TIR fails and light refracts out.

**Q.** Why does a bare fibre packed in a bundle show crosstalk?

**A.** Light leaks a short distance beyond the surface (evanescent field) and, across thin gaps, is coupled into neighbouring fibres by frustrated total internal reflection; a cladding of sufficient thickness prevents it.
