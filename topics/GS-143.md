## GS-143: Caste: changes and persistence

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Caste: changes and persistence and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Varna, jati and the logic of the system

The word caste comes from the Portuguese casta. Indian languages use two terms: varna, a four-fold all-India classification, and jati, a regional web of hundreds of castes and sub-castes. Early varna divisions in the late Vedic era were loose and not birth-based; rigidity came later. Textbook definitions list birth-ascribed membership, endogamy, food rules, a ranked hierarchy, segmental sub-division and hereditary occupation, but these were scriptural prescriptions, not always practice. Theoretically caste joins two principles: difference and separation between groups, and wholism and hierarchy that place every group on a ladder of purity and pollution. In practice ritual status and material power tended to coincide. Although rooted in Hindu society, caste spread among Muslims, Christians and Sikhs on the subcontinent.

### Colonial and post-colonial transformation

British administrators surveyed castes to govern efficiently, and the census, especially Risley's 1901 effort to rank castes, made identities more fixed as groups petitioned for higher places. The 1935 Act created the schedules that underpin today's SC and ST categories. After 1947 the state abolished caste in constitutional terms but avoided radical reforms touching its economic base, assuming a caste-blind approach would erode privilege; reservations for SCs and STs were the main exception. Industry and urban living created jobs and spaces without caste rules, yet factory recruitment often ran through caste and kin networks. Politics became the most dynamic arena: dominant castes such as the Yadavs, Vokkaligas, Reddys, Khammas, Marathas, Jats and Patidars converted land rights and numbers into regional power.

### Persistence: marriage, visibility and the caste-class link

Caste has proved strongest in domestic and cultural life: most marriages still occur within caste boundaries, even as inter-caste unions increase. For urban upper-caste middle classes, earlier advantages in subsidised professional education and public-sector jobs consolidated over generations, so caste now seems irrelevant to them, though not every upper-caste person is privileged. Disadvantaged castes, lacking inherited educational and social capital, cannot easily abandon caste identity because it anchors claims to protection. The Patterns of Inequality chapter adds that while the link between caste and occupation has loosened and rich and poor exist in every caste, the macro-level correlation between caste and economic status remains remarkably stable: privileged groups remain largely upper caste and the poorest largely lower caste.

### Worked example

Q: 'Caste has changed its form but not its hold.' Examine. Frame: define caste via separation and hierarchy; show change (occupational mobility, urban mixing, colonial census effects, reservations); show persistence (endogamy, caste-based parties since the 1980s, dominant castes, macro caste-class correlation); use the 'invisibility for the privileged, visibility for the disadvantaged' argument; conclude with constitutional abolition versus social persistence.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which two principles combine in the caste system, according to NCERT?
  - **a:** Difference/separation between castes, and wholism/hierarchy ranking them by purity and pollution.
- - **q:** What is a 'dominant caste'?
  - **a:** A numerically large intermediate caste that gained land rights through post-Independence land reforms and then economic and political power in a region (Srinivas).
- - **q:** Which colonial census tried to rank castes?
  - **a:** The 1901 Census directed by Herbert Risley.

### Common trap

Saying caste has 'disappeared' in cities. NCERT argues it became invisible mainly for urban upper-caste groups whose inherited advantages now look like merit, while for SC, ST and OBC groups caste became more visible because it is a collective asset and the basis of protective policies.
