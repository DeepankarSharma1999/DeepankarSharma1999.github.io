## GS-297: Energy sources and nuclear basics

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Science · General science / technology

*Goal:* Explain Energy sources and nuclear basics and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why nuclear energy is so large

The binding energy per nucleon curve has a long flat middle between mass numbers 30 and 170, at roughly 8 MeV per nucleon, falling off for light and very heavy nuclei. The more tightly a system is bound, the less its total mass. So when nuclei with lower binding energy turn into nuclei with higher binding energy, the difference appears as energy. Chemical reactions such as burning coal involve electron-volt energies, while nuclear reactions involve MeV, roughly a million times more per unit of matter: about 10^14 J from fissioning 1 kg of uranium compared with about 10^7 J from 1 kg of coal.

### Fission and fusion compared

In fission, a neutron striking uranium-235 splits it into two intermediate-mass fragments plus more neutrons; the fragments are radioactive and decay further. Each fission yields around 200 MeV, first as kinetic energy of fragments and neutrons and finally as heat. Reactors use controlled fission to make electricity; an atom bomb is uncontrolled fission. In fusion, light nuclei combine into a more tightly bound nucleus, but they must overcome Coulomb repulsion of about 400 keV for two protons, which implies temperatures near 3 × 10^9 K for average particles. In the Sun, hydrogen fuses to helium through the proton-proton cycle, releasing 26.7 MeV for every four hydrogen nuclei.

### Nuclear in India's transition

Policy treats nuclear as part of the non-fossil mix. India's capacity growth has been led by solar and wind, with steady growth in hydro and nuclear power. Non-fossil sources, which include nuclear, reached more than half of installed electricity capacity, meeting the updated NDC goal ahead of 2030. At COP30, India highlighted momentum from the Nuclear Mission and Green Hydrogen Mission, along with the International Solar Alliance and Global Biofuel Alliance, as part of its pathway to net zero by 2070. Nuclear is therefore discussed in exams both as physics and as a climate-policy lever.

### Worked example

Compute: a nucleus with A = 240 (about 7.6 MeV per nucleon) splits into two A = 120 fragments (about 8.5 MeV per nucleon). Gain = 0.9 MeV × 240 ≈ 216 MeV, consistent with the ~200 MeV per fission quoted. This explains why heavy nuclei release energy on splitting.

### Recall

- - **q:** Roughly how much energy does one uranium fission release?
  - **a:** About 200 MeV.
- - **q:** What process powers the Sun?
  - **a:** Thermonuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium (proton-proton cycle).
- - **q:** Which nuclear process do present reactors use?
  - **a:** Controlled nuclear fission.

### Common trap

Saying fusion is used in present-day power reactors; existing nuclear power comes from fission.
