## GS-161: Features, sources and schedules

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: Parts, Articles and Schedules: the vocabulary of the book

## Four words you will meet on every page

A **constitution** is the document that sets out who makes the laws, what the government may not do, and which goals the State pursues. The Indian one is a single printed book. Its parts have fixed names.

- **Preamble**: the short opening statement of aims and ideals. It comes before Article 1.
- **Article**: one numbered rule, like a numbered clause in a contract. Article 14 is the equality rule; Article 368 is the amendment rule.
- **Part**: a group of Articles on one theme. Part III holds the Fundamental Rights (Articles 12 to 35). A Part can be split further into **Chapters**, as Part V is split into the Executive, Parliament and so on.
- **Schedule**: a list or table kept at the back of the book so that the Articles stay short. Article 1 says the States are 'as specified in the First Schedule'; the Schedule then names them.

## Why you see numbers like 21A, 31B and 243ZH

The Constitution was adopted with Articles numbered 1 to 395. When Parliament later wants to add a rule, it avoids renumbering every Article (which would break every old court judgment and textbook). It inserts a lettered Article instead: 21A (the right to elementary education) sits between 21 and 22. The same method creates new Parts such as IV-A and IX-A, and the later Schedules are simply numbered 9 to 12.

## Reading a reference

'Article 352, Part XVIII, Emergency Provisions' means: the rule is numbered 352, it belongs to the eighteenth Part, and that Part is about emergencies. Roman numerals mark Parts; Arabic numbers mark Articles and Schedules (though Schedules are often written in words: Ninth Schedule).

## One caution

Counts such as 'about 470 Articles' are approximate because some Articles have been deleted and many lettered ones inserted. Learn the structure and the key numbers; do not treat the totals as exact.

### Bridge: Written or unwritten, rigid or flexible: two ways to classify constitutions

## Two separate questions

Constitutions are classified on two independent axes. Do not mix them up.

**Axis 1: written or unwritten.** A written constitution is gathered in one document (or a small set of documents) that can be opened and read, such as the American or the Indian one. An unwritten constitution, such as the British, is not a single text; NCERT says the United Kingdom has a series of documents and decisions that taken together are called its constitution. 'Unwritten' does not mean no writing exists anywhere; it means there is no single constitutional code.

**Axis 2: rigid or flexible.** This asks how hard it is to change the constitution. Laxmikanth defines a **rigid** constitution as one needing a special procedure for amendment (the American example) and a **flexible** one as amendable in the same way as ordinary laws (the British example). NCERT puts it simply: flexible means open to change, rigid means resistant to change.

## Why both extremes cause trouble

NCERT explains that a constitution that is too rigid may break under the weight of change, while one that is too flexible gives people no security, predictability or identity. A good design keeps core values firm and lets the rest adapt.

## Where India sits

India has a written constitution. On the second axis it is neither fully rigid nor fully flexible. Three levels of difficulty exist: some provisions change by an ordinary majority of Parliament (outside Article 368); some need a special majority; a few need a special majority plus approval by half the States. The details of the three routes belong to GS-167 and GS-172; here you only need the label **'blend of rigidity and flexibility'** and the reason for it.

## A memory pair

Written / unwritten answers 'is it in one book?'. Rigid / flexible answers 'how hard is it to amend?'. The British Constitution is unwritten and flexible; the American is written and rigid; the Indian is written and a blend.

### 1. Orientation: three questions about one document

*Objective:* State what 'features, sources and schedules' covers and why the Constitution is studied both for what it is and for where its ideas came from.

## What the title asks

The topic bundles three questions about the same book.

1. **Features**: what makes the Indian Constitution distinctive, such as its length, its blend of rigidity and flexibility, or its federal system with a unitary bias.
2. **Sources**: where its ideas came from. Laxmikanth says the Constitution has borrowed from almost every Constitution of the world, yet has features that distinguish it from all of them.
3. **Schedules**: the twelve lists at the back of the book. Together with the Parts and Articles in front, they are the skeleton of the document.

If you have not met the words Article, Part and Schedule, read bridge module GS-161-B1 first; if 'rigid' and 'flexible' are new, read GS-161-B2.

## Why have a constitution at all?

NCERT (Class 11, chapter 1) gives five jobs of a constitution. It provides basic rules so that a diverse group can coordinate and be assured that others will obey. It specifies who has the power to make decisions. It sets limits on what a government may impose on citizens. It enables the government to fulfil the aspirations of society, which the Indian Constitution does especially through the Directive Principles. And it expresses the fundamental identity of a people. Keep these five jobs in mind; every salient feature is one of them in action. Federalism divides power; Fundamental Rights limit it; Directive Principles state the goals.

## A moving target

Laxmikanth warns at the outset that many original features have changed through amendments, particularly the 7th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 97th and 101st. The 42nd Amendment (1976) is called the 'Mini-Constitution' because it altered so much. At the same time the Supreme Court ruled in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) that Parliament cannot alter the basic structure. So when you learn a feature, learn both its present form and whether it can be changed. Module 8 returns to this; the doctrine itself is the subject of GS-168.

### 2. Size and structure: Articles, Parts and why the book is so long

*Objective:* Recall the original and present counts of Articles, Parts and Schedules, the four reasons for the size, and the map of the main Parts.

## The numbers

Laxmikanth gives two snapshots.

| | Original (1949) | Present (count given for 2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Articles | 395 | about 470 |
| Parts | 22 | 25 |
| Schedules | 8 | 12 |

Since 1951 the amendments deleted about 20 Articles and one Part (Part VII), and added about 95 Articles, four Parts (IV-A, IX-A, IX-B and XIV-A) and four Schedules (9, 10, 11 and 12). Check the arithmetic, because examiners like consistent numbers: 395 minus 20 plus 95 gives 470; 22 minus 1 plus 4 gives 25, as Laxmikanth prints it [standard knowledge; verify: the 7th Amendment also repealed the original Part IX (Part D territories) and the 73rd inserted a new Part IX, so the true net is 22 minus 2 plus 5 equals 25]; 8 plus 4 gives 12. The word 'about' matters. Laxmikanth himself says 'about'.

One puzzle: the 73rd Amendment is said to have added a 'new Part IX' (Panchayats), yet the list of added Parts leaves Part IX out. The likely reason is that the number IX was already used in the 1950 text for a different, since-removed Part (the original Part IX, repealed by the 7th Amendment in 1956 along with Part VII) and was reused. So Laxmikanth's one deleted Part is 'as printed'; the true count is two deleted and five added. The extract does not explain this, so treat it as standard knowledge to verify. For the exam the safe rule is: the Parts added by amendment that carry letters are IV-A, IX-A, IX-B and XIV-A; Part IX itself is Panchayats.

## Why so long? Four factors (Laxmikanth)

1. **Geography**: a vast and diverse country.
2. **History**: the bulky Government of India Act, 1935 was a model (GS-158).
3. **One Constitution for both Centre and States**: unlike the United States, the States have no separate constitutions, so State matters are also written here.
4. **Legal luminaries**: lawyers dominated the Constituent Assembly (GS-159).

In addition, the document contains administrative details that other democracies leave to ordinary laws or political conventions. Laxmikanth's footnote says the American Constitution originally had only 7 Articles, the Australian 128, the Chinese 138 and the Canadian 147.

## The map of the Parts

You do not need all 25 on the first pass. Anchor on these.

- **I** The Union and its territory (Articles 1 to 4); **II** Citizenship (5 to 11).
- **III** Fundamental Rights (12 to 35); **IV** Directive Principles (36 to 51); **IV-A** Fundamental Duties (51A).
- **V** The Union Government (52 to 151); **VI** The State Governments (152 to 237).
- **IX** Panchayats; **IX-A** Municipalities; **IX-B** Co-operative Societies.
- **XI** Union-State relations (245 to 263); **XII** Finance, property, contracts and suits (264 to 300A).
- **XIV** Services; **XIV-A** Tribunals; **XV** Elections; **XVIII** Emergency provisions (352 to 360); **XX** Amendment (368); **XXII** Short title, commencement, authoritative Hindi text and repeals (393 to 395).

The complete Part-by-Part table is in the Master notes (Table 3.1 of Laxmikanth). Note Laxmikanth's remark that Part IV-A and Part XIV-A came with the 42nd Amendment (1976), Part IX-A with the 74th (1992) and Part IX-B with the 97th (2011).

## Which were added later

The first eight were in the 1950 text. The other four came by amendment.

- **Ninth**: the 1st Amendment (1951), to protect land-reform and similar laws from judicial scrutiny for violating Fundamental Rights. It originally held 13 Acts; Laxmikanth prints 282 as the present number (a dated count). The Supreme Court ruled in 2007 that laws placed in it after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review (Coelho; see GS-168).
- **Tenth**: the 52nd Amendment (1985), the anti-defection law.
- **Eleventh**: the 73rd Amendment (1992), with Part IX for Panchayats.
- **Twelfth**: the 74th Amendment (1992), with Part IX-A for Municipalities.

## Numbers worth knowing

- **Seventh Schedule** as printed: Union List 98 subjects (originally 97), State List 59 (originally 66), Concurrent List 52 (originally 47). Later changes, such as those linked to the 101st (GST) Amendment, may have altered the totals, so treat the figures as the textbook's and verify if a question depends on them. NCERT adds a quick feel for the lists: defence and foreign affairs are Union subjects; police, prisons and agriculture are State subjects; education and forests are Concurrent.
- **Eighth Schedule**: originally 14 languages, now 22. Sindhi was added by the 21st Amendment (1967); Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali by the 71st (1992); Bodo, Dongri (Dogri), Maithili and Santhali by the 92nd (2003). Oriya was renamed Odia by the 96th (2011). Check: 14 plus 1 plus 3 plus 4 equals 22.

### 4. Salient features I: shape of the Constitution and of the State

*Objective:* Explain features 1 to 7 of Laxmikanth's list and give one concrete illustration of each.

Laxmikanth's list of salient features 'as it stands today' has seventeen items. This module takes the first seven, which describe the shape of the document and of the State.

## 1. Lengthiest written constitution

Written (not British-style unwritten) and the longest of its kind. Illustration: about 470 Articles in 25 Parts and 12 Schedules (module 2).

## 2. Drawn from various sources

Ambedkar said it was framed after ransacking all known constitutions. Illustration: the structure from the Government of India Act, 1935; Fundamental Rights from the American; Directive Principles from the Irish; Cabinet government from the British. Laxmikanth adds that more than half the provisions are identical or close to the 1935 Act. The full table is in module 6.

## 3. Blend of rigidity and flexibility

Article 368 provides two routes: a special majority of Parliament (two-thirds of the members present and voting, and a majority of the total membership) and the same plus ratification by half of the States. Yet some provisions can be amended by a simple majority in the ordinary way, and those do not come under Article 368. Illustration: NCERT shows that Articles 2 and 3 say Parliament 'may by law' admit or reshape States. Such laws pass by simple majority because Article 4(2) declares that laws under Articles 2 and 3 are not amendments of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368 (standard knowledge; verify). The phrase 'by law' alone is not the test: Article 241 needs ratification (see GS-167).

## 4. Federal system with unitary bias

The Constitution has the usual federal features (two governments, division of powers, written constitution, supremacy of the Constitution, independent judiciary, bicameralism) and also many non-federal ones (a strong Centre, single Constitution, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, Governors appointed by the Centre, All-India Services, emergency provisions). Illustration: Article 1 calls India a **Union of States**; the word 'federation' is never used. Laxmikanth draws two implications: the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among States, and no State has a right to secede. Scholars gave it many labels: quasi-federal (Wheare), bargaining federalism (Morris Jones), co-operative federalism (Granville Austin), federation with a centralising tendency (Jennings). Trap: Laxmikanth lists rigidity among federal features and flexibility among unitary ones, so both words appear on both sides of the argument.

## 5. Parliamentary form of government

Britain's Westminster model, not the American presidential model, at the Centre and in the States. Features: nominal and real executives, majority-party rule, collective responsibility to the legislature, ministers who are legislators, Prime Minister or Chief Minister as leader, and dissolution of the lower House. Differences from Britain: India's Parliament is not sovereign and the head of State is elected (a republic), not hereditary.

## 6. Synthesis of parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy

Britain gives Parliament the last word; America gives the courts a wide review power under 'due process of law'. India chose a middle path. The Supreme Court can declare laws unconstitutional; Parliament can amend the major portion of the Constitution. Judicial review is narrower than in America because Article 21 says 'procedure established by law'. Caveat: in Maneka Gandhi (1978) the Supreme Court read fairness into that procedure, so the gap with 'due process' has narrowed in practice (GS-173).

## 7. Integrated and independent judiciary

One hierarchy: Supreme Court, High Courts, subordinate courts, enforcing both central and State laws, unlike the dual court systems of America. Independence safeguards include security of tenure, fixed service conditions, charging expenses on the Consolidated Fund, a bar on discussing judges' conduct in legislatures, a ban on practice after retirement, contempt powers and separation from the executive.

### 5. Salient features II: rights, duties, society and institutions

*Objective:* Explain features 8 to 17 with one illustration each and note the amendment dates attached to them.

## 8. Fundamental Rights

Part III guarantees six rights: Equality (Articles 14 to 18), Freedom (19 to 22), Against Exploitation (23 and 24), Freedom of Religion (25 to 28), Cultural and Educational Rights (29 and 30) and Constitutional Remedies (32). They promote political democracy, are justiciable, and can be enforced directly in the Supreme Court through writs. They are not absolute, can be curtailed by amendment (subject to the basic structure, GS-168) and can be suspended in a National Emergency, except those under Articles 20 and 21. Trap: the original list had seven rights; the right to property (Article 31) was deleted by the 44th Amendment (1978) and became a legal right under Article 300A.

## 9. Directive Principles of State Policy

Part IV, a 'novel feature' in Ambedkar's words. Classified as socialistic, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual. They promote social and economic democracy and a welfare State, are non-justiciable, but the Constitution calls them fundamental in governance. Their real sanction is political. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court spoke of a balance between Rights and Directives. Detail: GS-165.

## 10. Fundamental Duties

Absent in 1950; added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation, in Part IV-A, which has one Article, 51A. The 86th Amendment (2002) added one more, making eleven. They are non-justiciable. Detail: GS-166.

## 11. A secular State

No official religion. The word 'secular' was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment, but the substance already lay in Articles 14, 15, 16 and 25 to 30. Laxmikanth stresses the **positive** concept: equal respect for all religions, not the Western strict separation of church and State. NCERT, Class 11 chapter 10, uses the phrase 'principled distance': the State may help or hinder religion depending on what promotes liberty, equality and social justice. It cites aid to community institutions and action against untouchability. Communal electorates were abolished; seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

## 12. Universal adult franchise

Every citizen aged 18 or more may vote regardless of caste, race, religion, sex, literacy or wealth. The age was lowered from 21 to 18 by the 61st Amendment (1988), with effect from 1989. NCERT notes that this was almost the only provision of the Constitution passed in the Assembly with virtually no debate.

## 13. Single citizenship

Only Indian citizenship, though there are two levels of government. In the United States a person is a citizen of both the nation and a State.

## 14. Independent bodies

Election Commission, Comptroller and Auditor-General, Union Public Service Commission and State Public Service Commissions, secured by tenure and by charging expenses on the Consolidated Fund.

## 15. Emergency provisions

Three kinds: National Emergency (Article 352; war, external aggression or armed rebellion, the last word replacing 'internal disturbance' through the 44th Amendment), State Emergency or President's Rule (Articles 356 and 365) and Financial Emergency (Article 360). The structure turns unitary without a formal amendment.

## 16. Three-tier government

The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) added Panchayats (Part IX, Eleventh Schedule) and Municipalities (Part IX-A, Twelfth Schedule), a third tier that Laxmikanth says is not found in any other constitution.

## 17. Co-operative societies

The 97th Amendment (2011) made the right to form co-operative societies a Fundamental Right (Article 19), added Article 43B as a Directive Principle and inserted Part IX-B (Articles 243-ZH to 243-ZT). Flag: the Supreme Court later partly struck down the 97th Amendment's Part IX-B, as it relates to State co-operative societies, for want of State ratification [standard knowledge; verify].

### 7. Criticism of the Constitution and the 'borrowed Constitution' debate

*Objective:* State the six criticisms, the Ambedkar and NCERT replies, and write a balanced paragraph on whether India's Constitution is a 'borrowed' one.

## The six criticisms (Laxmikanth)

1. **A borrowed Constitution**: called a 'bag of borrowings', a 'hotch-potch' or a patchwork of other documents.
2. **A carbon copy of the 1935 Act**: Jennings said many provisions were copied almost textually; N. Srinivasan called it a close copy in language and substance; the Assembly member P. R. Deshmukh said it was the 1935 Act with only adult franchise added.
3. **Un-Indian or anti-Indian**: K. Hanumanthaiya complained of hearing the 'music of an English band' instead of the veena or sitar; Lokanath Misra called it a slavish imitation; Lakshminarayan Sahu said it would break down soon.
4. **An un-Gandhian Constitution**: it is not built on village and district panchayats, the critics said (a gap partly addressed later by the 73rd Amendment and by Directive Principle Article 40 on organising village panchayats).
5. **Elephantine size**: Jennings, and H. V. Kamath, who joked about the Assembly's elephant emblem.
6. **A paradise of lawyers**: Jennings's phrase, echoed by H. K. Maheswari and P. R. Deshmukh, who feared litigation and 'words, words and words'.

## The framers' reply

Laxmikanth calls the borrowing charge unfair and illogical, because the framers modified borrowed features to Indian conditions and avoided their faults. Ambedkar, as Chairman of the Drafting Committee, argued in the Assembly that in a Constitution framed so late in history, the only new things can be variations that remove faults and fit the country's needs; the charge of blind copying rests on inadequate study. On the 1935 Act he made no apology: there is nothing to be ashamed of in borrowing, nobody holds patent rights in fundamental ideas of a constitution; he regretted only that what was taken from the 1935 Act related mostly to administrative detail.

## What NCERT adds

NCERT chapter 1 says the framers took the best from everywhere, but 'borrowing was not slavish imitation' because each provision had to be defended as suited to Indian problems and aspirations. NCERT chapter 10 calls it innovative borrowing and 'selective adaptation', and discusses three criticisms: it is **unwieldy** (answer: a country's constitution includes other documents with constitutional status, and many details such as the Election Commission and civil service commissions are in the one document); it is **unrepresentative** (the Assembly was not chosen by universal suffrage, so it lacks 'voice'; but it was broad in 'opinion'); it is **alien** (answer: modern ideas were adopted by Indians, who adapted them). Chapter 10 then admits three limitations: a centralised idea of national unity; a gloss over gender justice within the family; and the placing of basic socio-economic rights in the Directive Principles rather than the Fundamental Rights.

## Building a balanced answer

- State the charge with a name (Jennings, Hanumanthaiya).
- Concede what is true: many provisions do come from other countries, and about half from the 1935 Act.
- Show adaptation: universal adult franchise from the start; Directive Principles tied to Indian social goals; a positive concept of secularism; Union of States with asymmetric provisions (NCERT cites Article 371A for Nagaland).
- Close with the test of a good constitution: does it work, and does it give everyone a reason to go along with it (NCERT)?

This topic links to GS-159 (the Assembly's critics) and to GS-160 (ideals of the Preamble).

### 8. Features and amendment: what changed and what cannot

*Objective:* Explain how amendments reshaped the features and distinguish a 'salient feature' from a 'basic feature'.

## Amendments that reshaped the features

Laxmikanth names the amendments that changed the original features most: the 7th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 97th and 101st. Using the text of the chapter, here is what each did to the features of this lesson.

| Amendment | Effect on a salient feature |
|---|---|
| 1st (1951) | Added the Ninth Schedule and Article 31B |
| 7th (1956) | Deleted Part VII (States in Part B) as Laxmikanth lists it; the 7th Amendment also repealed the original Part IX [standard knowledge; verify] |
| 42nd (1976) | Added Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A), Part XIV-A (Tribunals) and the word 'secular' |
| 44th (1978) | Deleted the Fundamental Right to property; replaced 'internal disturbance' by 'armed rebellion' |
| 52nd (1985) | Added the Tenth Schedule |
| 61st (1988) | Lowered the voting age to 18 |
| 73rd and 74th (1992) | Added the third tier, Parts IX and IX-A, Schedules 11 and 12 |
| 86th (2002) | Added one Fundamental Duty |
| 97th (2011) | Co-operative societies (Article 19, Article 43B, Part IX-B) |
| 101st (2016) | Listed by Laxmikanth among the amendments that changed features (GST; see GS-172) |

Each entry connects to the blend of rigidity and flexibility (feature 3): the Constitution has changed substantially, yet its core stands.

## Salient feature versus basic feature

A **salient feature** is a distinctive characteristic described in a textbook. A **basic feature** is one the Supreme Court says Parliament cannot destroy by amendment. The first is descriptive; the second is a legal limit. Laxmikanth's list of basic features (from Chapter 11) includes supremacy of the Constitution, sovereign democratic republic, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, unity and integrity, welfare State, judicial review, parliamentary system, rule of law, free and fair elections, independence of the judiciary and the limited power of Parliament to amend. Several overlap with the salient features of module 4 (federal character, parliamentary system, independent judiciary, secularism).

Two cautions:

1. Not every salient feature is shown on that list. But the list is not exhaustive: Laxmikanth says the Court is yet to define the basic structure, so absence from the list proves nothing either way. Do not claim that single citizenship, for example, can be freely abolished or cannot be; the sources do not decide.
2. 'Basic' does not mean 'unamendable in every detail'. Parliament may reshape the details of a feature (the 42nd Amendment added the word 'secular'; the 44th removed property from Part III) provided the amendment does not destroy the feature's essence. The question is always whether the feature as a whole is destroyed.

## How this helps in answers

A good answer on 'Does the Constitution's character change with amendments?' says: yes, in details (name amendments), no in its basic structure (name Kesavananda Bharati, 1973). NCERT describes the doctrine as keeping the Constitution rigid for core parts and flexible for the rest. For the cases and their sequence see GS-167, GS-173 and GS-168.

### 9. Examination application: patterns in ten verified questions

## Ten verified PYQs, five shapes

The attached questions (PYQ links in Exam Intelligence) fall into five shapes. No official answer key is stated here; the notes reason from the textbooks.

1. **Structure and Parts** (Prelims 2024, 2026). Which Part holds Municipalities, Emergency, Amendment; which Articles name the Constitution, repeal earlier Acts, and fix the date of commencement.
2. **Schedules** (Prelims 2018, 2019). The Ninth Schedule: who can add laws, whether the courts can examine them, and under which Prime Minister it came.

## Worked example: 2024 Q74 (thinking aloud)

The statements: Municipalities are in Part IX-A; Emergency provisions are in Part XVIII; amendment is in Part XX. Check each against the Parts table. Municipalities: Part IX-A (Articles 243-P to 243-ZG): yes. Emergency: Part XVIII (Articles 352 to 360): yes. Amendment: Part XX (Article 368): yes. The analysis therefore points to all three statements being correct. The trap is to confuse Part IX (Panchayats) with Part IX-A (Municipalities), and Part XX with Part XXI (temporary and transitional provisions, Articles 369 to 392).

## Worked example: 2018 Q5 (Ninth Schedule)

Statement 1 says Parliament can place a law in the Ninth Schedule. The Schedule was created by the 1st Amendment and laws have been added through later amendments, so Parliament can place laws in it through the amending process. Statement 2 says no court can ever examine a law in it. The 2007 ruling (Laxmikanth, Table 3.3) says laws added after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review. So statement 2 conflicts with that ruling, and the analysis points to statement 1 only. Careful: the word 'cannot' and 'no judgement can be made' are absolute words, a common trap sign.

## Elimination habits

- **Absolute words** ('only', 'cannot', 'never', 'no court') are warning flags in this topic, especially for Fundamental Rights and the Ninth Schedule.
- **Part versus Article** confusion: learn the Parts map before the Article numbers.
- **Source pairs**: a 'borrowed from' option swaps countries; keep the compact cue from module 6.
- **'Federal in character'** options: choose the structural feature (independent judiciary settling Union-State disputes, per NCERT) over a political or generic one.

## For the 2023 'purpose' question

NCERT's five functions say a constitution specifies who decides, limits government, and states aspirations. An option that says it 'defines and limits the powers of government' captures two of those at once, which makes it the broadest and most basic of the four, though the final key should be checked.

## Model the thinking: a 15-mark question

Question: 'The Indian Constitution is called a borrowed Constitution. Critically examine.' (A directive such as 'Critically examine' asks you to give both sides and a judgement.)

**Step 1: Opening (about 40 words).** Define the charge: critics such as Jennings and Hanumanthaiya called it a bag of borrowings and a carbon copy of the 1935 Act. Mention the fact base: about half the provisions resemble the 1935 Act, with ideas from Britain, the United States, Ireland and others.

**Step 2: The case for the charge (about 60 words).** Use three examples: parliamentary government (Britain), Fundamental Rights and judicial review (America), Directive Principles (Ireland), plus the 1935 Act's federal scheme, Governors and emergency provisions.

**Step 3: The case against (about 90 words).** Ambedkar's reply; adaptation: universal adult franchise from the start, positive secularism, asymmetric federalism (Article 371A), and the third tier of government not found in any other constitution (73rd and 74th).

**Step 4: Judgement and conclusion (about 60 words).** The test is fitness for Indian conditions and survival; NCERT's phrase 'innovative borrowing' summarises it; add one limitation (centralised unity or gender justice in the family).

### Notes: Key definitions

- Constitution: the fundamental document that provides basic rules, says who makes decisions, limits government, states society's aspirations and expresses a people's identity (NCERT chapter 1).
- Article: one numbered rule of the Constitution; Part: a group of Articles on a theme; Schedule: a list or table attached at the back.
- Written constitution: one gathered text (American, Indian); unwritten: a collection of documents and conventions (British).
- Rigid constitution: special amendment procedure; flexible: amendable like ordinary law (Laxmikanth).
- Union of States: the description in Article 1 which implies that the federation is not an agreement among States and that no State can secede.
- Positive secularism: equal respect for all religions or protecting all religions equally, as opposed to strict separation of religion and State (Laxmikanth).
- Principled distance: NCERT's term for the State's freedom to help or abstain from religion according to what promotes liberty, equality and social justice.
- Justiciable: enforceable by courts (Fundamental Rights); non-justiciable: not enforceable by courts (Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties).
- Universal adult franchise: every adult citizen (18 or more) may vote without discrimination of caste, race, religion, sex, literacy or wealth.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Part IX (Panchayats) versus Part IX-A (Municipalities) versus Part IX-B (Co-operative Societies).
- Part XX (Amendment, Article 368) versus Part XXI (Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions).
- Fundamental Rights (justiciable, Part III) versus Directive Principles (non-justiciable, Part IV) versus Fundamental Duties (non-justiciable, Part IV-A).
- Eleventh Schedule (Panchayats, 29 matters) versus Twelfth Schedule (Municipalities, 18 matters).
- Sixth Schedule (tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram) versus Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes generally).
- Salient feature (descriptive) versus basic feature (legal limit on amendment).
- Positive secularism (Indian) versus strict separation (Western).
- Federation versus Union of States: the Constitution uses only the latter.

### Notes: Exceptions and cautions

- The counts 395/22/8 and about 470/25/12 are Laxmikanth's; 'about' is deliberate. Do not quote the later number as exact.
- The Seventh Schedule totals (98, 59, 52) and the Ninth Schedule count (282) are textbook-edition figures that later events may change; verify before using in an answer.
- Laxmikanth prints the Fundamental Rights as six. The right to property was removed in 1978; it remains a legal right (Article 300A).
- Fundamental Rights can be suspended during a National Emergency except Articles 20 and 21 (as stated in chapter 3 of Laxmikanth).
- NCERT chapter 7 still mentions the Planning Commission and Article 370's special status as then in force; check the dates of the statements. Laxmikanth's footnote says the special status ended in 2019.
- The 97th Amendment's Part IX-B was partly struck down by the Supreme Court as it relates to State co-operatives [standard knowledge; verify].

### Notes: Number cross-check (arithmetic done by program)

- Articles: 395 - 20 + 95 = 470.
- Parts: 22 - 1 + 4 = 25 as Laxmikanth prints it (his list names only Part VII as deleted). [Standard knowledge; verify] The 7th Amendment also repealed the original Part IX; true net 22 - 2 + 5 = 25. Counting rows of Table 3.1 gives 25 Parts, excluding the deleted Part VII.
- Schedules: 8 + 4 = 12.
- Eighth Schedule: 14 originals + 1 (21st) + 3 (71st) + 4 (92nd) = 22. The list printed by Laxmikanth contains 22 languages.

### Core concepts

- The Constitution is written, lengthy, borrowed and adapted; it blends rigidity and flexibility.
- It is federal in form with a strong Centre; it is parliamentary; it synthesises parliamentary sovereignty and judicial review.
- Part III Fundamental Rights, Part IV Directive Principles and Part IV-A Fundamental Duties form a trio of different legal force.
- Schedules 9 to 12 were added by amendment; Parts IV-A, IX-A, IX-B and XIV-A were added; Part VII was deleted.
- Ambedkar defended borrowing as adaptation; NCERT calls it innovative borrowing and selective adaptation.

### Practice

**Q.** How many Articles, Parts and Schedules did the original Constitution contain?

**A.** 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules.

**Q.** Name the four reasons Laxmikanth gives for the size of the Constitution.

**A.** Geographical factors, historical factors (the 1935 Act), a single Constitution for the Centre and States, and the dominance of legal luminaries in the Constituent Assembly.

**Q.** Which Schedule contains the Union, State and Concurrent Lists?

**A.** The Seventh Schedule.

**Q.** What does 'rigid' mean for a constitution?

**A.** It requires a special procedure for amendment.

**Q.** Why did Laxmikanth list both rigidity and flexibility as features of the Indian Constitution?

**A.** Because Article 368 sets special procedures for some provisions while other provisions can be changed like ordinary law, so the Constitution is a synthesis of both. The simple-majority route exists because Article 4(2) declares that laws under Articles 2 and 3 are not amendments for Article 368; 'by law' alone is not the test (Article 241 needs ratification; see GS-167).

**Q.** Why is the word 'Union' in Article 1 important for understanding Indian federalism?

**A.** It implies the federation was not formed by an agreement among States and that no State can secede.

**Q.** How does positive secularism differ from the Western conception?

**A.** Western secularism separates religion and State completely; the Indian version gives equal respect to all religions and lets the State help or intervene as needed.

**Q.** Why does NCERT say India's borrowing was 'not slavish imitation'?

**A.** Each provision had to be defended as suited to Indian problems and aspirations; the framers adapted ideas rather than copying them.

**Q.** A State's Governor reserves a Bill for the President's assent and the Union later issues directions under Article 257. Which salient feature and which bridge between Centre and States do these illustrate?

**A.** The federal system with unitary bias: the Governor's role and the Union's executive power over States (NCERT chapter 7) show the strong Centre.

**Q.** A student says 'The Ninth Schedule exists to hide laws from judges forever.' Give a two-line reply.

**A.** It was added by the 1st Amendment (1951) to protect land-reform laws from challenge under Fundamental Rights, but since 2007 laws placed in it after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed.
