### Bridge: Colour, refractive index and the dispersive power of a glass

White light is a mixture of colours, each a wave of a different wavelength. Glass slows light by a factor, the **refractive index** mu, and mu is **not the same for every colour**: it is larger for blue (short wavelength) than for red (long wavelength). That is why a prism spreads white light into a spectrum, and it is called **dispersion**. Because the focal length of a lens depends on mu, a lens is not one lens but a slightly different lens for each colour.

To compare glasses we need one number. The **dispersive power** is omega = (mu_B - mu_R)/(mu_Y - 1), where B, R and Y are blue, red and a middle (yellow) colour. It has no units. Brijlal writes it with the Fraunhofer lines F (4861 Angstrom, blue), D (5893 Angstrom, yellow) and C (6563 Angstrom, red): omega = (mu_F - mu_C)/(mu_D - 1) (PDF p. 182). A crown glass has a small omega, a flint glass a large one. *Example (Brijlal Example 8.1, recomputed):* crown with mu_C, mu_D, mu_F = 1.5145, 1.5170, 1.5230 gives omega = 0.0085/0.5170 = 0.01644; flint with 1.6444, 1.6520, 1.6637 gives 0.0193/0.6520 = 0.02960.

### Bridge: Paraxial rays, marginal rays and what "first-order" optics leaves out

The formulas of PH-043 to PH-046 (1/v - 1/u = 1/f and the rest) were derived by replacing the sine of every angle by the angle itself, which is accurate only when each ray lies **close to the axis** and makes a **small angle** with it. Such a ray is called **paraxial**. A ray that enters the lens far from the axis, near its rim, is called a **marginal** ray.

The first correction to sin theta = theta is sin theta = theta - theta^3/6. The cube makes the error grow quickly with the height h at which a ray meets the lens: doubling h makes the angle error about eight times larger in absolute terms and, as module 6 shows, the miss distance at the focus grows as h squared. So the exact image of a point is not a point; marginal rays focus at a different place from paraxial rays. This failure exists even for light of one colour. *Numbers (recomputed):* for theta = 0.1 rad, sin theta = 0.09983, an error of 0.17 per cent; for theta = 0.5 rad, sin theta = 0.4794, an error of 4.1 per cent.

### 1. Orientation: why a real lens does not make a point image, and the two families of aberration

*Objective:* Name the two families of lens aberration, state the cause of each in one sentence and say which verified questions fall on which.

## The promise and the failure

In PH-045 a thin lens obeyed 1/v - 1/u = 1/f exactly: every ray from a point object met in one image point. A real glass lens only approximates this. Light from a point comes out as a small **blur**, and the departure of the real image from the ideal one is called an **aberration** (a "straying"). Optical designers reduce it; a Physics Optional answer must show where it comes from and how a combination of lenses cancels part of it.

## Two families

1. **Chromatic aberration.** The index of glass depends on colour (bridge module B1), so the focal length depends on colour. White light is therefore focused at different points for different colours: blue nearest the lens and red farthest. This happens even for rays close to the axis.
2. **Monochromatic aberrations.** These occur for light of **one** colour. They arise because the thin-lens formula is only the first term of a series (bridge module B2). The one this syllabus line names is **spherical aberration**: rays at different heights h from the axis focus at different points. Four others (coma, astigmatism, curvature of field and distortion) are named in standard texts; the Brijlal extracts on this day do not treat them, so this lesson only names them (verify against your class notes).

### 2. Foundation: dispersive power, Abbe number, Cauchy's formula and the change of lens power with colour

*Objective:* Compute dispersive power and Abbe number from three indices and derive dP = omega P for a thin lens.

## Dispersive power and the Abbe number

Brijlal Section 8.4 (PDF pp. 182 to 183) defines the **dispersive power** of a glass as the ratio of the angular dispersion to the mean deviation of a thin prism, which reduces to

$$ omega = (mu_V - mu_R)/(mu - 1) $$

where mu is the index for the mean colour. It does not depend on the prism angle. For measurement the book uses the Fraunhofer lines: omega = (mu_F - mu_C)/(mu_D - 1), with F = 4861 Angstrom (blue), D = 5893 Angstrom (yellow) and C = 6563 Angstrom (red). Its inverse is the **Abbe number** V = (mu_D - 1)/(mu_F - mu_C). A small V means high dispersion; glasses with V above 55 are called **crowns** and those below 55 **flints** (PDF p. 183). Book Example 8.1 recomputed: crown omega = 0.01644 (V = 60.8), flint omega = 0.02960 (V = 33.8). The crown has V above 55 and the flint below it, as the rule says. **Dimension check:** omega is a ratio of two dimensionless differences, so it is dimensionless; V likewise.

## Why the index changes with colour: Cauchy

Brijlal Chapter 13 (PDF pp. 316 to 317) gives Cauchy's formula, mu = A + B/lambda^2, with A and B constants of the medium. It says that mu falls as the wavelength grows, which is **normal dispersion**. Differentiating, d mu/d lambda = -2B/lambda^3 (PDF p. 318). The slope is steeper at short wavelengths: the book notes that at 4000 Angstrom it is eight times its value at 8000 Angstrom, which checks: (8000/4000)^3 = 8. The book calls d mu/d lambda the "dispersive power" at that point, whereas Chapter 8 (PDF pp. 183 to 184) calls it the **chromatic dispersion** and keeps "dispersive power" for the ratio omega. In answers use omega for dispersive power and say d mu/d lambda for the rate of change. Over small regions near an absorption band the index can rise with wavelength; this is **anomalous dispersion** (PDF p. 318) and is not needed below.

## From glass to lens: the key relation dP = omega P

For a thin lens the lens-maker's formula (Brijlal Eq. 4.6, PDF p. 95; PH-045) is 1/f = (mu - 1) K with K = 1/R1 - 1/R2 fixed by the curvatures. The **power** is P = 1/f = (mu - 1) K. For the two colours blue and red,

$$ P_V - P_R = (mu_V - mu_R) K = omega (mu_Y - 1) K = omega P_Y $$

where P_Y is the power for the mean colour. This is the single most used step in the lesson: **the change of a thin lens's power between the two end colours equals omega times its mean power**. Dimension check: both sides have units of reciprocal length. Limiting case: a glass with no dispersion (omega = 0) has the same power for all colours.

## Numbers

Take a crown lens with P_Y = 5.00 per metre (f = 20 cm) and omega = 0.01644: P_V - P_R = 0.0822 per metre. A flint lens of the same P_Y with omega = 0.02960 changes by 0.148 per metre, 1.8 times as much. A glass of higher omega is the worse choice for a single lens and the better choice for the negative half of an achromat (module 5).

## Note on Brijlal's prism example

Brijlal Problem 1 of Chapter 8 (PDF p. 188), a prism pair with omega = 0.021 (mu = 1.62) and omega = 0.045 (mu = 1.65, A = 4.2 degrees), prints A = 11.25 degrees. Recomputed from the zero-dispersion condition omega (mu - 1) A = omega' (mu' - 1) A', A = 0.045 x 0.65 x 4.2/(0.021 x 0.62) = 9.44 degrees, which gives the book's own resultant deviation 3.1 degrees; so the printed A looks like a slip (check against the page image).

### 3. Core: axial and lateral chromatic aberration of a single thin lens (object at infinity)

*Objective:* Derive f_R - f_V = omega f_R f_V / f_Y (about omega f), the lateral colour difference and the blur diameter omega D, and check the dimensions.

## Derivation 1: the focal lengths for two colours

**Assumptions.** Thin lens in air, paraxial rays, a distant on-axis object, Cartesian convention (convex lens f > 0), refractive indices mu_V, mu_Y, mu_R for blue, yellow and red.

1. From the lens-maker's formula 1/f = (mu - 1) K: 1/f_V = (mu_V - 1) K and 1/f_R = (mu_R - 1) K.
2. Subtract: 1/f_V - 1/f_R = (mu_V - mu_R) K.
3. Write K = 1/((mu_Y - 1) f_Y) from the yellow lens: 1/f_V - 1/f_R = (mu_V - mu_R)/((mu_Y - 1) f_Y) = omega/f_Y. This is exact within the model.
4. Put the left side over a common denominator: (f_R - f_V)/(f_R f_V) = omega/f_Y, hence

$$ f_R - f_V = omega f_R f_V / f_Y  ≈  omega f $$

because f_R, f_V and f_Y differ by only a few per cent (error of order omega squared). The distance f_R - f_V is the **axial** (longitudinal) chromatic aberration: the red focus lies farther from the lens than the blue one.

*Dimension check:* both sides are lengths. *Limiting case:* omega -> 0 gives f_R = f_V. *Sign:* for a convex lens f > 0, so f_R - f_V > 0.

## Derivation 2: lateral colour and the blur disc

Let the distant object subtend a small angle theta. In each colour the image height is theta times the focal length, so the red image is larger than the blue one by theta (f_R - f_V) = theta omega f. This is the **lateral** (transverse) chromatic aberration for a distant object: a coloured fringe on the edge of the image.

Now take a screen at the blue focus. A red ray from the lens rim of diameter D crosses the axis a distance f_R - f_V beyond. By similar triangles the red cone has diameter D (f_R - f_V)/f_R on the screen:

$$ blur diameter ≈ omega D $$

*Unit check:* omega has no units, so the blur has the units of D. It depends on the aperture D and not on the focal length. *Numbers (recomputed):* crown omega = 0.01644 and D = 5 cm give 0.082 cm = 0.82 mm; for f = 20 cm the axial aberration is 0.01644 x 20 = 0.33 cm.

The diagram below the module body shows the rim rays and the blur disc.

## What this tells you

- The aberration is proportional to omega: choose a low-dispersion glass for a single lens.
- It does not shrink by making the lens longer in focal length (the axial difference grows with f), but the blur disc depends only on D; stopping down the lens (smaller D) reduces the blur disc, though it also cuts the light (the same idea as the stop in module 7).
- There is no one-lens cure for a given glass: since f_R - f_V = omega f and f is fixed by the job, only a second lens (module 5) can cancel the effect.

### 4. Core: chromatic aberration when the object is at a finite distance (the 2019 numerical)

*Objective:* Derive v_R - v_V = omega v_R v_V / f_Y for a fixed object distance and use it to solve the 2019 question.

## Derivation 3: fixed object, two colours

**Assumptions.** Thin lens in air, paraxial rays, a real object at a fixed distance u (u < 0 in the Cartesian convention of PH-045), so only the focal length changes with colour. v_V and v_R are the image distances for blue (violet) and red light.

1. Thin-lens formula for each colour: 1/v_V - 1/u = 1/f_V and 1/v_R - 1/u = 1/f_R.
2. Subtract. The term 1/u is the same for both colours and cancels:

$$ 1/v_V - 1/v_R = 1/f_V - 1/f_R $$

3. The right side was found in module 3 (step 3): it equals omega/f_Y, with f_Y the mean focal length. The left side is (v_R - v_V)/(v_R v_V). Hence

$$ v_R - v_V = omega v_R v_V / f_Y $$

*Check 1 (limit):* for u -> minus infinity, v -> f, so v_R - v_V -> omega f_R f_V / f_Y, which is module 3. *Check 2 (dimension):* omega is a number, v_R v_V / f_Y is a length. *Check 3 (size):* since v_R v_V is about v^2, v_R - v_V ≈ omega v^2/f. The axial colour error **grows as the square of the image distance**: it is larger than the object-at-infinity value omega f by the factor (v/f)^2.

## The 2019 question, solved as printed

Stem (2019 Q1(d), 10 marks): what is axial chromatic aberration? A convex lens has f = 15.5 x 10^-2 m for red and 14.45 x 10^-2 m for violet; the object is 40 cm from the lens; find the longitudinal chromatic aberration.

**Definition for the answer.** The axial (longitudinal) chromatic aberration is the distance along the axis between the images formed in two colours. For a distant object it is f_R - f_V; for a fixed object it is v_R - v_V.

**Numbers (recomputed in Python).** f_R = 15.5 cm, f_V = 14.45 cm, u = -40 cm.

1. v_R = 1/(1/15.5 - 1/40) = 1/(0.064516 - 0.025) = 25.306 cm.
2. v_V = 1/(1/14.45 - 1/40) = 1/(0.069204 - 0.025) = 22.622 cm.
3. v_R - v_V = **2.684 cm** (the violet image is nearer the lens).
4. Check with the formula: 1/f_V - 1/f_R = 0.004688 per cm, and v_R v_V = 572.5 cm^2; product 2.684 cm. The two routes agree.

**The reading to state.** The distant-object value f_R - f_V is only 1.05 cm; the finite object distance of 40 cm raises it by 2.56 times because the images are farther out. If a marking scheme expects the focal-length difference, the 1.05 cm would be written as the remark; the question gives an object distance, so the image-distance difference of 2.68 cm is the value this lesson gives (analysis, not an official key).

**A plausibility remark.** The stem data imply a mean-focal-length dispersive power omega = (f_R - f_V)/f_Y = 0.070 (using the mean 14.975 cm), more than twice the 0.030 of the dense flint in module 2. The data are used as printed; treat 0.07 as a large but harmless exam value, not as a glass property.

## Lateral effect for the same object

The magnification is v/u. For violet it is 22.622/(-40) = -0.5656 and for red -0.6327, so the red image is larger by 11.9 per cent, an image-size (lateral) difference of 0.067 per unit height of the object. The two images differ in size because they lie at different distances from the lens, so one screen cannot show both sharply: the edge of an extended object appears coloured.

### 5. Core: the achromatic doublet of two thin lenses in contact, and why the same glass cannot do it

*Objective:* Derive omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0, find f1 and f2 from F, omega1 and omega2, and answer the 2021 same-material question.

## Derivation 4: lenses in contact

**Assumptions.** Two thin lenses touching, in air, paraxial rays, Cartesian convention (convex f > 0). Lens 1 has focal length f1 and dispersive power omega1; lens 2 has f2 and omega2.

1. Lenses in contact (d = 0 in PH-046): the powers add, P = P1 + P2, that is 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2.
2. The change with colour is the sum of the changes: P_V - P_R = (P1)_V - (P1)_R + (P2)_V - (P2)_R. By module 2 each term is omega times the mean power, so P_V - P_R = omega1 P1 + omega2 P2.
3. **Achromatism** means the doublet has the same power for blue and red, P_V - P_R = 0:

$$ omega1 P1 + omega2 P2 = 0   i.e.   omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0 $$

4. Both omegas are positive, so f1 and f2 have **opposite signs**: one lens is convex and the other concave. Further, |f2|/|f1| = omega2/omega1.

*Dimension check:* each term is a number divided by a length. *Limit:* if omega2 = 0 for the second lens, only omega1/f1 = 0 remains, which is possible only for f1 infinite.

## Finding the two focal lengths

Eliminate f2. From step 3, f2 = -(omega2/omega1) f1. Substitute into 1/f1 + 1/f2 = 1/F:

$$ 1/f1 (1 - omega1/omega2) = 1/F,   so   f1 = F (omega2 - omega1)/omega2,   f2 = -F (omega2 - omega1)/omega1 $$

For a converging doublet (F > 0) we need f1 > 0, so omega2 > omega1: the **convex lens is the low-dispersion crown and the concave lens the high-dispersion flint**. The convex lens has the shorter focal length, so the doublet is converging.

## Numbers: the two book problems, recomputed

- Brijlal Chapter 5, Problem 3 (PDF p. 133): F = 0.9 m, omega1 = 0.024 and omega2 = 0.036. f1 = 90 (1 - 0.024/0.036) = 30 cm; f2 = -(0.036/0.024) x 30 = -45 cm. Check: 1/30 - 1/45 = 0.01111 = 1/90 and 0.024/30 + 0.036/(-45) = 0.0008 - 0.0008 = 0.
- Problem 4: crown omega = 0.012, flint omega = 0.020, F = 30 cm. f1 = 30 (1 - 0.6) = 12 cm; f2 = -(0.020/0.012) x 12 = -20 cm. Check: 1/12 - 1/20 = 0.03333 = 1/30.

## The 2021 same-material question

If both lenses are of the same glass, omega1 = omega2 and step 3 gives 1/f1 + 1/f2 = 0. By step 1 this means 1/F = 0, F infinite: the combination has no power. So two thin lenses **in contact** made of the same glass cannot form an achromatic doublet of finite focal length; one lens would only cancel the other. The condition that does work for the same glass needs a gap: d = (f1 + f2)/2 (Day 19 lesson PH-046, module 8; specialised in module 9).

## What an achromatic doublet does and does not do

It equalises the focal length for two chosen colours (here blue and red). It does not remove spherical aberration, and for all the colours between them a small residual error remains, called secondary spectrum (standard knowledge; verify against your class notes, since the extracts for this day do not cover it). A practical doublet is a crown convex lens cemented to a flint concave lens, as in the figure, with the shapes also chosen to reduce spherical aberration (module 9).

### 6. Core: spherical aberration of a thin lens, in numbers (plano-convex lens, which face first)

*Objective:* Define longitudinal and transverse spherical aberration, state how they scale with the ray height h, and read the numbers for a plano-convex lens in the two orientations.

## Definition

**Spherical aberration** is the failure of a lens (or mirror) with spherical surfaces to bring light of one colour from an axial point to one image point. A ray at height h from the axis meets the axis at a different point from a paraxial ray. In the Cartesian convention the **longitudinal spherical aberration** is LSA = z_m - z_p, the marginal focus minus the paraxial focus, both measured from the lens. For a convex lens the marginal ray is bent more, so z_m < z_p and LSA < 0 (the lens is "undercorrected"; a concave lens has the opposite sign). The **transverse spherical aberration** TSA is the height at which the marginal ray meets the paraxial focal plane; for small angles TSA = LSA x (h/f), with sign following the ray. Between the two foci there is a smallest blur patch, the "circle of least confusion" (standard knowledge; verify the exact position in your class text).

## Why it happens

At the curved surface the angle of incidence of a ray at height h is about h/R. The refraction law sin i = mu sin r, with the series sin x = x - x^3/6 (bridge B2), means a large-angle ray is bent a little too much compared with the paraxial rule. The excess grows as the cube of the angle, and the miss distance at the focus therefore grows as h squared. **Scaling:** LSA proportional to h^2, TSA proportional to h^3 (third-order theory; verify in your text).

## Numbers (exact ray trace, recomputed in Python)

A plano-convex lens, mu = 1.5, f = 10 cm (so the radius of the curved face is R = (mu - 1) f = 5 cm), parallel light, light of one colour. Zero centre thickness is assumed (both surfaces at the vertex plane); a real lens of finite thickness changes the h = 2 cm values slightly.

| Orientation | LSA for h = 0.5 cm | h = 1 cm | h = 2 cm | LSA/(h^2/f) at h = 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| curved face towards the light | -0.0293 cm | -0.1180 cm | -0.4906 cm | -1.18 |
| plane face towards the light | -0.1130 cm | -0.4582 cm | -1.9514 cm | -4.58 |

Four facts show in the table. (1) Doubling h from 1 cm to 2 cm multiplies LSA by 4.2 (curved face first) and 4.3: close to h^2, with a small excess because the third-order rule is only the leading term. (2) The tiny-h limit of the ratio is -1.167 and -4.5 (third-order theory: the lesson's own check; the exact values at h = 1 are a little larger). (3) **Putting the plane face towards the incident light makes the aberration 3.9 times larger** at h = 1 cm. So a plano-convex lens used on a distant object should have its curved face towards the object. (4) The h = 1 cm aberration is 0.118 cm, about 1 per cent of f, and the transverse error is LSA x h/f = 0.0118 cm = 0.12 mm.

## Why the shape matters

In the curved-face-first lens the bending of the ray is shared between two surfaces (paraxial shares 2 : 1); in the plane-face-first lens all of the bending occurs at one surface, at a large angle of incidence. The aberration rises faster than the bending, so avoiding a single heavily loaded surface helps. This is a reason to prefer curved-face-first, not a rule that equal sharing is best for a single lens: module 7 shows that the smallest aberration for mu = 1.5 occurs for a lens that is not symmetrical. ### 7. Master: lens shape, the best form and the other cures (third-order numbers)

*Objective:* Read a table of spherical aberration against lens shape, identify the best form and list what stops, doublets and aspheric surfaces change.

## The bending factor

For a thin lens of fixed focal length the two curvatures c1 = 1/R1 and c2 = 1/R2 may still be chosen in different ways, because only the difference c1 - c2 = 1/((mu - 1) f) is fixed. The choice is described by the **shape factor** q = (R2 + R1)/(R2 - R1) (Cartesian radii). With distances positive in the direction of the light (R > 0 means the centre of curvature lies beyond the surface): q = 0 is equiconvex; q = +1 is plano-convex with the curved face first (R1 > 0, R2 infinite); q = -1 is plano-convex with the plane face first (R1 infinite, R2 < 0); q = +2 is a meniscus convex towards the object (for example R1 = 5 cm, R2 = 15 cm).

## Third-order numbers for mu = 1.5, f = 10 cm, distant object

The table in the diagram box below comes from a paraxial-plus-third-order surface-by-surface calculation (Python, the lesson's own check; the exact ray trace of module 6 agrees to a few per cent for h = 1 cm). Every entry is LSA divided by h^2/f, so it applies to any focal length and any height small enough for third-order theory.

Reading the table: (1) the worst orientation is plane face first (-4.50); (2) the equiconvex lens is better (-1.67); (3) the **best form** for mu = 1.5 has q about +0.71, giving -1.07, and is almost plano-convex with the curved face towards the object (-1.17 is within 9 per cent of the best); (4) strongly bent meniscus shapes are worse again. A standard result (verify in your text) is that the best-form shape is q = +2 (mu^2 - 1)/(mu + 2) (hence +0.714 for mu = 1.5, that is R2 = -6 R1), and that its LSA is 15/14 = 1.071 h^2/f here.

**Consequence for the 2018 question.** The question uses plano-convex lenses, so the practical advice is: curved faces towards the incident beam. The plano-convex lens is chosen because its aberration is close to the best and it is easy to make.

## Other cures

- **A stop.** An iris that cuts the rim reduces LSA and the blur but also the light and the resolving power. Brijlal (PDF p. 486) describes a stop on the "central portion" of a large telescope objective that leaves the resolving power unchanged: a central stop keeps the full diameter D (so 1.22 lambda/D is unchanged) and leaves only an outer zone of rays, which narrows the spread of foci (check the page image).
- **Convex plus concave lenses.** The sign of the aberration changes with the sign of the power, so a concave lens can cancel part of the aberration of a convex lens. This is also how achromatic doublets are made to reduce spherical aberration (standard knowledge; verify).
- **Two separated lenses** with the bending shared (module 8).
- **Aspheric surfaces** (non-spherical): the spherical form itself is the cause; a surface can be shaped to give zero third-order aberration (standard knowledge; verify).

## Scaling and what it means

LSA scales as h^2/f for a fixed shape (table), so a lens of twice the aperture has four times the longitudinal aberration, which is why photographic lenses are stopped down for sharpness. The comparison with chromatic aberration is instructive: the chromatic blur disc is omega D (module 3), growing as D, while the spherical blur diameter for a fixed shape is proportional to D^3/f^2 (third-order theory; verify). Stopping down therefore helps spherical aberration much faster than it helps chromatic aberration.

### 8. Core: minimum spherical aberration of two thin lenses at d = f1 - f2 (the 2018 question)

*Objective:* Derive d = f1 - f2 from the equal-deviation argument, apply it to Brijlal problem 5, and state in one line what the argument assumes.

## The question (2018 Q4(c), 15 marks)

"What do you mean by spherical aberration of a lens? Show that if two plano-convex lenses are kept at a distance equal to the difference of their focal lengths, the spherical aberration would be minimum." Part one is module 6; part two follows.

## Derivation 5

**Assumptions.** Two thin plano-convex lenses of the same glass in air, curved faces towards the incident light (module 6), a parallel beam of one colour along the axis, paraxial ray heights, f1 > f2, separation d. A ray enters lens 1 at height h1.

1. Lens 1 deviates the ray towards the axis by the angle delta1 = h1/f1 (thin-lens rule).
2. After a distance d the ray has fallen to the height h2 = h1 - d delta1 = h1 (1 - d/f1).
3. Lens 2 deviates it further by delta2 = h2/f2 = h1 (1 - d/f1)/f2.
4. The total deviation is delta = delta1 + delta2 = h1 [1/f1 + 1/f2 - d/(f1 f2)] = h1/F (the equivalent-focal-length formula of PH-046).
5. **Principle.** The aberration produced by a lens of fixed shape grows faster than the deviation it produces, taken as the cube of it. For a fixed total delta, the sum Q = delta1^3 + (delta - delta1)^3 has dQ/d delta1 = 3 delta1^2 - 3 delta2^2 = 0 at delta1 = delta2, and Q'' = 6 delta > 0: a minimum, Q = delta^3/4, four times less than one lens doing all the work.
6. **Equal deviation:** delta1 = delta2 gives h1/f1 = h1 (1 - d/f1)/f2, so f2 = f1 - d:

$$ d = f1 - f2 $$

7. Check: d = f1 - f2 gives F = f1 f2/(f1 + f2 - d) = f1/2 and h2/h1 = f2/f1, so delta2 = h1/f1 = delta1.

## Numbers: Brijlal Chapter 5, Problem 5 (PDF p. 133)

A doublet corrected for spherical aberration has F = 10 cm and d = 2 cm. With d = f1 - f2, F = f1/2, so f1 = 20 cm and f2 = 18 cm. Check: 20 x 18/36 = 10 cm.

## What the argument assumes (state it in the answer)

1. Each lens is used in the same configuration and its aberration follows the cube law of its own deviation.
2. The ray heights at the two lenses differ (h2 = h1 f2/f1). Weighting by height, Q = h1 delta1^3 + h2 delta2^3, makes dQ/d delta1 at equal sharing equal to d delta^3/4 (sympy), which is positive: with the total deviation (that is F) held fixed, the true minimum has lens 1 doing slightly less than half (delta1 = 0.49 of the total at d/f1 = 0.1; 0.46 at d/f1 = 0.5).
3. Python check (third-order sums and an exact ray trace agree): plano-convex, mu = 1.5, f1 = 30 cm, f2 = 10 cm, curved faces first, h1 = 1 cm. The longitudinal aberration is least at d = 19.8 cm, almost exactly f1 - f2 = 20 cm. The transverse aberration in the focal plane is 0.00796 cm at d = 0, 0.00105 cm at d = 20 cm and least, 0.00097 cm, near d = 23.5 cm: the rule is about 9 per cent above that minimum and 7.6 times better than contact. Item 2 holds F fixed; here f1 and f2 are fixed and F changes with d (7.5 cm at d = 0, 15 cm at d = 20 cm), so the shifts need not agree. The best d depends on the measure: a good design rule, not an exact theorem (the lesson's own check; verify).

### 9. Master: correcting both aberrations at once, the Huygens eyepiece and the other monochromatic aberrations

## Two conditions on two lenses of the same glass

Brijlal Chapter 5, Problem 7 (PDF p. 133, Madurai Kamaraj 2003): two thin lenses of the same material separated by d have F = 50 cm and satisfy both no chromatic aberration and minimum spherical aberration; find f1, f2 and d.

**Condition 1 (achromatism, same glass).** omega1 = omega2 in the separated-pair formula d = (omega1 f2 + omega2 f1)/(omega1 + omega2) of PH-046 (module 8) gives d = (f1 + f2)/2. This is the answer to "can two lenses of the same glass be achromatic?": yes, if separated, and the lens pair keeps the same focal length for blue and red.

**Condition 2 (minimum spherical aberration, module 8).** d = f1 - f2.

**Solve.** Equate: f1 - f2 = (f1 + f2)/2 gives 2 f1 - 2 f2 = f1 + f2, so **f1 = 3 f2**. Then d = f1 - f2 = 2 f2. The equivalent focal length is F = f1 f2/(f1 + f2 - d) = 3 f2^2/(4 f2 - 2 f2) = 1.5 f2. For F = 50 cm: f2 = 33.3 cm, f1 = 100 cm, d = 66.7 cm.

**Check.** d = (100 + 33.3)/2 = 66.7 cm and f1 - f2 = 66.7 cm: both hold. F = 100 x 33.3/(133.3 - 66.7) = 3333/66.7 = 50 cm.

**Recognise it.** The ratio f1 : f2 = 3 : 1 with separation 2 f2 is the **Huygens eyepiece**, treated in PH-046 (module 7, Brijlal Example 7.7): focal lengths 3f and f separated by 2f. It satisfies both conditions at once, and that is a standard reason given for its design (the extract itself gives no design reason, so this is analysis, not a quoted statement).

## What is and is not corrected

The separated-pair condition makes the **equivalent focal length** the same for blue and red; as PH-046 module 8 states, the back focal distance still varies with colour, so the two coloured images are equal in size for a distant object but not at the same place. This is acceptable in an eyepiece, where the image is formed by an objective lens, and not for a camera lens. The equal-deviation rule reduces spherical aberration but, by module 8, does not remove it.

## Other aberrations (named only)

Standard texts add **coma** (a comet-shaped blur for object points off the axis), **astigmatism** (different foci for rays in two perpendicular planes), **curvature of field** (a curved instead of flat image surface) and **distortion** (the magnification varying across the field). Together with spherical aberration they are the five Seidel (third-order) monochromatic aberrations (standard knowledge; verify). The Brijlal extracts of this day do not treat them, and no verified question in this set asks about them, so they are listed here for completeness and not taught.

### Notes: Definitions

- Aberration: the departure of the real image of a point from the ideal point image of the paraxial thin-lens formula.
- Dispersion: the variation of the refractive index with wavelength; normal dispersion has mu falling as lambda grows (Cauchy, mu = A + B/lambda^2).
- Dispersive power: omega = (mu_V - mu_R)/(mu_Y - 1) = (mu_F - mu_C)/(mu_D - 1); the Abbe number is V = 1/omega; V above 55 crown, below 55 flint (Brijlal PDF p. 183).
- Axial (longitudinal) chromatic aberration: the distance along the axis between the images in two colours; lateral (transverse) chromatic aberration: the difference of image sizes in the two colours.
- Achromatic combination: a lens combination whose equivalent focal length is the same for two chosen colours.
- Spherical aberration: marginal rays focus at a different point from paraxial rays, for one colour; LSA = z_m - z_p (Cartesian), TSA = LSA x h/f for small angles.

### Notes: Equations

- P_V - P_R = omega P_Y for a thin lens (power change with colour), from 1/f = (mu - 1) K.
- 1/f_V - 1/f_R = omega/f_Y exactly, so f_R - f_V = omega f_R f_V/f_Y, about omega f (object at infinity).
- Fixed object distance: 1/v_V - 1/v_R = omega/f_Y, so v_R - v_V = omega v_R v_V/f_Y, about omega v^2/f.
- Blur diameter at the focus of one colour about omega D (D = aperture).
- Contact doublet: omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0; 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2; f1 = F (omega2 - omega1)/omega2, f2 = -F (omega2 - omega1)/omega1.
- Separated pair (PH-046 module 8): d = (omega1 f2 + omega2 f1)/(omega1 + omega2); same glass: d = (f1 + f2)/2.
- Minimum spherical aberration of two thin lenses (equal-deviation rule): d = f1 - f2, so F = f1/2.
- Third-order LSA of a thin lens of fixed shape: proportional to h^2/f (plano-convex, mu = 1.5: -1.17 h^2/f curved face first, -4.5 h^2/f plane face first).

  - Choosing the right quantity (f or v) for the object distance given
  - Using the sign of each focal length to decide which lens is convex
  - Stating the assumption behind a design rule
- **examinerTraps:**
  - A finite object distance hidden in a chromatic aberration question
  - Same-material wording that tests whether you separate contact from separated pairs
  - Swapped dispersive powers in a numerical
  - Mixed units (10^-2 m)
- **distractorAnalysis:**
  - 2023 numerical: the swapped formula gives 7.083 cm, the same-glass value would be 7.5 cm, and 7.917 cm is correct (module 10).
  - 2019 numerical: 1.05 cm is the distant-object value; 2.68 cm is the value for the stated object.
- **prelimsElimination:** - Physics Optional is assessed in Mains; for the lesson's own multiple-choice items use dimensions (omega is a number), signs (contact doublet needs opposite signs) and limiting cases to eliminate options.
- **mainsDirectives:** - Derive; show that; what do you mean by; obtain the condition; calculate
- **physicsRequirements:** - State the sign convention and assumptions; show each algebra step; give a labelled ray diagram; give a dimension check or a limiting case; present numbers with units; timed-solution targets (practice targets, not official allotments): 10 marks about 12 minutes, 15 marks about 18 minutes, 20 marks about 24 minutes.
- **commonMistakes:**
  - Using f_R - f_V for a near object
  - Believing the same glass is always impossible
  - Forgetting that d = f1 - f2 needs f1 > f2
  - Calling a doublet aberration-free

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Why does the refractive index depend on colour and how does this give a chromatic aberration?
    - **a:** Cauchy: mu = A + B/lambda^2, so mu_B > mu_R; 1/f = (mu - 1) K gives f_V < f_R, so the colours focus at different points.
  - - **q:** Compute omega for mu_C = 1.5145, mu_D = 1.5170, mu_F = 1.5230.
    - **a:** 0.0085/0.5170 = 0.01644.
  - - **q:** What is the sign of LSA for a convex lens in the Cartesian convention?
    - **a:** Negative: the marginal focus is nearer the lens than the paraxial focus.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why does the same-glass doublet in contact have zero power if it is achromatic?
    - **a:** With equal omegas, omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0 gives 1/f1 + 1/f2 = 0, so 1/F = 0.
  - - **q:** Why does a plano-convex lens show less spherical aberration with the curved face to a distant object?
    - **a:** The bending is shared between two surfaces rather than loaded on one at a large angle; the exact trace gives 3.9 times less LSA for mu = 1.5.
  - - **q:** Why is the axial colour error larger for a near object than for a distant one?
    - **a:** The images lie farther from the lens; v_R - v_V = omega v_R v_V/f_Y, about omega v^2/f.

    - **task:** Derive the axial chromatic aberration f_R - f_V = omega f_R f_V/f_Y for a thin lens and the blur-disc estimate omega D (10 marks; practice target about 12 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: thin lens in air, paraxial rays, distant axial object, Cartesian convention, convex lens f > 0.
      - Lens-maker's formula for each colour: 1/f_V = (mu_V - 1) K and 1/f_R = (mu_R - 1) K.
      - Subtract: 1/f_V - 1/f_R = (mu_V - mu_R) K.
      - Use K = 1/((mu_Y - 1) f_Y): the right side is (mu_V - mu_R)/((mu_Y - 1) f_Y) = omega/f_Y.
      - Combine the left side: (f_R - f_V)/(f_R f_V) = omega/f_Y, so f_R - f_V = omega f_R f_V/f_Y, about omega f.
      - Blur: at the blue focus the red cone of aperture D has diameter D (f_R - f_V)/f_R, about omega D.
      - Diagram: blue focus nearer the lens than the red focus, the screen at F_V showing the red disc.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimension: omega is a number, so f_R - f_V is a length and omega D has the units of D.
      - Limit: omega -> 0 gives f_R = f_V.
      - Numbers: omega = 0.0164, f = 25 cm give 0.41 cm; D = 5 cm gives a blur of 0.82 mm.

    - **task:** Derive the achromatism condition for two thin lenses in contact and the separated same-glass case (15 marks; practice target about 18 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: thin lenses in air, paraxial, fixed mounts, dP_i = omega_i P_i.
      - Contact: P = P1 + P2.
      - Colour change: dP = omega1 P1 + omega2 P2.
      - Achromatic: dP = 0 so omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0.
      - Solve with 1/f1 + 1/f2 = 1/F: f1 = F (omega2 - omega1)/omega2 and f2 = -F (omega2 - omega1)/omega1.
      - Same glass in contact: 1/F = 0, impossible for finite F.
      - Separated pair (PH-046 module 8): P = P1 + P2 - d P1 P2; dP = 0 gives omega1/P2 + omega2/P1 = d (omega1 + omega2); with equal omegas d = (f1 + f2)/2.

    - **task:** Derive d = f1 - f2 for minimum spherical aberration of two thin lenses by the equal-deviation argument, and state its limits (15 marks; practice target about 18 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: two thin lenses of one glass in air, curved faces first, parallel ray at height h1, paraxial heights.
      - delta1 = h1/f1 (deviation by lens 1).
      - h2 = h1 - d delta1 = h1 (1 - d/f1).
      - delta2 = h2/f2.
      - Total delta = delta1 + delta2 = h1/F, which agrees with 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 - d/(f1 f2).
      - Principle: aberration of each lens follows the cube of its deviation; Q = delta1^3 + delta2^3 with fixed sum is least for delta1 = delta2.
      - delta1 = delta2 gives f2 = f1 - d, i.e. d = f1 - f2; then F = f1/2.

### Memory skeleton

- Dispersion: Cauchy μ = A + B/λ²; dispersive power ω = (μ_V − μ_R)/(μ_Y − 1); Abbe V = 1/ω, crown above 55
- Key step: 1/f = (μ − 1)K gives P_V − P_R = ω P_Y
- Distant object: f_R − f_V = ω f_R f_V/f_Y ≈ ω f; blue nearer; lateral colour; blur ≈ ω D
- Fixed object: 1/u cancels, v_R − v_V = ω v_R v_V/f_Y ≈ ω v²/f (2019: 2.68 cm against 1.05 cm)
- Contact achromat: ω₁/f₁ + ω₂/f₂ = 0; convex crown + concave flint; same glass in contact has no power
- Separated pair: d = (ω₁f₂ + ω₂f₁)/(ω₁ + ω₂); same glass d = (f₁ + f₂)/2
- Spherical aberration and d = f₁ − f₂: lesson flags verify; achromatic is not aberration-free

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, derive P_V − P_R = ω P_Y from the lens-maker's formula, get f_R − f_V and v_R − v_V, then derive the contact achromat condition and solve for f₁ and f₂.

### Last-week summary

- ω = (μ_F − μ_C)/(μ_D − 1), dimensionless; V = 1/ω, crown V > 55 (ω ≈ 0.016), flint V < 55 (ω ≈ 0.030); dμ/dλ = −2B/λ³ is chromatic dispersion, not ω.
- P_V − P_R = ω P_Y; f_R − f_V = ω f_R f_V/f_Y ≈ ω f (red farther); blur disc ≈ ω D, independent of f; lateral = image-size difference θ ω f.
- Fixed object: v_R − v_V = ω v_R v_V/f_Y ≈ ω v²/f, larger by (v/f)². 2019 (15.5, 14.45, −40 cm): v_R = 25.306, v_V = 22.622, 2.684 cm; distant 1.05 cm.
- Contact: ω₁/f₁ + ω₂/f₂ = 0, f₁ = F(ω₂ − ω₁)/ω₂, f₂ = −F(ω₂ − ω₁)/ω₁; Problem 3 (90 cm, 0.024, 0.036): +30, −45 cm.
- Separated: d = (ω₁f₂ + ω₂f₁)/(ω₁ + ω₂) (never ω₁f₁ + ω₂f₂); same glass (f₁ + f₂)/2; leaves spherical aberration and the colour of the back focal distance.
