### Bridge: Deciding among the three conservation laws

A conservation law is a statement of the form 'if a certain kind of push is absent, a certain quantity stays fixed'. The three laws need *different* absent pushes:

- **Linear momentum** is fixed (for the system you chose) when the total **external force** is zero.
- **Angular momentum about a chosen point** is fixed when the total **external torque** about that point is zero.
- **Mechanical energy** is fixed when nothing does non-conservative work (friction, an inelastic collision, a motor) and no internal source supplies or removes energy.

Because the requirements differ, one event can conserve one quantity and violate the others. A ball that bounces off a fixed wall does not conserve its momentum (the wall's impulse reverses it), although an elastic bounce keeps its kinetic energy and its angular momentum about any point on the line of the impulse; a bullet hitting a hinged door conserves angular momentum about the hinge but not linear momentum. The *point* about which you take angular momentum is a free choice, and a clever choice (the hinge, the contact point) removes unknown forces from the equation because a force whose line of action passes through the chosen point has zero torque about it.

Habit to build: before using any law, say the system, say the point, and say which external agent could break the law. Module 8 gives a flowchart.

### Bridge: Impulsive forces and the 'short time' approximation

An **impulsive force** is a very large force that acts for a very short time, such as a bat hitting a ball or a bullet entering wood. Its **impulse** is Π = ∫F dt, which equals the change in momentum (PH-001 module 3). Typical values: a bat applies an average force of thousands of newtons for about a thousandth of a second.

The **short-time approximation** says: during the impact, any *finite* force (gravity, a spring, kinetic friction from slow sliding, tension from a hanging weight) contributes an impulse proportional to the very short duration and so is negligible against the impulsive one. For example, gravity of 20 N acting for 0.001 s gives an impulse of 0.02 N s; the bat's impulse is perhaps 10 N s. The positions of the bodies also do not change appreciably during the impact, so the lever arms stay fixed.

Consequences used in this lesson: (i) just before and just after an impact the *same* positions apply; (ii) unknown impulsive reactions at a hinge or a contact point can be eliminated by taking angular momentum about that point; (iii) energy is **not** assumed conserved unless the collision is stated to be elastic, because the impact usually deforms the bodies.

Angular impulse is the same idea for torque: ∫τ dt = change in angular momentum (Upadhyaya, extract page 171).

### 2. Foundation: one table for the three theorems

*Objective:* Write the momentum, angular-momentum and energy theorems for a system of particles and for a rigid body, with their conditions.

## Conventions

Inertial frame; right-handed axes; positions rᵢ from a fixed origin O; R the CM position and V = dR/dt its velocity; r′ᵢ = rᵢ − R and v′ᵢ = vᵢ − V measured **relative to the CM**; M = Σmᵢ; anticlockwise as seen from +z is a positive L_z. By the definition of the CM, Σmᵢr′ᵢ = 0 and so Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0 (PH-004 module 6).

## The statement table

| Quantity | System of particles | Rigid body (planar motion or fixed axis) | Theorem (cause and effect) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear momentum | P = Σmᵢvᵢ = MV | P = MV_cm | dP/dt = F_ext; internal forces cancel in pairs (Newton's third law) |
| Angular momentum about a fixed point O | L_O = Σrᵢ × mᵢvᵢ = R × MV + L_cm | L_O = R × MV + I_cm ω (planar); I_O ω (fixed axis through O) | dL_O/dt = τ_O,ext; needs internal forces along the line joining each pair |
| Angular momentum about the CM | L_cm = Σr′ᵢ × mᵢv′ᵢ | I_cm ω (axis through the CM, principal axis) | dL_cm/dt = τ_cm,ext, **valid even if the CM accelerates** |
| Angular momentum about a moving point A | L_A = Σ(rᵢ − r_A) × mᵢvᵢ | same | dL_A/dt = τ_A − V_A × P |
| Kinetic energy | T = ½MV² + Σ½mᵢv′ᵢ² | ½MV² + ½I_cm ω² | dT = W_ext + W_int |
| Mechanical energy | E = T + U | same | conserved if non-conservative work is zero; internal forces of a rigid body do no net work |

## The conditions in plain words

- **Momentum**: F_ext = 0 gives P constant; if only one component of F_ext is zero, only that component of P is constant (a ball thrown under gravity keeps its horizontal momentum).
- **Angular momentum about O**: τ_O,ext = 0 gives L_O constant in magnitude and direction. The point matters: the same motion can have τ ≠ 0 about one point and τ = 0 about another.
- **Energy**: the work done on the system by *all* forces, internal and external, equals ΔT. For a rigid body the internal work is zero, so only external work counts. A collision that is perfectly inelastic loses kinetic energy to heat, sound and deformation; an explosion or a skater pulling in the arms *gains* kinetic energy from internal stores.

## Derivation of the energy theorem (dT = W_ext + W_int, and when E is conserved)

**Assumptions.** Inertial frame; particle i has external force Fᵢ and internal force Fᵢⱼ from particle j, with Fⱼᵢ = −Fᵢⱼ (Newton III).

Step 1. mᵢ dvᵢ/dt = Fᵢ + Σⱼ Fᵢⱼ. Take the dot product with vᵢ dt = drᵢ: d(½mᵢvᵢ²) = Fᵢ·drᵢ + Σⱼ Fᵢⱼ·drᵢ.

Step 2. Sum over i. The internal terms pair up: Fᵢⱼ·drᵢ + Fⱼᵢ·drⱼ = Fᵢⱼ·d(rᵢ − rⱼ). So dT = Σ Fᵢ·drᵢ + Σ_pairs Fᵢⱼ·d(rᵢ − rⱼ) = dW_ext + dW_int.

Step 3 (internal work). If Fᵢⱼ is central, Fᵢⱼ = f(r) r̂ with r = |rᵢ − rⱼ|, then Fᵢⱼ·d(rᵢ − rⱼ) = f(r) dr. For a **rigid body** every r is constant, so dW_int = 0 and dT = dW_ext. For central conservative forces f(r) dr = −dUᵢⱼ, so dT + dU_int = dW_ext.

Step 4 (conservation). If the external forces split into conservative ones (potential energy U_ext) and non-conservative ones, dW_ext = −dU_ext + dW_nc, so d(T + U_int + U_ext) = dW_nc. Mechanical energy E = T + U is **conserved when dW_nc = 0** (no friction, drag, motor or inelastic deformation).

**Check.** Dimensions: every term is a force times a length, J. Limit: one particle in uniform gravity gives dT = −d(mgy), the familiar result. This derivation is standard and written here; it is not copied from an extract (verify against Upadhyaya Chapter 2).

## Two things the table hides

1. The angular-momentum theorem for a *system* uses **strong** Newton III: the force between two particles is equal, opposite **and along the line joining them**, so that internal torques cancel (PH-004 module 5). Without it, internal torques need not cancel.
2. For a rigid body in *three-dimensional* motion L_cm = I·ω (a tensor product) and need not be parallel to ω; I_cm ω is valid for planar motion about an axis through the CM that is a principal axis (PH-017 module 9).

These are exactly the kind of conditions marks are given for.

### 3. Core: exact conditions, misapplications and constraint forces

*Objective:* Decide which law survives a given event and which constraint forces do no work.

## Which law survives which event

| Event | Momentum P | Angular momentum | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic collision of free bodies | conserved | conserved about any fixed point | conserved |
| Perfectly inelastic collision of free bodies | conserved | conserved | not conserved |
| Particle sticks to a rod **hinged** at O | **not** conserved (hinge impulse) | conserved **about O only** | not conserved |
| Block sliding on a rough floor | not conserved (friction) | not conserved (friction exerts a torque about most points) | not conserved (heat) |
| Ball sliding then rolling | not conserved | **not** about the CM; **conserved about the ground line** | not conserved |
| Skater pulls in the arms | conserved (no horizontal force) | conserved about the vertical axis | **increases** (internal work) |
| Explosion in flight (across the instant of explosion) | conserved (gravity's impulse is negligible) | conserved about any point during the explosion | **increases** |
| Rolling without slipping on a slope | not conserved | not conserved | conserved (static friction does no work) |

In the explosion row, gravity acts throughout the flight, so P and L about an arbitrary point are conserved only across the brief explosion (impulsive approximation). Over the whole flight the CM continues on the same parabola (NCERT 6.3).

## Why the entries differ

**The hinge case** is the classic trap. A hinge can push with any force, so P is not conserved during the impact. But the hinge force has *zero moment about the hinge*, so L about the hinge is conserved (PH-004 module 8; module 7 here).

**Friction.** Kinetic friction acts at a point sliding over the ground, so it removes mechanical energy as heat. In a sliding-to-rolling ball (module 8) friction's torque about the *centre* is non-zero, so L_cm changes; but friction acts along the ground line, so its torque about *any point on the ground line* is zero and L about that line is conserved.

**Static friction in rolling** acts where the surface and the body are momentarily at rest, so it does no work: energy is conserved while P and L_cm change.

## Which forces do no work

| Constraint force | Why its work is zero |
|---|---|
| Normal force from a fixed smooth surface | perpendicular to the velocity |
| Hinge force at a fixed hinge | the point of application does not move |
| Static friction in pure rolling | the contact point is at rest |
| Tension in an inextensible string between two bodies | the pair do equal and opposite work |
| Internal forces in a rigid body | the distances do not change |

If the surface or the hinge itself moves (a lift floor, a sliding wedge), the force *does* work on the moving body; check the point of application.

## Component, point and time choices

Conservation can hold for a **component** (horizontal momentum under gravity; angular momentum about the vertical axis for a central force plus gravity). It can hold during a **short time** only (module 7). A law that fails for the whole motion may hold for the impact alone, as in the ballistic pendulum.

Do not say a quantity is conserved merely because the 'system is isolated': the isolation is about *external forces and torques*, and torque depends on the chosen point.

### 4. Core: König's theorems for kinetic energy and angular momentum

*Objective:* Derive T = ½MV² + T′ and L_O = R × P + L_cm and use them for rolling bodies.

## Why the centre of mass is special

Both the kinetic energy and the angular momentum of a system split into **two clean parts**: the motion of the CM as if all the mass sat there, and the motion *about* the CM. These are **König's theorems**. They are why a spinning, translating body can be handled by two separate calculations. PH-012 module 8 already derives the n-particle energy split (and PH-009 module 4 the two-body case); here the proof is rewritten in the form examiners expect (the 2024 question) and the angular-momentum split (PH-004 module 6) is proved the same way, so that both sit together.

## Derivation 1: kinetic energy (König's first theorem)

**Assumptions.** n particles of masses mᵢ and velocities vᵢ in an inertial frame; M = Σmᵢ; V = Σmᵢvᵢ/M the CM velocity; v′ᵢ = vᵢ − V the velocity relative to the CM.

Step 1 (the key identity). Σmᵢv′ᵢ = Σmᵢvᵢ − VΣmᵢ = MV − MV = **0**.

Step 2 (expand the square). Since vᵢ = V + v′ᵢ,

$$ T = ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ·vᵢ = ½ Σ mᵢ (V + v′ᵢ)·(V + v′ᵢ) = ½ V² Σmᵢ + V·Σmᵢv′ᵢ + ½ Σ mᵢ v′ᵢ² $$

Step 3 (use Step 1). The middle term is V·0 = 0, and Σmᵢ = M:

$$ T = ½ M V² + ½ Σ mᵢ v′ᵢ² = ½ M V² + T′ $$

**Interpretation.** ½MV² is the energy of the CM motion; T′ is the internal kinetic energy (about the CM). Only T′ can be changed by internal forces, which is why a collision's lost energy comes from T′ (PH-011 module 3). T′ is the same for every inertial observer, because v′ᵢ is measured relative to the CM. **Check.** Three particles with masses 2, 3, 5 kg and velocities (3, 1, 0), (−2, 4, 1), (1, −1, 2) m s⁻¹ give V = (0.5, 0.9, 1.3) m s⁻¹, T = 56.5 J, ½MV² = 13.75 J, T′ = 42.75 J: the parts add to 56.5 J and Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0.

**Rigid body.** In planar rotation v′ᵢ = ω × r′ᵢ has magnitude ωρᵢ, with ρᵢ the distance from the axis through the CM, so T′ = ½ω² Σmᵢρᵢ² = ½I_cm ω² and

$$ T = ½ M V² + ½ I_cm ω² $$

(Upadhyaya shows the rolling case, extract pages 172-173.) **Rolling without slipping**, V = ωR: T = ½MV²(1 + k²/R²), with k the radius of gyration about the CM. The factor is 3/2 for a disc or solid cylinder (T = ¾MV²), 7/5 for a solid sphere (T = 0.7MV²), 5/3 for a thin spherical shell (T = (5/6)MV²) and 2 for a ring (T = MV²). It also equals ½I_Pω² about the contact point, since I_P = I_cm + MR² (parallel axis).

## Derivation 2: angular momentum (König's second theorem)

About a fixed point O, with rᵢ = R + r′ᵢ and vᵢ = V + v′ᵢ:

$$ L_O = Σ (R + r′ᵢ) × mᵢ(V + v′ᵢ) = R × MV + R × Σmᵢv′ᵢ + (Σmᵢr′ᵢ) × V + Σ r′ᵢ × mᵢv′ᵢ $$

The second and third terms vanish (Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0 and Σmᵢr′ᵢ = 0), so

$$ L_O = R × P + L_cm,    L_cm = Σ r′ᵢ × mᵢ v′ᵢ $$

The orbital part R × P depends on the origin O; the spin part L_cm does not. L_cm can equally be computed with the *lab* velocities, because Σr′ᵢ × mᵢV = (Σmᵢr′ᵢ) × V = 0. PH-004 module 6 gives the same proof with the Earth's orbital and spin parts as numbers; Mathur section 6.13 states it (extract page 295).

**Rigid body (planar).** L_cm = Σmᵢρᵢ²ω = I_cm ω, so L_O = R × MV + I_cm ω.

### 5. Core: dL_cm/dt = τ_cm in general, and the contact-point method for rolling

*Objective:* Prove the torque law about the centre of mass for an accelerating CM and apply the moving-point theorem to rolling.

## The aim

Show that **dL_cm/dt = τ_cm,ext** for a system whose CM may be accelerating, and then use the same idea to justify taking torque about the contact point of a rolling body.

## General proof (about the centre of mass)

**Assumptions.** Inertial frame; fixed origin O; internal forces equal and opposite along the line joining each pair (strong Newton III); external force Fᵢ on particle i.

Step 1 (internal torques cancel). For a pair (i, j) the total torque about O is rᵢ × Fᵢⱼ + rⱼ × Fⱼᵢ = (rᵢ − rⱼ) × Fᵢⱼ = 0 because Fᵢⱼ is parallel to rᵢ − rⱼ.

Step 2 (torque about O). Hence dL_O/dt = τ_O,ext = Σ rᵢ × Fᵢ = Σ (R + r′ᵢ) × Fᵢ = R × F_ext + Σ r′ᵢ × Fᵢ, with F_ext = ΣFᵢ.

Step 3 (differentiate the König split). L_O = R × P + L_cm, so dL_O/dt = V × P + R × dP/dt + dL_cm/dt. Now V × P = V × MV = 0 and dP/dt = F_ext, so dL_O/dt = R × F_ext + dL_cm/dt.

Step 4 (compare). Equate Steps 2 and 3:

$$ dL_cm/dt = Σ r′ᵢ × Fᵢ = τ_cm,ext $$

**Why the accelerating CM does not spoil it.** In the CM frame each particle also feels the fictitious force −mᵢA (A the CM acceleration), whose torque about the CM is Σr′ᵢ × (−mᵢA) = −(Σmᵢr′ᵢ) × A = 0. The centre of mass is the one point for which this works for any A.

**Check.** Dimensions: [L_cm/t] = ML²T⁻² = [torque]. Limit: a free spinning ball with no external force has constant L_cm; a thrown spinning ball in uniform gravity has τ_cm = 0 (weight acts at the CM) so its spin is constant.

## The moving-point theorem and rolling

For any reference point A with velocity V_A, dL_A/dt = τ_A − V_A × P (PH-004 module 3). The extra term vanishes if V_A = 0 (a fixed point), if A is the CM (V × MV = 0), or **if A moves with a velocity parallel to P**. For a body rolling on a level or inclined surface, take A as the **geometric contact point** on the surface line: it moves with the CM's velocity V along the surface, so V_A × P = 0 and the torque equation holds about it. (The material point of the body at the contact is instantaneously at rest, V_A = 0, and also works.)

## Application: a solid cylinder rolling down an incline of angle α

About the contact point: L = I_cm ω + MRV = (I_cm + MR²)ω with V = Rω (both terms in the same sense). Gravity's component along the slope acts at the CM at lever arm R, torque MgR sinα; the normal and friction forces act at the contact point, torque zero, and the perpendicular component of gravity passes through it. So

$$ (I_cm + MR²) dω/dt = MgR sinα    ⟹    a = Rdω/dt = g sinα/(1 + I_cm/MR²) $$

For the solid cylinder I_cm = ½MR²: **a = (2/3) g sinα**; for α = 30°, a = 3.27 m s⁻² (a frictionless slide would give 4.90). After 2 m from rest v² = 2as = 13.08, v = 3.617 m s⁻¹; energy conservation gives the same, v² = (4/3) g s sinα. The two routes agreeing is the standard check. Static friction f = ⅓Mg sinα is needed; it does no work.

## Caution

L_cm = I_cm ω needs planar motion about a principal axis through the CM; for a general 3-D rotation L_cm is not parallel to ω (PH-017 module 9).

### 6. Core: impulse for rigid bodies and the centre of percussion

*Objective:* Derive the centre of percussion for a pivoted body and a uniform rod, and the free-body impulse results.

## Impulse for a rigid body

An **impulse** Π is delivered in a short time (bridge B2). For a rigid body of mass M the two impulse equations are

$$ Π_total = M ΔV_cm,    ∫τ_cm dt = I_cm Δω $$

(linear impulse changes the CM momentum; angular impulse about the CM changes the spin). If the body is pivoted at O the pivot supplies an unknown impulse H, so one takes the angular impulse about O instead.

## Free rod struck at distance d from the CM

No pivot. V_cm = Π/M and I_cm ω = Πd. The point of the rod momentarily at rest lies at distance s = I_cm/(Md) = k²/d from the CM on the side opposite to the blow (k the radius of gyration about the CM). PH-004 module 8 has the numbers (v_cm = 5.0 m s⁻¹, ω = 12.5 rad s⁻¹, s = 0.40 m for its data).

## Derivation 3: the centre of percussion

**Set-up.** A body of mass M is pivoted at O on a smooth horizontal axis; its CM is at distance h from O; I_O = I_cm + Mh² = M(k² + h²). An impulse Π perpendicular to the line from O through the CM acts at distance ℓ_p from O (same side as the CM). Let H be the impulse of the pivot, taken positive in the direction of Π.

Step 1 (angular impulse about O; H has no moment about O): Πℓ_p = I_O ω.

Step 2 (linear impulse): Π + H = M V_cm = M h ω.

Step 3 (eliminate ω = Πℓ_p/I_O): H = Π (M h ℓ_p/I_O − 1).

Step 4: H = 0 (the pivot feels no jolt) when

$$ ℓ_p = I_O/(M h) = (k² + h²)/h $$

This is the **centre of percussion**. Equivalent statement: (ℓ_p − h) h = k², so if the blow lands at distance h′ = ℓ_p − h beyond the CM, then h h′ = k²: the pivot and the centre of percussion are interchangeable. It is also the **centre of oscillation**: I_O/(Mh) is the length of the equivalent simple pendulum (PH-017 module 8).

**Uniform rod of length ℓ pivoted at one end.** h = ℓ/2, I_O = Mℓ²/3, so ℓ_p = (Mℓ²/3)/(Mℓ/2) = **2ℓ/3**. Then H = Π(3ℓ_p/(2ℓ) − 1): for ℓ_p < 2ℓ/3 the pivot pushes *against* the blow (H < 0); for ℓ_p > 2ℓ/3 it pushes *with* the blow (H > 0); at 2ℓ/3 it vanishes.

**Numbers.** Rod ℓ = 1.2 m, M = 3 kg, Π = 5 N s at ℓ_p = 1.0 m from the pivot: ω = 3Πℓ_p/(Mℓ²) = 3.472 rad s⁻¹, V_cm = 2.083 m s⁻¹, H = +1.25 N s; the centre of percussion is at 0.80 m (python check).

**Checks.** Dimensions: ℓ_p is I/(Mh) = ML²/(ML) = L. Limit: if h → ∞ (CM far away) ℓ_p → h, the point mass at the CM. **Meaning**: a cricket bat or a hammer struck at the centre of percussion does not sting the hand.

### 7. Core: worked problems I - a rod struck by a sticking particle, and a bullet into a door

*Objective:* Solve impulsive problems by choosing the point that removes the unknown reaction.

## Problem A: a particle sticks to a free rod

*A uniform rod (M = 1.0 kg, ℓ = 1.0 m) lies on a smooth horizontal table. A particle (m = 0.20 kg) moving at u = 10 m s⁻¹ perpendicular to the rod strikes it at d = 0.40 m from its centre and sticks. Find the motion just after, and the energy lost.*

**Choose the theorems.** The table is smooth and the impact is short, so no horizontal external impulse: **momentum** and **angular momentum** about a fixed point are conserved. The collision is perfectly inelastic, so energy is not.

Step 1 (momentum): mu = (M + m)V gives V = 2/1.2 = **1.667 m s⁻¹** for the CM of the combined body.

Step 2 (where is the combined CM?): from the rod's centre, toward the particle, x_c = md/(M + m) = 0.0667 m.

Step 3 (moment of inertia about the combined CM): rod about its own centre Mℓ²/12 = 0.08333, plus the parallel-axis terms Mx_c² + m(d − x_c)² = Mm d²/(M + m) = 0.02667, so I = **0.1100 kg m²**.

Step 4 (angular momentum about the lab point at the combined CM at the instant of impact): before, only the particle moves, with lever arm d − x_c = 0.3333 m: L = mu(d − x_c) = 0.6667 kg m² s⁻¹. After, the CM is at that point, so only spin remains: Iω. Hence

$$ ω = mu(d − x_c)/I = 0.6667/0.1100 = 6.06 rad/s $$

**Check with another point** (the rod's initial centre): before, mud = 0.800; after, (M + m)V x_c + Iω = 0.1333 + 0.6667 = 0.800. ✓

Step 5 (energy): before 10.00 J; after ½(1.2)(1.667²) + ½(0.11)(6.06²) = 1.667 + 2.020 = **3.687 J**. Lost 6.313 J (63.1%) to deformation and heat. Velocities just after: rod centre V − ωx_c = 1.263 m s⁻¹ and the particle's point V + ω(d − x_c) = 3.687 m s⁻¹, so the particle has slowed from 10 m s⁻¹.

## Problem B: a bullet into a door

*A door (M = 15 kg, width w = 0.90 m, hinged at one vertical edge, I_door = Mw²/3) is struck by a bullet (m = 0.020 kg, 300 m s⁻¹) that embeds at x = 0.80 m from the hinge.*

The hinge impulse is unknown, so take angular momentum about the **hinge**: mvx = I_tot ω with I_tot = Mw²/3 + mx² = 4.0628 kg m². Then ω = 0.020 × 300 × 0.80/4.0628 = **1.181 rad s⁻¹**. The kinetic energy afterwards is ½I_totω² = 2.835 J out of 900 J (0.32% retained; the rest is heat and wood deformation). The bullet's speed after is ωx = 0.945 m s⁻¹.

**Hinge impulse.** Linear impulse: mv + H = (Mw/2 + mx)ω = 7.994 N s, so H = 7.994 − 6.000 = **+1.99 N s**, along the bullet's direction: **linear momentum is not conserved** (6.0 before, 7.99 after). Had the bullet struck at x = 2w/3 = 0.60 m (the centre of percussion of the door-bullet system, module 6) the hinge impulse would be zero.

**Why the energy 'loss' is not a mystery.** The hinge does no work (its point does not move), so the 897 J goes into the bullet and door as heat and deformation.

## Method summary

1. Name the unknown reaction (hinge, table).
2. Choose the point that removes it.
3. Write L before and after about that point.
4. Add P only if no support acts; state energy is lost unless elastic.
5. Check with a second point or with P.

### 8. Core: worked problems II - disc on disc, a rolling ball at a step, sliding turning to rolling

*Objective:* Apply L about a contact line or edge and account for the energy lost.

## Problem C: a disc dropped on a rotating disc

*Two coaxial discs on a frictionless axle: I₁ = 0.090 kg m² (spinning at ω₁ = 40 rad s⁻¹) and I₂ = 0.045 kg m² (at ω₂ = 10 rad s⁻¹ in the same sense). They are brought into contact and friction makes them rotate together.*

The friction between them is **internal**; the axle exerts no torque about the axis, so L about the axis is conserved: I₁ω₁ + I₂ω₂ = (I₁ + I₂)ω, giving ω = (3.6 + 0.45)/0.135 = **30 rad s⁻¹**. Energy before 74.25 J, after 60.75 J, **lost 13.5 J**. General algebra: (I₁ω₁² + I₂ω₂²)(I₁ + I₂) − (I₁ω₁ + I₂ω₂)² = I₁I₂(ω₁ − ω₂)², so

$$ ΔE = −½ [I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂)] (ω₁ − ω₂)² $$

For ω₂ = −10 rad s⁻¹ (opposite sense) ω = 23.33 rad s⁻¹ and the loss is 37.5 J: more relative motion, more loss.

## Rolling collision 1: a ball meets a step

*A uniform solid sphere (radius R = 0.10 m) rolls at v = 1.0 m s⁻¹ and strikes a step of height h = 0.020 m. Assume, to be checked afterwards, that it then pivots about the edge E without slipping or bouncing.*

The impact impulse acts at E, so take angular momentum about **E**. Before (CM at height R, velocity v, lever arm R − h): L = I_cm(v/R) + Mv(R − h). After: pivoting about E, L = (I_cm + MR²)ω′. With I_cm = βMR² (β = 2/5):

$$ ω′ = (v/R)[1 − h/((1 + β)R)] = (v/R)(1 − 5h/7R) = 8.571 rad/s $$

so the CM speed after is ω′R = 0.857 m s⁻¹. Kinetic energy per kg: before 0.7v² = 0.700 J, after ½(1.4R²)ω′² = 0.514 J, ratio (1 − 5h/7R)² = 0.735.

**Check the assumptions (do not skip this).** After the impact the CM moves on a circle of radius R about E, which needs a centripetal acceleration v′²/R = 7.35 m s⁻² toward E. The edge can only push, and gravity supplies g(R − h)/R = 7.85 m s⁻² along that line, so contact is kept only if v′² ≤ g(R − h), that is v′ ≤ 0.886 m s⁻¹, which means an incoming v ≤ 1.03 m s⁻¹. Here v′ = 0.857 m s⁻¹ is below the limit, so the pivoting assumption holds at the start. (With v = 2.0 m s⁻¹ the same algebra gives v′ = 1.714 m s⁻¹ and v′²/R = 29.4 m s⁻², far above 7.85: the sphere would leave the edge at once and the pivot model would not apply.) No-slip at E also needs enough friction there; that is assumed, not computed. To mount the step the CM must rise h = 0.020 m, which needs gh = 0.196 J per kg, less than the 0.514 J per kg left, so the sphere climbs (the contact condition is checked at the start of the pivot only).

## Rolling collision 2: sliding turns into rolling

*A solid sphere is launched along a rough floor with v₀ = 4.0 m s⁻¹ and no spin; μ = 0.30.* Friction acts along the ground line, so its torque about **any point on that line** is zero: L about the ground line is conserved. Initially L = Mv₀R; finally (rolling, v = ωR) L = (1 + β)MRv:

$$ v = v₀/(1 + β) = (5/7)v₀ = 2.857 m/s $$

(β = 1/2 gives 2v₀/3, β = 1 gives v₀/2.) The time: friction decelerates the CM at μg, so t = (v₀ − v)/μg = 2v₀/(7μg) = 0.388 s, over a distance (v₀ + v)t/2 = 1.33 m. The fraction of kinetic energy lost is β/(1 + β) = 2/7 = 28.6% (2.29 J per kg here). **Linear momentum and L_cm are not conserved**: friction supplies an external force and a torque about the CM.

### 9. Master: choosing the theorem, energy accounting and dimensions

*Objective:* Use a flowchart to pick the conservation law, account for lost energy and check units.

## The decision flowchart

Before writing equations, answer these questions in order.

1. **What is the system and the time interval?** (A short impact, or a long motion?)
2. **Impact?** Use the short-time approximation (bridge B2): ignore finite forces. Find where unknown impulses act (hinge, support, step edge, contact line). Take angular momentum about a point on their line of action. With no support (a free rod or free particles), use P and L about the CM or any fixed point. Use energy only if the collision is stated to be elastic.
3. **Ongoing motion?** Do any non-conservative forces do work (kinetic friction, drag, a motor)? If not, mechanical energy is conserved; if yes, include the work.
4. **Torque and force:** is the external torque about the chosen point zero (L conserved)? Is a component of the external force zero (that component of P conserved)?

## Energy accounting in inelastic rotational collisions

Where does the lost kinetic energy go? For a perfectly inelastic collision of two bodies with a common final motion, the loss is the kinetic energy *of the relative motion*: ½μ_red v_rel² for linear motion (PH-009 module 4) and ½[I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂)](Δω)² for two discs (module 8). Always compute T before and after with König's split (module 4); never assume the loss is zero because 'nothing is dissipated'. If the problem says *elastic*, add the energy equation, which together with P and L determines the final state.

## Units and dimensions

| Quantity | SI unit | Dimensions | Quick check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear momentum P | kg m s⁻¹ = N s | MLT⁻¹ | impulse has the same unit |
| Angular momentum L | kg m² s⁻¹ = N m s = J s | ML²T⁻¹ | L = Iω = r × p |
| Linear impulse Π | N s | MLT⁻¹ | = ΔP |
| Angular impulse ∫τ dt | N m s | ML²T⁻¹ | = ΔL |
| Moment of inertia I | kg m² | ML² | I ω has units of L |
| Energy T | J = N m | ML²T⁻² | ½Iω², ½MV² |

Dimensional check of the disc result: I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂) has units kg m²; times (rad/s)² gives kg m² s⁻² = J. ✓ A common slip is to mix angular momentum (J s) with energy (J): their ratio has units of s⁻¹ (an angular speed).

### 10. Exam: the two verified questions, presentation and guided exercises

*Objective:* Write answers to the 2018 and 2024 questions and rehearse choosing the law.

## 2018 Q1(b), 10 marks: angular momentum of a sliding block

*A block of negligible size slides freely in the x-direction with velocity v î as shown in the diagram. Find its angular momentum about the origin O and about the point A on the y-axis.*

The diagram supplies the coordinates; this analysis uses symbols (read the figure for the numerical positions) and is not an official solution. Let the block move along the line y = y₀ (distance y₀ from the x-axis), and let A = (0, y_A). With p = mv î:

$$ L_O = r × p = (x î + y₀ ĵ) × mv î = −m v y₀ k̂,    L_A = −m v (y₀ − y_A) k̂ $$

because ĵ × î = −k̂ and î × î = 0. Both are **constant** (the block is free: x drops out), perpendicular to the plane, and clockwise for y₀ > 0. The relation L_A = L_O − r_A × p (here r_A × p = −mv y_A k̂) gives L_A = L_O + mv y_A k̂. Example m = 2 kg, v = 3 m s⁻¹, y₀ = 4 m, y_A = 1 m: L_O = −24 k̂ and L_A = −18 k̂ kg m² s⁻¹. If the block moves along the x-axis itself, L_O = 0. Sign caveat: L_A = −m v (y₀ − y_A) k̂ is clockwise only if y₀ > y_A; it is anticlockwise if A lies above the line of motion (y_A > y₀) and zero if A is on that line. Analysis only; PH-004 modules 2 and 3 give the same method.

## 2024 Q1(b), 10 marks: König's kinetic-energy theorem

*Show that T = ½MV_cm² + ½Σmᵢv′ᵢ².* The written answer is module 4's Derivation 1: define V and v′ᵢ = vᵢ − V; prove Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0; expand ½Σmᵢ(V + v′ᵢ)²; the cross term vanishes; interpret; give a two-particle check (T′ = ½μv²). Practice target: about 12 minutes (practice target, not an official allotment). The marks follow each stage: the key identity, the expansion, the vanishing term, the interpretation.

## How to present a conservation-theorem answer

1. Draw a diagram and name the system.
2. State the theorem with its condition and justify the condition for this system.
3. Choose the point for angular momentum and say why.
4. Write each equation with signs and a stated sense of rotation.
5. Solve, check (dimensions, a limiting case, a second method) and give units.
6. Say what is not conserved (energy in an inelastic collision, momentum with a hinge).

## Three guided thinking exercises

**(i) A rod hanging from a frictionless pivot is hit by a clay ball.** Thinking: the pivot supplies an unknown impulse, so L about the pivot; clay sticks, so energy is lost; afterwards the pivoting rod swings and energy is conserved: *two stages, two different laws* (PH-004 module 8).

**(ii) A disc rolling at speed V meets a rough wall corner.** Thinking: the corner supplies an impulse; take L about the corner; check whether the motion afterwards is rolling.

**(iii) Two people jump onto a merry-go-round.** Thinking: axle torque is zero, so L about the axis is conserved; the ground does not take part; momentum is not conserved because the axle exerts a force. Choose the law by naming what is absent.

### Notes: Definitions and symbols

- System: the bodies you follow; internal forces act between members, external forces come from outside.
- Linear momentum P = Σmᵢvᵢ = MV_cm; angular momentum L_O = Σrᵢ × mᵢvᵢ about a fixed point O; kinetic energy T = Σ½mᵢvᵢ².
- Impulse Π = ∫F dt = ΔP; angular impulse ∫τ dt = ΔL; impulsive force: large force over a very short time.
- r′ᵢ and v′ᵢ are position and velocity relative to the CM; Σmᵢr′ᵢ = 0 and Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0.
- Centre of percussion: the point of a pivoted body at which a blow produces no impulse at the pivot, ℓ_p = I_O/(Mh).
- Radius of gyration k: I_cm = Mk²; β = I_cm/(MR²) for round bodies (disc ½, solid sphere ⅖, ring 1, thin shell ⅔).

### Notes: Statement table: three theorems for a system and for a rigid body

- Conditions are for an inertial frame; angular momentum about a fixed point or the CM.

### Notes: Equations to memorise

- König: T = ½MV² + ½Σmᵢv′ᵢ²; L_O = R × P + L_cm.
- Rolling: T = ½MV²(1 + k²/R²); a = g sinα/(1 + k²/R²) on a slope; solid cylinder (2/3)g sinα.
- Moving point: dL_A/dt = τ_A − V_A × P.
- Centre of percussion: ℓ_p = (k² + h²)/h = I_O/(Mh); uniform rod pivoted at an end: 2ℓ/3; h h′ = k².
- Free body struck at distance d from the CM: V = Π/M, ω = Πd/I_cm, point at rest at k²/d on the other side.
- Two-body inelastic loss: ΔT = −½μ_red v_rel²; two discs: ΔT = −½[I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂)](Δω)².
- Sliding to rolling (solid, β = I_cm/MR²): v = v₀/(1 + β), fraction of T lost β/(1 + β).
- Step collision: ω′ = (v/R)[1 − h/((1 + β)R)].

### Notes: König proofs in four lines each

- KE: Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0; expand ½Σmᵢ(V + v′ᵢ)²; the cross term V·Σmᵢv′ᵢ is zero; T = ½MV² + T′.
- Angular momentum: expand Σ(R + r′ᵢ) × mᵢ(V + v′ᵢ) into four sums; two vanish; L = R × P + L_cm.
- dL_cm/dt: internal torques cancel (pairs along the line joining); dL_O/dt = R × F_ext + dL_cm/dt; equate with τ_O = R × F_ext + τ_cm.
- Centre of percussion: Πℓ_p = I_Oω; Π + H = Mhω; H = Π(Mhℓ_p/I_O − 1); H = 0 gives ℓ_p = I_O/(Mh).

### Notes: Standard counter-examples

- A particle sticking to a hinged rod: P not conserved, L about the hinge conserved, E lost.
- A ball sliding then rolling: P and L_cm change, L about the ground line is conserved.
- Rolling down a slope: E conserved (static friction does no work), P and L change.
- A skater pulling in the arms or an explosion: E increases because internal forces do work.
- A pendulum bob: tension does no work so E is conserved, but the weight's torque means L about the pivot changes.
- A moving constraint (a lift floor, a sliding wedge) can do work even though a fixed one cannot.

### Notes: Worked results to remember

- Rod and sticking particle (M = 1, ℓ = 1, m = 0.2, u = 10, d = 0.4): V = 1.667 m s⁻¹, ω = 6.06 rad s⁻¹, 63.1% of T lost.
- Door and bullet: ω = 1.181 rad s⁻¹; hinge impulse +1.99 N s; zero if struck at 2w/3.
- Two discs (0.090 and 0.045 kg m²; 40 and 10 rad s⁻¹): ω = 30 rad s⁻¹, loss 13.5 J (opposite sense 37.5 J).
- Sphere and step (R = 0.10 m, h = 0.02 m, v = 1.0 m s⁻¹): ω′ = 8.571 rad s⁻¹, v′ = 0.857 m s⁻¹, T ratio 0.735; stays on the edge only if v′² ≤ g(R − h) (v ≤ 1.03 m s⁻¹).
- Sphere sliding to rolling: v = (5/7)v₀, 2/7 of T lost.
- Cylinder on a 30° slope: a = 3.27 m s⁻².

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Law versus theorem: dP/dt = F_ext is the theorem; P = constant is the law when F_ext = 0.
- Angular momentum depends on the chosen point; torque too. 'Conserved' needs a point named.
- Internal forces cancel in P and in L (strong Newton III) but may do work and so change T.
- Perfectly inelastic collision: bodies move together after, but kinetic energy is lost (not zero afterwards).
- 'Isolated system' means no external force or torque; it does not mean energy is conserved in an inelastic collision.
- Centre of percussion versus centre of mass: ℓ_p lies beyond the CM from the pivot.

### Notes: Units and dimensions

- P: kg m s⁻¹ = N s [MLT⁻¹]; L: kg m² s⁻¹ = N m s = J s [ML²T⁻¹]; energy: J [ML²T⁻²]; I: kg m².
- Angular impulse has the units of L; linear impulse the units of P.
- Ratio L/T has the units of s⁻¹ (an angular speed), a useful check.
- A relation between P and T needs a mass factor: T = P²/2M for a single body.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** State the theorem for linear momentum of a system of particles and what it needs.
    - **a:** dP/dt = F_ext; internal forces cancel in pairs (Newton's third law); P is constant if F_ext = 0.
  - - **q:** State König's two theorems.
    - **a:** T = ½MV² + T′ and L_O = R × P + L_cm.
  - - **q:** Why is angular momentum about a hinge conserved in an impact although the hinge force is large?
    - **a:** The hinge force passes through the hinge: zero moment about it.
  - - **q:** Give the centre of percussion of a uniform rod pivoted at one end.
    - **a:** 2ℓ/3 from the pivot.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is energy lost in a perfectly inelastic collision although P and L are conserved?
    - **a:** The conservation of P and L fixes the final common motion of the CM and spin; the remaining kinetic energy of relative motion is converted to heat and deformation.
  - - **q:** Why does static friction do no work in pure rolling?
    - **a:** Its point of application, the contact point, is instantaneously at rest.
  - - **q:** Why is L about the CM not conserved for a sliding ball but L about the ground line is?
    - **a:** Friction exerts a torque about the CM; about a point on the ground line its torque is zero.
  - - **q:** Why is the torque law dL_cm/dt = τ_cm valid for an accelerating CM?
    - **a:** The fictitious force −mᵢA has zero net torque about the CM because Σmᵢr′ᵢ = 0.

  - - **q:** A particle of mass 0.1 kg moving at 20 m s⁻¹ perpendicular to a free rod (mass 0.9 kg, length 1.0 m) on a smooth table strikes it at its midpoint and sticks. What is ω? Then repeat for a strike at the end of the rod (d = 0.50 m from the centre).

### Memory skeleton

- Three theorems: dP/dt = F_ext; dL/dt = τ_ext (fixed point or CM); work–energy
- Each has its own conservation condition; name the point for L
- König: T = ½MV² + T′; L_O = R × P + L_cm
- dL_cm/dt = τ_cm,ext even when the CM accelerates; moving point needs −V_A × P
- Impulse for rigid bodies; centre of percussion ℓ_p = I_O/(Mh)
- Multi-theorem problems: hinge conserves L about the hinge, not P; inelastic loses T

### Last-week summary

- P conserved if F_ext = 0 (or a component, or short impact); L about a named point if τ_ext about it = 0; E if no non-conservative work.
- König: cross terms vanish because Σmᵢr′ᵢ = 0 and Σmᵢv′ᵢ = 0: T = ½MV² + T′, L_O = R × P + L_cm.
- dL_cm/dt = τ_cm,ext always; about a moving A: dL_A/dt = τ_A − V_A × P.
- Centre of percussion ℓ_p = I_O/(Mh) = (k² + h²)/h; rod pivoted at end 2ℓ/3.
- Hinge impact: P not conserved, L about hinge conserved, E lost; rolling: static friction does no work.
