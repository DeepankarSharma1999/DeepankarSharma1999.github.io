### Bridge: Small-number expansions and the two meanings of Delta

The graded-index results rely on expanding expressions in a small number. The **binomial series** says (1 + x)^p is about 1 + p x + p(p - 1) x²/2 when |x| is small. For p = −1/2 and x = −2y: (1 − 2y)^(−1/2) = 1 + y + (3/2) y² + (5/2) y³ + … . Check at y = 0.01: exact 1.010153, series 1 + 0.01 + 0.00015 + 0.0000025 = 1.0101525.

**Two small numbers.** Fibre texts use Delta = (n1 − n2)/n1 (fractional index step). The n² profile law n²(r) = n1² [1 − 2 Δ′ (r/a)^α] uses Δ′ = (n1² − n2²)/(2 n1²) so that the index reaches exactly n2 at the core edge. They are related by Δ′ = Δ (1 − Δ/2). For n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43: Δ = 0.04667 and Δ′ = 0.04558. Day 18 lesson PH-065 uses the symbol Δ for what is called Δ′ here. Also q = n2/n1 = 1 − Δ.

**Why it matters.** Spreads are proportional to Δ (step index) or Δ² (parabolic). A 2 per cent change in Δ is a 2 or a 4 per cent change in the result, so pick one definition and say which.

### 1. Orientation: the comparison question, the vocabulary and what this lesson adds

*Objective:* State in plain words what "pulse dispersion in step index and parabolic index fibres" compares, define the words and symbols used, and say which parts of the topic are already taught elsewhere.

## The question
The official syllabus clause (Mains Physics Paper I, modern optics) names "pulse dispersion in step index and parabolic index fibres". Take two multimode fibres with the same central core index n1 and the same cladding index n2. In one the core index is flat (a **step-index** fibre). In the other it falls smoothly from n1 on the axis to n2 at the core edge (a **graded-index** fibre; the **parabolic** profile is the favourite). Send the same very short pulse down both. How much wider is the pulse at the far end, and therefore how many bits per second can each carry?

This lesson answers with formulas you can derive and numbers you can check. Reading it is not mastery: the practice set and the delayed-retention items test that.

## Words and symbols
- **Pulse spread δt**: earliest-to-latest arrival time of the light of one pulse, over a fibre of length L, counting only the different ray paths (intermodal spread). Material dispersion is added in module 6.
- **Bit rate B**: bits per second; the **bandwidth-distance product** is B × L (Mb/s km), the quantity the extract uses (Brij Lal, PDF p. 700).
- **Profile exponent α** and the n² profile n²(r) = n1² [1 − 2Δ′ (r/a)^α], with α = 2 parabolic, α → ∞ step (Day 18 lesson PH-065, section "Core: graded-index profile and ray paths, the ray equation and the sinusoidal path").
- **Two small numbers** (do not mix them): Δ = (n1 − n2)/n1 is the Day 17 definition; **Δ′ = (n1² − n2²)/(2 n1²)** is what appears inside the n² profile, and Δ′ = Δ(1 − Δ/2). Day 18 lesson PH-065 writes Δ for what is called Δ′ here. Also q = n2/n1, so Δ = 1 − q.

### 2. Foundation: the step-index spread turned into a bit-rate limit (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Recall the step-index delay in three lines, then derive the bit-rate limit B = 1/(2δt), the product B·L, the longest link for a given bit rate, and the scaling assumption behind them.

## Recap (taught in Day 17 lesson PH-064)
In a step-index fibre of length L a ray at angle θ to the axis takes t(θ) = L n1/(c cos θ). The axial ray (θ = 0) is first: t_min = L n1/c. The steepest guided ray has cos θ_c = n2/n1 and is last: t_max = L n1²/(c n2). The full spread is

$$ δt = L n1 (n1 − n2) / (c n2) = (n1 L / c)(1 − q)/q,   q = n2/n1 $$

The full derivation, the NA link and the check n2 → n1 give δt → 0 are in PH-064, section "Core: intermodal (pulse) dispersion in a step-index fibre, bit-rate limit and graded-index cure (derivation 2)". Nothing is re-derived here.

## Derivation 1: from spread to bit rate
**Assumptions.** Meridional rays only; all guided angles equally important (worst case); the input pulse is short compared with δt; material dispersion is ignored (module 6); no mode coupling, so the spread grows in proportion to L.

1. A very short input pulse arrives as a smear of duration about δt: the earliest light at t_min, the latest at t_max.
2. Bits are sent at spacing T = 1/B. The output smear for one bit must not run into the smear of the next bit. If the smear occupies a slot of width δt, the receiver also needs a gap of about δt so that a 1 followed by a 0 can be told apart.
3. So T ≥ 2 δt. This is a **convention**, not a law; the extract's first approximation is B ≈ 1/τ with τ the pulse duration (PDF p. 700), which is the same idea with a different safety factor. This lesson uses

$$ B_max = 1 / (2 δt) $$

4. Put in δt ∝ L: B_max = c n2 / (2 n1 (n1 − n2) L). The product B_max × L is therefore a property of the fibre only:

$$ B·L = c n2 / (2 n1 (n1 − n2)) $$

5. For a required bit rate B the longest link is L_max = (B·L)/B.

*Units:* the ratio n2/(n1(n1 − n2)) is dimensionless, so B·L has the units of c, metres per second, read as (bits per second) × metres. In practice quote Mb/s km: B·L = 2.04 Mb/s km means 2.04 Mb/s over 1 km, 1.02 Mb/s over 2 km, 20.4 Mb/s over 100 m.

*Dimension check:* δt is a time, B = 1/time. *Limit:* n2 → n1 gives B → ∞ (no spread, bit rate set by something else).

## Numbers (recomputed)
- Fibre A, n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43: δt = 244.8 ns/km, B·L = 1/(2 × 244.8 ns) = 2.04 Mb/s km. For 100 Mb/s: L_max = 20.4 m.
- Fibre B, n1 = 1.48, Δ = 0.01 (n2 = 1.4652): δt = 49.8 ns/km, B·L = 10.0 Mb/s km; for 100 Mb/s L_max = 100 m.
- Day 17's extract example (n1 = 1.55, n2 = 1.51): 136.9 ns/km (the book prints 138), B·L = 3.65 Mb/s km.

## The scaling assumption
Treating δt as proportional to L is what the extract does when it multiplies per-km dispersion by the length (Example 24.11, PDF p. 713). Real multimode fibres mix modes along the way, so the spread grows more slowly than L over long lengths (standard; verify). Always write the assumption in an answer.

## Why the result is so poor
A step-index fibre with NA about 0.45 managing only a few Mb/s over a kilometre is why such fibres serve short data links (the extract, PDF p. 701). The cure, graded index, is the next module.

### 3. Core: the parabolic-index delay by the ray integral (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive the exact transit time of a meridional ray in the n² parabolic profile, T/L = (n1/c)(1 − y)/√(1 − 2y), and from it the full spread δt = L (n1 − n2)²/(2 c n2).

## Setting and assumptions
A straight graded-index fibre, core radius a, index law **n²(r) = n1² [1 − 2Δ′ ρ²]** with ρ = r/a (α = 2), n = n2 at ρ = 1. We use **meridional rays** (rays that cross the axis) in ray optics, no mode coupling, no material dispersion. Coordinates: z along the axis, r the distance from the axis, θ(r) the ray angle with the axis. Ray paths from Day 18 lesson PH-065 (section "Core: graded-index profile and ray paths, the ray equation and the sinusoidal path"): the invariant β = n(r) cos θ(r) is constant along a ray, and r obeys r'' = −K r with K = 2Δ′ n1²/(β² a²), so r = r0 sin(√K z) with turning radius r0.

## Derivation 2
1. **Time element.** A step ds along the ray takes dt = n ds/c, and ds = dz/cos θ. So dt/dz = n/(c cos θ). Multiply top and bottom by n and use cos θ = β/n:

$$ dt/dz = n² / (c β) $$

2. **Average over a period.** β is fixed, only n² changes along the ray. The transit time per unit length is T/L = ⟨n²⟩ / (c β), where ⟨ ⟩ is the average over z. (Whole periods; over many periods the end effect is negligible.)
3. **Average of n².** n² = n1² [1 − 2Δ′ r²/a²], and for r = r0 sin(√K z) the average of sin² over a period is 1/2, so ⟨r²⟩ = r0²/2. Define **y = Δ′ ρ0²** (ρ0 = r0/a, so y runs from 0 for the axial ray to Δ′ for the marginal ray). Then

$$ ⟨n²⟩ = n1² (1 − y) $$

4. **Value of β.** At the turning point the ray is parallel to the axis (cos θ = 1), so β = n(r0): β² = n1² (1 − 2y).
5. **Result.**

$$ T/L = (n1/c) (1 − y) / √(1 − 2y) $$

*Dimension check:* n1/c is time per length. *Limit:* y = 0 gives n1/c, the axial ray moving straight in the highest index.

6. **Why the first order cancels.** For small y: (1 − y)(1 + y + (3/2) y² + …) = 1 + y²/2 + y³ + … . The path lengthening (which increases the time by about y) and the lower average index (which decreases it by about y) cancel. The leading excess is **y²/2**.
7. **Which ray is slowest?** The derivative of (1 − y)/√(1 − 2y) is y/(1 − 2y)^{3/2} ≥ 0, so T grows steadily with y: the axial ray is the fastest and the marginal ray (y = Δ′, turning at the core edge, β = n2) is the slowest.
8. **Marginal ray.** At y = Δ′ = (1 − q²)/2 we have 1 − y = (1 + q²)/2 and √(1 − 2y) = q, so T/L = (n1/c)(1 + q²)/(2q).
9. **Full spread.**

$$ δt = (n1 L / c) [ (1 + q²)/(2q) − 1 ] = (n1 L/c)(1 − q)²/(2q) = L (n1 − n2)² / (2 c n2) $$

10. **Small-Δ form.** With Δ = 1 − q: δt = n1 L Δ² / (2c (1 − Δ)), about **n1 L Δ²/(2c)** for small Δ.

## Interpretation
Compare the step-index result L n1 (n1 − n2)/(c n2). The ratio of the two spreads is exactly

$$ δt_step / δt_parabolic = 2 n1/(n1 − n2) = 2/Δ $$

so a fibre with Δ = 0.01 improves by exactly 200 times, and one with Δ = 0.0467 by 42.9 times.

## Numbers (recomputed)
Fibre A: n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43: δt = 0.07² × 1000/(2 × 3.0e8 × 1.43) = 5.711e-9 s = **5.71 ns/km**. The small-Δ form n1 L Δ²/(2c) gives 5.44 ns (4.7 per cent low).

### 4. Core: checking the ray integral by computer and reconciling the quoted forms

*Objective:* Verify the closed form against a direct numerical ray integral, and say which quoted form (n1Δ²L/(2c), n1Δ²L/(8c), 5.4, 6.0 and so on) is which, with its assumptions.

## The check
Derivation 2 (module 3) used a trick: the average of n² along a sinusoidal ray. A computer can avoid the trick. For a ray with invariant β and turning radius r0 the exact time per unit length is the ratio of two integrals over one quarter period (Day 18 lesson PH-065, graded-index ray section, gives the path):

$$ T/L = (1/c) ∫ n² dr/√(n² − β²) ÷ ∫ β dr/√(n² − β²),   r from 0 to r0 $$

Both integrals have a square-root infinity at r0. The substitution r = r0 (1 − u²) removes it. Numerical quadrature (scipy) for fibre A gave, as c T/(n1 L):

| turning point ρ0² | numerical | closed form (1 − y)/√(1 − 2y) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 1.000042317 | 1.000042317 |
| 0.5 | 1.000272030 | 1.000272030 |
| 1.0 | 1.001142191 | 1.001142191 |

They agree to the digits shown. The same code with α = 1.9 and α = 3 also matches the general closed form of module 7. So the closed form is not an approximation inside ray optics for this index law.

## Which number is which
The word "parabolic spread" hides three choices.

1. **Which small number sits in the index law.** The n² law n² = n1²(1 − 2Δ′ρ²) with Δ′ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²) reaches exactly n2 at the core edge, which is the physical fibre. Putting Δ = (n1 − n2)/n1 in its place makes the edge index 1.5 √(1 − 2 × 0.04667) = 1.4283 instead of 1.43. Day 17 lesson PH-064 quoted a numerical 6.0 ns/km for the parabolic fibre; that figure belongs to this second law (recomputed: 6.00 ns). With the correct edge index the answer is 5.71 ns.
2. **How many orders of the small number are kept.** Exact: δt = L (n1 − n2)²/(2 c n2) = n1LΔ²/(2c(1 − Δ)). Leading order: n1LΔ²/(2c) (5.44 ns), or with Δ′: n1LΔ′²/(2c) (5.19 ns). Keeping the next term of module 3, n1L (Δ′²/2 + Δ′³)/c gives 5.67 ns. All are "right" as Δ → 0; the exact one is right here.
3. **Full spread or rms width.** Every number in this lesson is the **full spread**: slowest minus fastest meridional ray. Some books quote an rms width for a stated mode population, with different numerical factors; compare only like with like.

## The optimum profile
The profile exponent can be tuned (modules 7 and 8). For fibre A the exact optimum spread is 1.427 ns/km (best α = 1.9067). The form n1LΔ²/(8c) gives 1.361 ns; the exact result is n1LΔ²/(8c) times a factor close to 1/(1 − Δ), and (n1 − n2)²L/(8 c n2) = 1.428 ns agrees to 0.05 per cent. PH-064's "about 1.5 ns" comes from the same Δ-inside-n² choice as its 6.0 ns (recomputed 1.50 ns).

## What the extract says
Brij Lal states the graded-index mechanism in words only (PDF p. 688 and p. 700): rays at larger angles travel longer paths in lower-index regions, so all arrive "almost" together. No Δ² formula is printed in the extract's text layer. The Δ² and Δ²/8 forms in this lesson are standard results that the lesson derives and checks by computer; mark them "verify" against your class notes. The extract's statement on PDF p. 701 that intermodal dispersion in a graded-index fibre is "zero" is a loose first-order statement: it is only very small (modules 5 and 8).

## Trap
Do not mix a number from one convention with a formula from another. When an exam gives n1 and n2, use the exact forms L n1 (n1 − n2)/(c n2) and L (n1 − n2)²/(2 c n2) and say so.

### 5. Core: step-index versus parabolic-index, the numerical comparison and the bit-rate rule

*Objective:* Compute spread, B·L and the longest 100 Mb/s link for step, parabolic and optimum profiles for given n1 and n2, state the scaling with Δ, and explain why the ideal figures are upper bounds.

## What is compared
Two sample fibres, both with n1 and n2 chosen to match textbook ranges (the extract lists NA about 0.3 and diameters of 50 to 100 micrometres for multimode step-index fibre, PDF p. 701; the indices below are the Day 17 sample and a typical Δ = 1 per cent fibre).

- **Fibre A**: n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43, Δ = 0.04667 (NA 0.453).
- **Fibre B**: n1 = 1.48, Δ = 0.01, so n2 = 1.4652 (NA 0.209).

Formulas, all per length L, exact in ray optics for meridional rays: step L n1 (n1 − n2)/(c n2); parabolic L (n1 − n2)²/(2 c n2); optimum profile (module 8) (n1 L/c) Δ²/[(1 + s)²(1 + s²)] with s = √(1 − Δ), about L (n1 − n2)²/(8 c n2).

## Results (recomputed, per km)
| Fibre, profile | δt (ns/km) | B·L = 1/(2δt) per km | L_max at 100 Mb/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, step | 244.8 | 2.04 Mb/s km | 20.4 m |
| A, parabolic (α = 2) | 5.71 | 87.6 Mb/s km | 0.876 km |
| A, optimum (α = 1.9067) | 1.427 | 350 Mb/s km | 3.50 km |
| B, step | 49.83 | 10.0 Mb/s km | 100 m |
| B, parabolic (α = 2) | 0.2492 | 2.007 Gb/s km | 20.1 km |
| B, optimum (α = 1.98) | 0.0623 | 8.03 Gb/s km | 80.3 km |

## Patterns to remember
1. **Scaling with Δ.** The step-index spread is proportional to Δ (to first order); the parabolic spread is proportional to Δ². Halve Δ and the step spread halves while the parabolic spread falls to a quarter.
2. **Ratios.** Step to parabolic is exactly 2/Δ (43 for A, 200 for B). Step to optimum is about 8/Δ (171 for A, 800 for B).
3. **Why a smaller Δ helps both, at a price.** NA ≈ n1√(2Δ), so a small Δ also means a small acceptance cone and poorer light collection, and in a graded fibre the local acceptance angle falls further from the axis (Day 18 lesson PH-065, section "Core: local numerical aperture in graded-index fibre and the three fibre types compared"). The extract's table agrees in direction: graded index has a smaller NA than multimode step index and a better bandwidth (PDF p. 701).
4. **Sharp optimum.** For fibre B the spread is 0.0623 ns/km at α = 1.98 but 0.249 at α = 2.00, 0.496 at 2.02, 1.46 at 2.10 and 1.02 at 1.90. Errors of 0.08 to 0.12 in α cost a factor of 16 to 23. The optimum is sharp, so real fibres fall short of the ideal figures.

## Why the table is an upper bound
- The numbers assume linear scaling (module 2). Mode mixing in real fibres makes the spread grow more slowly than L over long lengths (outside the extract; verify).
- Material dispersion (module 6) is absent from the table. For fibre B it dominates the parabolic result.
- Profile imperfections lower the real bandwidth, and B = 1/(2δt) is a convention. Catalogue bandwidths of graded-index multimode fibre are typically far below the ideal 2 to 8 Gb/s km here (verify; not in the extract).

## The rule in one line
Compute δt per km from the exact formula for the profile, take B·L = 1/(2 δt/km), divide by the required bit rate for the longest length, and state "linear scaling, ray optics, no material dispersion".

## Trap
The extract (PDF p. 701) lists "intermodal dispersion is zero" for graded-index fibre. Saying so in an answer loses marks; the correct statement is "greatly reduced; residual of order n1LΔ²/(2c)".

### 6. Core: when material dispersion takes over

*Objective:* Combine the modal and material spreads by the root-sum-of-squares rule, find which one dominates for a given fibre and source, and state the design consequences for the bit rate.

## Setting
So far the pulse spread came only from different ray paths. A real source is not a single wavelength: an LED may span tens of nanometres, a laser diode a few. Each wavelength travels at its own group speed in glass. The spread over length L is |D| L δλ, derived in Day 17 lesson PH-064, section "Core: material dispersion, the D parameter and the spread formula (derivation 3)". Nothing is re-derived here. We need only these values (Malitson fit for silica; standard, not in the extract; verify): D = −84.0 ps/(nm km) at 850 nm, +2.6 at 1300 nm, zero at 1.273 micrometres.

## The combination rule
The extract (PDF p. 700, Eq. 24.49) combines independent contributions by the root sum of squares. With modal spread m, material spread μ (waveguide dispersion is small in multimode fibre, PDF p. 699, and dropped here):

$$ δt_total = L √( (m/km)² + (μ/km)² ),   B_max = 1 / (2 δt_total) $$

Both terms are taken proportional to L. If one term is three times the other the total is within 5.4 per cent of the larger, so "the largest term wins".

## Which term wins? Fibre B (Δ = 0.01), recomputed per km
| Profile | modal | LED 850 nm, 38 nm: μ = 3.191 | laser 850 nm, 2 nm: μ = 0.168 | laser 1300 nm, 2 nm: μ = 0.005 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| step | 49.83 | 49.93 (10.0 Mb/s km) | 49.83 (10.0) | 49.83 (10.0) |
| parabolic | 0.2492 | 3.200 (156 Mb/s km) | 0.3005 (1.66 Gb/s km) | 0.2492 (2.01 Gb/s km) |
| optimum | 0.0623 | 3.191 (157 Mb/s km) | 0.1791 (2.79 Gb/s km) | 0.0625 (8.0 Gb/s km) |

Values are ns/km, with B·L in brackets. Read the table in three lines.
1. **Step index:** the modal term is 15 times the LED term. The source hardly matters.
2. **Graded index with an LED at 850 nm:** the material term is 13 times the modal term, so the 2 Gb/s km promised by the profile collapses to 156 Mb/s km. Improving the profile from parabolic to optimum gains nothing (156 to 157).
3. **Graded index with a laser near the zero of D:** the material term all but vanishes and the modal term sets the limit again.

## The crossover
Set the parabolic modal spread equal to the material spread. With small Δ: n1Δ²/(2c) = |D| δλ gives **Δ_cross ≈ √(2 c |D| δλ / n1)**. Solving the exact form n1Δ²/(2c(1 − Δ)) gives Δ_cross = 0.035 for the 850 nm LED and 0.0082 for the 850 nm laser. Below Δ_cross material dispersion dominates. A Δ = 1 per cent graded fibre is therefore material-limited with an LED, while fibre A (Δ = 0.0467) is still modal-limited: total √(5.711² + 3.191²) = 6.54 ns/km, B·L = 76.4 Mb/s km (143 Mb/s km with the optimum profile, total 3.50). For step index the crossover is Δ ≈ 0.0006, so modal dispersion always wins.

## Design consequences
- To cut the **modal** term: graded profile, small Δ (module 5), or a single mode (Day 18 lesson PH-065).
- To cut the **material** term: a narrower source (laser instead of LED) and a wavelength where |D| is small: 1300 nm in silica (Day 17 lesson PH-064). The extract says the same (PDF p. 699: narrower sources, longer wavelengths).
- Reducing only one term is wasted once the other dominates: the 1300 nm laser on a step-index fibre gains nothing; a graded profile with an LED gains nothing beyond parabolic.

## Trap
Adding the terms linearly gives 3.44 ns/km in the Check; the extract's rule is the root sum of squares (3.20). In an answer state which rule you use.

### 7. Master: the general profile exponent, the exact transit time for every α (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Derive T/L = (n1/c)(1 − 4y/(α + 2))/√(1 − 2y) for the power-law n² profile, check it against α = 2 and α → ∞, and read the spread for any α from it.

## Why generalise
Module 3 treated α = 2 because the ray path is a sine and ⟨r²⟩ = r0²/2 gives the answer in one line. For any other α the path is not sinusoidal (Day 18 lesson PH-065 shows the period then depends on the ray). We still get a closed form, because the integrals reduce to a ratio of Beta functions.

## Setting and assumptions
Index law n²(r) = n1² [1 − 2Δ′ ρ^α], ρ = r/a, 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 1, α ≥ 1. Meridional rays, β = n cos θ constant, turning radius ρ0, **y = Δ′ ρ0^α** (0 ≤ y ≤ Δ′), β² = n(r0)² = n1² (1 − 2y). Ray optics, no mode coupling, no material dispersion.

## Derivation 3
1. From dt/dz = n²/(cβ) (module 3), T/L = ⟨n²⟩/(cβ) with the average over z.
2. The ray equation gives dr/dz = tan θ = √(n² − β²)/β, so dz = β dr/√(n² − β²). Therefore ⟨n²⟩ = ∫ n² dr/√(n² − β²) ÷ ∫ dr/√(n² − β²), the integrals from 0 to r0 (quarter period).
3. Split n² = β² + (n² − β²). Then ⟨n²⟩ = β² + J1/J0, with J0 = ∫ dr/√(n² − β²) and J1 = ∫ √(n² − β²) dr.
4. For the power law, n² − β² = A (1 − s^α) with s = ρ/ρ0 and A = 2Δ′ n1² ρ0^α = 2 y n1². The factors ρ0 and √A come out of the integrals, giving J1/J0 = A R, where R = ∫0¹ (1 − s^α)^{1/2} ds ÷ ∫0¹ (1 − s^α)^{−1/2} ds.
5. Substitute t = s^α: ∫0¹ (1 − s^α)^p ds = (1/α) B(1/α, p + 1), where B is the Beta function. So R = B(1/α, 3/2)/B(1/α, 1/2). Writing B(x, p) = Γ(x)Γ(p)/Γ(x + p) and using Γ(z + 1) = z Γ(z):

$$ R = [Γ(3/2)/Γ(1/2)] × [Γ(1/α + 1/2)/Γ(1/α + 3/2)] = (1/2) / (1/α + 1/2) = α/(α + 2) $$

6. So ⟨n²⟩ = n1² [1 − 2y + 2yα/(α + 2)] = n1² [1 − 4y/(α + 2)], and

$$ T/L = (n1/c) (1 − 4y/(α + 2)) / √(1 − 2y) $$

*Checks.* α = 2: (1 − y)/√(1 − 2y), module 3. α → ∞: the step-index time (see Check). Dimension: n1/c. Computer check: scipy quadrature of the original two integrals gave cT/(n1L) = 1.00506561 (α = 3, ρ0 = 0.8) and 0.99968426 (α = 1.9, ρ0 = 0.8), identical to the formula to the eight digits shown.

7. **Spread.** The spread for a given α is (n1L/c) times (maximum minus minimum of f(y) = (1 − ky)/√(1 − 2y), k = 4/(α + 2)) over 0 ≤ y ≤ Δ′. Evaluate numerically for the table in the diagram: for fibre A it falls from 28.4 ns/km at α = 1.5 to a minimum near α = 1.91, then rises towards the step-index 244.8 ns/km (240.0 at α = 200).

## Interpretation
- For α = 1.9 the value 0.99968 is **below** 1: that ray arrives before the axial ray. Choosing α < 2 over-corrects the first-order term so that the rays that turn near ρ0 ≈ 0.70a (u ≈ 0.5) run ahead. The best choice balances this against the late edge rays (module 8).

### 8. Master: the optimum exponent, its residual spread, and why exact cancellation is impossible (derivation 4)

*Objective:* Derive α_opt = 2 − 2Δ and the exact residual spread (n1L/c)[1 − 2√(1 − Δ)/(2 − Δ)] ≈ n1LΔ²/(8c), and explain why a one-parameter power law cannot make all rays arrive together.

## Setting
Module 7 gave T/L = (n1/c) f(y), f(y) = (1 − ky)/√(1 − 2y), k = 4/(α + 2), 0 ≤ y ≤ Δ′; the axial ray has f = 1. The spread is (f_max − f_min) n1L/c. Choose α to minimise it.

## Derivation 4
1. **Expand.** (1 − ky)(1 + y + (3/2) y² + …) = 1 + (1 − k) y + (3/2 − k) y² + … . The linear coefficient is tunable: it vanishes at k = 1 (α = 2). The quadratic coefficient 3/2 − k then equals 1/2, and no value of k can make **both** vanish.
2. **Small correction.** Put α = 2 − ε: k ≈ 1 + ε/4 and f − 1 ≈ −(ε/4) y + y²/2, which dips below zero and then rises.
3. **Minimax condition.** The spread is (value at the larger end) minus (value at the dip). Making the two ends equal, f(0) = f(Δ′) = 1, is the best compromise. Exactly: f(Δ′) = (1 − kΔ′)/q = 1 gives k = (1 − q)/Δ′ = Δ/Δ′. Since Δ′ = Δ(2 − Δ)/2, this is k = 2/(2 − Δ), hence

$$ α_opt = 4/k − 2 = 2 − 2Δ $$

A numerical search for fibres A, B and the extract example agrees to eight digits.
4. **The dip.** f′(y) = 0 gives 1 − k + ky = 0, so y_m = (k − 1)/k, and then ky_m = k − 1 and 1 − 2y_m = (2 − k)/k. Hence f_min = (2 − k)/√((2 − k)/k) = √(k(2 − k)) = √(1 − (1 − k)²).
5. **Exact residual.** With 1 − k = −Δ/(2 − Δ):

$$ δt_opt = (n1 L/c) [ 1 − √(1 − (1 − k)²) ] = (n1 L/c) [ 1 − 2√(1 − Δ)/(2 − Δ) ] $$

6. **Simplify.** Let s = √(1 − Δ), so 2 − Δ = 1 + s², and 1 − s = Δ/(1 + s). Then 1 − 2s/(1 + s²) = (1 − s)²/(1 + s²) and

$$ δt_opt = (n1 L/c) Δ² / [ (1 + s)² (1 + s²) ] → n1 L Δ² / (8c)   (Δ → 0) $$

*Limit:* s → 1 gives 4 × 2 = 8.

## Numbers (recomputed)
Fibre A: α_opt = 1.9067, exact δt = 1.4271 ns/km (small-Δ form 1.361, 4.6 per cent low). Fibre B: α_opt = 1.98, 0.06229 ns/km. Extract example (Δ = 0.02581): α_opt = 1.9484, 0.4414 ns/km.

## Why exact cancellation is impossible
- In this family of profiles one number (α) is adjusted; the arrival time as a function of y has two terms that matter, with coefficients (1 − k) and (3/2 − k) differing by the fixed constant 1/2. The second term stays, so the arrival time cannot be constant. The best compromise puts all rays inside a window of width about n1LΔ²/(8c), with the rays turning near ρ0 ≈ 0.70a (u ≈ 0.5) early (diagram above).
- The extract does not discuss this. It says only that rays have "almost the same optical path length" (PDF p. 688) and lists graded-index intermodal dispersion as "zero" (PDF p. 701), an idealisation. The optimum α = 2 − 2Δ is derived here.
- **Other profiles.** A hyperbolic-secant law n = n1 sech(g r) gives every meridional ray the same ray-optics transit time n1L/c (quadrature check here: 1.000000000 for four rays; standard, not in the extract; verify). It is not a power law, ignores skew rays and wave optics, and is not what is manufactured.
- **The optimum moves with wavelength** because the index step changes slightly with wavelength (Olshansky and Keck; standard, not in the extract; verify).

## Trap
Writing α_opt = 2 without saying "to first order". The first-order optimum is 2; the minimax optimum is 2 − 2Δ.

### Notes: Definitions

- Pulse spread (intermodal) δt: the time between the arrival of the earliest and the latest meridional ray of one pulse over length L, in the ray picture, no mode coupling, no material dispersion.
- Step-index fibre: the core index is the constant n1 up to the cladding n2. Graded-index fibre: n falls from n1 on the axis to n2 at the core edge; the parabolic profile has exponent α = 2 in n²(r) = n1²[1 − 2Δ′ (r/a)^α].
- Δ = (n1 − n2)/n1 = 1 − q, with q = n2/n1; Δ′ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²) = Δ(1 − Δ/2). y = Δ′ ρ0^α labels a ray by its turning radius ρ0 = r0/a: y = 0 is the axial ray and y = Δ′ the marginal ray.
- Bit rate B (bits per second); bandwidth-distance product B·L (Mb/s km); this lesson's rule B_max = 1/(2 δt_total), with δt proportional to L (linear scaling).
- Optimum profile: the exponent α_opt = 2 − 2Δ that minimises the full spread within the power-law family.

### Notes: Equations to know

- Ray invariant β = n cos θ; dt/dz = n²/(cβ); T/L = ⟨n²⟩/(cβ).
- Step index: δt = L n1 (n1 − n2)/(c n2) = (n1 L/c)(1 − q)/q ≈ n1 L Δ/c.
- Parabolic (α = 2): T/L = (n1/c)(1 − y)/√(1 − 2y); δt = L (n1 − n2)²/(2 c n2) = n1 L Δ²/(2c(1 − Δ)) ≈ n1 L Δ²/(2c). Ratio step to parabolic = 2/Δ exactly.
- General α: T/L = (n1/c)(1 − 4y/(α + 2))/√(1 − 2y), y = Δ′ ρ0^α, 0 ≤ y ≤ Δ′.
- Optimum: α_opt = 2 − 2Δ; δt_opt = (n1 L/c) Δ²/[(1 + s)²(1 + s²)], s = √(1 − Δ); ≈ n1 L Δ²/(8c) ≈ L (n1 − n2)²/(8 c n2).
- Bit rate: B_max = 1/(2 δt); B·L = c n2/(2 n1 (n1 − n2)) for step index.
- Total with material dispersion: δt_total = L √[(modal/km)² + (|D| δλ)²]; crossover for parabolic fibre Δ_cross ≈ √(2 c |D| δλ/n1).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Explain in physical terms why a graded-index fibre has a smaller pulse spread than a step-index fibre of the same n1 and n2.
    - **a:** Rays at larger angles travel longer paths but spend more of the path in lower index where light is faster. The two effects cancel at first order, leaving only a second-order residual.
  - - **q:** Why can the exponent of a power-law profile not remove the spread entirely?
    - **a:** The arrival time is 1 + (1 - k) y + (3/2 - k) y^2 + ...; choosing k = 1 removes the linear term but leaves 1/2 in the quadratic term, and choosing another k leaves a linear term. One parameter cannot zero both.

    - **a:** When material dispersion dominates (the material spread exceeds the modal spread), which happens for small Delta with a wide source such as an LED at 850 nm. Then only a narrower source or a wavelength near the zero of D helps.

### Memory skeleton

- Ray integral: β = n cos θ gives dt/dz = n²/(cβ), so T/L = ⟨n²⟩/(cβ)
- Step index: δt = L n1(n1 − n2)/(c n2) ≈ n1LΔ/c, proportional to Δ
- Parabolic: ⟨n²⟩ = n1²(1 − y), β² = n1²(1 − 2y), y = Δ′ρ0²; the first-order term cancels
- Parabolic result: δt = L(n1 − n2)²/(2c n2) ≈ n1LΔ²/(2c); step : parabolic = 2/Δ exactly
- Optimum α = 2 − 2Δ: spread ≈ n1LΔ²/(8c); no power law removes it
- Material dispersion takes over once the modal spread is small; combine by root sum of squares

### Last-week summary

- Two deltas: Δ = (n1 − n2)/n1; Δ′ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²) = Δ(1 − Δ/2) inside n² = n1²[1 − 2Δ′(r/a)^α]; PH-065 writes Δ′ as Δ.
- Step δt = L n1(n1 − n2)/(c n2) ≈ n1LΔ/c; parabolic L(n1 − n2)²/(2c n2) ≈ n1LΔ²/(2c); ratio 2/Δ exactly.
- T/L = (n1/c)(1 − y)/√(1 − 2y) = (n1/c)(1 + y²/2 + …); fibre A (1.50, 1.43): 244.8 against 5.71 ns/km.
- α_opt = 2 − 2Δ gives ≈ n1LΔ²/(8c), a sharp optimum; never call the graded spread zero.
- Small modal spread: material dispersion (source width, wavelength) sets the limit; root sum of squares, never linear addition.
