## GS-095: Latitude, longitude, time zones and maps

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Bridge: angles, degrees, minutes and seconds

An **angle** measures how far you turn. A full circle is a turn of **360 degrees**, written 360°. Half a circle is 180°, a quarter circle (the corner of a page) is 90°.

For small amounts a degree is split into **60 minutes**, written 60', and a minute into **60 seconds**, written 60". This is like hours, minutes and seconds on a clock, but for angles. So 37°6' means 37 degrees and 6 minutes, which is 37 + 6/60 = 37.1 degrees. NCERT's India chapter prints the southern boundary as 6°45'N: that is 6.75 degrees.

To convert minutes to a decimal, divide by 60. To convert a decimal back, multiply the part after the point by 60: 0.25 degree = 15 minutes.

Why this matters: latitude and longitude are angles measured from the centre of the Earth, and the Earth turns through 360° in 24 hours. Every time problem in this lesson is angle arithmetic: 15° of turn equals 1 hour of time, so 1° equals 4 minutes and 15' (fifteen minutes of arc) equals 1 minute of time.

**Direction words.** The four cardinal directions are north (N), east (E), south (S), west (W). On a map, north is usually at the top, east at the right. A **bearing** is an angle measured clockwise from north, so east is 90°, south 180° and west 270°.

### Bridge: Bridge: ratios, fractions and unit conversion for map scale

A **ratio** compares two quantities. 1:50,000 means 1 part to 50,000 parts. When used as a map scale it means any length on the map, in any unit, stands for 50,000 times that length on the ground, in the same unit. Written as a fraction it is 1/50,000.

To get from map to ground you multiply; to go from ground to map you divide. The only skill left is converting units: 1 km = 1,000 m = 100,000 cm.

So at 1:50,000, 1 cm on the map is 50,000 cm on the ground. Divide by 100,000 to change cm to km: 50,000 / 100,000 = 0.5 km. A useful shortcut: remove five zeros from the scale number and the result is the km represented by 1 cm. At 1:200,000 that is 2 km per cm; at 1:25,000 it is 0.25 km per cm.

**Bigger fraction, bigger scale.** 1/25,000 is larger than 1/1,000,000, so 1:25,000 is the larger scale even though 25,000 is the smaller number. A large-scale map shows a small area in great detail; a small-scale map shows a large area in little detail.

**Squares.** If a length is multiplied by k, an area is multiplied by k squared. This matters when a map is reduced or enlarged: halving the lengths makes the area one quarter.

### 1. Orientation: the globe, its spin and why we need a grid

*Objective:* Describe the shape, rotation and tilt of the Earth in simple terms and state why a grid of imaginary lines is needed to locate places.

## What this topic is about

This lesson answers four everyday questions. Where exactly is a place on the Earth? What time is it there? How do we draw the curved Earth on flat paper? How do we read that paper? The tools are **latitude**, **longitude**, **time zones** and **maps**. UPSC asks about all four, and Prelims often asks a calculation that can be solved in under a minute if you hold a fixed method.

Day 17 is the first Geography day, so every term is defined before it is used.

## The Earth in five facts

1. **Shape.** The Earth is nearly a sphere, but slightly flattened at the two ends, like an orange pressed from top and bottom. Leong gives the equatorial circumference as 40,084 km and says the polar circumference is less by 133 km, so about 39,951 km; the equatorial diameter is 12,761 km and the polar diameter shorter by 42 km, so about 12,719 km (Leong, PDF p11). The shape is called a **geoid**, meaning earth-shaped. (Leong prints a different circumference, 40,232.5 km, a few lines later on PDF p19; the commonly used figure is about 40,075 km. These differ by under 0.5 per cent and do not change any method below.)
2. **Evidence it is round.** A ship's mast appears before its hull; the Earth's shadow on the Moon in a lunar eclipse is always circular; sunrise happens at different times in different places; Magellan's crew sailed round the world between 1519 and 1522 (Leong, PDF pp11-12).
3. **Rotation.** The Earth spins on its own **axis**, an imaginary line through the North and South Poles, from **west to east** once in about 24 hours. This causes day and night and makes the Sun appear to move east to west (Leong, PDF p13).
4. **Revolution.** The Earth also goes round the Sun once in about 365 and a quarter days, which is why a leap year adds one day every four years.
5. **Tilt.** The axis is tilted at 66½° to the plane of the Earth's orbit (the **ecliptic**), which is the same as 23½° from the vertical to that plane. The tilt, not the distance from the Sun, produces the seasons and the changing length of day (Leong, PDF p14).

## Why a grid

The Earth's surface is vast and featureless in places, such as open sea. To say exactly where something is, geographers draw imaginary lines on the globe: one set running east-west, another running north-south. Where two lines cross, a place is pinned down. Leong gives examples: Lagos is 6°N and 3°E; Sydney is 33°55'S and 151°12'E (PDF p18). The whole network is called the **graticule**.

### 2. Foundation: latitude, parallels and the special parallels

*Objective:* Define latitude, state the five special parallels with their values and explain the sun facts and heat zones attached to them.

## Definition

**Latitude** is the angular distance of a place north or south of the **Equator**, measured as an angle at the centre of the Earth (Leong, PDF p18). The Equator is the imaginary circle midway between the poles and is **0°**. The poles are **90°N** and **90°S**. Lines joining places of equal latitude are called **parallels of latitude**; they are circles that are parallel to the Equator and get smaller towards the poles. Between 0° and 90° a line is drawn at every 1° (Leong).

A latitude needs its letter. 30° alone could mean north or south, so write 30°N or 30°S.

## Distance from latitude

One degree of latitude covers almost the same ground distance everywhere because parallels are evenly spaced. Leong gives about 110 km at the Equator, 111 km at 45° and 111.7 km at the poles, and says to use **111 km per degree** (PDF p19). So Cairo at 30°N is about 30 x 111 = 3,330 km from the Equator. NCERT's India chapter makes the same point: the distance between two latitudes stays the same everywhere (kegy101, PDF p2). The Earth is a little flatter at the poles, so a degree there is slightly longer; 111 km is an average.

## The special parallels

| Parallel | Value | Why special |
|---|---|---|
| Equator | 0° | Midway between poles; the Sun is overhead at noon on about 21 March and 23 September |
| Tropic of Cancer | 23½°N | Northern limit of the overhead Sun, reached about 21 June |
| Tropic of Capricorn | 23½°S | Southern limit of the overhead Sun, reached about 22 December |
| Arctic Circle | 66½°N | Southern edge of the region with at least one full day of no sunrise and one of no sunset |
| Antarctic Circle | 66½°S | The same, in the south |

Leong prints the Arctic Circle as 66½°S on PDF p19; that is a slip, since the Arctic is in the north. Note the pairing: the tropics sit at 23½° because that is the tilt of the axis from the vertical; the polar circles sit at 66½° because 90° minus 23½° equals 66½°.

The tropics mark the limits of the overhead Sun: beyond them the Sun is never directly overhead at any time of the year (Leong, PDF p15).

## Heat zones (standard teaching)

The **torrid zone** lies between the tropics: the Sun is overhead at least once a year and days and nights stay nearly equal (Leong, PDF p15). The **temperate zones** lie between a tropic and a polar circle, with distinct seasons. The **frigid zones** lie beyond the polar circles, where the Sun is never high and there are days of 24-hour light and darkness.

## India and latitude

NCERT says southern India lies within the tropics and northern India in the sub-tropical zone, which explains large variation in landforms, climate, soils and vegetation (kegy101, PDF p2). The Tropic of Cancer crosses the middle of India; standard knowledge, not printed in the extract, puts it across eight states (Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura, Mizoram; verify with an atlas). NCERT gives the north-south distance as 3,214 km, a little below 30 x 111 = 3,330 km because the mainland spans about 29 degrees (standard extremes 8 deg 4 min N and 37 deg 6 min N; verify). NCERT also gives 6 deg 45 min N (in the Bay of Bengal) as the southern boundary of the whole territory, so a 'latitudinal extent' question depends on whether the mainland or the whole territory is meant.

### 3. Foundation: longitude, meridians and the prime meridian

*Objective:* Define longitude, say how meridians are numbered, and explain why the length of a degree of longitude shrinks towards the poles while that of latitude does not.

## Definition

**Longitude** is the angular distance of a place east or west of the **prime meridian**, measured as an angle at the centre of the Earth (Leong, PDF p19). A **meridian** is a half-circle running from the North Pole to the South Pole. All meridians meet at the poles and are widest apart at the Equator. They cross every parallel at right angles.

The prime meridian is the **0°** line through the Greenwich Observatory in London, called the Greenwich meridian. All other lines are numbered east or west from it, up to 180° each way. Because the circle has 360 degrees, **180°E and 180°W are the same line**. Always write the letter: 30°E is a different place from 30°W.

The word **position** of a place is its latitude and longitude together. Moscow is about 55°N and 37°E (Leong, PDF p20).

## Why a degree of longitude shrinks

Meridians converge at the poles, so the ground distance between two meridians becomes smaller as you move away from the Equator. At the Equator one degree of longitude is about 111 km, since a circle of about 40,075 km divided into 360 parts gives 111.3 km (the same as a degree of latitude). Leong lists 111.318 km at the Equator, 100.95 km at 25°, 78.856 km at 45°, 28.967 km at 75° and 0 km at the poles (PDF p19).

You can check these with one rule: the length of a degree of longitude at latitude φ is about 111.3 km multiplied by the cosine of φ. Recomputed: at 25° it is 100.9 km, at 45° it is 78.7 km, at 75° it is 28.8 km. These match Leong's values to within about 0.5 per cent (the largest gap is at 75 degrees: 28.8 km against his 28.967 km); his numbers use a slightly flattened Earth.

A degree of longitude is therefore **not a fixed distance**: multiply 111.3 km by the cosine of the latitude to turn it into kilometres. Its main use is time.

## NCERT's India example

India spans roughly 30° of latitude and roughly 30° of longitude, but the north-south distance is 3,214 km and the east-west distance is only 2,933 km (NCERT, kegy101, PDF p2). The reason: a degree of latitude is constant, but a degree of longitude shortens towards the poles. A check: if the east-west extent is about 29.3° (from standard extremes of about 68°7'E and 97°25'E, verify) and the average length of a degree is 2,933 / 29.3 = 100 km, then the average cosine is 100 / 111.3, about 0.90, which corresponds to a latitude near 26°. That is a sensible mid-latitude for the widest part of the country, so the NCERT numbers are consistent.

## Great circle versus small circle

A **great circle** is a circle on the globe whose centre is the Earth's centre, so it halves the globe. **Only one parallel is a great circle: the Equator** (Leong, PDF p23); all other parallels are small circles. Module 6 uses this idea. A comparison table of latitude and longitude is in the master notes.

### 4. Core: longitude and time, standard time and IST

*Objective:* Apply 15 degrees = 1 hour to find local, GMT and standard time, and explain why India uses the 82 deg 30 min E meridian for one national time.

## The one relation that drives everything

The Earth turns through 360° in about 24 hours, so it passes through **15° in one hour**, **1° in 4 minutes**, 15' in 1 minute (Leong, PDF p20). The Earth turns from west to east, so places to the east meet the Sun first. Therefore **going east, local time is ahead (you add); going west, it is behind (you subtract)**. Leong's memory aid is *East-Gain-Add, West-Lose-Subtract* (EGA, WLS), PDF p21.

**Local time** (local solar time) is the time shown by the Sun at one place: noon is when the Sun is highest. Two places on different meridians have different local times. If every town kept its own time, travellers in a large country would reset their watches constantly (Leong, PDF p21).

**Greenwich Mean Time (GMT)** is the local time on the prime meridian (0°). It is the reference for all time zones. (Modern practice uses UTC, the same for these purposes; verify.)

**Standard time** is the single time a country adopts, usually the local time of one central meridian, so that all clocks in the country agree. The world is divided into **24 standard time zones**, each 15° wide and one hour apart, although the shapes of countries force deviations (Leong, PDF p22).

## A fixed method (use it every time)

1. Find the **longitude difference** (or the difference between the standard meridians). If both places are on the same side of Greenwich, **subtract**; if on opposite sides, **add**.
2. Convert to time: divide by 15 for hours, or multiply by 4 for minutes.
3. Put the place to the east **ahead**. Add to the western place's time to get the eastern place's time; subtract in the other direction.
4. Check whether the answer crosses midnight, which changes the date. If the path crosses the 180° meridian, use module 5.

The classic blunder is to subtract 45°W from 30°E and get 15°. The places are on opposite sides, so the difference is 30° + 45° = 75°, which is 5 hours.

## Worked examples (all recomputed)

- **Greenwich noon to IST.** IST is GMT + 5 h 30 min. When it is 12:00 at Greenwich, it is **17:30** in India.
- **IST to GMT.** At 09:00 IST the time in London (GMT) is 09:00 minus 5:30 = **03:30**.
- **Ship's longitude.** On a ship the local noon coincides with GMT 08:00 (Leong, PDF p21). The ship is 4 hours ahead of Greenwich, so it is east: 4 x 15° = **60°E**.
- **Another ship.** Local noon when GMT is 14:16: the ship's clock is 136 minutes behind, so it is west: 136 / 4 = **34°W**.

## India and the standard meridian

NCERT explains that the standard meridian of India is **82°30'E**. Countries generally choose a standard meridian in multiples of 7°30' of longitude, and 82°30' = 11 x 7°30'. Indian Standard Time is **ahead of GMT by 5 hours 30 minutes** (kegy101, PDF p2). Check: 82.5 / 15 = 5.5 hours. The standard knowledge that the meridian passes near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh is not in the extract (verify; NCERT's own activity asks you to find places on it).

India stretches about 30° of longitude, which is a time difference of about **two hours** between the easternmost and westernmost parts: 30 x 4 = 120 minutes. The Sun rises in the north-eastern states about two hours earlier than in Jaisalmer, yet the watches in Dibrugarh, Imphal, Jaisalmer, Bhopal and Chennai show the same time (NCERT, PDF p2). Delhi (about 77.2°E, standard knowledge) lies (82.5 - 77.2) x 4 = **21 minutes** west of the IST meridian, so its local solar noon falls at about 12:21 IST. That is the whole idea of standard time: clocks agree even though the Sun does not. Other standard city longitudes (verify): Bengaluru 77.6°E, Hyderabad 78.5°E, Nagpur 79.1°E, Pune 73.9°E; of these, Nagpur lies closest to 82°30'E.



### 5. Core: time zones and the International Date Line

*Objective:* Solve date-line problems with the rule that eastward crossing sets the date back one day, and count time zones with a stated counting rule.

## World time zones

The 24 zones are centred on multiples of 15° of longitude. Large east-west countries use several. Leong says Russia spans almost 165° and uses eleven zones (165 / 15 = 11), and Canada and the USA mainland have five, with nearly five hours between the Atlantic and Pacific coasts (PDF p22). NCERT says the USA has seven (kegy101, PDF p2). The counts differ because a precise count depends on whether Alaska, Hawaii and overseas territories are included; for a question on countries with more than four zones, the counting rule matters more than any one figure (module 12).

## The International Date Line

If you travel east from Greenwich your clock goes ahead one hour per 15°; at 180°E you are 12 hours ahead. Going west you lose 12 hours at 180°W. The two sides of 180° therefore differ by 24 hours: the same clock time but **a calendar day apart**. The 180° meridian (with detours) is the **International Date Line (IDL)** (Leong, PDF p22).

The rule to memorise, with the direction made explicit:

- **Crossing the IDL travelling east (towards America): the calendar date goes back one day** (subtract 1).
- **Crossing it travelling west (towards Asia): the date goes forward one day** (add 1).

Leong describes the same thing as 'gaining' and 'losing' a day. Those words confuse because 'gain' sounds like a later date. Use the date-change rule above. Magellan's crew, sailing west round the world from 1519 to 1522, thought they landed on 5 September 1522 when the date at home was 6 September, because they never added the day (Leong, PDF p22).

The IDL does not follow 180° exactly. It bends around the Bering Strait, Tonga and other island groups so that one island group has one date (Leong, PDF pp22-23).

## Worked problems (recomputed in python)

1. **Tokyo to Vancouver (Leong's example).** Tokyo is on 135°E (UTC+9) and Vancouver on 120°W (UTC-8). The clocks differ by 9 + 8 = 17 hours, Vancouver being behind. Tokyo 10:00 on 4 December becomes Vancouver **17:00 on 3 December** (Leong gets the same answer: 105° east-west gap plus the day set back).
2. **Fiji to Honolulu.** Fiji is at UTC+12, Honolulu UTC-10: a 22-hour gap. At 20:00 on Monday in Fiji it is **22:00 on Sunday** in Honolulu.
3. **Samoa and Fiji (Leong exercise).** Samoa at about UTC-11 and Fiji at UTC+12 differ by 23 hours: noon on 1 November in Samoa is **11:00 on 2 November** in Fiji, which matches the data in Leong's exercise (PDF p25). (Samoa is reported to have moved to the west side of the line in 2011, so present-day clocks differ from the book's setting; standard knowledge, verify.)
4. **Anadyr and Nome (the 2025 PYQ setting).** Anadyr (Siberia) is at about UTC+12 and Nome (Alaska) at about UTC-9 (UTC-8 in summer), a gap near 21 hours, or 20 in summer (standard data; verify). If it is 07:00 on Tuesday in Anadyr, it is 10:00 on **Monday** in Nome, so the Asian side is the later day. The two towns are roughly 800 km apart (python on approximate coordinates gives about 810 km; verify with an atlas), not a few kilometres. The pair that really is a few kilometres apart across the line is Big Diomede (Russia) and Little Diomede (USA) in the Bering Strait. So judge Statement I of the PYQ in two parts: its day logic (the same morning hour falls on different days) agrees with the calculation, but its distance wording ('a few kilometers') does not fit Anadyr and Nome. Statement II (Monday in Anadyr, Tuesday in Nome) reverses the direction. This is analysis, not an official key.

### 6. Core: great circles and air routes

*Objective:* Define a great circle, name the parallel and meridians that are great circles, and explain why great-circle tracks look curved on flat maps and bend towards the pole.

## Great circles

A **great circle** is a circle whose plane passes through the Earth's centre, so it cuts the globe into two equal halves. The Equator is the only parallel that is a great circle; a meridian paired with the meridian on the opposite side of the globe (0° with 180°, for example) also forms one (Leong, PDF p23). The shortest path between two points on a sphere lies along a great circle. On a globe it looks like a straight line; on many flat maps it appears curved because the flat map distorts the sphere.

## Why the shortest route bends towards the pole

Meridians converge towards the poles, so a path between two places at similar high latitudes is shorter if it bends poleward than if it follows their common parallel. A parallel other than the Equator is a small circle, never the shortest path, whereas the Equator and any meridian are already great circles. Leong lists polar great-circle routes such as London to Vancouver or Los Angeles and Tokyo to Stockholm (PDF p24). For London to Vancouver (approximate standard coordinates, verify) the great circle is about 7,580 km and climbs to about 68°N; flying along the 50°N parallel would cover about 8,800 km, roughly 1,200 km more. The working is in the master notes under 'Great-circle check: London to Vancouver'.

## What aircraft really do

Aircraft cannot always follow great circles: routes link many cities, some airspace is closed, and planes stay near land for safety. Over unpopulated regions and the poles the great circle is used (Leong, PDF pp23-24).

### 7. Core: noon sun, solstices, equinoxes and day length

*Objective:* Use latitude to find the altitude of the noon sun and the length of day on the solstices and equinoxes, and explain why the seasons are caused by tilt.

## The Sun's overhead position moves

Because the axis stays tilted while the Earth goes round the Sun, the **latitude where the noon Sun is directly overhead** moves between the two tropics through the year (Leong, PDF p14). The value is called the **declination** of the Sun.

| Date (about) | Event | Overhead at | Northern hemisphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| 21 March | March (spring) equinox | Equator, 0° | Equal day and night |
| 21 June | June (summer) solstice | Tropic of Cancer, 23½°N | Longest day, shortest night |
| 23 September | September (autumn) equinox | Equator, 0° | Equal day and night |
| 22 December | December (winter) solstice | Tropic of Capricorn, 23½°S | Shortest day, longest night |

**Equinox** means 'equal night': day and night are equal everywhere. **Solstice** is a turning point of the Sun's overhead position. Dates shift by a day because a year is not exactly 365 days (Leong, PDF p14). In the southern hemisphere the seasons are reversed (Leong, PDF p14).

## Noon altitude of the Sun

The **angle of elevation (altitude)** of the noon Sun at latitude φ, when the Sun is overhead at latitude δ, is

$$ noon altitude = 90° - |φ - δ| $$

where |φ - δ| is the gap in degrees between your latitude and the overhead latitude (if the two are on opposite sides of the Equator, add the numbers). Check with Leong's worked example: Milan at 46°N on 21 June, δ = 23½°N, so altitude = 90 - (46 - 23.5) = **67½°** (PDF p16). At an equinox δ = 0, so altitude = 90° - φ, and latitude = 90° - altitude: an altitude of 37° gives a latitude of **53°** (Leong's southern-hemisphere example, 53°S, PDF p16).

A table of noon altitudes for Chennai and Delhi on the solstices and the equinox is in the master notes ('Indian noon altitude of the Sun').

The Sun is vertical (90°) only if φ equals δ. Delhi is north of the Tropic of Cancer, so it never has an overhead Sun. Leong's exercise of a 1.4 m wall casting a 0.2 m northward shadow at noon on 21 March gives an altitude of arctan(1.4/0.2) = 81.9° and so a latitude of 8.1°N; Leong says 'about 7°N', which is a rounding difference in the book (the arithmetic gives 8.1°). The shadow points north, so the Sun is to the south and the observer is north of the Equator.

## Length of day

Leong (PDF pp14-15): at the Equator days and nights are almost equal all year; between the tropics the midday Sun hardly changes; in the northern summer daylight grows with latitude, so that the **Arctic Circle has 24 hours of daylight on 21 June** and none on 22 December; the North Pole has about six months of each.

On 21 June: the Tropic of Cancer has more than 12 hours of daylight (it is in the northern summer); the Tropic of Capricorn has less than 12; the Antarctic Circle has no sunrise; the Equator has about 12 hours. UPSC problems use the idealised 12 hours at the Equator (real days are slightly longer because of refraction; verify).

## Why the tilt, not the distance, gives seasons

A high Sun concentrates heat; a low Sun spreads rays over a larger area and through more atmosphere. Longer days add heat in summer (Leong, PDF p15). Distance from the Sun is not the cause: NCERT notes that the Earth is nearest the Sun (perihelion) around 3 January and farthest (aphelion) around 4 July (kegy213, PDF p3), which is northern winter and summer respectively.

### 8. Core: maps, scale, measuring distance and area

*Objective:* Classify maps by scale and purpose, convert between representative fraction and ground distance, and measure distance and area.

## What a map is

A **map** is a graphical representation of information about the Earth's surface, drawn on a flat material such as paper using symbols and signs, and made to a definite **scale** (Leong, PDF p587). Symbols are needed because real features cannot be drawn; scale lets a large area fit on a small sheet.

## Types of maps

**By scale (Leong, PDF p587).**

- **Large-scale map**: shows a small area in great detail and with high accuracy, for example a village or a township plan; scales such as 1:10,000 or 1:5,000. A cadastral map (property boundaries of plots) is also large-scale.
- **Medium-scale map**: Leong treats scales from 1:10,000 to 1:100,000 as medium; the **topographical map** at 1:50,000 is the standard case (some series use 1:25,000).
- **Small-scale map**: shows a large area with little detail and high generalisation; all atlas maps are small scale.

Leong's printed definition of large scale is garbled (it says the ratio is 'not large'). The correct reading: a large-scale map has a **larger fraction**, such as 1/10,000, so 1:25,000 is larger scale than 1:1,000,000. Survey of India sheets are commonly 1:50,000 and 1:250,000 (standard; verify).

**By purpose (Leong, PDF pp587-588).** **General-purpose** maps show many features: atlas and wall maps and the **topographical (sheet) map**, which shows relief, elevation, vegetation, land use, settlements, roads, railways and airports. **Special-purpose (thematic)** maps show one theme (road, vegetation, weather, soil, population, political, geological, land use); a **political map** uses different line symbols for different levels of boundary. A **sketch map** is not drawn to scale.

## Scale in three forms (Leong, PDF p589)

1. **Representative fraction (RF)**, e.g. 1:50,000. One unit on the map is 50,000 of the same unit on the ground.
2. **Statement scale**, e.g. '1 cm to 1 km'. If the map is reduced or enlarged, the statement becomes false.
3. **Linear (graphic) scale**, a drawn line with primary and secondary divisions. It stays true when the map is enlarged or reduced, so it is the best form.

## Distance and area (worked, recomputed)

**Straight distance** = measured length x ground value of 1 cm.

- 14 cm at 1:200,000 (1 cm = 2 km) is 28 km. 8.5 cm at 1:50,000 (1 cm = 0.5 km) is 4.25 km (Leong, PDF p590).
- **Winding route**: lay a thread or paper strip along it, or step with dividers set at 1 cm. 31 steps at 1:200,000 give 31 x 2 = 62 km (PDF pp590-591).

**Area** of a regular shape: convert each side to ground distance first, then multiply. A 16 cm x 10 cm rectangle at 1:50,000 is 8 km x 5 km = **40 km²** (PDF p592).

**Irregular shape**: trace it onto a 1 cm grid; count whole squares, count the part squares and halve that number, add; multiply by the area of one square. Leong's example: 27 whole + 24 part (half = 12) = 39 squares; at 1:200,000 one square is 2 km x 2 km = 4 km²; area 39 x 4 = **156 km²**.

**Reduction and enlargement.** To redraw a 1:50,000 map at 1:200,000: lengths are multiplied by 50,000 / 200,000 = 1/4, and the area becomes 1/16. To enlarge a 1:50,000 map to twice its size the new scale is 1:25,000 (statement scale: 1 cm to 0.5 km becomes 4 cm to 1 km), and the area becomes 4 times (PDF pp593-596).


### 9. Core: direction and bearing

*Objective:* Give a bearing and a 16-point direction for a line, and find the back bearing.

## Direction and bearing

**True north** is the direction of the North Pole. **Magnetic north** is where a compass needle points; the angle between the two is the **magnetic declination (variation)**, and it changes with time and place. **Grid north** is the north of a map's grid lines (Leong, PDF p597).

A **bearing** is an angle in degrees measured **clockwise from north**, from 000° to 360°: east is 090°, south 180°, west 270°. A **direction** is the compass-point name; Leong's worked example uses the two words the wrong way round.

## The 16 points

The compass card has 16 points, 22½° apart: N at 0°, NNE at 22½°, NE at 45°, and so on round to NNW at 337½° (full list in the master notes under 'Sixteen compass points'; Leong, PDF pp597-598). The four cardinal points are 90° apart, the four intercardinals (NE, SE, SW, NW) lie halfway between them, and the other eight lie halfway again.

## From bearing to direction

Take the nearest point. 235° is 10° from SW (225°) and 12½° from WSW (247½°), so the direction is **south-west** (PDF p598). A bearing of 70° is 2½° from ENE (67½°), so the direction is ENE.

## Back bearing

The reverse (back) bearing of a line is its bearing plus 180° if the bearing is below 180°, and minus 180° if it is 180° or more (standard). Edge cases: 135° gives 315°; 270° gives 090°; 000° gives 180°. The bearing from A to B is therefore not the bearing from B to A, so always say 'from' which point it is measured.

## Using it on a map

Place the protractor at the starting point with its zero line along north and read clockwise. Distance and bearing together fix a position: 'B is 3.6 km from A on a bearing of 135°' puts B south-east of A, and 3.6 km is 7.2 cm on a 1:50,000 map.

## Diagram idea

Draw a compass rose marked in degrees, then sketch two towns A and B with a protractor-measured bearing from A to B and the back bearing from B to A.


### 10. Master: relief on maps, contours, gradient and section drawing

*Objective:* Read relief from contours, spot heights and shading, and compute a gradient and a vertical exaggeration.

## Methods of showing relief (Leong, PDF pp599-601)

**Relief** means the heights and shapes of the land. A map shows it in several ways.

- **Contour lines** join points of equal height above mean sea level. Their two advantages: they give heights and also show shapes. Sea level is the datum (0 m) and scientists use an agreed mean sea level.
- **Spot height**: an accurately measured point shown by a dot and a figure; gives height but not shape.
- **Trigonometrical station** (a surveyed point), and **bench mark** (a surveyor's mark on a durable object, shown as B.M. with a number).
- **Form lines**: approximate contours between spot heights.
- **Layer tinting**: colours for height bands (green for lowland, then yellow and browns for higher land). It separates highlands from lowlands but does not give exact heights.
- **Hill shading**: imaginary light from the north-west shades the opposite slopes; shows form, not height.
- **Hachures**: short lines drawn down the slope; close and heavy lines mean steep slope, wide and light mean gentle.

Contours, spot heights, bench marks and trigonometrical stations suit large-scale maps; hachures and hill shading suit small-scale maps (PDF p604).

## Contour patterns (PDF pp613-616)

The **contour interval** is the constant vertical gap between neighbouring contours. **Spacing tells the slope**: close contours mean steep, wide contours gentle, even spacing a uniform slope. A **convex slope** has close contours low down and wide ones higher up (steep below, gentle above); a **concave slope** is the reverse. A **conical hill** shows rings with the innermost highest; a **knoll** has only one or two rings. A **valley** shows V-shapes (youthful V, mature more open, plain stage broad); a **spur** also shows V-shapes but with the smallest V the highest. A **ridge** has closely spaced stretched contours (a cuesta has a steep scarp slope and a gentle dip slope). A **col** is low ground between two peaks, a **saddle** a large col, and a **pass** a col used as a route. A **plateau** has close contours at its edge and almost none in the centre. A **watershed** is the line from which streams flow in opposite directions. Standard rule (not printed in the extract; verify): the V of a valley points up towards higher ground, the V of a spur points down towards lower ground. With a convex slope two points on either side are not intervisible (PDF p613).

**Gradient** = vertical interval ÷ horizontal distance. Example: contours with a 100 m interval are 2 cm apart on a 1:50,000 map. The 2 cm is 1 km on the ground, so the gradient is 100 m in 1,000 m, which is **1 in 10**. A steeper slope has smaller horizontal distance for the same interval.

## Section drawing and vertical exaggeration

A **section drawing** is the profile you would see if the land were sliced along a line (PDF pp602-603). **Vertical exaggeration (VE)** = horizontal-scale denominator ÷ vertical-scale denominator, i.e. how many times the heights are stretched compared with distances. Leong's example: vertical scale 1 inch to 200 ft; horizontal scale 1.25 inches to a mile, which is 1 inch to 5,280 / 1.25 = 4,224 ft, so VE = 4,224 / 200 = **21.12**, about 21. A metric example: horizontal 1:50,000 (1 cm = 500 m), vertical 1 cm = 100 m, so VE = 500 / 100 = **5**.


### 11. Master: drainage patterns, conventional signs and settlement patterns

*Objective:* Name the six common drainage patterns with their conditions, list the five groups of conventional signs and recognise nucleated, linear and dispersed settlement patterns on a map.

## Drainage patterns (PDF pp606-607)

A **drainage pattern** is the arrangement of a main stream and its tributaries, set by slope, rock hardness and geological history. **Dendritic** (tree-like, acute junctions) is the commonest and forms on uniform rock; **trellis** has near right-angle junctions on alternating hard and soft rock bands and is linked with river capture; **radial** streams diverge from a central high point such as a dome or volcanic cone (Amarkantak is a textbook Indian example; verify); **annular** streams encircle a central elevation; **braided** channels split and rejoin around dry ground; **centripetal** streams drain into a central depression. A table is in the master notes.

## Conventional signs (PDF pp604-605)

Cartographers use shapes, colours, lines and sizes accepted by convention. Five groups: relief, water, vegetation, man-made features, geo-political features. **Water features are blue** (thicker line, bigger river; darker blue, deeper water; broken blue line, an indefinite course). **Vegetation is green**, with black tree symbols for forest and stipple for plantation. Different line symbols distinguish international, state and local boundaries. Roads, rail, buildings and cultural features are shown by symbols explained in the map **key**; always read the key first.

## Settlement patterns on a map (PDF pp608-612)

On a map of buildings: **nucleated** (compact, close dots or built-up area), **linear** (strung along a road or river), **dispersed** (widely scattered farmsteads). A nodal settlement forms where routes cross, a confluence settlement where rivers meet.

## How to use this on a question

For a drainage question, identify the junction angle of tributaries first (acute or right angle), then whether streams run away from, round, or into a central feature. For a settlement question, check the spacing of dots: close dots or a built-up area suggest a nucleated pattern, a line of dots along a route suggests a linear one, and widely spaced dots suggest dispersed farmsteads. Always read the key before naming a sign.

### Notes: Core terms of the grid

- Latitude: the angular distance of a place north or south of the Equator, measured at the Earth's centre; the Equator is 0 degrees and the poles are 90 degrees N and S.
- Parallel of latitude: a circle joining places of equal latitude, parallel to the Equator; it shrinks towards the poles.
- Longitude: the angular distance of a place east or west of the prime meridian (Greenwich), up to 180 degrees each way.
- Meridian: a half-circle from pole to pole; all meridians meet at the poles and cross parallels at right angles.
- Graticule: the network of parallels and meridians; a place is located by its latitude and longitude together.
- Great circle: a circle on the globe whose centre is the Earth's centre; the Equator is the only parallel that is a great circle.
- Local time: time set by the Sun at one place; GMT is the local time at 0 degrees; standard time is the time a country adopts from one chosen meridian.

### Notes: Time method, step by step

- 1. Longitude difference: subtract if both places are on the same side of Greenwich, add if on opposite sides.
- 2. Convert: 15 degrees = 1 hour; 1 degree = 4 minutes; 15 minutes of arc = 1 minute of time.
- 3. East is ahead (add), west is behind (subtract).
- 4. Standard-time questions: use the standard meridians or UTC offsets, not city longitudes. IST = GMT + 5 h 30 min.
- 5. Check the date: crossing midnight changes it.
- 6. Crossing the International Date Line eastwards sets the calendar date back one day; crossing westwards moves it forward one day.

### Notes: Confusions to avoid

- Large scale versus large number: 1:25,000 is larger scale than 1:1,000,000.

- Time zone versus date: the IDL changes the date, not the clock time.
- Equinox (equal day and night) versus solstice (the Sun at its farthest overhead latitude).
- Local time at a city versus the standard time that clocks show.

### Core concepts

- Latitude and longitude are angles measured from the Earth's centre; parallels and meridians form the graticule.
