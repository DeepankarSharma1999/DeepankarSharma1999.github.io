## GS-012: Temple economy and inscription evidence

Prelims GS I (Mains culture cross-links) · History · Ancient India

*Goal:* Explain Temple economy and inscription evidence and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### The temple as an economic hub

Chola temples were far more than shrines. Land given by kings and other donors funded the people who served the deity and usually lived close by: priests, cooks, garland makers, sweepers, musicians and dancers. Settlements and workshops clustered around these institutions, and bronze casting became the craft most closely tied to them. In Tamil temple towns between the eighth and twelfth centuries temples turned into wealthy administrative centres with large landholdings, and big complexes kept adding enclosure walls and gateways; Srirangam has seven concentric enclosures. Inscriptions name devadana or tirunamattukkani as the category for land gifted to temples, alongside brahmadeya, shalabhoga and pallichchhandam.

### What inscriptions reveal about economy and governance

Chola records show a dense fiscal system: more than 400 tax terms, from forced labour (vetti) and land revenue (kadamai) to levies on thatching a house or climbing palm trees with a ladder. Brahmana settlements were run by assemblies of leading landholders whose work was recorded in detail, often on temple walls. The Uttaramerur inscriptions set rules for membership: owning revenue-paying land and a house, age 35 to 70, Vedic learning, honesty and administrative skill; no one could sit on another committee within three years, and those who had not submitted accounts were barred. Lotteries using palm-leaf tickets drawn by a boy filled each committee.

### Limits of the evidence and exam angles

Epigraphy has technical and structural limits. Letters may be faint or damaged, word meanings can be specific to a place or time, and many inscriptions have not survived or remain unpublished. More basically, inscriptions favour grand events and the perspective of whoever paid for them, so ordinary farming and daily life seldom appear. Literary texts such as the Periyapuranam help fill gaps. Prelims distinctions: devadana (temple) versus brahmadeya (Brahmana) land; vetti is labour, not cash. Mains dimensions: temple as landlord, employer and urban nucleus; assemblies as local self-government with property-based eligibility; need to cross-check inscriptions with other sources.
