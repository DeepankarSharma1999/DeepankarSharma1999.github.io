## PH-058: Fresnel half period zones

Physics Paper I · Physics · Wave optics: diffraction

### Bridge: Small-quantity algebra: square roots of 1 plus a small number

Diffraction problems compare two long paths that differ by a tiny amount. You need one trick.

**The trick.** For a number x much smaller than 1, √(1 + x) is very nearly 1 + x/2. Test it: x = 0.04 gives √1.04 = 1.0198 and 1 + 0.02 = 1.02. For x = 0.0001 they differ by only 1.2 × 10⁻⁹.

**Where it is used.** The distance from a point at height r on a wavefront to a point on the axis at distance b is √(b² + r²) = b √(1 + r²/b²). With x = r²/b² small this becomes

$$ √(b² + r²) = b + r²/(2b) $$

So the path is longer than b by r²/(2b). Numbers: r = 0.7071 mm and b = 1 m give r²/(2b) = 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ m, which is exactly λ/2 for λ = 500 nm. That is why the edge of the first zone of a 500 nm plane wave at 1 m has radius 0.7071 mm.

**Trap.** Drop terms only when you can say why: here r²/b² is about 5 × 10⁻⁷, far smaller than 1.

### Bridge: Adding signed vibrations and an alternating series

Two vibrations of the same frequency add by superposition. If they are in step (path difference 0, λ, 2λ …) their amplitudes add. If they are half a period apart (path difference λ/2, 3λ/2 …) the phase difference is π and one is the **negative** of the other, so the amplitudes subtract. Intensity is proportional to the **square of the total amplitude**, not to the sum of the squares.

**Alternating series.** Fresnel's zones give a list of amplitudes m_1, m_2, m_3, … that slowly decrease and alternate in sign: A = m_1 − m_2 + m_3 − …. The trick is that for slowly changing terms m_n is almost the average of its neighbours.

**Example.** Take m_n = 1, 0.9, 0.8, …, 0.1 (n = 1 to 10, m_11 = 0). The sum is 1 − 0.9 + 0.8 − 0.7 + 0.6 − 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.3 + 0.2 − 0.1 = 0.5, which is m_1/2. Group in pairs to see it: five pairs each equal to 0.1.

**Trap.** The intensity of the first zone alone is not the sum of the intensities of the pieces; it is the square of the summed amplitude.

### 1. Orientation: a bright spot in a shadow, and what the examiner asks about Fresnel zones

*Objective:* Say in plain words what Fresnel diffraction is, name the five ideas of this lesson (zones, resultant amplitude, aperture and disc, zone plate, Fresnel number) and match them to the three verified questions.

## The picture first
Put a small, very round disc (a ball bearing will do) in a narrow laser beam a few metres from a wall. The shadow is dark, but at its exact centre there is a tiny **bright dot**. Now shine light through a round hole of radius 0.5 mm and slide a card towards the hole: the centre of the light patch goes bright, dark, bright, dark. Straight-line rays cannot explain either effect. One idea of Augustin Fresnel (1815, Brij Lal PDF p. 413) explains both: add up the secondary waves from every part of the wavefront with the correct phases, and count how many "rings" of the wavefront are letting light through.

## Words you need first
- **Wavefront**: the surface joining all points the wave has reached at the same instant, vibrating in step. A distant source gives a plane wavefront; a point source gives a spherical one.
- **Diffraction**: the spreading of waves into the region a straight-line picture calls shadow (Brij Lal PDF pp. 411 to 413).
- **Fresnel diffraction**: source or screen at a finite distance, so the wavefront is curved and no lens is needed (PDF p. 421). **Fraunhofer diffraction**: plane waves in, parallel rays out, usually with a lens. Day 17 lesson PH-057 (Circular aperture and Airy pattern) owns the Fraunhofer side; module 9 here compares the two.
- **Half-period zone**: one of the rings into which Fresnel cuts a wavefront, chosen so that neighbouring rings send light to the point of observation half a wavelength apart (module 3).
- **Zone plate**: a screen with every second zone blocked; it focuses like a lens (modules 7, 8). **Poisson spot**: the bright dot in a round disc's shadow (module 6).

## The one idea behind the whole topic
Cut the wavefront into rings so that the light from each ring reaches the point P half a wave behind the previous ring. Neighbouring rings then cancel. Open the wavefront completely and the total is only half of what the first ring gives alone. Block rings in a chosen pattern and the total changes in a way you can count. Everything here is counting rings.

### 2. Foundation: the Huygens-Fresnel principle, Fresnel's assumptions and the obliquity factor

*Objective:* State the Huygens-Fresnel principle and Fresnel's three assumptions, evaluate the obliquity factor K = (1 + cos θ)/2 at 0, 90 and 180 degrees, and explain why it removes the backward wave.

## Huygens, then Fresnel
Huygens' principle (lesson PH-042) says every point of a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets whose envelope is the new wavefront. It explains reflection and refraction but not diffraction: it gives no **strength** for each wavelet and it predicts a backward wave as well as a forward one. In 1815 Fresnel joined Huygens' wavelets to the principle of interference (Brij Lal PDF p. 413). The result is the **Huygens-Fresnel principle**:

> Every unobstructed point of a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets. The disturbance at any point P is the **superposition**, with correct phases, of all the wavelets that reach P.

Superposition means adding the vibrations as signed quantities, then squaring the total for intensity (bridge B2 of this lesson).

## Fresnel's three assumptions (PDF pp. 413 to 414)
To get the effect at an external point P from a wavefront, Fresnel assumed:
1. The wavefront can be cut into a large number of small zones, and the effect at P is the combined effect of the wavelets from all zones.
2. The effect of a zone at P depends on its **distance** from P (farther means weaker).
3. The effect also depends on the **obliquity**: the angle θ between the forward normal to the wavefront at that zone and the line from the zone to P.

## The obliquity factor
The book states that the effect is proportional to (1 + cos θ) (PDF p. 414). Scaled so that the straight-ahead value is 1, the standard form is

$$ K(θ) = (1 + cos θ)/2 $$

| θ (from the forward normal) | K(θ) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0° (straight ahead) | 1 | maximum |
| 60° | 0.75 | |
| 90° (along the wavefront) | 0.5 | half of the forward value |
| 120° | 0.25 | |
| 180° (straight back) | 0 | **no backward wave** |

This answers the old difficulty of Huygens' construction. A wavelet that spread equally in all directions would send a wave backwards towards the source. Because K(180°) = 0 the backward amplitude is zero, so there is no back wave (PDF p. 414). For the zones that matter here θ is a fraction of a degree, so K is almost exactly 1 (module 4 gives numbers).

## Strength of one wavelet
A spherical wavelet's **amplitude** falls as 1/distance. With the obliquity factor, the contribution of a small patch of area dS to the amplitude at P is proportional to

$$ dS · K(θ) / (distance to P) $$

and its phase is set by the path length (a path longer by λ/2 means a phase lag of π). Fresnel chose K and the 1/distance law from observation; the later Kirchhoff theory justifies the form (standard; verify).

## Why shadows look sharp and yet are not
An ordinary shadow looks sharp because the obstacle is enormous compared with the small region of wavefront that matters at P (module 4). A small obstacle, or a point close to the shadow edge, lets border wavelets reach P, so light "bends" into the shadow (PDF pp. 412 to 413). An extended source such as a frosted bulb makes overlapping patterns that wash out the fringes (PDF p. 413).

## Defining the pole
For an external point P, the **pole** of a wavefront is the point nearest to P, the foot of the perpendicular from P (PDF p. 415). Zones are counted outwards from the pole; for another point P′ the pole moves (module 5).

### 3. Core: constructing the half-period zones, their radii and equal areas (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Construct half-period zones on a plane and a spherical wavefront, derive r_n = √(n b λ) and the equal-area result, and say how exact the equality is.

## Setting and assumptions
A monochromatic **plane wave** (source very far away) has a plane wavefront in the plane of the page edge-on. P is an external point on the axis at perpendicular distance b from the wavefront; O is the pole (foot of the perpendicular), so OP = b. Wavelength λ, with λ much smaller than b. Here r is the distance from O measured in the wavefront.

## Step 1: the construction (Brij Lal PDF p. 414)
Draw spheres centred on P with radii b + λ/2, b + 2(λ/2), b + 3(λ/2), and so on. They cut the wavefront in circles of radii r_1, r_2, r_3 (the circles M_1, M_2, M_3 of the figure). The ring between circle n − 1 and circle n is the n-th **half-period zone**. A point on the edge of zone n is farther from P than the point of the edge of zone n − 1 by λ/2, so wavelets from corresponding points of neighbouring zones reach P with a path difference λ/2, that is a phase difference π.

## Step 2: the radii
Edge of zone n: M_nP = b + nλ/2 and OP = b. The triangle O M_n P has a right angle at O, so by Pythagoras

$$ r_n² = (b + nλ/2)² − b² = n b λ + n² λ²/4 $$

Because λ is tiny compared with b, drop the λ² term:

$$ r_n = √(n b λ) $$

so r_1 : r_2 : r_3 = 1 : √2 : √3, and the radii go as the **square roots of the natural numbers** (PDF p. 415). For b = 1 m and λ = 500 nm the neglected term is a fraction 1.25 × 10⁻⁷ of the main term; r_1, r_2, r_3 = 0.7071, 1.0000, 1.2247 mm.

## Step 3: the areas (derivation 1, the 2017 Q3(b) first part)
Area of zone n = π r_n² − π r_(n−1)². Using the exact radii:

$$ Area_n = π [ b λ + (2n − 1) λ²/4 ] $$

The first term does not contain n. Dropping λ², **every zone has area π b λ** (PDF p. 415). The relative error is (2n − 1)λ/(4b): for b = 1 m, λ = 500 nm and n = 10 it is 2.4 × 10⁻⁶. In words: a ring's circumference grows as r but its radial width falls as 1/r, so the product stays constant.

Dimension check: b λ is an area. Bigger λ or b gives bigger zones (PDF p. 415).

## Step 4: a spherical wavefront
Let a point source S be at distance a from the wavefront (radius of the spherical wavefront a), and P at distance b beyond the pole O. A point M of the wavefront at angle φ from the axis (angle at S) satisfies PM² = a² + (a + b)² − 2a(a + b) cos φ. Differentiate: PM d(PM) = a(a + b) sin φ dφ. The ring area element is dS = 2π a² sin φ dφ, so

$$ dS = [2π a/(a + b)] · PM d(PM) $$

Integrate PM from b + (n − 1)λ/2 to b + nλ/2:

$$ Area_n = [π a b λ/(a + b)] · [1 + (2n − 1) λ/(4b)] $$

Again equal for every n, to the order of λ/b. Putting a → infinity gives π b λ. The radius of zone n becomes r_n = √(n λ a b/(a + b)). Numbers (python): a = 2 m, b = 3 m, λ = 500 nm gives r_1 = 0.7746 mm and area 1.885 mm².

### 4. Core: the resultant amplitude of a whole wavefront, and rectilinear propagation (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Prove that the unobstructed wavefront gives amplitude m_1/2 (intensity one quarter of the first zone alone) by the alternating-series argument, and use it to explain why light seems to travel straight.

## Setting and sign convention
Let m_1, m_2, m_3, … be the amplitudes at P due to zones 1, 2, 3 (Brij Lal PDF p. 415). Neighbouring zones arrive half a period apart, a phase difference π, so their vibrations are opposite: take odd zones as positive, even zones as negative at the same instant (PDF p. 416). All zones lie on the same side of the pole, and every wavelet is taken in phase at the wavefront. The **assumptions**: (i) the zone areas are equal (module 3); (ii) m_n falls slowly with n, only through the obliquity factor K, because for a plane wave dS/distance = 2 pi d-rho exactly, so area growth cancels the 1/distance fall (module 2); (iii) m_n is almost the mean of its neighbours, m_n ≈ (m_(n−1) + m_(n+1))/2, true when the fall is smooth and slow.

## Step 1: the series
For N zones the resultant amplitude is

$$ A_N = m_1 − m_2 + m_3 − m_4 + … ± m_N $$

with the last term positive if N is odd, negative if N is even.

## Step 2: regroup with assumption (iii)
Write m_1 − m_2 + m_3 − … as

$$ A = m_1/2 + (m_1/2 − m_2 + m_3/2) + (m_3/2 − m_4 + m_5/2) + … $$

Each bracket equals ½(m_(n−1) − 2 m_n + m_(n+1)), which is zero by (iii). For an unobstructed wavefront the number of zones is infinite and the last term m_N → 0, so

$$ A = m_1/2 $$

(Brij Lal, PDF p. 416, eq. 17.3). For finite N the leftover is ± m_N/2, which is why partial sums wobble around m_1/2.

## Step 3: a second proof
Write A = m_1 − (m_2 − m_3 + m_4 − …) = m_1 − A′. A′ starts at m_2 instead of m_1 and m_2 is almost m_1, so A′ is almost A. Then A = m_1 − A gives 2A = m_1.

## Step 4: intensity and meaning
Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude, so

$$ I = m_1²/4 = I_1/4 $$

The unobstructed wavefront gives **one quarter of the intensity of the first zone alone**. Equivalently the first zone alone gives four times the free-space intensity I_0; the other zones cancel the excess.

## Numbers: how slowly m_n falls
Plane wave, b = 1 m, λ = 500 nm (python, m_n ∝ K, since dS/distance is constant for a plane wave): m_10/m_1 = 0.9999989, m_100/m_1 = 0.999988, m_1000/m_1 = 0.999875. The first thousand zones are equal to within 0.013 per cent; the series closes on m_1/2 only because infinitely many terms are present.

## Rectilinear propagation (PDF pp. 416 to 417)
Because the whole wavefront gives only half the amplitude of the first zone, the zones near the line from the source to P decide the result: only a patch of radius of order r_1 = √(bλ) really matters. For light (500 nm) at b = 10 m, r_1 = 2.24 mm. An ordinary obstacle is much bigger, so it blocks or leaves open a huge number of zones and gives a sharp shadow: this is why light seems to travel straight. For sound (λ about 0.34 m, 1 kHz) at b = 10 m, r_1 = 1.84 m, so ordinary obstacles are comparable to a zone and sound bends round them; for radio of 3 m at 1 km, r_1 = 55 m (Brij Lal question 19). Rectilinear propagation is thus an approximation valid when the obstacle size is far greater than r_1 (PDF p. 416).

## Source slip to avoid
The extract says an obstacle of half the area of the first zone would screen the whole wavefront (PDF p. 416). That conflicts with its own disc result (module 6): covering the first zone leaves the centre bright. The correct statement is about **amplitude**, not area.

### 5. Core: a circular aperture, the number of open zones and the bright-dark centre

*Objective:* Find the number of half-period zones open in a circular aperture, deduce whether the axial point is bright or dark, and solve for the screen positions or the wavelength.

## Setting
A point source S is at distance a from a circular aperture of radius r; P is on the axis at distance b beyond it, with S, the centre O and P in a line (Brij Lal PDF pp. 421 to 423). Light is monochromatic, and r is much smaller than a and b so that small-angle algebra holds. For a plane wave put a = ∞.

## Step 1: how many zones fit in the aperture
The path from S to P over the edge A of the aperture is longer than the straight path SOP by δ = SA + AP − (a + b). With SA = √(a² + r²) ≈ a + r²/(2a) and AP = √(b² + r²) ≈ b + r²/(2b) (bridge B1):

$$ δ = (r²/2)(1/a + 1/b) $$

Exactly n half-period zones fit if δ = nλ/2, so

$$ n = (r²/λ)(1/a + 1/b) $$

(eq. 17.11a, b). For a plane wave n = r²/(b λ). The number n need not be a whole number; it is the **number of zones exposed**.

## Step 2: what the axis does
- n = 1 (one zone): amplitude m_1, intensity I_1 = 4 I_0, **four times the free-space value**.
- n = 2: amplitude m_1 − m_2, nearly zero, the centre is **dark**.
- n = 3: m_1 − m_2 + m_3, nearly m_1, **bright** again.
Odd n gives a bright centre, even n a dark centre (PDF p. 422). As b decreases n grows and the centre flickers between bright and dark. For general n the standard result (ideal, neglecting obliquity; verify) is I/I_0 = 4 sin²(πn/2).

| n | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 2.5 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I / I_0 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 0 | 2 | 4 |

Real minima are not perfectly dark because the zone amplitudes are not exactly equal.

## Step 3: moving the screen (plane wave)
The centre is dark when r² = 2bλ, 4bλ, … so b_k = r²/(kλ) for k = 2, 4, …. Take r = 0.5 mm, λ = 500 nm: r²/λ = 0.5 m, so b = 0.50 m (one zone, bright), 0.25 m (dark), 0.1667 m (bright), 0.125 m (dark), 0.10 m (bright). The farthest dark centre is at 0.25 m (n = 2); farther away n < 2 and the centre is never dark.

**Example 17.2 (recomputed).** A hole of diameter 1.2 mm (r = 0.6 mm) is lit by plane waves; a screen moved in from far away first shows a dark centre at b = 30 cm. The first dark centre is n = 2, so λ = r²/(2b) = (6 × 10⁻⁴)²/(0.60) = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m = 6000 Å, as in the book (PDF p. 440).

## Step 4: points off the axis (qualitative)
For another screen point P′, join S to P′; this line meets the wavefront at a new pole O′. The zones must be counted outwards from O′, and part of them is cut off by the edge of the aperture. If an odd number is exposed, P′ is bright; if even, dark (PDF p. 422). The pattern is therefore concentric bright and dark rings round the axial point. The rings crowd together farther out and fade, because the strong central zones about O′ are cut off and the exposed outer zones are more oblique.

## A source note
Section 17.8.2 (PDF pp. 423 to 424) estimates ring radii x_n = n b λ/(2r) from the two opposite edge points only: a rough, Fraunhofer-type estimate, not a Fresnel result. Do not use it here; Airy's 1.22 is derived in Day 17 lesson PH-057 (Circular aperture and Airy pattern).

### 6. Core: the opaque circular disc and the bright spot in its shadow

*Objective:* Show by the zone method that the centre of the shadow of a circular disc is always bright, estimate its intensity, and say when and why it is seen.

## Setting
An opaque circular disc of radius R (Brij Lal PDF pp. 424 to 425) is lit by a point source S at distance a; P is on the axis behind the disc at distance b, and EF in the book's figure marks the geometrical shadow. As for the aperture (module 5), the number of half-period zones covered by the disc is

$$ k = (R²/λ)(1/a + 1/b) $$

For a plane wave k = R²/(b λ). Assumptions: point source, monochromatic light, a very round sharp edge, small angles, the zone amplitudes m_n falling slowly with n.

## Step 1: the amplitude at P
The first k zones are blocked. The surviving zones are k + 1, k + 2, and so on to infinity. With the signs of module 4 the amplitude is a series that starts at m_(k+1):

$$ A = m_(k+1) − m_(k+2) + m_(k+3) − … $$

The regrouping argument of module 4 applies to this series exactly as it did to the full one, so

$$ A = (−1)^k m_(k+1)/2 $$

(the sign is that of zone k + 1 in module 4's convention; the intensity is unchanged). Take k = 1: A = −m_2/2, magnitude m_2/2 as the book writes (PDF p. 424). Take k = 2: A = +m_3/2. Since m_(k+1) is never zero, **the centre of the geometrical shadow is always bright**, as if the disc were almost absent (PDF p. 425).

## Step 2: how bright?
Compare with the unobstructed value A_0 = m_1/2. The ratio of intensities is (m_(k+1)/m_1)². For b = 1 m and 500 nm the ratio is 0.9999975 for k = 10 and 0.99975 for k = 1000 (python; m_n ∝ K, as dS/distance is constant for a plane wave), so for any realistic disc the spot is essentially as bright as the unobstructed light. The book says the spot weakens as the disc grows (PDF p. 425): true, because m_(k+1) falls, but the fall is very slow. In the ideal model with equal zone amplitudes the intensity of the spot equals I_0 exactly (python check).

## Step 3: numbers
A disc of radius R = 1.0 mm, plane wave of 600 nm. At b = 2.0 m, k = 0.83: the disc covers less than one zone and the spot is as bright as with no disc. At b = 1.667 m, k = 1.00, A = m_2/2 and the spot is still bright. The size of the spot for a **large** disc (standard result; verify): its intensity near the axis is I_0 [J_0(2πRρ/(λb))]², where ρ is the distance from the axis. A numerical integral (python) agrees for R = 1 cm, λ = 500 nm, b = 5 m (k = 40): the first dark ring of the spot is at ρ = 2.405 λ b/(2πR) = 0.096 mm. So the spot is a pinpoint, which is one reason it escapes notice.

## Step 4: why it is rarely seen, and its history
It needs a point-like monochromatic source and a very round, smooth-edged disc. An extended source makes overlapping patterns that wash out the rings, and ordinary obstacles are far larger than the first zone (PDF p. 413). **History** (standard; verify): about 1818 Poisson, who favoured the particle theory, noted that Fresnel's wave theory predicts a bright spot at the centre of a disc's shadow and took it as absurd; Arago then found the spot in an experiment. It is called the Poisson spot (or Arago spot).

## Away from the axis
Rings of alternate bright and dark appear round the spot (PDF p. 425), because off the axis the pole moves and the count of zones exposed differs from side to side. The master notes add the Babinet link between a disc and a hole.

### 7. Core: the zone plate, its radii, its focal length and the lens formula (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Describe how a zone plate is made, derive its focal length f = r_1²/λ and the lens-like formula 1/a + 1/b = 1/f, and explain why it brightens the focal point.

## What a zone plate is
A **zone plate** is a screen in which light is blocked from every alternate half-period zone (Brij Lal PDF p. 416). One kind blocks the even zones, the other the odd zones. To make one, draw concentric circles with radii proportional to √1, √2, √3, … ink the odd zones and photograph the drawing much reduced; the negative is clear in the odd zones and opaque in the even ones (PDF p. 417). The central zone is zone 1; a plate that blocks the odd zones works equally well.

## Step 1: why a focus appears
Send a plane wave of wavelength λ at a plate whose radii are r_n. Choose the point P on the axis at distance b so that the zone radii of the plate are the half-period zones of P: r_n² = n b λ (module 3). If the even zones are blocked, only zones 1, 3, 5, … send light, and they all arrive at P with the same sign:

$$ A = m_1 + m_3 + m_5 + … ≈ N_t m_1 $$

where N_t is the number of open zones. The unobstructed wave gives m_1/2, so the amplitude is 2N_t times larger and the intensity 4N_t² times larger. With N_t = 10 (20 zones) the ideal gain is 400 (python check). The path from two corresponding points of successive open zones differs by λ, a phase difference 2π, which is why they add (PDF p. 420).

## Step 2: the focal length
From r_n² = n b λ the distance b of the bright spot for a parallel beam is the focal length:

$$ f = r_n²/(n λ) = r_1²/λ $$

(eq. 17.6, PDF p. 417). It does not depend on n, because r_n² = n r_1². Therefore

$$ r_1 = √(f λ), r_n = √n · r_1 $$

Dimension check: f λ is an area. For f = 60 cm and λ = 6000 Å, r_1 = √(0.60 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁷) = 0.6 mm.

## Step 3: wavelength dependence
Since f = r_1²/λ, f varies as 1/λ: shorter wavelength, **longer** focal length. For a plate with r_1 = 0.6 mm, f = 0.90 m at 400 nm, 0.60 m at 600 nm and 0.514 m at 700 nm (python). A glass lens does the reverse: violet is bent more and focuses nearer (PDF p. 419).

## Step 4: a spherical wave (derivation 3)
A point source S is on the axis at distance a from the plate, P at distance b. Light from S to P through the edge M_n of zone n must travel nλ/2 farther than through the pole O (PDF p. 418):

$$ SM_n + M_nP = a + b + nλ/2 $$

With SM_n = √(a² + r_n²) ≈ a + r_n²/(2a) and M_nP = √(b² + r_n²) ≈ b + r_n²/(2b) (bridge B1):

$$ r_n²/(2a) + r_n²/(2b) = nλ/2 $$

Multiply by 2/r_n²:

$$ 1/a + 1/b = nλ/r_n² = 1/f $$

(eq. 17.8 and 17.9). Here a and b are positive distances on opposite sides of the plate, so this is the real-image form. The book writes it as 1/b − 1/a = 1/f with the Cartesian sign convention, matching the lens formula 1/v − 1/u = 1/f (PDF p. 419). A zone plate therefore acts as a **converging lens**.

**Book problem 3 (recomputed).** Central-zone diameter 3 mm (r_1 = 1.5 mm), λ = 6000 Å, source at a = 5 m. f = r_1²/λ = (1.5 × 10⁻³)²/(6 × 10⁻⁷) = 3.75 m. 1/b = 1/3.75 − 1/5 = 0.0667 m⁻¹, so b = 15 m, as the book says.

### 8. Core: multiple foci of a zone plate, and how it differs from a convex lens

*Objective:* Prove that a zone plate has foci at f, f/3, f/5, …, say why even fractions are dark, and write the zone plate versus convex lens comparison as asked in 2020 Q3(b).

## The question to settle
Module 7 found one bright point b = f = r_1²/λ. Is it the only one? Brij Lal says no: a zone plate has "multiple foci" (PDF pp. 418 to 419), because a plate zone may equal an **odd number** of half-period zones of P, not only one.

## Step 1: foci at f/(2m − 1)
Plane wave, plate radii r_n² = n r_1² with r_1² = f λ. Move the observation point nearer to the plate, to a distance b. Its half-period zones have radii ρ_j² = j b λ. Suppose the plate zone n holds (2m − 1) of them, that is r_n² = n (2m − 1) b λ. Then

$$ b_m = r_n²/[n (2m − 1) λ] = f/(2m − 1) $$

(eq. 17.10). For m = 1, 2, 3, 4 and f = 0.50 m the foci are 0.500, 0.1667, 0.100 and 0.0714 m.

## Step 2: why these are bright and others are not
At b_m each open plate zone contains an **odd** number (2m − 1) of half-period zones, whose alternating sum is about one m_1 (m_1 − m_2 + m_3 for three). Two successive open plate zones are separated by one blocked zone of (2m − 1) half-period zones, so their paths differ by (2m − 1)λ and they arrive in phase. All open zones therefore add, as at the main focus. If each plate zone held an **even** number, such as two (b = f/2), its alternating sum is about m_1 − m_2 ≈ 0 and the axis is dark. Hence the real foci are at f, f/3, f/5, … (odd only), as the book states (PDF p. 419). A numerical sum over 10 open zones (python) confirms bright peaks at f/b = 1, 3, 5, 7 and a null at f/b = 2.

## Step 3: brightness of the other foci
Brij Lal says the image gets fainter at the shorter foci (PDF p. 419): at f/3 the first zone contributes (m_1 + m_3)/2, a little less than m_1 because m_3 < m_1. In the ideal equal-amplitude model the **axial peak** at f/3 equals that at f, but it is narrower and the share of the incident power focused there is about 1/9 of the main focus (standard; verify). Write "fainter" in an answer.

## Step 4: zone plate versus convex lens (PDF pp. 419 to 420)
| Feature | Zone plate | Convex lens |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | diffraction by zones | refraction at surfaces |
| Foci | many real: f, f/3, f/5, …, plus virtual foci (b can be negative in 1/a + 1/b = 1/f) | one |
| Image type | real and virtual together | real (or virtual by position) |
| Wavelength | f ∝ 1/λ: violet longer than red | glass: violet shorter than red |
| Chromatic aberration | far more severe | smaller |
| Path through the image point | alternate zones differ by λ, phase 2π | all rays equal optical path |
| Brightness | much of the light goes elsewhere | all light to one point |
| Range | any wavelength for which the zones can be made (X-ray plates exist; verify) | limited by the lens material |

Chromatic row (python): r_1 = 0.6 mm gives f = 0.90 m at 400 nm and 0.514 m at 700 nm, a spread of 64 per cent of the 600 nm value; a crown-glass lens varies by a few per cent (standard; verify).

### 9. Master: the Fresnel number, Fresnel versus Fraunhofer diffraction, and the limits of the zone model

*Objective:* Define the Fresnel number as a zone count, use it to decide which regime applies, and write the Fresnel versus Fraunhofer comparison asked in 2023 Q4(c)(i).

## The Fresnel number is a zone count
In module 5 a hole of radius r exposes n = r²/(b λ) half-period zones to an axial point at distance b (plane wave). Rename r as a and b as L: the **Fresnel number** is

$$ N_F = a²/(λ L) $$

the number of half-period zones that the opening exposes at the observation point. It is dimensionless: a² and λL are both areas. This is the same quantity Day 17 lesson PH-057 (Circular aperture and Airy pattern, module on Fraunhofer versus Fresnel) quotes as the test of regime; the extract itself does not name it, so treat the name as standard (verify).

## What the number says
- **N_F much greater than 1** (many zones): the beam is close to its geometrical shape, with fine fringes at the edges. This is the regime of ordinary shadows.
- **N_F of order 1** (a few zones): the centre of the pattern switches between bright and dark as L changes (module 5). This is the heart of **Fresnel diffraction**.
- **N_F much less than 1** (a fraction of one zone): the pattern keeps its shape and only grows in proportion to L. This is **Fraunhofer diffraction**, with the Airy result of PH-057.

The boundaries are conventions, not sharp lines. Numbers (python): a = 0.5 mm and λ = 600 nm give N_F = 0.417 at L = 1 m and 0.0417 at L = 10 m; N_F falls to 0.01 only at L = 41.7 m. A lens changes the picture: with a lens behind the aperture the Fraunhofer pattern is formed in the lens's focal plane at a modest distance (PDF p. 421).

## Fresnel versus Fraunhofer diffraction (2023 Q4(c)(i), 5 marks)
The comparison below follows the extract (PDF p. 421) and adds the Fresnel number.

| Point | Fresnel diffraction | Fraunhofer diffraction |
|---|---|---|
| Source and screen | finite distances from the obstacle | effectively infinite distances |
| Incident wavefront | spherical or cylindrical | plane |
| Lens | not needed | needed (collimator and focusing lens) |
| Phase of wavelets across the opening | not the same | same |
| Treatment | experimentally simple, analysis complex | easily observed with two lenses, analysis simple |
| Fresnel number | about 1 or larger | much less than 1 |
| Examples | circular aperture on axis, disc, zone plate | single slit, double slit, grating, Airy pattern |

## The limits of the zone model
Fresnel's zone method treats each zone's wavelet as a scalar with a chosen obliquity factor and uses only signs. It is rigorous enough for **points on the axis** of a circular system, which is why this lesson counts zones only on the axis. Off-axis it needs either the shifted pole of module 5 or integrals (Cornu's spiral treats the straight edge, outside this clause). In the later Kirchhoff theory (standard; verify) the obliquity factor (1 + cos θ)/2 follows from the wave equation, and each secondary wave leads the primary by a quarter of a period; the quarter-period shift changes the phase of the resultant, not the intensities used here.

### Notes: Definitions

- Wavefront: the surface joining all points the wave has reached at the same instant, vibrating in step.
- Pole of a wavefront with respect to P: the point of the wavefront nearest to P, the foot of the perpendicular from P.
- Half-period zone: a ring of the wavefront between the circles whose points are at distances b + (n - 1) lambda/2 and b + n lambda/2 from P; light from corresponding points of neighbouring zones reaches P half a period apart.
- Obliquity factor K(theta) = (1 + cos theta)/2: the dependence of a wavelet's effect on the angle theta from the forward normal; K = 1 ahead, 0.5 along the wavefront, 0 backward.
- Fresnel number N_F = a^2/(lambda L): the number of half-period zones that an opening of radius a exposes at distance L (plane wave).
- Zone plate: a screen that blocks every alternate half-period zone, so the open zones all add at the focus.
- Poisson (Arago) spot: the bright point at the centre of the geometrical shadow of a round opaque disc.
- Rectilinear propagation: light travelling in straight lines, an approximation that holds when the obstacle is much larger than the first zone radius sqrt(b lambda).

### Notes: Equations to know

- r_n^2 = n b lambda + n^2 lambda^2/4 (exact, plane wave); r_n = sqrt(n b lambda) when lambda is much smaller than b.
- Zone area = pi[b lambda + (2n - 1) lambda^2/4], about pi b lambda for every n.
- Spherical wave from a point source at distance a: r_n = sqrt(n lambda a b/(a + b)); area = pi a b lambda/(a + b) [1 + (2n - 1) lambda/(4b)].
- Whole wavefront: A = m_1/2, I = m_1^2/4.
- Circular aperture radius r: n = (r^2/lambda)(1/a + 1/b), and n = r^2/(b lambda) for a plane wave. Odd n bright, even n dark; ideal I/I_0 = 4 sin^2(pi n/2).
- Opaque disc covering k zones: A = (-1)^k m_(k+1)/2 (intensity m_(k+1)^2/4, always bright).
- Zone plate: f = r_n^2/(n lambda) = r_1^2/lambda; r_1 = sqrt(f lambda); r_n = sqrt(n) r_1; 1/a + 1/b = 1/f; f_m = f/(2m - 1).
- Fresnel number N_F = a^2/(lambda L).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton 1: equal zone areas (15 marks, 2017)

- Assumptions: plane wave, lambda much smaller than b, P on the axis at distance b from the pole O.
- Spheres about P of radii b + n lambda/2 cut the wavefront in circles of radii r_n (figure of module 3).
- Pythagoras in the right triangle O M_n P: r_n^2 = (b + n lambda/2)^2 - b^2 = n b lambda + n^2 lambda^2/4.
- Area of zone n = pi (r_n^2 - r_(n-1)^2) = pi [b lambda + (2n - 1) lambda^2/4].
- Drop the lambda^2 term (relative size (2n - 1) lambda/(4 b)): area = pi b lambda for every n.
- Check: units length x length = area; the same result follows from the spherical-wave cap integral with a tending to infinity.
- Number: first zone of a plate with f = 50 cm and 500 nm: r_1 = sqrt(f lambda) = 0.50 mm.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton 2: amplitude of the whole wavefront

- Signs: odd zones positive, even zones negative (phase difference pi).
- Series A = m_1 - m_2 + m_3 - m_4 + ...
- Regroup: A = m_1/2 + (m_1/2 - m_2 + m_3/2) + (m_3/2 - m_4 + m_5/2) + ...; each bracket is (1/2)(m_(n-1) - 2 m_n + m_(n+1)) = 0 for slowly varying m_n.
- Last term tends to zero for infinitely many zones, so A = m_1/2 and I = I_1/4.
- Second proof: A = m_1 - A' with A' nearly A, so 2A = m_1.
- Meaning: the unobstructed wave has one quarter of the first-zone intensity; the first zone alone gives four times the free-space intensity.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Equal areas versus equal amplitudes: the zone areas are equal, but the amplitudes m_n fall slowly because of obliquity and distance.
- Half the first zone's amplitude versus half its area: the whole wavefront gives half the amplitude; it does not mean an obstacle of half the area blocks everything.
- Zone radius versus zone plate focal length: r_n = sqrt(n b lambda) uses the observation distance b; for a parallel beam on a plate b is the focal length f.
- Bright versus dark centre: odd number of exposed zones means bright, even means dark, for a hole; for a disc the centre is always bright.
- Zone plate versus lens: a plate's focal length is longer for violet; a lens's is shorter for violet. A plate has many foci; a lens has one.
- Fresnel versus Fraunhofer diffraction: Fresnel has a finite-distance curved wavefront and large or moderate Fresnel number; Fraunhofer has plane waves and a Fresnel number much less than 1 (PH-057).

### Core concepts

- A wavefront can be cut into half-period zones about the pole; neighbouring zones send light to P half a period apart and cancel.
- The zone radii go as sqrt(n) and the zone areas are equal (pi b lambda), so the zone amplitudes differ only slowly.
- The unobstructed wavefront gives amplitude m_1/2 (intensity one quarter of the first zone), which explains rectilinear propagation.
- A circular hole shows a bright centre for an odd number of open zones and a dark centre for an even number; a round disc always shows a bright centre (Poisson spot).
- A zone plate blocks alternate zones, focuses at f = r_1^2/lambda, has foci f/(2m - 1), and differs from a lens in its chromatic behaviour and number of foci.

**A.** f = 0.50 m; outer radius 3.16 mm; foci 0.50, 0.167, 0.100, 0.0714 ... m; ideal gain 1600

**Q.** A hole of radius 0.5 mm is used with 600 nm light. Find the Fresnel number at L = 1 m and at L = 10 m, and the distance for N_F = 0.01.

**A.** 0.417, 0.0417 and 41.7 m

**Q.** A zone plate with r_1 = 0.6 mm is used with white light of 400 nm to 700 nm. Find the focal length at each end and the spread as a fraction of the 600 nm focal length.

**A.** 0.900 m and 0.514 m; spread about 64 per cent

**Q.** Write r_n, the area of each zone and the amplitude of the whole wavefront relative to the first zone.

**A.** r_n = sqrt(n b lambda); area = pi b lambda; amplitude = m_1/2 (intensity one quarter).

**Q.** State the zone plate focal length, the other real foci and one difference from a convex lens.

**A.** f = r_1^2/lambda; foci f/(2m - 1); focal length is longer for violet (opposite to a glass lens); also many foci against one.

**Q.** A hole shows a dark centre: how many zones are exposed, and what does a disc of the same size show at its centre?

**A.** An even number of zones; the disc always shows a bright centre (Poisson spot).

**Q.** Define the Fresnel number and say which regime N_F much less than 1 describes.

**A.** N_F = a^2/(lambda L), the number of exposed half-period zones; N_F much less than 1 is the Fraunhofer regime (Day 17 lesson PH-057).

### Memory skeleton

- Huygens-Fresnel: wavelets from the wavefront, obliquity factor K = (1 + cos θ)/2
- Half-period zones about the pole: neighbours reach P half a period apart
- Radii r_n = √(nbλ), equal areas πbλ (2017 derivation)
- Whole wavefront A = m₁/2: rectilinear propagation
- Circular hole: odd number of zones bright, even dark; opaque disc: always a bright centre
- Zone plate: f = r₁²/λ, 1/a + 1/b = 1/f, foci f, f/3, f/5
- Zone plate versus convex lens; Fresnel versus Fraunhofer
