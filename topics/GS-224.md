## GS-224: Fiscal versus monetary policy

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Basic macro and monetary

*Goal:* Explain Fiscal versus monetary policy and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Fiscal policy and the multiplier

Through its budget, the government can raise demand by buying more goods and services or by cutting taxes and raising transfers, which lift disposable income and consumption. NCERT shows that an increase in government purchases shifts aggregate demand upward; firms respond by producing more, and the multiplier spreads the initial spending into a larger rise in income. A lump-sum tax works in the opposite direction by lowering disposable income. These levers give fiscal policy its stabilisation role: expanding demand when resources are idle and restraining it when demand outruns capacity. The cost is a deficit, which must be financed by borrowing and adds to public debt and future interest payments.

### Monetary levers

Monetary policy belongs to the central bank. NCERT lists the quantitative instruments: varying the cash reserve ratio, which changes how much banks can lend; the bank rate at which the RBI lends to banks; and open market operations, either outright or through repo and reverse repo agreements, which add or drain reserves. Qualitative measures such as moral suasion and margin requirements steer the direction of credit. In 2025 the Economic Survey shows these tools used together: the MPC cut the repo rate by 100 bps to 5.25% and the RBI cut CRR to 3.0%, while OMO purchases kept liquidity in surplus so that lower policy rates reached lending rates.

### Where the two meet: deficits, inflation and crowding out

NCERT presents the debate on deficits in balanced terms. Critics say deficits are inflationary because extra demand meets fixed output; but when there is slack, higher demand raises production rather than prices. Borrowing may crowd out private investment by absorbing savings, yet if output rises, savings rise too, and infrastructure spending can benefit future generations if its return exceeds the interest rate. Ricardian equivalence argues forward-looking households save more to offset government dissaving. Finally, the deficit itself responds to the economy: tax revenue falls in recessions, widening the deficit without any policy change. For Mains, combine these points to judge whether fiscal and monetary stances reinforce or offset each other.
