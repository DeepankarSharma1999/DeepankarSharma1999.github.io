## GS-180: Money Bills, Finance Bills and budget process

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Union government

### Bridge: Money vocabulary: receipts, expenditure and the three deficits

A government is like a very large household with a very large accounting book. Money coming in is called **receipts**; money going out is called **expenditure**. Both are split into two kinds.

- **Revenue receipts** are the regular, non-borrowed earnings, mainly tax revenue (income tax, GST and so on) and non-tax revenue (for example fees, dividends and interest the government earns). They do not create a liability for the government.
- **Capital receipts** are money that changes the government's balance sheet: loans it raises (for example by issuing treasury bills or bonds) money it recovers when earlier loans are repaid to it, and non-debt items such as disinvestment (sale of government shares in public enterprises). Laxmikanth's description of the Consolidated Fund lists exactly these: revenues received, loans raised, and repayments of loans to the government.
- **Revenue expenditure** is day-to-day running cost: salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies. It creates no lasting asset.
- **Capital expenditure** builds assets or reduces liabilities: roads, dams, defence equipment, repaying a loan.

Now the three **deficits**, which Mains GS III asks about and which Prelims uses as vocabulary. These definitions are standard economics rather than text from the Laxmikanth extract; treat them as vocabulary and verify against an economics source when you reach the economy topics.

1. **Revenue deficit** = revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts. If the government earns 70 in revenue receipts but spends 85 on running costs, the revenue deficit is 15.
2. **Fiscal deficit** = total expenditure minus total receipts *excluding borrowings*. It is the amount the government must borrow in the year. With expenditure 100 and non-borrowing receipts 80, the fiscal deficit is 20.
3. **Primary deficit** = fiscal deficit minus interest payments. If interest payments are 15, the primary deficit is 20 - 15 = 5. It shows how much borrowing is needed for things other than paying interest on old debt.

You do not need to analyse deficits in this lesson. You only need the words, because the budget documents in module 5 use them.

### Bridge: Appropriation, grant, demand, charged and voted: the five money words

Five small words carry most of this topic. Learn them before the Articles.

**Financial year.** The government's accounting year. For the Government of India it runs from 1 April to 31 March of the next calendar year (Laxmikanth, p. 538). Everything in the budget is estimated for one financial year at a time.

**Consolidated Fund of India (CFI).** The main government account into which taxes, borrowings and loan repayments flow and out of which almost all government payments are made. Module 2 explains it fully.

**Appropriation.** To *appropriate* money means to set it aside by law for a stated purpose and authorise the government to spend it. The Constitution's rule is that no money can be taken out of the Consolidated Fund except under an appropriation made by law. Think of it as a permission slip signed by Parliament: without it, the government's money sits in the fund and cannot be drawn.

**Demand and grant.** A **demand for grant** is the request a ministry makes, through the government, to the Lok Sabha for money to carry out its work. Demands are presented ministry by ministry. When the Lok Sabha votes a demand, it becomes a **grant**. A demand is a request; a grant is the approved amount.

**Charged versus voted.** Some items of expenditure are **charged** on the Consolidated Fund: they are fixed by the Constitution or by law, and Parliament may discuss them but does not vote on them. The rest is **voted**: it appears as demands that the Lok Sabha must pass. Charged expenditure protects institutions whose independence should not depend on a yearly vote, such as judges or the Comptroller and Auditor General.

A short worked example: a ministry wants 1,000 crore for a scheme. It appears as a demand. The Lok Sabha votes it, so it becomes a grant. An Appropriation Bill then authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund. Only after that law is in force can the money be paid out. You will see this chain again in modules 6 and 7.

### 1. Why Parliament holds the purse: the money map of this lesson

*Objective:* State why a democracy makes the legislature control taxing and spending, and name the chain of steps that turns a budget into legal spending.

Governments need money for salaries, roads, defence and welfare. They get it from taxes, fees and borrowing. In a democracy the people's representatives, not the ministers alone, decide whether the government may take that money and how it may spend it. NCERT's chapter on the legislature puts this as the financial function of Parliament: if the Government of India wants to bring in a new tax it must get the Lok Sabha's approval, and it must account to the legislature for what it has spent and plans to raise. The legislature, NCERT says, also checks that the government does not misspend or overspend, and does this through the budget and the annual financial statements.

Two constitutional rules sit under everything else in this lesson. Both are listed by Laxmikanth among the Constitution's provisions on the budget:

- **No tax may be levied or collected except by authority of law** (Article 265). The executive cannot simply announce a tax.
- **No money may be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund of India except under appropriation made by law.** The executive cannot simply spend.

So taxing needs a law, and spending needs a law. The first law is the **Finance Bill / Finance Act**. The second is the **Appropriation Bill / Appropriation Act**. The budget process is the road that leads to those two laws.

6. How UPSC tests all of this (modules 9 and 10).

### 2. Three funds: Consolidated Fund, Public Account and Contingency Fund

*Objective:* Distinguish the Consolidated Fund, the Public Account and the Contingency Fund by what flows in, who can authorise payments, and which Article creates each.

The Constitution gives the Union government three separate accounts, a little like a household keeping salary money, other people's deposits, and an emergency tin in different places. Laxmikanth (pp. 546-547) lists them with their Articles.

| Fund | Article | What goes in | Who authorises payment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consolidated Fund of India | 266 | All revenues; loans raised (treasury bills, loans, ways and means advances); repayments of loans to the government | Parliament, by law (appropriation) |
| Public Account of India | 266 | All other public money received by or on behalf of the government: provident fund deposits, judicial deposits, savings bank deposits, departmental deposits, remittances | The executive, by its own action |
| Contingency Fund of India | 267 | Sums fixed by law and paid in from time to time | The executive (the President), by advance, pending Parliament's later approval |

**Consolidated Fund of India.** This is the main account. All legally authorised payments on behalf of the government are made from it, and nothing can be issued from it except in accordance with a law of Parliament. That is why the Appropriation Bill exists. Note carefully what goes into it: not only taxes but also borrowed money. A common slip is to say loans are credited to the Public Account; they are not.

**Public Account of India.** This is money the government holds but that is not its own income: for example, provident fund deposits. Because the government is more like a banker here, payments are made by executive action without a fresh Appropriation Act. Laxmikanth says such payments are mostly banking transactions.

**Contingency Fund of India.** The Constitution authorised Parliament to create this fund, and Parliament did so by the Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950. It is placed at the President's disposal, and the Finance Secretary holds it on the President's behalf. The President can make advances from it to meet *unforeseen* expenditure before Parliament has authorised it. Like the Public Account, it is operated by executive action. The advance is later brought before Parliament for approval and the fund is replenished; that last step is standard practice rather than a point in the extract, so treat it as supporting detail.

**Why three accounts?** The separation makes Parliament's control sharp where it matters. Money that belongs to the government's own budget needs a law before it is spent. Money held in trust, and small urgent advances, do not need the full budget machinery, so they are operated by the executive.

**Exam hook.** The 2018 Prelims question on Money Bills (PYQ upsc-2018-prelims-gs-i-q31) uses these funds in its options: a Money Bill can deal with *custody* of the Consolidated Fund or the Contingency Fund, but *appropriation* is out of the Consolidated Fund only. The distinction between the two funds is the heart of that question.

**Article numbers often mixed up.** Remember 266 (two funds) and 267 (one fund); 265 is the tax rule; 112 is the annual financial statement. Rehearse them as a set: 265 tax, 266 funds, 267 contingency.

### 3. Money Bills in detail: Article 110, Article 109 and the Rajya Sabha

*Objective:* Apply the 'only' test of Article 110 to a Bill, and state exactly what the Rajya Sabha, the Lok Sabha, the Speaker and the President can do with a Money Bill.

GS-179 gave the outline of a Money Bill. Here we make the definition usable.

**The test is the word 'only'.** Under Article 110 a Bill is deemed a Money Bill if it contains *only* provisions on one or more of these matters (Laxmikanth, p. 531):

1. imposing, abolishing, remitting, altering or regulating any tax;
2. regulating the government's borrowing (Laxmikanth's wording; the constitutional text also covers guarantees and financial obligations, which UPSC used in option (d) of the 2018 question: standard text, verify at legislative.gov.in);
3. custody of the Consolidated Fund or the Contingency Fund, and payments into or out of them;
4. appropriation of money out of the Consolidated Fund;
5. declaring any expenditure to be charged on the Consolidated Fund, or increasing such expenditure;
6. receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or the Public Account, its custody or issue, or the audit of accounts of the Union or a State;
7. any matter incidental to the above.

**What does not make a Bill a Money Bill.** A Bill is not a Money Bill merely because it provides for fines or other money penalties, for fees for licences or services rendered, or for taxes imposed by a local authority for local purposes. A Bill that imposes a tax *and* also changes some general law is not a Money Bill, because it has more than the listed matters.

**Worked sorting.** (a) A Bill raising the rate of a Union tax: only a tax matter, so a Money Bill. (b) A Bill that creates a new regulator and also contains a borrowing clause: general legislation plus borrowing, so not a Money Bill (it is a Financial Bill I; see module 4). (c) A Bill that fixes penalty amounts for traffic offences: only fines, so not a Money Bill. (d) The Appropriation Bill: appropriation from the Consolidated Fund, so a Money Bill.

**The special procedure (Article 109).** NCERT reproduces Article 109(1): a Money Bill shall not be introduced in the Council of States. The path is:

- Introduced only in the Lok Sabha, only on the recommendation of the President, and only by a minister.
- Passed by the Lok Sabha, certified by the Speaker as a Money Bill, and sent to the Rajya Sabha.
- The Rajya Sabha may only *recommend*: it cannot reject or amend. It must return the Bill within **14 days**.
- The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all recommendations. If it accepts some, the Bill is deemed passed in that modified form; if it rejects them, or the Rajya Sabha is silent for 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed as the Lok Sabha originally passed it.
- No joint sitting is possible, because there is no disagreement to resolve.

**The Speaker's certificate.** The Speaker's decision on whether a Bill is a Money Bill is final, according to the Constitution as Laxmikanth reports it: Article 110(3) declares the Speaker's decision final; Rojer Mathew (2019) indicates it is not wholly beyond review (verify). Flag for later study: the Supreme Court has examined judicial review of this certification (Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank, 2019, and a larger-bench reference in the Aadhaar case). The current position is not in the extracts; verify before relying on it in Mains.

**At the President's desk.** The President may assent to a Money Bill or withhold assent, but cannot return it for reconsideration (Laxmikanth, p. 532). Because the Bill was introduced on the President's recommendation, assent is the norm. Defeat of a Money Bill in the Lok Sabha leads to the resignation of the government.

**Why this design?** NCERT gives the logic: the Rajya Sabha is elected by MLAs and not directly by the people, and in a democracy the directly elected House should control finances. The Council of Ministers is also responsible only to the Lok Sabha.

### 4. Financial Bills I and II (Article 117) and the full comparison

*Objective:* Define the three kinds of financial Bill and compare Money Bill, Financial Bill I and Financial Bill II on introduction, Rajya Sabha power, joint sitting and presidential options.

The Constitution uses 'financial Bill' as a technical term with three kinds (Laxmikanth, p. 532): the **Money Bill** (Article 110), the **Financial Bill I** (Article 117(1)) and the **Financial Bill II** (Article 117(3)). Every Money Bill is a financial Bill, but not every financial Bill is a Money Bill. Think of 'financial Bill' as the family and Money Bill as the strictest member.

**Financial Bill I (Article 117(1))** contains matters of Article 110 *plus other matters of general legislation*, for example a Bill with a borrowing clause that is not exclusively about borrowing. It is like a Money Bill in two respects: it can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and only on the President's recommendation. In all other respects it follows ordinary-Bill procedure. So the Rajya Sabha can amend or reject it, a joint sitting is possible if the Houses disagree, and the President may assent, withhold assent or return it.

**An important detail for amendments.** Not every amendment needs the President's recommendation: an amendment that makes provision for a matter of Article 110 needs it in either House, except an amendment that only reduces or abolishes a tax, which does not need it (verify against the Laxmikanth wording). This explains Laxmikanth's remark that Parliament may reduce or abolish a tax but cannot increase it on its own: the government alone takes the initiative to raise burdens.

**Financial Bill II (Article 117(3))** contains provisions involving expenditure from the Consolidated Fund but none of the Article 110 matters. It is treated as an ordinary Bill in all respects. Its one special feature is timing: neither House can *pass* it unless the President has recommended that the House consider it. It can be introduced in either House, and no recommendation is needed at the introduction stage; the recommendation is needed at the consideration stage. Either House can reject or amend it, a joint sitting is available, and the President has all three options.

| Point | Money Bill | Financial Bill I | Financial Bill II |
|---|---|---|---|
| Article | 110 (procedure 109) | 117(1) | 117(3) |
| What it contains | Only Article 110 matters | Article 110 matters plus general legislation | Expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, no Article 110 matter |
| Where introduced | Lok Sabha only | Lok Sabha only | Either House |
| President's recommendation | Needed for introduction | Needed for introduction | Not needed at introduction; needed at consideration |
| Introduced by | Minister only | As an ordinary Bill | As an ordinary Bill |
| Rajya Sabha power | Recommend only; return within 14 days | Amend or reject, like an ordinary Bill | Amend or reject, like an ordinary Bill |
| Speaker's certificate | Required | Not required | Not required |
| Joint sitting | Not available | Available | Available |
| President's options | Assent or withhold; cannot return | Assent, withhold or return | Assent, withhold or return |

**Money Bill versus Ordinary Bill.** Laxmikanth's Table 22.4 compares the two: an ordinary Bill may start in either House, may be introduced by a private member, needs no President's recommendation, can be amended or rejected by the Rajya Sabha, may be detained by it for up to six months, and goes to a joint sitting on deadlock. A Money Bill reverses each of these.

**How to remember.** Ask three questions about any finance Bill. *Does it contain only Article 110 matters?* If yes, Money Bill. *Does it contain Article 110 matters plus other things?* Financial Bill I. *Does it only spend from the Consolidated Fund?* Financial Bill II.

### 5. The budget as the Annual Financial Statement: Article 112, charged and voted

*Objective:* Explain what the Constitution means by the budget, list its contents and documents, and sort expenditure into charged and voted.

**The word 'budget' is not in the Constitution.** Article 112 calls it the *annual financial statement*: a statement of the estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for a financial year (1 April to 31 March). 'Budget' is its popular name (Laxmikanth, p. 538). The President is the constitutional actor: the President causes the statement to be laid before both Houses. In practice, the Finance Minister presents it with a speech in the Lok Sabha.

**What the budget contains.** Laxmikanth lists five elements: estimates of revenue and capital receipts; ways and means of raising revenue; estimates of expenditure; the actual receipts and expenditure of the closing year with reasons for any surplus or deficit; and the economic and financial policy for the coming year, including taxation proposals and new schemes. That is why the vocabulary in bridge module B1 matters.

**A short history.** Until 2017 India had two budgets: the Railway Budget and the General Budget. The railway budget had been separated in 1924 on the recommendations of the Acworth Committee (1921), to give railway finance flexibility and a business approach, and to secure an assured contribution to general revenues. In 2017 the two were merged, and there is now a single Union Budget.

**The budget documents.** Laxmikanth lists them: Budget Speech, Annual Financial Statement, Demands for Grants, Appropriation Bill, Finance Bill, the statements mandated under the FRBM Act (Macro-Economic Framework Statement, Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement, Medium Term Fiscal Policy Statement), Expenditure Budget, Receipts Budget, Expenditure Profile, a Memorandum explaining the Finance Bill, Budget at a Glance and Outcome Budget. The Economic Survey, prepared by the finance ministry, is now presented one day or a few days before the budget and reports the state of the economy.

**Constitutional provisions worth memorising** (Laxmikanth, p. 539): the President causes the statement to be laid; no demand for a grant except on the President's recommendation; no withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund without a law of appropriation; no tax without authority of law; the budget must show separately the expenditure *charged* on the Consolidated Fund and the expenditure *made* from it; and it must distinguish revenue-account expenditure from other expenditure.

**Charged expenditure** cannot be put to a vote, though Parliament may discuss it. Laxmikanth's list includes: the President's emoluments and office expenses; salaries and allowances of the presiding officers of both Houses; salaries, allowances and pensions of Supreme Court judges and pensions of High Court judges; salary, allowances and pension of the CAG; salaries, allowances and pensions of the UPSC chairman and members; administrative expenses of the Supreme Court, the CAG's office and the UPSC; the government's debt charges (interest, sinking fund and redemption charges); sums needed to satisfy any court or arbitral award; and anything else Parliament declares charged.

**Why charge these items?** They either protect independent institutions from yearly political bargaining or are legal obligations the government cannot sensibly refuse to pay, such as interest on debt. (This is an explanation of purpose, not a quotation from the extract.)

**Voted expenditure** is everything else: the ministries' demands for grants, which the Lok Sabha must pass.

**The Rajya Sabha at this stage.** The budget is laid before the Rajya Sabha at the end of the speech in the Lok Sabha. It may discuss it but cannot vote on the demands for grants.

### 6. The six stages, part 1: presentation, discussion, committees and voting on demands

*Objective:* Name the six stages of the budget in order, describe stages 1 to 4, and explain the three cut motions and the guillotine.

Laxmikanth (p. 541) says the budget goes through six stages in Parliament: **(1) presentation, (2) general discussion, (3) scrutiny by departmental committees, (4) voting on demands for grants, (5) passing of the Appropriation Bill, (6) passing of the Finance Bill.** This module covers 1 to 4; module 7 covers 5 and 6.

**Stage 1: Presentation.** The Finance Minister presents the budget in the Lok Sabha with the budget speech. Conventionally this was the last working day of February; since 2017 it has been advanced to 1 February. The budget can be presented in two or more parts, each treated as the budget. There is no discussion on the day of presentation. At the end of the speech the budget is laid before the Rajya Sabha, which can only discuss it and has no power to vote on demands for grants.

**Stage 2: General discussion.** It begins a few days after presentation, takes place in both Houses and usually lasts three to four days. The Lok Sabha may discuss the budget as a whole or any question of principle in it, but **no cut motion can be moved and the budget is not put to the vote**. The Finance Minister has a general right of reply.

**Stage 3: Scrutiny by departmental committees.** The Houses then adjourn for about three to four weeks. In this gap the 24 departmental standing committees examine the demands for grants of their ministries in detail and report to both Houses. Laxmikanth notes that the standing committee system, established in 1993 and expanded in 2004, makes control over ministries detailed and in-depth. (NCERT dates the growth of committees differently; both accounts appear in GS-179.)

**Stage 4: Voting on demands for grants.** Informed by the committee reports, the Lok Sabha votes the demands, which are presented ministry-wise. A demand becomes a grant after it is voted. Two points: voting is the **exclusive privilege of the Lok Sabha**, and it is confined to the **votable part**, since charged expenditure is not put to the vote. Each demand is voted separately.

**Cut motions.** During this stage members may move motions to reduce a demand. They are of three kinds:

| Cut motion | What it says | What it expresses |
|---|---|---|
| Policy cut | Reduce the demand to Re 1 | Disapproval of the policy behind the demand; members may suggest an alternative policy |
| Economy cut | Reduce the demand by a stated amount (a lump sum, or omit or reduce an item) | The economy that could be made in the proposed expenditure |
| Token cut | Reduce the demand by Rs 100 | Ventilates a specific grievance within the Government of India's responsibility |

To be admissible a cut motion must, among other things, relate to one demand only, be clear and confined to one specific matter, not suggest amending or repealing existing laws, not concern a matter that is not primarily the Union's concern, not relate to charged expenditure, not relate to a matter under adjudication by a court, and not raise a question of privilege. Laxmikanth lists thirteen conditions; learn the ones that matter, especially *no cut motion on charged expenditure*.

**Why bother with cut motions?** They start concentrated discussion on a specific demand and uphold responsible government by probing the government's activities. In practice they are moved and discussed but not passed, since the government has a majority. If one were passed, it would show want of confidence in the government and could lead to its resignation.

**The guillotine.** On the last day of the days allotted for discussing and voting on demands, the Speaker puts all remaining demands to the vote together and disposes of them, whether or not they have been discussed. This is the guillotine. Many demands are therefore passed without any discussion at all.

### 7. The six stages, part 2: Appropriation Bill, Finance Bill and the vote on account

*Objective:* Explain what the Appropriation Bill and the Finance Bill each authorise, why a vote on account is needed, and the rules on amendment and timing.

**Stage 5: The Appropriation Bill (Article 114).** Once demands are voted, the government needs legal authority to take the money out of the Consolidated Fund. The Constitution says no money can be withdrawn from it except under an appropriation made by law. So an **Appropriation Bill** is introduced to appropriate out of the Consolidated Fund the money needed for (a) the grants voted by the Lok Sabha and (b) the expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund. Because it deals with appropriation from the Consolidated Fund, it is a Money Bill and follows the special procedure of module 3.

A key restriction: **no amendment can be moved to the Appropriation Bill, in either House, that would vary the amount or alter the destination of any voted grant, or vary the amount of any charged expenditure.** The grants were fixed in stage 4; stage 5 only gives them legal form. Once the President assents, the Appropriation Bill becomes the Appropriation Act, and that Act authorises (legalises) payments from the Consolidated Fund. Until it is enacted, the government cannot draw the money.

**The vote on account (Article 116).** The Appropriation Act usually takes until the end of April, but the financial year starts on 1 April. To avoid a gap, the Constitution authorises the Lok Sabha to make an **advance grant** for part of the year, pending the voting of demands and the Appropriation Act. This is the vote on account. It is passed after the general discussion on the budget. It is generally for two months, for an amount equal to one-sixth of the total estimate (Laxmikanth, p. 545). Since presentation moved to 1 February (2017), the budget is generally passed before 1 April, so a vote on account is now mainly used with interim budgets in election years (verify). Flag: in a general-election year, governments have commonly presented an interim budget and taken a vote on account for a longer period before the new government presents the full budget. That practice is not in the extract; verify the details before using it in an answer.

**Stage 6: The Finance Bill.** The **Finance Bill** gives effect to the government's financial proposals for the coming year, that is, its tax proposals. Laxmikanth says it is subject to all the conditions applicable to a Money Bill (so the Rajya Sabha's role is as limited as module 3 describes). One contrast with the Appropriation Bill is worth memorising: **amendments seeking to reject or reduce a tax can be moved on the Finance Bill**, but no amendment can alter the amount or destination of a grant in the Appropriation Bill.

According to the **Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931**, the Finance Bill must be enacted within **75 days**. The reason, in plain words, is Article 265: a tax cannot be collected without law, yet the tax changes cannot wait for Parliament to finish, so the Act allows provisional collection for a limited time. This explanation of purpose is mine; the 75-day figure is from Laxmikanth.

**The income side and the spending side.**

| Point | Appropriation Bill / Act | Finance Bill / Act |
|---|---|---|
| Stage | 5 | 6 |
| Purpose | Authorise payments from the Consolidated Fund (voted grants and charged items) | Give effect to the tax and income proposals |
| Side of the budget | Expenditure | Income (receipts) |
| Amendments | None that vary a grant's amount or destination | Amendments to reject or reduce a tax can be moved |
| Timing note | Delay before enactment bridged by a vote on account | Must be enacted within 75 days |

The Finance Act legalises the income side of the budget and completes the enactment of the budget.

**One more bridge: Article 265.** The Finance Act is the legal authority that Article 265 demands. Together, the Appropriation Act (spending) and the Finance Act (taxing) are the two laws the whole process exists to produce, as you saw in module 1.

### 8. Other grants, and how Parliament checks spending after the money is gone

*Objective:* Distinguish supplementary, additional, excess, exceptional and token grants and the vote of credit, and outline the committees and bodies that scrutinise spending.

The regular budget cannot foresee everything. Laxmikanth (pp. 545-546) lists special grants made in extraordinary or special circumstances. Learn each by the *situation* it answers.

| Grant | Situation | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| Supplementary grant | The amount authorised by the Appropriation Act for a service in the current year is insufficient | Top-up for something already funded |
| Additional grant | A need has arisen in the current year for expenditure on a *new service* not contemplated in the budget | New item, mid-year |
| Excess grant | Money has been spent on a service in excess of the amount granted | Voted by the Lok Sabha after the financial year; first approved by the Public Accounts Committee |
| Vote of credit | An unexpected demand upon India's resources whose magnitude or indefinite character prevents stating details | Like a blank cheque to the executive |
| Exceptional grant | For a special purpose that forms no part of the current service of any financial year | Special one-off purpose |
| Token grant | A new service can be funded by *reappropriation* (transfer of funds from one head to another) | Re 1 token demand is voted; no additional expenditure |

Supplementary, additional, excess and exceptional grants and the vote of credit follow the same procedure as the regular budget. Standard knowledge adds that supplementary, additional and excess grants fall under Article 115, and votes on account, votes of credit and exceptional grants under Article 116; verify at legislative.gov.in.

**Trap to avoid.** *Vote on account* (advance for part of the year, before the budget is finally passed) and *vote of credit* (blank cheque for an unexpected, undefined demand) sound alike but answer completely different needs.

**Checking after spending.** Passing the budget is the first half of financial control. The second half asks: was the money spent as authorised?

- The **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** audits government accounts. NCERT says the Lok Sabha can enquire into misuse of funds on the basis of the CAG's report and the Public Accounts Committee. The CAG's salary is charged on the Consolidated Fund, which protects the office's independence.
- The **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** of Parliament examines the CAG's audit reports and the accounts of the government, and it must approve demands for excess grants before they go to the Lok Sabha. (Membership figures, commonly stated as 22 members drawn from both Houses, are standard knowledge outside the extracts; verify.)
- The **Estimates Committee**, a committee drawn from the Lok Sabha alone in standard accounts, examines the estimates and suggests economies. (Standard knowledge; verify.)
- The **departmental standing committees** of stage 3 scrutinise demands before voting. Their system makes financial control over ministries more detailed.

**Link to Finance Commission (GS-193).** The budget decides what the Union spends. The Finance Commission, under Article 280, recommends how tax revenues are shared between the Union and the States and how grants-in-aid are given. That is a different part of the money map. This lesson stays with Parliament's own procedure; GS-193 covers the Commission.

**Why the Lok Sabha is the money House.** NCERT sums up the logic: the Rajya Sabha can criticise the government but cannot remove it, cannot initiate, amend or reject Money Bills, and cannot vote the demands for grants, because in a democracy the directly elected representatives should control finances.

### 9. How UPSC tests this topic: patterns and previous-year questions

*Objective:* Recognise the four question patterns UPSC uses on money Bills and the budget, and apply elimination to the verified questions without relying on an answer key.

UPSC's six verified questions on this topic fall into four patterns. For each question below, the reasoning is *analysis from this lesson, not an official answer key*.

**The four patterns in brief.** (1) Definition with a slipped word (2018 Prelims Q31: Consolidated Fund versus Contingency Fund in Article 110). (2) Which Article or Act requires this (2020 Prelims Q6: the FRBM Act). (3) Money Bill versus Finance Bill (2023 Prelims Q37; 2024 Prelims Q68: the Rajya Sabha can suggest, not reject, and need not be obeyed). (4) Who does what in the budget (2024 Prelims Q85: the President causes the statement to be laid; the demand-for-grant recommendation). The full reasoning for each question is in Exam intelligence: PYQ notes and in Master notes: 'UPSC pattern analysis (relocated from module 9)'.

**Mains: 2017 GS II Q13** asks for occasions when a joint session happens and when it cannot, with reasons. - **Introduction (1-2 lines).** Federal second chamber, yet the Constitution gives the Lok Sabha primacy in finance because it is directly elected and the government is responsible to it.
- **Body, four points.** (1) Money Bills cannot be introduced in the Rajya Sabha; it can only recommend within 14 days and the Lok Sabha may reject all recommendations. (2) It does not vote on demands for grants; the budget is only laid before it for discussion. ### Notes: Definitions for the money chapter

- Financial year: 1 April to 31 March; the budget estimates one financial year at a time.
- Annual financial statement: the Constitution's term (Article 112) for the budget, a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for a financial year.
- Demand for grant: a ministry's request for money, put to the Lok Sabha; it becomes a grant once voted.
- Appropriation: authorisation by law to withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund for specified purposes.
- Charged expenditure: expenditure placed on the Consolidated Fund that Parliament may discuss but does not vote.
- Voted expenditure: expenditure presented as demands for grants and put to the Lok Sabha's vote.
- Money Bill: a Bill containing only Article 110 matters, certified by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
- Financial Bill: a technical term covering three kinds: Money Bill (Article 110), Financial Bill I (Article 117(1)) and Financial Bill II (Article 117(3)).
- Guillotine: the Speaker's putting of all undiscussed demands to the vote on the last allotted day.
- Revenue deficit, fiscal deficit, primary deficit: revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts; total expenditure minus non-borrowing receipts; fiscal deficit minus interest payments (standard economics vocabulary, verify against the economy topics).

### Notes: Articles at a glance

- Article 109: special procedure for Money Bills; a Money Bill shall not be introduced in the Council of States (NCERT reproduces 109(1)).
- Article 110: definition of Money Bill; Speaker's decision on whether a Bill is a Money Bill is final (as stated in the textbook; see the verification note on judicial review).
- Article 111: assent of the President (details in GS-179; the President cannot return a Money Bill).
- Article 112: annual financial statement, laid before both Houses by the President's direction; shows charged expenditure separately.
- Article 113 (standard text, verify): procedure in Parliament on estimates; charged items are not voted; demands for grants go to the Lok Sabha; no demand except on the President's recommendation.
- Article 114 (standard text, verify): Appropriation Bills; no money from the Consolidated Fund except under appropriation made by law; no amendment varying a voted grant's amount or destination.
- Article 115 (standard text, verify): supplementary, additional or excess grants.
- Article 116 (standard text, verify): votes on account, votes of credit and exceptional grants.
- Article 117: special provisions for financial Bills (I) under 117(1) and (II) under 117(3).
- Article 265: no tax levied or collected except by authority of law.
- Article 266: Consolidated Fund of India and Public Account of India.
- Article 267: Contingency Fund of India.

### Notes: Money Bill versus Financial Bill I versus Financial Bill II

- All Money Bills are financial Bills; not all financial Bills are Money Bills.
- Only the Money Bill removes the Rajya Sabha's power to amend or reject and excludes a joint sitting.

### Notes: The six stages of the budget in Parliament

- 1. Presentation: Finance Minister's budget speech in the Lok Sabha (1 February since 2017); budget laid before the Rajya Sabha; no discussion on the day.
- 2. General discussion: both Houses, usually three to four days; no cut motion and no vote; Finance Minister replies.
- 3. Scrutiny by departmental committees: Houses adjourn for about three to four weeks; the 24 departmental standing committees report on demands for grants.
- 4. Voting on demands for grants: Lok Sabha only; votable part only; each demand separately; cut motions; guillotine.
- 5. Appropriation Bill: authorises payments from the Consolidated Fund for voted grants and charged expenditure; no amendment varying a grant.
- 6. Finance Bill: gives effect to tax proposals; amendments to reject or reduce a tax can be moved; enactment within 75 days under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931.

### Notes: Charged expenditure on the Consolidated Fund (Laxmikanth's list)

- Emoluments and allowances of the President and other expenditure relating to the office.
- Salaries and allowances of the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
- Salaries, allowances and pensions of Supreme Court judges; pensions of High Court judges.
- Salary, allowances and pension of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India.
- Salaries, allowances and pensions of the chairman and members of the UPSC.
- Administrative expenses of the Supreme Court, the CAG's office and the UPSC, including salaries, allowances and pensions of staff.
- Debt charges of the Government of India: interest, sinking fund and redemption charges, and other expenditure on raising loans and servicing or redeeming debt.
- Any sum required to satisfy a judgment, decree or award of a court or arbitral tribunal.
- Any other expenditure declared by Parliament to be so charged.

### Notes: The three funds of the Union

- Consolidated Fund: all revenues, loans raised and loan repayments; payments only under an appropriation law.
- Public Account and Contingency Fund: operated by executive action.

### Notes: Special grants and the vote on account

- Vote on account is an advance for part of the year; vote of credit is a blank cheque for an unforeseeable demand.
- Excess grants are voted after the year and need prior Public Accounts Committee approval.

### Notes: Cut motions

- Policy cut motion: demand to be reduced to Re 1; disapproves of the policy; an alternative policy may be advocated.
- Economy cut motion: demand to be reduced by a specified amount (a lump sum, or omission or reduction of an item).
- Token cut motion: demand to be reduced by Rs 100; ventilates a specific grievance.
- Admissibility highlights: one demand only; confined to one matter; no suggestion to amend or repeal existing law; no charged expenditure; no matter under court adjudication.
- Practical status: moved and discussed but not passed while the government has a majority; passage would mean want of confidence.

### Notes: Why the Rajya Sabha is limited in money matters

- Cause: the Rajya Sabha is indirectly elected by MLAs, and the Council of Ministers answers to the Lok Sabha only.
- Effect: it cannot initiate, amend or reject Money Bills (it recommends within 14 days).
- Effect: it cannot vote on demands for grants; the budget is laid before it for discussion.
- Counterweight: it holds equal powers on ordinary Bills and constitutional amendments and fuller powers on Financial Bills I and II, which are subject to a joint sitting on deadlock.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Vote on account (advance for part of the year) versus vote of credit (blank cheque for an unforeseeable demand).
- Appropriation Act (spending side) versus Finance Act (tax side).
- Money Bill (Article 110, Speaker certifies) versus Financial Bill (Article 117; two kinds).
- Consolidated Fund (Parliament's law needed) versus Public Account and Contingency Fund (executive action).
- Article 266 (two funds) versus Article 267 (Contingency Fund) versus Article 265 (tax needs law).
- Demand for grant (request) versus grant (voted amount).
- Charged (discussed, not voted) versus voted (put to the Lok Sabha's vote).

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- A Bill is not a Money Bill merely because it imposes fines or fees or local-purpose taxes.
- No joint sitting on a Money Bill or a Constitution Amendment Bill.
- No president's recommendation is needed to move an amendment that reduces or abolishes a tax.
- No cut motion lies against charged expenditure and none is allowed during the general discussion.
- Defeat of a Money Bill in the Lok Sabha leads to the resignation of the government.

### Notes: UPSC pattern analysis (relocated from module 9)

- **Pattern 1: definition with a slipped word.** *2018 Prelims Q31* asks which statement about a Money Bill is *not* correct. The options mix true statements (a Bill is a Money Bill if it contains only tax provisions; it can deal with custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund; it can regulate borrowing) with one slipped word. Module 3 and module 2 together show that Article 110 covers appropriation out of the *Consolidated* Fund, not the Contingency Fund. The statement that says appropriation out of the Contingency Fund conflicts with that.
- **Pattern 2: which Article or Act requires this?** *2020 Prelims Q6* asks what mandates the Macro Economic Framework Statement placed with the budget. Laxmikanth's list of budget documents places it among the statements mandated under the FRBM Act. Articles 112 and 110(1) establish the annual financial statement and the Money Bill definition; Article 113 deals with estimates in Parliament; none of those is the source of that statement. The reasoning points to the FRBM Act option.
- **Pattern 3: Money Bill versus Finance Bill.** *2023 Prelims Q37* gives three statements. Statement 2 (the Rajya Sabha cannot amend or reject a Money Bill, it can only recommend) matches module 3. Statement 3 (no joint sitting for a Money Bill but one is *necessary* for a Finance Bill) overstates: a joint sitting is only available after disagreement on a Bill that allows it, and not necessary as such. Statement 1 depends on what 'Finance Bill' means: for Financial Bills I and II the Rajya Sabha may amend or reject, but Laxmikanth says the annual Finance Bill is subject to all conditions applicable to a Money Bill. The ambiguity of the term is itself the examiner's trap; module 4 equips you to handle each reading.
- *2024 Prelims Q68* asks about Money Bill statements. NCERT reproduces Article 109 (special procedure for Money Bills), including 109(1) (not to be introduced in the Council of States), and says the Rajya Sabha can approve or suggest changes but not reject, and that its suggested amendments may or may not be accepted. So statements 1, 2 and 3 are supported by NCERT and statement 4 ('have to be accepted') conflicts with it.
- **Pattern 4: who does what in the budget?** *2024 Prelims Q85* gives a statement that the Finance Minister on behalf of the Prime Minister lays the Annual Financial Statement, and one that no demand for a grant can be made except on the President's recommendation. Laxmikanth records that the President causes the statement to be laid; no demand for a grant except on the President's recommendation. The first statement's 'on behalf of the Prime Minister' does not match Article 112, while the second matches the budget provisions in module 5.

### Core concepts

- Parliament controls both taxing (Article 265, Finance Act) and spending (Appropriation Act).
- A Money Bill contains only Article 110 matters; the Rajya Sabha can only recommend within 14 days.
- Financial Bills I and II are Article 117 Bills; only the Money Bill removes the joint sitting and Rajya Sabha rejection.
- The budget is the annual financial statement (Article 112), with charged expenditure not voted and voted expenditure put to the Lok Sabha.
- The budget passes in six stages ending with the Appropriation Bill and the Finance Bill.

**Q.** What are the two laws that Parliament must pass so that the government can tax and spend?

**A.** The Finance Act for taxes and the Appropriation Act for spending from the Consolidated Fund.

**Q.** What does 'charged on the Consolidated Fund' mean?

**A.** The expenditure is fixed by the Constitution or law; Parliament may discuss but does not vote it.

**Q.** Which House votes on demands for grants?

**A.** The Lok Sabha only.

**Q.** Name the three funds of the Union with their Articles.

**A.** Consolidated Fund (266), Public Account (266) and Contingency Fund (267).

**Q.** Explain why a Bill that imposes a tax and also alters the powers of a regulator is not a Money Bill.

**A.** Article 110 requires that the Bill contain only the listed financial matters. The regulator provisions are general legislation, so the Bill is at most a Financial Bill (I).

**Q.** Why does the Rajya Sabha's Money Bill role differ from its role on ordinary Bills?

**A.** Because it is indirectly elected and the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha, so the directly elected House controls the purse.

**Q.** What is the purpose of the vote on account?

**A.** It bridges the period between 1 April and the enactment of the Appropriation Act so the government can keep spending. Since presentation moved to 1 February (2017), the budget is generally passed before 1 April, so a vote on account is now mainly used with interim budgets in election years (verify).

**Q.** Why is no cut motion allowed during the general discussion?

**A.** The general discussion is for debate on the budget as a whole and principles, not for reducing specific demands; cut motions belong to the voting stage on demands.

**Q.** The Lok Sabha passes a Bill containing a tax change and a new regulatory provision. The Rajya Sabha rejects it and the Houses disagree. Can a joint sitting be called? Give the reasoning.

**A.** If the Bill is a Financial Bill I (tax matters plus general legislation), yes: it follows ordinary procedure apart from the Lok Sabha-only introduction, so a joint sitting is available. If it were a Money Bill, no joint sitting would apply, but a Money Bill cannot contain the regulatory provision.

**Q.** A ministry has spent more than its sanctioned grant by year end. Which grant regularises this, who must approve it first, and which House votes it?

**A.** An excess grant; the Public Accounts Committee approves the demand first, and the Lok Sabha votes it after the financial year.
