### Bridge: Phase and phase lag: what 'x lags F by δ' means

Two oscillations of the same frequency, F = F₀ cos ωt and x = A cos(ωt − δ), reach their peaks at different instants. x peaks when ωt = δ, which is later than the force peaks (ωt = 0) by the time δ/ω. So x **lags** the force by the phase angle δ (in radians). A full cycle is 2π, so a lag of π/2 is a quarter of a period (T/4) and a lag of π is half a period, which means x is the exact opposite of F at every moment. Phase is measured against the *driver*, and a lag is written as a negative phase (−δ) in the expression cos(ωt + phase). A lag of 0 means 'in step'. Example: for ω = 10 rad/s, T = 0.628 s, and a lag of π/2 means x peaks T/4 = 0.157 s after the force does.

### Bridge: Cycle averages of cos² and of products of cosines

Power is the product of two oscillating quantities, so its average over a cycle is needed. Use the identity cos a cos c = ½[cos(a − c) + cos(a + c)]. For two oscillations of the same frequency, cos ωt cos(ωt − ψ) = ½[cos ψ + cos(2ωt − ψ)]. The first term is a constant. The second oscillates at twice the frequency, going equally positive and negative, so its average over a full cycle is zero. Hence the average of cos ωt cos(ωt − ψ) is ½ cos ψ. With ψ = 0 this gives the average of cos² as ½; with ψ = π/2 it gives zero (a quarter-cycle apart, the product averages to nothing). A check: the average of cos²ωt is ½ because cos² + sin² = 1 and the two have equal averages.

**Notation (read once, return when confused).** In this lesson:

- **m** mass (kg), **k** spring constant (N/m), **b** drag coefficient in F = −b v (kg/s), **γ = b/2m** the decay constant (s⁻¹), as in PH-036 module 1.
- **ω₀ = √(k/m)** the natural angular frequency, **ω′ = √(ω₀² − γ²)** the damped one, **Q = ω₀/2γ = mω₀/b** (PH-036 module 7).
- **F₀** the amplitude of the driving force (N), **f₀ = F₀/m** the force per unit mass (m/s²), **ω** the angular frequency of the driver (rad/s).

**Trap: the books use other letters.** Mathur and Upadhyaya call the driver's angular frequency **p** and use F₀ sin pt (only the time origin changes, so A and the lag are unchanged). Upadhyaya writes **b** for the decay constant (his 2b = drag/m), so his b is this lesson's γ. The 2024 paper writes **γ** for the drag coefficient (unit N s/m = kg/s), so its γ is this lesson's b. Rule from PH-036: a symbol in kg/s is the drag coefficient b; a symbol in s⁻¹ is the decay constant γ.

**Warning on Mathur's k.** Mathur (PDF p. 417) writes g/m = 2k and C/m = ω₀². So his g is the drag coefficient (our b), his k is the damping (decay) constant (our γ), and his C is the spring constant (our k). Do not read Mathur's k as a spring constant.

### 2. Foundation: the driven equation, transient and steady state

*Objective:* Write the equation of motion of a driven damped oscillator and explain why the long-time motion has the driver's frequency and does not depend on the start.

**Forces.** Take x as displacement from equilibrium, positive in the direction of the push. Three horizontal forces act on the mass: the spring −kx, the drag −b ẋ (PH-036 module 2) and the driver F₀ cos ωt. Newton's second law m ẍ = (sum of forces) gives

$$ m ẍ + b ẋ + k x = F₀ cos ωt $$

Divide by m and use b/m = 2γ, k/m = ω₀², F₀/m = f₀:

$$ ẍ + 2γ ẋ + ω₀² x = f₀ cos ωt $$

**Dimension check.** Every term must be an acceleration, m/s². ẍ is m/s². 2γẋ is s⁻¹ × m/s = m/s². ω₀²x is s⁻² × m. f₀ = N/kg = m/s². ✓

**What kind of equation?** It is linear (x and its derivatives appear only to the first power) and inhomogeneous (the right side does not contain x). Compare PH-036 module 2, where the right side was zero.

**Structure of the solution.** Suppose x_p(t) is any one solution of the full equation (a *particular* solution) and x_h(t) is any solution of the damped equation with zero on the right. Then x = x_p + x_h is again a solution: substituting, the left side gives f₀ cos ωt + 0. Conversely, if x₁ and x₂ both solve the full equation, their difference solves the homogeneous one, so every solution has this form. This is the superposition principle (Mathur 8.12). For the underdamped case (γ < ω₀), PH-036 module 4 gives

$$ x_h = e^(−γt) [C₁ cos ω′t + C₂ sin ω′t] $$

with C₁, C₂ fixed by the initial position and velocity.

**Transient and steady state.** x_h dies out: its envelope e^(−γt) is below 1% after about 5/γ = 10m/b seconds, because e⁻⁵ = 0.0067. This part is the **transient**. What survives is x_p, the **steady state**. In the early moments the two 'tussle': the oscillator tries to ring at ω′ and the driver forces ω (Upadhyaya 8.6). Then the driver wins. In the steady state each cycle the driver supplies exactly the energy that drag removes (module 6).

**Three facts to carry forward.**

1. The steady motion has the **driver's** frequency ω, not ω₀ and not ω′.
2. Its amplitude and phase depend on F₀, m, b, k and ω, and **not** on the initial conditions. The initial conditions only change the transient.
3. If b = 0 there is no damping, the transient never dies, and at ω = ω₀ the response grows without limit. Real systems always have b > 0.

**Numbers.** For m = 0.25 kg, b = 1 kg/s (the 2024 data): γ = 2 s⁻¹, so the transient is below 1% after 5/γ = 2.5 s. NCERT only notes that damped oscillators can be kept going by an external periodic agency (extract p. 2); the theory is in Mathur and Upadhyaya.

### 3. Core: steady-state amplitude and phase by the complex-exponential method

*Objective:* Derive A(ω) and δ(ω) from ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = f₀ cos ωt, showing every step, and check them.

**Setting.** x is the displacement, positive along the force. We want the steady-state (particular) solution. Use the complex numbers of PH-036 bridge B2 (Euler: e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ).

**Step 1: complexify.** Consider the complex equation z̈ + 2γ ż + ω₀² z = f₀ e^(iωt). Its coefficients are real, so taking the real part of every term shows that if z solves it, then x = Re z solves the real equation (the real part of f₀e^(iωt) is f₀ cos ωt).

**Step 2: trial solution.** Try z = X e^(iωt), where X is a complex constant that will carry both the amplitude and the phase. Then ż = iωX e^(iωt) and z̈ = (iω)² X e^(iωt) = −ω² X e^(iωt).

**Step 3: substitute.**

$$ (−ω² + 2iγω + ω₀²) X e^(iωt) = f₀ e^(iωt) $$

Divide by e^(iωt), which is never zero:

$$ X = f₀ / D ,   D = (ω₀² − ω²) + i 2γω $$

**Step 4: polar form of D.** Write D = Z e^(iδ), where Z = |D| (units s⁻²; the letter Z is kept for the circuit resistance in module 8). Its real part is ω₀² − ω² and its imaginary part is 2γω, so

$$ Z = √[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω²] ,   tan δ = 2γω/(ω₀² − ω²) $$

Because the imaginary part 2γω is positive for ω > 0 and γ > 0, δ lies between 0 and π; use the two-argument arctangent (the diagram), not plain arctan, which would return a negative angle once ω > ω₀.

**Step 5: back to real.** X = f₀/(Z e^(iδ)) = (f₀/Z) e^(−iδ). Then

x = Re[(f₀/Z) e^(−iδ) e^(iωt)] = (f₀/Z) cos(ωt − δ). Therefore

$$ x(t) = A cos(ωt − δ) ,   A(ω) = f₀ / √[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω²] $$

**Step 6: in the original constants.** Multiply the numerator and denominator by m, with mω₀² = k and 2mγ = b:

$$ A = F₀ / √[(k − mω²)² + b²ω²] $$

**Checks.**

- *Dimensions:* (k − mω²) is N/m; bω is (N s/m)(1/s) = N/m. So A = N/(N/m) = m. ✓
- *Static limit:* ω → 0 gives A → f₀/ω₀² = F₀/k, the stretch of a spring under a steady force F₀. ✓
- *No damping:* γ = 0 gives A = f₀/|ω₀² − ω²| and δ = 0 (below ω₀) or π (above), the textbook undamped result, infinite at ω = ω₀.
- *Sign of the lag:* X has argument −δ, so the displacement lags the force, matching Mathur's result x = A sin(pt − θ) for a sine force (PDF pp. 417-418) and Upadhyaya equations (6) to (8).

**Quadrant summary.** ω < ω₀: ω₀² − ω² > 0, so 0 < δ < π/2. ω = ω₀: the real part vanishes, so δ = π/2 **exactly, for any γ**. ω > ω₀: the real part is negative, so π/2 < δ < π.

**Timed target (practice target, not an official allotment).** This derivation is a 15-mark core: about 8 minutes of an 18-minute answer.

### 4. Core: the trigonometric method, three regimes and phase behaviour

*Objective:* Re-derive A and δ with real trigonometry, then read the response in the low-, resonance- and high-frequency limits.

**Why twice?** The trig method (Mathur 8.6, Upadhyaya 8.6) needs no complex numbers; it must agree with module 3.

**Trig method, every step.** Assume x = A cos(ωt − δ) with constants A, δ to find. Let θ = ωt − δ. Then ẋ = −Aω sin θ and ẍ = −Aω² cos θ.

*Step 1.* Substitute into ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = f₀ cos ωt:

A(ω₀² − ω²) cos θ − 2γωA sin θ = f₀ cos ωt

*Step 2.* Write the right side with θ: ωt = θ + δ, so f₀ cos(θ + δ) = f₀ cos θ cos δ − f₀ sin θ sin δ.

*Step 3.* The equation holds for all t, so the coefficients of cos θ and of sin θ must match:

$$ A(ω₀² − ω²) = f₀ cos δ ,   2γωA = f₀ sin δ $$

*Step 4.* Square and add (cos²δ + sin²δ = 1): A²[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω²] = f₀², so A = f₀/Z, the same as module 3. Divide the second by the first: tan δ = 2γω/(ω₀² − ω²). Both signs (sin δ > 0 always, and cos δ has the sign of ω₀² − ω²) fix δ in the correct quadrant, in agreement with module 3.

**Three regimes (the 'three controls').**

| Region | Dominant term | Amplitude | Phase lag δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| ω ≪ ω₀ | stiffness kx | A ≈ F₀/k, the static stretch | ≈ 0 (moves with the force) |
| ω = ω₀ | drag bẋ (kx and mω²x cancel) | A = F₀/(bω₀) = f₀/(2γω₀) | π/2 exactly |
| ω ≫ ω₀ | inertia mẍ | A ≈ F₀/(mω²) = f₀/ω², falling as ω⁻² | → π (opposite to the force) |

Middle row: at ω₀ the spring and inertial terms cancel (k − mω₀² = 0), so drag alone balances the driver: halve b and the amplitude doubles.

**Phase behaviour, with numbers (computed).** With ω₀ = 1:

| Q | δ at ω = 0.5 | δ at ω = 1 | δ at ω = 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 7.6° | 90° | 172.4° |
| 1 | 33.7° | 90° | 146.3° |
| 0.5 | 53.1° | 90° | 126.9° |

Larger Q gives a sharper switch from 0 to π; every curve passes through π/2 at ω₀. The displacement phase relative to the force is **0, −π/2, −π** in the three regimes (negative: a lag). With b = 0 the switch is a jump (Mathur 8.8).

**Amplitude ratio A(ω)/A(0), computed (ω₀ = 1).**

| ω/ω₀ | 0.4 | 0.8 | 1.0 | 1.2 | 1.4 | 2.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q = 5 | 1.19 | 2.54 | 5.00 | 2.00 | 1.00 | 0.33 |
| Q = 2 | 1.16 | 1.86 | 2.00 | 1.34 | 0.84 | 0.32 |
| Q = 1 | 1.07 | 1.14 | 1.00 | 0.78 | 0.59 | 0.28 |
| Q = 0.5 | 0.86 | 0.61 | 0.50 | 0.41 | 0.34 | 0.20 |

Notice that the entry at ω = ω₀ equals Q in every row: A(ω₀)/A(0) = Q, proved in module 7. The curves are not exactly symmetric about ω₀ (Q = 5: 2.54 at 0.8 against 2.00 at 1.2).

### 5. Core: amplitude resonance, its frequency, and when no peak exists

*Objective:* Derive ω_r = √(ω₀² − 2γ²) and A_max = f₀/(2γω′), state the condition b² < 2mk, and order ω_r, ω′ and ω₀.

**Setting.** From module 3, A(ω) = f₀/√S with S(ω) = (ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω², for ω ≥ 0. A is largest where S is smallest. This maximum is called **amplitude resonance** and its frequency is ω_r (Mathur 8.6).

**Step 1: use u = ω².** Then S(u) = (ω₀² − u)² + 4γ²u, for u ≥ 0.

**Step 2: differentiate.** dS/du = −2(ω₀² − u) + 4γ². Setting it to zero: u = ω₀² − 2γ².

**Step 3: minimum, not maximum.** d²S/du² = 2 > 0, so the stationary point is a minimum of S, hence a maximum of A.

**Step 4: existence.** u = ω² must be positive, so the peak at non-zero frequency exists only if ω₀² > 2γ². In the original constants k/m > 2(b/2m)² = b²/(2m²), that is

$$ b² < 2mk $$

If b² ≥ 2mk, then for every u ≥ 0 we have dS/du = 2u − 2ω₀² + 4γ² ≥ 4γ² − 2ω₀² ≥ 0, so S only increases and A **falls steadily from its static value F₀/k**: there is no peak. (At b² = 2mk, ω_r = 0 and the curve is flat-topped at ω = 0.)

**Step 5: the frequency.**

$$ ω_r = √(ω₀² − 2γ²) = √(ω₀² − b²/(2m²)) $$

**Step 6: the peak value.** Insert u = ω₀² − 2γ² in S: S_min = (2γ²)² + 4γ²(ω₀² − 2γ²) = 4γ⁴ + 4γ²ω₀² − 8γ⁴ = 4γ²(ω₀² − γ²) = 4γ²ω′². Hence

$$ A_max = f₀/(2γω′) = F₀/(bω′) $$

**In terms of Q.** With γ = ω₀/2Q: ω_r = ω₀√(1 − 1/2Q²) and A_max = (F₀/k) Q/√(1 − 1/4Q²). The condition b² < 2mk is Q > 1/√2 = 0.707.

**Order of the three frequencies.** ω_r² = ω₀² − 2γ² < ω₀² − γ² = ω′² < ω₀², so

$$ ω_r < ω′ < ω₀ $$

(Mathur says ω_r is below both ω₀ and the damped frequency.) For light damping all three nearly coincide: ω_r ≈ ω₀(1 − 1/4Q²), which is a 1.0% shift at Q = 5 and 0.25% at Q = 10.

**Dimensional and limiting checks.** ω_r is in s⁻¹ ✓ (γ² and ω₀² are s⁻²). As γ → 0, ω_r → ω₀ and A_max → ∞ (the undamped blow-up). A_max ∝ 1/b: halving the drag doubles the peak.

**Numbers (computed).** m = 0.25 kg, k = 100 N/m, b = 1 kg/s, F₀ = 5 N: ω₀ = 20, γ = 2, ω′ = 19.900, ω_r = √392 = 19.799 rad/s, A(ω₀) = 0.2500 m, A_max = 0.2513 m. Second case: m = 2, k = 18: then 2mk = 72, so b = 8 kg/s gives a peak (γ = 2, 2γ² = 8 < 9, ω_r = 1 rad/s) while b = 9 does **not** (γ = 2.25, 2γ² = 10.125 > 9), although b = 9 is still below critical damping b_c = 2√(mk) = 12 and the free motion still oscillates.

**Trap.** Three different thresholds exist: free oscillation needs b² < 4mk (PH-036); an amplitude peak needs b² < 2mk; the velocity and power peaks (module 6) exist for every b. Do not use 4mk for the amplitude peak.

**Graph description.** Both curves start at 1 for ω = 0. The Q = 5 curve rises slowly, reaches about 5.03 near ω = 0.99ω₀ and drops to about 0.33 at 2ω₀. The Q = 1 curve has a shallow maximum of 1.15 near 0.71ω₀ and then falls, and curves with Q below 0.707 fall monotonically from 1.

### 6. Core: velocity resonance, power absorbed and the energy balance

*Objective:* Show that the velocity amplitude and the average absorbed power both peak exactly at ω₀, and derive ⟨P⟩ = ½bV².

**Velocity.** From x = A cos(ωt − δ): v = ẋ = −ωA sin(ωt − δ) = ωA cos(ωt − δ + π/2). So the velocity amplitude is V(ω) = ωA and the velocity **leads the displacement by π/2**, hence leads the force by π/2 − δ.

**Step 1: V(ω).** V = f₀ω/Z. Divide the numerator and the root by ω:

$$ V = f₀ / √[(ω₀²/ω − ω)² + 4γ²] $$

**Step 2: maximum.** The denominator is smallest when ω₀²/ω − ω = 0, i.e. ω = ω₀, for every γ. Then

$$ V_max = f₀/(2γ) = F₀/b $$

This is **velocity resonance** (Mathur 8.9). It occurs at ω₀ exactly, not at ω_r.

**Step 3: phase at velocity resonance.** At ω = ω₀, δ = π/2 (module 3), so the lead π/2 − δ = 0: the velocity is **in phase with the force** (see the table in the diagram). The force then always pushes the way the mass moves and does positive work throughout.

**Step 4: instantaneous and average power.** The driver delivers P = F v. Write v = V cos(ωt − ψ) with ψ = δ − π/2. Then P = F₀V cos ωt cos(ωt − ψ) = ½F₀V[cos ψ + cos(2ωt − ψ)]. Averaged over a cycle the second term vanishes (the cycle average of a cosine is zero), so

$$ ⟨P⟩ = ½ F₀ V cos ψ = ½ F₀ V sin δ $$

since cos(δ − π/2) = sin δ.

**Step 5: remove δ.** From module 4, 2γωA = f₀ sin δ, i.e. sin δ = 2γV/f₀. With F₀ = m f₀:

⟨P⟩ = ½ m f₀ V · 2γV/f₀ = mγV², and since b = 2mγ,

$$ ⟨P⟩ = ½ b V² $$

**Step 6: energy balance.** The average rate at which drag dissipates energy is ⟨b v²⟩ = b·V²/2, the same number. So in steady state the energy input equals the dissipation, and the spring and mass absorb nothing on average (their energy only sloshes back and forth). Mathur gets the same result (8.11, PDF pp. 423-424).

**Step 7: as a function of ω.** ⟨P⟩ = ½bV² with V = f₀ω/Z gives

$$ ⟨P⟩ = (F₀²/2b) · 4γ²ω²/[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω²] $$

(check: ½b f₀²ω²/Z² = γF₀²ω²/(mR²) equals this, using b = 2mγ). The fraction is at most 1, reached when ω₀² = ω², so

$$ ⟨P⟩_max = F₀²/(2b) = ½ F₀ V_max $$

at ω = ω₀: the absorbed power peaks at velocity resonance, not at the amplitude resonance ω_r (Mathur p. 424).

**Dimension check.** F₀²/b is N² / (N s/m) = N m/s = W ✓.

**Numbers (computed).** m = 0.25, b = 1, F₀ = 5: V_max = 5 m/s (also ωA = 20 × 0.25 = 5 ✓), ⟨P⟩_max = 25/2 = 12.5 W. For m = 2, k = 50, b = 4, F₀ = 10: ⟨P⟩_max = 100/8 = 12.5 W, and at ω = 3 rad/s ⟨P⟩ = 1.54 W.

**Timed target (practice target, not an official allotment).** Velocity resonance plus power is a 10-mark item: about 12 minutes.

### 7. Core: sharpness, width of the resonance and the quality factor Q

*Objective:* Derive the exact half-power width Δω = 2γ, show Q = ω₀/Δω, A(ω₀)/A(0) = Q and the exact energy form of Q at resonance.

**Sharpness.** For light damping the curve of A against ω falls steeply on either side of the peak: the oscillator answers only to frequencies very near ω₀ and the resonance is **sharp**. For heavy damping the top is broad and the resonance is **flat** (Mathur 8.7; he contrasts a sonometer wire, sharp, with an air column, flat). A number is needed for this.

**Step 1: half-power frequencies.** From module 6, ⟨P⟩/⟨P⟩_max = 4γ²ω²/Z². Setting it to ½: Z² = 8γ²ω², i.e. (ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω² = 8γ²ω², so (ω₀² − ω²)² = 4γ²ω². Take square roots: ω₀² − ω² = ±2γω.

**Step 2: solve both quadratics.** ω² + 2γω − ω₀² = 0 has the positive root ω₁ = −γ + √(γ² + ω₀²). ω² − 2γω − ω₀² = 0 has the positive root ω₂ = +γ + √(γ² + ω₀²). Hence

$$ Δω = ω₂ − ω₁ = 2γ  (exactly),   ω₁ω₂ = ω₀² $$

(the product is (γ² + ω₀²) − γ² = ω₀² ✓).

**Step 3: define Q by the width.**

$$ Q = ω₀/Δω = ω₀/2γ = mω₀/b $$

the same Q as PH-036 module 7. Hence **fractional width Δω/ω₀ = 1/Q**: Q = 5 gives 20%, Q = 100 gives 1%. Mathur reaches Q = ω₀τ = ω₀/2Δp, where his Δp = γ is the one-sided half-width of the power curve (PDF p. 425).

**Step 4: Q as an amplitude ratio.** A(0) = f₀/ω₀² and A(ω₀) = f₀/(2γω₀), so A(ω₀)/A(0) = ω₀/2γ = **Q** exactly (Upadhyaya 8.8; Mathur p. 419). A resonating system magnifies the static response Q times; A_max/A(0) = Q/√(1 − 1/4Q²) ≈ Q.

**Step 5: Q as an energy ratio, exact at resonance.** At ω = ω₀: x = A sin ω₀t, v = V cos ω₀t, V = ω₀A. Total energy ½mv² + ½kx² = ½mV²cos²ω₀t + ½kA²sin²ω₀t, and kA² = mω₀²A² = mV², so it is constant: E = ½mV². Energy lost per period T = 2π/ω₀ is ⟨P⟩T = (F₀²/2b)(2π/ω₀). With V = F₀/b: 2πE/(⟨P⟩T) = π m F₀²/b² ÷ [π F₀²/(bω₀)] = mω₀/b = Q. So the 'energy stored over energy lost per cycle' form is exact for a driven oscillator at ω₀, whereas for free decay it needed light damping (PH-036 module 7).

**The other 'half-width'.** If the amplitude (not power) falls to A_max/2, Mathur finds Δp ≈ √3γ on each side (PDF pp. 422-423), a full width 2√3γ ≈ 3.46γ. Direct numerical check (ω₀ = 100, γ = 1): 3.465 against 3.464. **Always say which curve and whether you quote one side or both:** power, full width 2γ; amplitude to ½ of the peak, full width 2√3γ.

**Numbers (computed).** m = 2, k = 50, b = 4: γ = 1, ω₀ = 5, Q = 2.5, ω₁ = 4.099, ω₂ = 6.099, Δω = 2 = ω₀/Q ✓. LCR: ω₀ = 1000 rad/s, γ = 25 s⁻¹, Q = 20, ω₁ = 975.3, ω₂ = 1025.3, Δω = 50 ✓. Both satisfy ω₁ω₂ = ω₀².

### 8. Master: the driven LCR circuit, radio tuning, examples and the transient

*Objective:* Map the mechanical results onto the series LCR circuit, use them for tuning a radio, and write the full solution including the transient constants.

**The driven series LCR circuit.** An emf V₀ cos ωt acts in series with an inductor L, a resistor R and a capacitor C. Kirchhoff's voltage rule (PH-036 module 8 for the free case) gives L q̈ + R q̇ + q/C = V₀ cos ωt, with q the charge on the capacitor and I = q̇ the current. This is the mechanical equation with the dictionary of PH-035 module 8 plus the damping row:

| Mechanical | m | b | k | x | v | F₀ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electrical | L | R | 1/C | q | I | V₀ |

Hence γ = R/2L, ω₀ = 1/√(LC), Q = ω₀L/R. Translating module 6: the current amplitude is I₀ = V₀/√[R² + (ωL − 1/ωC)²], maximum **V₀/R at ω₀ = 1/√(LC)**, where the current is in phase with the emf (Mathur 8.13, PDF pp. 426-428; the quoted sentence is on p. 428: 'current resonance is analogous to velocity resonance'). The charge and the voltage on the capacitor peak at ω_r² = 1/LC − R²/2L², slightly lower, as in module 5. The half-power width is Δω = R/L.

**Numbers (computed).** L = 0.1 H, C = 10 μF, R = 5 Ω, V₀ = 10 V: ω₀ = 1000 rad/s (159.15 Hz), Q = ω₀L/R = 20, I_max = 2 A, Δω = 50 rad/s, and the capacitor voltage amplitude at ω₀ is I/(ω₀C) = 200 V = Q V₀: the voltage across C exceeds the supply by the factor Q.

**Tuning a radio (a simplified model).** Varying C makes ω₀ = 1/√(LC) equal to the wanted station's carrier; other stations drive the circuit off resonance and give a small response. With f₀ = 1000 kHz and Q = 100 the bandwidth is f₀/Q = 10 kHz. A neighbouring carrier 10 kHz away gives a relative current 1/√[1 + Q²(f/f₀ − f₀/f)²] = 0.449, and one 5 kHz away gives 0.708. Higher Q improves selectivity but narrows the band, which then cuts the sidebands (standard knowledge, flagged).
**Examples.** Sympathetic vibration of strings or pendulums of equal period; the resonance tube; the sonometer (sharp) and the air column (flat); Upadhyaya also lists the marching-soldiers bridge. **Flagged example: Tacoma Narrows (1940).** It is often quoted as resonance, but engineering analyses attribute the collapse to aeroelastic flutter, a self-excited effect that is not simple forced resonance; this is standard knowledge not in the sources (verify), so write it, if at all, as 'popularly cited'.

**The full solution including the transient.** For γ < ω₀: x = e^(−γt)[C₁ cos ω′t + C₂ sin ω′t] + A cos(ωt − δ). With x(0) = x₀ and ẋ(0) = v₀ (use ẋ_p(0) = Aω sin δ): C₁ = x₀ − A cos δ, C₂ = [v₀ + γC₁ − Aω sin δ]/ω′. Numerical check: m = 0.5, k = 50, b = 2, F₀ = 3, ω = 8, x₀ = 0.1, v₀ = 0 gives γ = 1, ω₀ = 10, A = 0.12457 m, δ = 41.63°, and x(2 s) = −0.11372 m, equal to a direct numerical integration of the equation to ten digits.

### Notes: Symbols and units (this lesson)

- m: mass (kg). k: spring constant (N/m). b: drag coefficient in F = −bv (kg/s). F₀: force amplitude (N). f₀ = F₀/m (m/s²).
- γ = b/2m: decay constant (s⁻¹). ω₀ = √(k/m): natural angular frequency. ω′ = √(ω₀² − γ²): damped angular frequency. ω: driver's angular frequency (rad/s).
- Q = ω₀/2γ = mω₀/b = √(mk)/b (dimensionless). A(ω): steady amplitude (m). δ(ω): lag of displacement behind force (rad).
- Steady state: the motion that remains after the transient e^(−γt) has died out; it has the driver's frequency, constant amplitude, and does not depend on initial conditions.

### Notes: Key equations

- m ẍ + b ẋ + k x = F₀ cos ωt, equivalently ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = f₀ cos ωt.
- x_steady = A cos(ωt − δ); A = f₀/√[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω²] = F₀/√[(k − mω²)² + b²ω²]; tan δ = 2γω/(ω₀² − ω²), 0 < δ < π.
- ω_r = √(ω₀² − 2γ²) = √(ω₀² − b²/2m²) (exists only if b² < 2mk); A_max = f₀/(2γω′) = F₀/(bω′); ω_r < ω′ < ω₀.
- V(ω) = ωA, V_max = F₀/b at ω₀; ⟨P⟩ = ½bV² = (F₀²/2b)·4γ²ω²/[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4γ²ω²]; ⟨P⟩_max = F₀²/2b at ω₀.
- Half-power points ω₁,₂ = √(ω₀² + γ²) ∓ γ; Δω = 2γ; ω₁ω₂ = ω₀²; Q = ω₀/Δω; A(ω₀)/A(0) = Q.
- Series LCR: L q̈ + R q̇ + q/C = V₀ cos ωt; γ = R/2L; ω₀ = 1/√(LC); Q = ω₀L/R; I_max = V₀/R; Δω = R/L.

### Notes: Three resonances compared

- The peaks of a driven damped oscillator occur at different frequencies.

### Notes: Derivation map (what to write, in order)

- Equation of motion → divide by m → complexify → trial z = Xe^(iωt) → X = f₀/D, D = (ω₀² − ω²) + 2iγω → polar form → A = f₀/Z, δ = arg D.
- Check by trig method: A(ω₀² − ω²) = f₀ cos δ and 2γωA = f₀ sin δ; square-add and divide.
- Resonance: minimise S(u) = (ω₀² − u)² + 4γ²u with u = ω²: u = ω₀² − 2γ², S_min = 4γ²ω′².
- Power: ⟨P⟩ = ½F₀V sin δ = ½bV²; half-power: (ω₀² − ω²)² = 4γ²ω² gives Δω = 2γ.

### Notes: Symbol conventions in the sources

- Always decide by units: kg/s is a drag coefficient, s⁻¹ is a decay constant.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Natural frequency ω₀, damped frequency ω′ and amplitude-resonance frequency ω_r are three different numbers: ω_r < ω′ < ω₀.
- Amplitude resonance (peak of A) is not velocity resonance (peak of ωA) and not the power peak; the last two are at ω₀ exactly.
- The steady-state frequency is the driver's ω, never ω₀ or ω′; those appear only in the transient and in the amplitude formula.
- 'Half width' is ambiguous: power curve full width 2γ (one side γ); amplitude falling to half its peak, one side √3γ (Mathur 8.10). State which curve.
- Q has several forms that agree for light damping; exactly: Q = ω₀/Δω (power width) and A(ω₀)/A(0) = Q hold for every γ.

### Notes: Exceptions and conditions

- If b² ≥ 2mk there is no amplitude peak: A decreases steadily from F₀/k, even though 2mk ≤ b² < 4mk is still underdamped free motion.
- At exactly b² = 2mk, ω_r = 0: the curve is flat-topped at ω = 0.
- The formula A_max = F₀/(bω′) needs b² < 2mk; the simpler A(ω₀) = F₀/(bω₀) holds for every b and is the usual 'amplitude at resonance' for weak damping.
- The relation Δω = 2γ is exact for the power curve; the approximate Δ ≈ √3γ for the amplitude curve needs γ ≪ ω₀.
