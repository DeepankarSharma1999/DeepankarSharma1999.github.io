### Bridge: Optical path = refractive index x geometric path, and what "stationary" means

**Speed in a medium.** Light travels at c in vacuum and at the smaller speed v = c/μ in a transparent medium of refractive index μ. In glass of μ = 1.5 the speed is 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

**Time along a path.** Over a short element of length dL the time taken is dt = dL / v = μ dL / c. Adding the elements, the time from P to Q along any path is

$$ t = (1/c) ∫ μ dL = Δ / c,   with   Δ = ∫ μ dL $$

The number Δ is the **optical path length**: the refractive index multiplied by the geometric length, summed along the path. In a single uniform medium it is simply μ × L. Because c is a constant, comparing times for different paths is the same as comparing Δ. This is why Brij Lal (PDF p. 36) restates the principle as one about the optical path length.

*Meaning:* Δ is the length of vacuum that would take the same time as the real path. It also counts waves: the number of wavelengths along a path of length L in a medium is L/λ = μL/λ₀, where λ₀ is the vacuum wavelength, so Δ/λ₀ is the number of vacuum wavelengths that fit (PH-042 module 6 explains why λ changes while frequency does not).

**What "stationary" means.** Draw the time (or Δ) against a variable x that labels the path, such as the point where the ray touches a mirror. A smooth curve has a slope dΔ/dx at each point. At the bottom of a valley, at the top of a hill, or at a level shoulder, the slope is zero; these are the three kinds of **stationary point**. Near a stationary point a small change in x changes Δ only through the square of the change, which is much smaller than the change in x itself. To find the real path we set the slope to zero.

**A partial derivative** is the slope with respect to one variable while the others are held fixed. If Δ depends on both x and z, ∂Δ/∂x means "differentiate with respect to x, treating z as a constant". Module 4 uses ∂/∂z and ∂/∂x in turn.

### Bridge: Light rays versus wave fronts, and why the optical path counts waves

**Recap (PH-042 module 2).** A **wavefront** is a surface on which all points oscillate in the same phase. A **ray** is a line drawn along the direction in which the energy of the wave travels; in the uniform media of this lesson it is perpendicular to the wavefront. Geometrical optics is the situation in which the wavelength is so small compared with the objects that we may ignore it, so that rays suffice.

**Why the optical path is the right measure.** Along a ray the phase of the wave increases by 2π for every wavelength travelled. In a medium of index μ the wavelength is λ₀/μ, so a geometric length L holds μL/λ₀ wavelengths. The phase gained along the ray is therefore

$$ φ = 2π Δ / λ₀ $$

which depends on the optical path Δ and not on the geometric length alone. Two rays that start in phase on one wavefront and meet at a point arrive in phase when their optical paths are equal. This is the link between Fermat's idea (time, Δ) and the wave idea (phase) and it is used again in PH-050 where path differences become phase differences.

**An image point.** A point where a bundle of rays from one source meet again is an **image**. Because the rays left a common wavefront and arrive together to reinforce, every ray of the bundle has the same optical path from the source to the image. Modules 6 to 8 use exactly this statement: for a mirror, a refracting surface or a lens to form an image, the optical path through the instrument must be the same (stationary) for all rays of the bundle.

*Beginner caution:* a wavefront is a surface and a ray is a line; the ray is a construction, not a thin physical beam of light.

### 1. Orientation: what Fermat's principle asks, the notation and the plan

*Objective:* Say in one sentence what Fermat's principle claims, name the two verified questions this lesson must answer, and fix the notation used throughout.

## What this topic asks

**Fermat's principle** is a single rule from which the straight-line travel of light, the law of reflection and the law of refraction (Snell's law) all follow. Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam (PDF p. 35) describe it as a concise way of thinking about light rather than a calculating device: it says nothing about the mechanism, only about which path is chosen. It is the basis of **geometrical optics**, the branch of optics in which light is described by rays.

## Notation used in this lesson

- **μ** is the refractive index (PH-042 writes n); μ = c/v, with c the speed of light in vacuum and v the speed in the medium.
- **Δ** (Delta) is the **optical path length**, defined in module 2.
- **i** is the angle of incidence and **r** the angle of reflection or refraction, both measured from the **normal** (the line perpendicular to the surface), never from the surface itself.
- **u, v, R** are object distance, image distance and radius of curvature; their sign convention is stated in module 7 before first use. Do not confuse the image distance v with the speed v of light in a medium; the text says which is meant each time.

### 2. Foundation: time, optical path and the careful statement of Fermat's principle

*Objective:* State Fermat's principle in its least-time form and its modern extremum (stationary) form, define the optical path length, and explain why refraction needs the time form and not Hero's shortest-distance form.

## From Hero to Fermat

**Hero of Alexandria** proposed that reflected light travels by the shortest path (Brij Lal PDF p. 35). For a plane mirror this is correct (module 4). For refraction it fails: the straight line AC across the boundary is the shortest route but light does not follow it. Brij Lal (PDF p. 36) says Fermat therefore replaced the "shortest path" by the "least time" in 1650; other histories place it a few years later, so verify the date before quoting it.

## The least-time statement

If light travels between two points P and Q, it follows, out of all possible paths, a path that takes the least time. Time on a small element is dt = dL/v (Brij Lal Eq. 2.1) and the total is t = ∫ dL/v (Eq. 2.2). Using v = c/μ gives

$$ t = (1/c) ∫ μ dL = Δ/c  ,   Δ = ∫ μ dL   (Eqs. 2.3 to 2.5) $$

Fermat's condition compares the actual path with its neighbours. Let s be a parameter that labels the family of paths; then the real path satisfies

$$ dt/ds = 0   or equivalently   dΔ/ds = 0   (Eqs. 2.4, 2.6) $$

so "least time" becomes "least optical path": Δ is the quantity to extremise.

## A first numerical feel

Take A at height 6 cm above a plane boundary (air, μ₁ = 1) and C at depth 4 cm below it in a medium with μ₂ = 4/3 (water), the horizontal separation being 10 cm. If the ray crosses the boundary at horizontal distance x from the foot of A, the optical path is

$$ Δ(x) = 1·√(x² + 6²) + (4/3)·√((10 − x)² + 4²) $$

The table in the figure shows Δ = 16.028 cm at x = 6 cm, where the straight line AC cuts the boundary. The smallest value, 15.882 cm, occurs at x = 7.184 cm, **beyond** the point where the straight line crosses. So the real ray is not the straight line AC: it spends less distance in the slower water. This is the point of the lifeguard analogy that Brij Lal quotes from Feynman: run farther on the sand to swim less in the water.

## The modern, extremum form

Brij Lal (PDF p. 37) points out that the real path is sometimes a maximum (a spherical reflector) and sometimes has the same time as all its neighbours (an ellipsoidal reflector), so the principle is restated:

> A light ray going from one point to another follows the path for which, compared with all neighbouring paths, the time is a minimum, a maximum or stationary.

This is **Fermat's principle of extremum path** (also called of stationary time). A stationary value is one where the slope is zero: a minimum, a maximum or a level inflection with a horizontal tangent. Near it, the optical path against x has a flattened region; the book's Fig. 2.4 shows this for refraction. A small change of x near the real path changes Δ hardly at all, but the same change far away changes it substantially. Hence "many neighbouring paths take almost exactly the same time".

## What to remember for an answer

Write the principle both ways (least time; extremum or stationary), define Δ = ∫ μ dL with t = Δ/c, and state that rectilinear propagation, reflection and refraction follow from it (modules 3 to 5).

*Sign and unit checks:* μ is dimensionless, dL is a length, so Δ is a length (metres) and Δ/c is a time (seconds).

### 3. Core: rectilinear propagation and reversibility of light rays

*Objective:* Prove from Fermat's principle that light travels in straight lines in a homogeneous medium and that the path is reversible, and state what each result does and does not claim.

## Rectilinear propagation

Brij Lal (PDF p. 37, section 2.3) argues: a straight line is the path of shortest distance between two points, so in a homogeneous medium the time along a straight line is a minimum compared with any other path. Here is the same argument in full steps.

**Assumptions.** The medium is **homogeneous** (the same μ everywhere between P and Q) and light travels at the same speed in every direction (**isotropic**).

1. Along any path the optical path is Δ = ∫ μ dL. Since μ is constant it comes out of the integral: Δ = μ ∫ dL = μ × (geometric length of the path).
2. Take any path other than the straight segment PQ. Approximate it by a broken line P-X₁-X₂-...-Q. By the triangle inequality, which says that the sum of two sides of a triangle exceeds the third, replacing two consecutive segments by the single side that joins their outer ends shortens the length unless the three points are collinear. Repeating this shows that the length of any path is at least PQ, with equality only for the straight segment.
3. Therefore Δ(path) ≥ μ·PQ = Δ(straight), and the straight line is the least-time path.

*Numerical check:* for P = (0, 0), Q = (6, 0) and X = (3, 2) the bent path P-X-Q is 7.211 long against PQ = 6, longer by 1.211 (worked values in masterNotes).

*What this shows:* in a uniform medium the rays are straight, which is why shadows have sharp outlines in geometrical optics. *What it does not show:* straight-line travel for non-uniform media (module 9), and it does not hold when the opening is as small as a wavelength (diffraction, a later topic).

## Reversibility of light rays

Brij Lal (PDF p. 37, section 2.4) states that the optical path that is a minimum for travel from P to Q is also a minimum for travel from Q to P, so a ray that went from P to Q will travel the same path backwards.

**Derivation.** Choose any path C from P to Q. Its optical path is Δ(C) = ∫_C μ dL. The element dL is a positive length and μ is a positive number depending only on position, so the sum does not care whether we add the elements starting from P or starting from Q:

$$ Δ(P → Q along C) = Δ(Q → P along C) $$

Hence every path has the same Δ in both directions. If a path C₀ makes Δ stationary for travel from P to Q, then, because all neighbouring paths also have the same Δ in either direction, C₀ makes Δ stationary for travel from Q to P too. The real ray from Q to P is therefore C₀ again, traversed backwards. ∎

*Check with the laws already known:* Snell's law μ₁ sin i = μ₂ sin r is unchanged when the two media are exchanged (swap i and r together with μ₁ and μ₂), and the law of reflection i = r does not change when the ray direction is reversed. In module 5 the reverse ray from C reaches A by the same crossing point.

## Limits of the statement

Reversibility is a statement about the **path**. It does not say that the brightness is the same along the way back; that depends on reflection and transmission coefficients (a later topic). Media that move, or have a direction-dependent speed such as some magneto-optic media, can break reversibility (standard physics, flagged for verification, outside the supplied extract).

### 4. Core: the laws of reflection from Fermat's principle (the 2019 proof)

*Objective:* Derive both laws of reflection at a plane mirror by differentiation, prove independently by the image method that the reflected path has the least time, and check both with numbers.

## Set-up and notation

Take the mirror as the plane y = 0 and place A = (0, a, 0) and B = (d, b, 0) above it, with a, b > 0 (Brij Lal Fig. 2.7, PDF pp. 37 to 38, uses the same arrangement with the origin at D, the foot of A). A ray goes from A to a point P = (x, 0, z) of the mirror and then to B; P may lie off the page (z ≠ 0). In one uniform medium Δ = μL, so minimising Δ means minimising

$$ L(x, z) = √(x² + a² + z²) + √((d − x)² + b² + z²)   (Brij Lal Eq. 2.7) $$

## Proof A: calculus (the book's route)

**Step 1, vary z.** ∂L/∂z = z/AP + z/PB = z (1/AP + 1/PB). The bracket is positive, so ∂L/∂z = 0 forces **z = 0**: P lies in the plane through A and B that contains the normal at P (the normal is parallel to the y-axis). The incident ray, reflected ray and normal are coplanar. **First law.**

**Step 2, vary x with z = 0.**

$$ dL/dx = x/√(x² + a²) − (d − x)/√((d − x)² + b²) = 0 $$

In the right triangles formed with the normal, x/√(x² + a²) = sin i and (d − x)/√((d − x)² + b²) = sin r. So **sin i = sin r**, and since both angles lie between 0 and 90°, **i = r**. **Second law.**

**Step 3, minimum.** d²L/dx² = a²/(x² + a²)^(3/2) + b²/((d − x)² + b²)^(3/2) > 0, and ∂²L/∂z² = 1/AP + 1/PB > 0 at z = 0, with zero mixed derivative there. Both curvatures are positive: a **minimum**.

## Proof B: the image method (a full proof of least time)

Reflect B in the mirror plane to B′ = (d, −b, 0). For every point P of the plane, PB = PB′ (P is equidistant from B and B′). Hence

$$ AP + PB = AP + PB′ ≥ AB′ $$

by the triangle inequality, with equality only when P lies on the segment AB′. That segment cuts the mirror plane in exactly one point O, so the minimum exists, is unique, and no other mirror point gives a shorter path. As t = μ(AP + PB)/c with constant μ, the path through O has the **least time of flight**. At O the segments OA and OB′ make vertically opposite angles with the mirror, and OB mirrors OB′, so i = r; the segment AB′ lies in the plane of A, B and the normal. ∎

## Numbers

a = 3, b = 5, d = 8 (any unit): the image method gives the minimum path √(8² + 8²) = √128 = 11.314 at x* = a d/(a + b) = 3.000, with sin i = 3/√18 = 0.7071 = sin r and i = r = 45°. Path lengths at other points, and the second derivative 0.1886, are in masterNotes.

## Boundary cases

- Normal incidence (d = 0): x* = 0 and the ray retraces itself. Grazing incidence (a, b → 0): i, r → 90°.
- If x* lies off the edge of a finite mirror there is no reflected ray joining A and B; the least-time point is the edge, where diffraction decides what is seen.

### 5. Core: the laws of refraction from Fermat's principle (Snell's law)

*Objective:* Derive both laws of refraction at a plane surface from the stationary optical path, show the stationary value is a minimum, and test the result and its boundary cases numerically.

## Set-up

A plane surface separates medium 1 (index μ₁) above from medium 2 (index μ₂) below; the boundary is the plane y = 0. Take A = (0, a, 0) in medium 1 and C = (c, −b, 0) in medium 2, with a, b > 0 and c the horizontal separation (Brij Lal Fig. 2.9, PDF p. 40, with c in place of the book's separation along the surface). Any path from A to C must cross the boundary at some point K = (x, 0, z). Each of the two legs lies in a uniform medium, so each is straight (module 3), and the optical path is

$$ Δ(x, z) = μ₁ √(x² + a² + z²) + μ₂ √((c − x)² + b² + z²)   (Brij Lal Eq. 2.10) $$

## Derivation

**Step 1, vary z.** ∂Δ/∂z = z (μ₁/AK + μ₂/KC), with AK = √(x² + a² + z²) and KC = √((c − x)² + b² + z²). The bracket is positive, so ∂Δ/∂z = 0 gives **z = 0**. The incident ray, refracted ray and the normal lie in one plane. **First law of refraction.**

**Step 2, vary x (z = 0).**

$$ ∂Δ/∂x = μ₁ x/√(x² + a²) − μ₂ (c − x)/√((c − x)² + b²) = 0 $$

The first fraction is sin i (the sine of the angle between AK and the normal); the second is sin r. Therefore

$$ μ₁ sin i = μ₂ sin r   ,   so   sin i / sin r = μ₂/μ₁   (Eq. 2.11) $$

**Second law of refraction: Snell's law.**

**Step 3, minimum.** The second derivative is

$$ ∂²Δ/∂x² = μ₁ a²/AK³ + μ₂ b²/KC³ > 0 $$

so the stationary optical path is a **minimum** for a plane boundary. (The mixed derivative vanishes at z = 0 and ∂²Δ/∂z² = μ₁/AK + μ₂/KC > 0.)

*Interpretation:* sin i/sin r = μ₂/μ₁ = v₁/v₂, the same ratio that Huygens' construction gave in PH-042 module 5; here it arises as the balance between the "cost" μ₁ sin i of moving the crossing point along the boundary on the A side and the matching cost μ₂ sin r on the C side.

*Dimension check:* both terms of ∂Δ/∂x are dimensionless (μ times a ratio of lengths).

## Numbers

For the module 2 values (a = 6, b = 4, c = 10, μ₁ = 1, μ₂ = 4/3) the stationary point is x* = 7.184 cm, with i = 50.13°, r = 35.14° and μ₁ sin i = μ₂ sin r = 0.7675. The minimum optical path is 15.882 cm, against 16.028 cm on the straight line AC and 20.360 cm directly below A. The real ray bends towards the normal on entering the denser medium (r < i).

## Boundary cases

- **Normal incidence** (c = 0): x* = 0, i = r = 0, no bending for any μ₁, μ₂.
- **Equal indices** (μ₁ = μ₂): r = i, and the straight line AC is the least-time path (module 3).
- **Grazing incidence** (i → 90°) into a denser medium (μ₂ > μ₁): sin r → μ₁/μ₂, the largest possible refraction angle; for air to μ = 1.5 it is arcsin(1/1.5) = 41.81°.
- **Dense to rare** (μ₁ > μ₂): for any A in medium 1 and C in medium 2, Δ(x) is convex and always has a minimum, so every such pair is joined by a refracted path. The limit is on the incident ray: if sin i > μ₂/μ₁, the law would need sin r = (μ₁/μ₂) sin i > 1, so an incident ray beyond the critical angle has no transmitted partner and is totally reflected (the critical-angle case in PH-042 module 7).

### 6. Core: parabolic and elliptical mirrors, and why the word "least" is not enough

*Objective:* Use Fermat's principle on a parabolic and an elliptical mirror, and show with an ellipse and two comparison mirrors that the real path can be a minimum, a maximum or constant.

## Parabolic mirror

A **parabola** is the set of points whose distance from a fixed point F (the **focus**) equals their distance from a fixed line (the **directrix**). Brij Lal (PDF p. 41, section 2.7) uses this to show that rays parallel to the axis of a parabolic mirror all pass through F.

1. Let Q′ be any mirror point and N′ the foot of the perpendicular from Q′ to the directrix. Then Q′N′ = Q′F by definition.
2. The path from a point P to Q′ to F has length PQ′ + Q′F = PQ′ + Q′N′, the length of a broken path from P to the directrix.
3. The shortest distance from P to a line is the perpendicular PN, so PQ′ + Q′N′ ≥ PN, with equality only when Q′ lies on the segment PN.
4. The axis is perpendicular to the directrix, so PN is parallel to the axis: the least-time ray from P to F meets the mirror where PQ is **parallel to the axis**. By reversibility (module 3), rays from F leave parallel to the axis.

*Consequence:* every point on a line perpendicular to the axis gives the same total PN, so all parallel rays from one plane take equal times to F: a plane wavefront becomes a converging spherical one (bridge B2). Numbers for y² = 4x are in masterNotes.

## Elliptical mirror: a stationary path

An **ellipse** is the set of points for which the sum of distances from two foci F₁ and F₂ is constant (2a, the major axis). A ray from F₁ reflected anywhere on an elliptical mirror reaches F₂ along a path of length 2a, so all take the same time: the optical path is stationary, neither a minimum nor a maximum (Brij Lal section 2.8, PDF pp. 41 to 42). The law i = r then holds at every point, consistent with module 4, because stationarity along the mirror is exactly the reflection law. *Example:* foci (±3, 0) and semi-axes 5 and 4 give F₁O + OF₂ = 10 at every point (checked values in masterNotes).

## A flatter mirror: minimum (section 2.8.1)

Replace the ellipse near O by a **flatter** tangent mirror, so that its neighbouring points M′ lie **outside** the ellipse. Let F₁M′ cut the ellipse at M″. Since F₁O + OF₂ = F₁M″ + M″F₂,

$$ Δ = (F₁O + OF₂) − (F₁M′ + M′F₂) = M″F₂ − (M″M′ + M′F₂) < 0 $$

because in triangle M′F₂M″ the sum of two sides exceeds the third (Eq. 2.12a). The real path is shorter than all neighbours: a **minimum**. Example: the plane mirror y = 4 tangent at O = (0, 4): the path via (x, 4) is 10.000 at x = 0 and larger elsewhere (values in masterNotes).

## A more curved mirror: maximum (section 2.8.2)

If the surface is **more curved** than the ellipse, its neighbouring points M′ lie inside the ellipse. Extend F₁M′ to meet the ellipse at M″ beyond M′. Then

$$ Δ = M″F₂ − (M′F₂ − M′M″) > 0 $$

since the difference of two sides of a triangle is less than the third (Eq. 2.12b). The real path is longer than every neighbour: a **maximum**. Example: a circle of radius 4 about the origin passes through O = (0, 4), where the ellipse has radius of curvature a²/b = 6.25, so the circle is more curved. The path via the circle point at angle φ is 10.000 at φ = 90° and smaller at every other angle tested: every neighbour is shorter (values in masterNotes). (A concrete case of the spherical-reflector maximum in Brij Lal PDF p. 37; numbers recomputed.)

*Conclusion:* the three cases agree with one rule, that the real ray makes the optical path **stationary** (module 2).

### 7. Core: refraction at a spherical surface from the optical path

*Objective:* Derive μ₁/u + μ₂/v = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R for a spherical refracting surface by making the optical path stationary, check that the stationary condition is Snell's law, and apply the formula with a sign convention.

## Set-up (Brij Lal section 2.9, PDF pp. 42 to 44)

A spherical surface of radius R separates medium 1 (index μ₁, left) from medium 2 (index μ₂, right); its centre of curvature C is on the right, so the surface bulges towards the light. An object O lies on the axis at OM = u from the vertex M and its image I at MI = v. In this first derivation u, v and R are positive magnitudes (real object, real image). P is the point where an off-axis ray meets the sphere, with ∠MCP = θ.

**Idea.** If every ray from O is to meet at I, the optical path from O to I must be the same for all of them, and for each ray stationary (Fermat). The axial ray OMI is the reference.

**Lengths.** OC = u + R and CP = R, so the cosine rule in triangle OCP gives

$$ OP² = (u + R)² + R² − 2R(u + R) cos θ   (Eq. 2.14) $$

and CI = v − R with the angle at C in triangle PCI equal to π − θ, so

$$ PI² = R² + (v − R)² + 2R(v − R) cos θ   (Eq. 2.15) $$

The optical path is L(θ) = μ₁ OP + μ₂ PI (Eq. 2.16).

## The exact stationary condition

With d(OP)/dθ = R(u + R) sin θ / OP and d(PI)/dθ = −R(v − R) sin θ / PI,

$$ dL/dθ = R sin θ [ μ₁ (u + R)/OP − μ₂ (v − R)/PI ] = 0 $$

Discarding sin θ = 0 (the axial ray), the exact condition is μ₁(u + R)/OP = μ₂(v − R)/PI.

**This is Snell's law.** The sine rule in triangle OCP gives (u + R)/OP = sin(∠OPC)/sin θ, and sin(∠OPC) = sin i because the two angles are equal or supplementary. In triangle PCI, (v − R)/PI = sin r / sin θ. So μ₁ sin i / sin θ = μ₂ sin r / sin θ, that is μ₁ sin i = μ₂ sin r.

## The paraxial approximation

For rays near the axis θ is small and cos θ ≈ 1. Then OP² ≈ (u + R)² + R² − 2R(u + R) = u² and PI² ≈ R² + (v − R)² + 2R(v − R) = v², so OP ≈ u and PI ≈ v, and the condition becomes μ₁(u + R)/u = μ₂(v − R)/v (Eq. 2.17). Expand, μ₁ + μ₁R/u = μ₂ − μ₂R/v, divide by R and rearrange:

$$ μ₁/u + μ₂/v = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R   (Eq. 2.18) $$

*Second-order check.* Expanding to θ², L(θ) − L(0) ≈ (R²θ²/2)[μ₁/u + μ₂/v − (μ₂ − μ₁)/R]. The bracket vanishes exactly when Eq. 2.18 holds: all paraxial rays then have equal optical path, which is what an image means. For larger θ the rays miss I slightly (spherical aberration).

## Sign convention (signed form)

Measure distances from the vertex M with the direction of the incident light positive: a real object has u negative, a real image on the far side has v positive, and R is positive when C lies on the far side. Then

$$ μ₂/v − μ₁/u = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R $$

For μ₁ = 1 and μ₂ = μ this is μ/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)/R, the form Brij Lal prints as Eq. 2.19. *Dimension check:* each term is dimensionless over length.

## Numbers

μ₁ = 1, μ₂ = 1.5, R = +10 cm, u = −30 cm gives v = 90 cm; u → −∞ gives v = 30 cm and v → ∞ gives u = −20 cm. The numerical path test and the limiting cases (μ₂ = μ₁; R → ∞, apparent depth) are in masterNotes.

### 8. Core: the thin lens formula from equal optical paths (the 2020 deduction)

*Objective:* Deduce 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) by equating the optical paths of the axial ray and an edge ray, state every approximation and sign, and verify it by chaining two surfaces and by numbers.

## Set-up (Brij Lal section 2.10, PDF pp. 44 to 45)

A thin convex lens has surfaces of radii R₁ (facing the object) and R₂, first taken as positive magnitudes. Object O is on the axis at distance u on the left, image I at distance v on the right. Consider two paths: the **axial ray** O-A-I through the thickest part of the lens, and the **edge ray** O-B-I touching the rim B at height h. At the rim the thickness is essentially zero. If the surfaces have depths Δ₁ and Δ₂ (from the plane of the rim to each vertex), the axial thickness is Δ₁ + Δ₂. Measure u from the first vertex and v from the second (the lens is thin, so the vertices are almost at one place).

## Derivation

**Step 1: optical paths.** The axial ray travels the air distances plus μ times the glass thickness; the edge ray has horizontal legs u + Δ₁ and v + Δ₂ and vertical offset h on each side:

$$ cT₁ = u + μ(Δ₁ + Δ₂) + v   (Eq. 2.20) $$
$$ cT₂ = √((u + Δ₁)² + h²) + √((v + Δ₂)² + h²)   (Eq. 2.21) $$

**Step 2: equate.** For a bundle of rays that meet at an image point the optical path is the same for every ray (bridge B2, module 7), so T₁ = T₂ (Eq. 2.22).

**Step 3: paraxial approximation.** For h ≪ P the binomial expansion gives √(P² + h²) = P√(1 + h²/P²) ≈ P + h²/(2P). Apply it with P = u + Δ₁ and P = v + Δ₂ (Eqs. 2.23, 2.24).

**Step 4: substitute.** The u and v terms cancel and μ(Δ₁ + Δ₂) − (Δ₁ + Δ₂) remains:

$$ (μ − 1)(Δ₁ + Δ₂) = (h²/2)[1/(u + Δ₁) + 1/(v + Δ₂)]   (Eq. 2.25) $$

The lens is thin and paraxial, so Δ₁ ≪ u and Δ₂ ≪ v:

$$ (μ − 1)(Δ₁ + Δ₂) = (h²/2)(1/u + 1/v)   (Eq. 2.26) $$

**Step 5: depths from radii.** A sphere of radius R has depth Δ = R − √(R² − h²) at height h; for h ≪ R, √(R² − h²) ≈ R(1 − h²/(2R²)), so Δ ≈ h²/(2R). Hence

$$ Δ₁ + Δ₂ = (h²/2)(1/R₁ + 1/R₂)   (Eq. 2.27) $$

**Step 6: result.** Substitute into Eq. 2.26 and cancel h²/2:

$$ 1/u + 1/v = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ + 1/R₂)   (Eq. 2.28, magnitudes) $$

**Step 7: signs.** Take distances from the lens, with the direction of the incident light positive: a real object has u negative, a real image v positive, R₁ positive (centre on the far side) and R₂ negative for a biconvex lens. Replacing u by −u and R₂ by −R₂ in the magnitude form:

$$ 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)   (Eq. 2.29) $$

This is the **thin lens formula**. Putting v → ∞ defines the focal length through 1/f = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), so 1/v − 1/u = 1/f.

*Checks:* every term has dimension 1/length; a flat plate (R₁, R₂ → ∞) or μ = 1 gives no focusing.

## Cross-check: two surfaces in succession

Apply the signed surface formula of module 7 twice. Surface 1 (air to glass): μ/v′ − 1/u = (μ − 1)/R₁. Surface 2 (glass to air) takes that image, at v′ on the right, as its object: 1/v − μ/v′ = (1 − μ)/R₂. Adding cancels μ/v′ and gives 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), the same formula without the edge ray.

## Numbers

μ = 1.5, R₁ = +20 cm, R₂ = −20 cm gives f = 20 cm; for u = −60 cm, v = +30 cm. The surface-by-surface check and the edge-ray path test are in masterNotes.

### 9. Master: why a stationary path, non-uniform media, and where the principle needs care

*Objective:* Explain physically why light follows a stationary optical path, extend the principle to media whose refractive index changes with position, and list what Fermat's principle does not say.

## Why should light follow a stationary path?

Fermat's principle is a rule about rays, but light is a wave. The link (standard physics; Brij Lal's extract states the principle without this argument, so treat the following as a reading to verify) is the relation between optical path and phase from bridge B2: the phase gained along a path is 2πΔ/λ₀.

Suppose many nearby paths join P to Q. If Δ differs from path to path, the waves arriving along them have different phases and, added together, largely cancel. Near a **stationary** path, however, neighbouring paths have almost the same Δ (the flat bottom of the curve in module 2), so their phases agree and their contributions reinforce. The light that arrives is therefore the light that travelled along paths close to the stationary one.

*Numbers (recomputed).* For a plane mirror with a = b = 1 m and d = 2 m the reflection point is x* = 1 m and d²L/dx² = 0.7071 m⁻¹. Moving a distance s along the mirror changes L by ½(0.7071)s². With λ = 550 nm this reaches half a wavelength at s = √(λ/0.7071) = 0.88 mm, and at s = 1 cm the path has changed by 64.3 wavelengths. Across the plane of incidence (the z direction) the curvature is 1/AP + 1/PB = 1.414 m⁻¹, so the same test gives 0.62 mm. So only a patch within about 0.88 mm of the reflection point along the plane of incidence (0.62 mm across it), a patch about 1 to 2 mm across, contributes in step; the rest cancels. That patch is tiny compared with ordinary mirrors, which is why the ray picture works so well (the wave treatment of this patch belongs to the diffraction topics).

## Non-uniform media

If μ changes from point to point, the principle still reads δ∫μ dL = 0 along the real path, but the real path is generally curved. A stack of parallel layers shows the simplest case. Let θ_k be the angle between the ray and the normal in layer k. At each flat interface, Snell's law (module 5) gives μ_k sin θ_k = μ_(k+1) sin θ_(k+1). Chaining these across all layers,

$$ μ sin θ = constant along the whole ray $$

For a continuous variation μ(y) with layers thinner and thinner, the same product stays fixed. A ray entering a region where μ falls towards the ground (hot air near a road) therefore bends so that sin θ grows as μ shrinks, and may be turned back up: a mirage. This conclusion is standard physics, not in the supplied extract (verify); the layer argument needs only module 5.

## What Fermat's principle does not say

1. **Amount of light.** It gives paths, not intensities. How much light is reflected and how much transmitted depends on Fresnel's coefficients and polarisation (later topics); PH-050 uses amplitude reflection coefficients for films.
2. **Wave effects.** When apertures or obstacles are comparable with the wavelength, diffraction occurs and the ray picture fails (the Airy pattern is treated in Day 17 lesson PH-057).
3. **Which kind of extremum.** The principle does not say minimum; it says stationary (module 6).
4. **Direction-dependent media.** In anisotropic or moving media the ray direction and the wave-normal direction differ, and reversibility can fail (standard; verify).
5. **Neighbouring paths only.** The comparison is with nearby paths, not with all conceivable routes; two different stationary paths can exist (for example a direct and a reflected ray).

## Comparison with the Huygens route (PH-042)

PH-042 obtained the same two laws from wavefronts; the methods agree because (standard physics) a Huygens envelope is a surface of equal optical path. Use the method the question names (PH-042 module 8 makes the same warning).

### Notes: Definitions

- Optical path length: Delta = integral of mu dL along a path; in one medium Delta = mu L; time t = Delta/c.
- Fermat's principle (least time form): between two points light follows a path of least time (Fermat, as reported by Brij Lal).
- Fermat's principle of extremum (stationary) path: the real path has a time that is a minimum, a maximum or stationary compared with all neighbouring paths; dt/ds = 0 or d(Delta)/ds = 0.
- Rectilinear propagation: in a homogeneous medium light travels in straight lines, because the straight line has the least optical path.
- Reversibility: a ray that has gone from P to Q along a path will go from Q to P along the same path.
- Image point: a point where all rays of a bundle from one object point meet with equal optical path.

### Notes: Equations to know

- t = (1/c) integral mu dL = Delta/c (Eqs. 2.1 to 2.5).
- Plane mirror: L = sqrt(x^2 + a^2 + z^2) + sqrt((d - x)^2 + b^2 + z^2); dL/dz = 0 gives z = 0; dL/dx = 0 gives sin i = sin r.
- Refraction: Delta = mu1 sqrt(x^2 + a^2 + z^2) + mu2 sqrt((c - x)^2 + b^2 + z^2); result mu1 sin i = mu2 sin r.
- Spherical surface (magnitudes): mu1/u + mu2/v = (mu2 - mu1)/R (Eq. 2.18); signed form mu2/v - mu1/u = (mu2 - mu1)/R.
- Thin lens: 1/u + 1/v = (mu - 1)(1/R1 + 1/R2) in magnitudes (Eq. 2.28); signed 1/v - 1/u = (mu - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2) (Eq. 2.29).
- Paraxial expansions: sqrt(P^2 + h^2) is about P + h^2/(2P); sag of a sphere at height h is about h^2/(2R).

### Notes: Derivation skeletons

- Reflection: set up L(x, z); take dL/dz = 0 (z = 0, first law); take dL/dx = 0 (sin i = sin r, second law); show d2L/dx2 > 0 (minimum); or use the image B' and AP + PB' >= AB'.
- Refraction: Delta(x, z) = mu1 AK + mu2 KC; dDelta/dz = 0 gives z = 0; dDelta/dx = 0 gives mu1 sin i = mu2 sin r; d2Delta/dx2 > 0.
- Spherical surface: L(theta) = mu1 OP + mu2 PI with cosine rule; dL/dtheta = 0 gives mu1 (u + R)/OP = mu2 (v - R)/PI (this is Snell's law by the sine rule); paraxial OP = u, PI = v gives Eq. 2.18.
- Thin lens: cT1 = u + mu(D1 + D2) + v; cT2 = sqrt((u + D1)^2 + h^2) + sqrt((v + D2)^2 + h^2); equate; binomial; D1 = h^2/2R1, D2 = h^2/2R2; get 1/u + 1/v = (mu - 1)(1/R1 + 1/R2); apply signs.

### Notes: Minimum, maximum or constant

- Test: compare the optical path through the real point with that through neighbouring points of the same mirror; the mirror's curvature against the ellipse with the same two foci decides the case (Brij Lal 2.8, 2.8.1, 2.8.2).

### Notes: Boundary cases and checks

- Normal incidence: i = r = 0, a ray retraces itself (reflection) or passes undeviated (refraction).
- Grazing incidence from air into mu = 1.5: largest refraction angle arcsin(1/1.5) = 41.81 deg.
- Dense to rare: for any A in medium 1 and C in medium 2 the optical path is convex in x and always has a minimum; an incident ray with sin i above mu2/mu1 has no transmitted partner and is totally reflected (PH-042 module 7).
- Mirror too small: the stationary point lies off the edge, so no ordinary reflected ray joins the two points.
- Spherical surface and lens formulas hold for paraxial rays only; non-paraxial rays show spherical aberration (fourth-order path error).
- Reversibility is about the path, not the intensity; moving or direction-dependent media can break it (verify).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Least time versus shortest distance: the two coincide only in a uniform medium (Hero's reflection); refraction shows they differ.
- Minimum versus stationary: every minimum is stationary, but stationary also includes a maximum and a constant.
- Angles of incidence and refraction are measured from the normal, not from the surface.
- v in the surface and lens formulas is an image distance; v in dt = dL/v is the speed of light in the medium.
- Huygens' construction (PH-042) and Fermat's principle give the same laws but a question that names one method must be answered by it.

  - t = Delta/c with Delta = integral mu dL.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Define the optical path length and give the relation between it and the travel time.
    - **a:** Delta = integral of mu dL along the path; in one medium Delta = mu L. The time is t = Delta/c.
  - - **q:** Why does Hero's shortest-path principle fail for refraction?
    - **a:** At a boundary the straight line AC is the shortest route but light bends there; the real path is longer in distance and shorter in time because light is slower in the denser medium.
  - - **q:** What is meant by a stationary value of the optical path?
    - **a:** A value at which the slope of the optical path against the path parameter is zero: a minimum, a maximum or a level inflection.
  - - **q:** In the plane-mirror derivation what do the two conditions dL/dz = 0 and dL/dx = 0 each give?
    - **a:** dL/dz = 0 gives z = 0 (coplanarity, first law); dL/dx = 0 gives sin i = sin r, so i = r (second law).
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Explain why an elliptical mirror makes the optical path stationary rather than a minimum or maximum.
    - **a:** Every point O of the ellipse has F1 O + O F2 equal to the same constant 2a, so all reflected paths from F1 to F2 take the same time; the path is constant, hence stationary.
  - - **q:** Why does reversibility of light rays follow from Fermat's principle, and what does it not guarantee?
    - **a:** Delta = integral of mu dL does not depend on the direction of traversal, so the stationary path is the same from either end. It guarantees the path only, not equal intensity along the way back.
  - - **q:** Why must the thin-lens derivation equate the optical paths of the axial and edge rays, and which approximations make the equation solvable?
    - **a:** Both rays go from O to the image point I, so for an image to form their optical paths must be equal (stationary for the bundle). The paraxial approximation h much smaller than u and v (binomial expansion) and the sag D = h^2/(2R) turn it into the lens formula.
  - - **q:** How does a mirror that is more curved than the ellipse through the same foci change the character of the real path?
    - **a:** Its neighbouring points lie inside the ellipse, where the sum of distances to the foci is less than 2a, so the real path is longer than its neighbours: a maximum.
- **application:**

### Memory skeleton

- Optical path Δ = ∫μ dL, t = Δ/c; Fermat: the real path makes Δ stationary (minimum, maximum or constant)
- Consequences: rectilinear propagation and reversibility
- Reflection: L(x, z); ∂L/∂z = 0 gives coplanarity, ∂L/∂x = 0 gives i = r, second derivative > 0 gives a minimum
- Refraction: same steps on μ₁ AK + μ₂ KC give μ₁ sin i = μ₂ sin r; least time is not shortest distance
- Ellipse test: mirror flatter than the ellipse gives a minimum, the ellipse a constant, more curved a maximum
- Spherical surface: L(θ) = μ₁ OP + μ₂ PI, paraxial OP = u, PI = v gives μ₂/v − μ₁/u = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R
- Thin lens: equate axial and edge-ray optical paths, sag h²/2R, signs give 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

### Last-week summary

- Δ = ∫μ dL, t = Δ/c; the real path has stationary Δ (minimum, maximum or constant), not just 'least'.
- Reflection and refraction: ∂Δ/∂z = 0 → z = 0 (coplanar, first law); ∂Δ/∂x = 0 → sin i = sin r or μ₁ sin i = μ₂ sin r; ∂²Δ/∂x² > 0 → minimum.
- Ellipse with the same foci: F₁O + OF₂ = 2a everywhere; flatter mirror → minimum, more curved mirror → maximum.
- Surface: μ₂/v − μ₁/u = (μ₂ − μ₁)/R (paraxial). Lens: sag h²/2R; 1/v − 1/u = (μ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), u and R₂ negative for a real object and biconvex lens.
