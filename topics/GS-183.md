### Bridge: What is a presiding officer?

Any meeting with many people needs someone to run it: a class has a teacher, a cricket match has an umpire. In a legislature, the person who runs the meeting is the **presiding officer**. He decides who may speak and in what order, keeps order, applies the written rules of the House (the **Rules of Procedure**), explains them when members disagree, counts or oversees the votes and announces the result.

A presiding officer is meant to be like an umpire: he is not a player. He does not argue for the government or for the Opposition during the debate. That is why the rules stop him from taking part in ordinary votes except to break a tie, and why so many constitutional safeguards exist for his independence.

Each House of Parliament has its own presiding officer: the Speaker for the Lok Sabha and the Chairman for the Rajya Sabha. Each also has a deputy to take over when the presiding officer is absent. The words you will use often are **chair** (the seat from which the presiding officer conducts the sitting, so 'in the chair' means 'presiding'), **House** (the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha) and **sitting** (a single meeting of the House on one day).

### Bridge: Deliberative vote against casting vote

A **deliberative vote** (also called an ordinary vote) is the vote that every member has on every question: he is one of the voters and his vote counts like anyone else's. A **casting vote** is a special extra vote that the presiding officer has **only when the votes are exactly equal**. It is not added to the count at the start; it is used only to decide a tie.

For the Speaker of the Lok Sabha the rule is: he **does not vote in the first instance** (he has no deliberative vote on ordinary questions) and he has a **casting vote** only if the House divides equally. GS-175 (bridge module B2) explains the first instance with a numerical example; keep the idea that the casting vote is a **tie-breaker, not a second vote**.

There is one special case. When a resolution to remove the Speaker himself is under consideration, the rule changes: he may **vote in the first instance**, because the vote concerns him as a member, but he has **no casting vote** if the votes are equal. In other words, in his own removal case he gets a deliberative vote and loses the casting vote; in all other cases it is the reverse. This mirrored rule is a favourite examination trap.

### Bridge: Majority of all the then members against majority present and voting

A **simple majority** means more than half of the members **present and voting** on the day. If 400 members vote and 100 are absent, a simple majority is more than half of 400, that is 201.

A **majority of all the then members** (sometimes called an effective majority) means more than half of **all the members the House has at that time**, whoever is present, and not counting vacant seats. Contrast: an absolute majority means more than half of the total membership including vacancies (543 seats: 272 even if some are vacant); do not confuse the two. If a 543-seat Lok Sabha has no vacancy, that is more than half of 543, which is 272 (543 ÷ 2 = 271.5, so the next whole number). If three seats are vacant, the House has 540 'then members' and the majority is 271.

Why the difference matters: a majority of all the then members is harder to reach, because absentees count against the motion. It is used for important matters such as removing the Speaker. Note the phrase 'then' members: vacancies are excluded; it is not the number of seats on paper (543) but the number of sitting members. A common slip is to write 'a majority of the members present', which is the simple majority.

### 1. Orientation: what a presiding officer is and where the Speaker fits

*Objective:* Explain what a presiding officer does, name the presiding officers of both Houses, and list the three sources of the Speaker's powers and the Articles map.

## What is a presiding officer?

A House of Parliament with hundreds of members cannot run without someone to keep order, give the floor to speakers, interpret the rules and call votes. That person is the **presiding officer**. Laxmikanth says each House has its own: the **Lok Sabha** has a **Speaker** and a **Deputy Speaker**; the **Rajya Sabha** has a **Chairman** and a **Deputy Chairman**. In addition, a **panel of chairpersons** (Lok Sabha) and a **panel of vice-chairpersons** (Rajya Sabha) are appointed so that someone can always take the chair.

The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha is the Vice-President, and GS-175 (module 7 and the master-note table) already compares the Speaker with the Chairman. This lesson therefore concentrates on the **Speaker** and the **Deputy Speaker** of the Lok Sabha and links to GS-175 instead of repeating that comparison.

## The Speaker in one paragraph

Laxmikanth describes the Speaker as the head of the Lok Sabha and its representative, the **guardian of the powers and privileges** of members, the House as a whole and its committees, the principal spokesman of the House. His decision in all parliamentary matters is final. He is, in Laxmikanth's words, much more than merely a presiding officer: he has vast, varied and vital responsibilities and supreme authority within the House.

## Three sources of the Speaker's powers

1. The **Constitution of India**.
2. The **Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business of the Lok Sabha**.
3. **Parliamentary conventions**: residuary, unwritten powers not specified in the Rules.

Remember this three-part split. Many confusions (for example whether the Speaker must resign from his party, or whether the Deputy Speaker must be from the Opposition) disappear once you ask: is it in the Constitution, in the Rules, or only a convention?

## The Articles map (standard knowledge; verify the numbers)

Laxmikanth's extract gives the content but not the Article numbers of this part. The numbers below are standard (verify against the official text):

- **Article 93**: the Lok Sabha chooses a Speaker and a Deputy Speaker.
- **Article 94**: vacation, resignation and removal.
- **Article 95**: the Deputy Speaker's power to perform the Speaker's duties.
- **Article 96**: neither presides while a resolution for his own removal is under consideration.
- **Article 97**: salaries and allowances.
- **Article 100**: voting in the House (the first-instance and casting-vote rule).

For State legislatures, see module 8.

### 2. Election, term and the Speaker Pro Tem

*Objective:* State how the Speaker is elected, how long he stays, when he vacates, why he stays through dissolution, and the role of the Speaker Pro Tem.

## Election

The Speaker is **elected by the Lok Sabha from amongst its members**, as soon as may be after its first sitting. Whenever the office falls vacant, the House elects another member. The **date of election is fixed by the President** (Laxmikanth). The election is therefore an internal act of the House, not an appointment from outside. Compare the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, who is ex officio the Vice-President and is not elected by the House at all (GS-175).

## Term and the three ways of vacating

Usually the Speaker remains in office during the **life of the Lok Sabha**. He vacates earlier in three cases:

1. he **ceases to be a member** of the Lok Sabha;
2. he **resigns** by writing to the **Deputy Speaker**; or
3. he is **removed** by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the House, after 14 days' notice (module 3).

Because the Speaker can resign in writing to the Deputy Speaker, and the Deputy Speaker to the Speaker, each post has a built-in channel for resignation.

## The dissolution point

When the Lok Sabha is **dissolved** (ended before or at the end of its term so that fresh elections can be held), the Speaker does **not vacate his office**. He continues until the newly elected Lok Sabha meets. In constitutional language, the Speaker of the last House vacates his office **immediately before the first meeting of the new House**. This is a high-frequency Prelims fact: the 2025 question tested exactly this. The point is continuity: the House's presiding office is never left empty until the new House assembles. This continuity proviso covers only the Speaker: the Deputy Speaker, like any member, vacates his office when the House is dissolved because he ceases to be a member (Article 94(a); verify Article text).

## The Speaker Pro Tem

Since the old Speaker leaves just before the first meeting, someone must preside over that first sitting. Laxmikanth explains that **the President appoints a member of the Lok Sabha as Speaker Pro Tem** ('pro tem' means 'for the time being'). Usually the **seniormost member** is chosen. Laxmikanth says the President himself administers the oath to the Speaker Pro Tem (in practice the President may authorise a person to do so; verify).

- The Speaker Pro Tem has **all the powers of the Speaker**.
- He presides over the first sitting of the new House.
- His **main duty** is to administer the oath to the new members; he also enables the House to elect the new Speaker.
- When the new Speaker is elected, the office of Speaker Pro Tem **ceases to exist**; it lasts only a few days.

## No separate oath

Laxmikanth adds that the Speaker and Deputy Speaker, when assuming office, do **not make or subscribe any separate oath or affirmation**. They have already taken the oath as members.

## Flag: recent elections

Standard knowledge, reported and to be verified from a current source: in June 2024 the 18th Lok Sabha re-elected Om Birla as Speaker by voice vote after a contest, the first contest for the office in many decades. Do not quote the detail without checking.

### 3. Resignation and removal: Article 94(c), 14 days' notice and the voting rule

*Objective:* State exactly how the Speaker can be removed, compute the votes needed, and state what the Speaker may and may not do while his own removal motion is pending.

## The rule in three parts

Laxmikanth states the removal procedure as follows. The Speaker can be removed by a **resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Lok Sabha**. Such a resolution can be moved only after **14 days' advance notice** (this is Article 94(c) with its proviso; the number is standard knowledge, verify). Laxmikanth adds, as a safeguard for the Speaker, that the motion of removal can be considered and discussed only when it has the support of **at least 50 members** (a rule of the House; the Rules number is not in the extract).

**Majority of all the then members** is a **special** (stronger) majority than a simple majority of those present and voting. Bridge module B3 explains the difference in detail.

## The arithmetic (checked by computation)

Suppose the Lok Sabha has **543** members and no seat is vacant. A majority of all the then members is more than half of 543, that is **272** (543 ÷ 2 = 271.5). Suppose instead there are **540 sitting members** because three seats are vacant: 540 ÷ 2 = 270, and a majority needs more than half, so **271** votes. Exactly 270 is only half, not a majority. Whatever the attendance on the day, the figure is counted against **all the then members**, not against the members who happen to be present.

## What the Speaker may do while his removal is pending

While the resolution is under consideration the Speaker **cannot preside** at the sitting, though he may be **present**. He **can speak and take part in the proceedings** and **vote in the first instance**, but **not in the case of an equality of votes**, so he has no casting vote (Laxmikanth; Article 96, standard knowledge, verify).

This is the 2024 question in one sentence: **cannot preside**, **can speak**, **can vote only in the first instance**. A learner who writes that he may not speak or may not vote at all has confused the Speaker with the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, who, when his own removal is pending as Vice-President, cannot preside and may speak and take part but **cannot vote at all** (GS-175 module 7 has the comparison).

## Resignation and the Deputy Speaker's removal

The Speaker resigns by writing to the **Deputy Speaker**; the Deputy Speaker resigns by writing to the **Speaker**. The Deputy Speaker is removed by the same kind of resolution (majority of all the then members, 14 days' notice) and while a resolution for **his** removal is under consideration he also cannot preside, though he may be present (Laxmikanth).

## Why the safeguards exist

Laxmikanth lists them under independence (module 6): a Speaker who could be removed by a simple majority of those present could be driven out by the ruling party on a thin night. The special majority, the notice period and the 50-member support make removal difficult and, therefore, the Speaker's independence real. The same stronger-majority idea appears for other offices, so keep the phrase 'effective majority' (a majority of all the then members, excluding vacancies) in mind: it is used again for the Vice-President (GS-175).

## When has it been used?

The extract does not discuss particular removal motions. Standard knowledge (verify): motions for removal have been moved in the Lok Sabha on a few occasions, and none has succeeded. This is context only; do not quote numbers.

### 4. Powers and duties of the Speaker

*Objective:* List the Speaker's ten powers and duties from Laxmikanth, group them, and link each to the lesson where it is developed.

## The ten items (Laxmikanth)

Laxmikanth lists these powers and duties. To avoid a flat list, they are grouped below.

**A. Running the House**

1. **Maintains order and decorum** in the House for conducting business and regulating proceedings. This is his primary responsibility and his power here is final.
2. **Final interpreter**, within the House, of the Constitution, the Rules of Procedure and the parliamentary precedents.
3. **Adjourns the House or suspends the meeting** when there is no quorum. **Quorum** (the minimum number of members whose presence is needed to transact business) is **one-tenth of the total strength** of the House (Article 100(3), standard knowledge, verify). For a 543-member House, one-tenth is 54.3, so the practical figure is stated as 55; verify the rounding in the Rules.
4. Can allow a **secret sitting** at the request of the Leader of the House, when no stranger may be present.

**B. Voting**

5. **Does not vote in the first instance**; he exercises a **casting vote** in a tie, to resolve a deadlock. (GS-175, bridge module B2, explains the 'first instance' and the casting vote with a worked example. Here keep one line: the Speaker's vote is a tie-breaker only.)

**C. Special constitutional functions**

6. **Presides over a joint sitting** of both Houses summoned by the President to settle a deadlock on a Bill (Article 108 and Article 118(4), standard knowledge). GS-179, module 3, explains the joint sitting.
7. **Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill**, and his decision is final. He endorses a certificate when the Bill goes to the Rajya Sabha and to the President (Article 110(3)). GS-179, module 5, explains the procedure and the Rojer Mathew (2019) caveat on judicial review of the certificate (verify). The Rajya Sabha Chairman does not have this power.
8. **Decides disqualification of Lok Sabha members on the ground of defection** under the **Tenth Schedule**. The Supreme Court ruled in **1992** that his decision is **subject to judicial review**. Module 5 develops this item.

**D. Institutional and committee roles**

9. He is the **ex-officio chairman of the Indian Parliamentary Group**, the link between the Indian Parliament and the world's parliaments, and the ex-officio chairman of the **conference of presiding officers** of legislative bodies in India.
10. He **appoints the chairmen of all the parliamentary committees of the Lok Sabha** and supervises their functioning. He himself is chairman of the **Business Advisory Committee, the Rules Committee and the General Purposes Committee** (GS-182 explains these committees).

## What 'final' means

The words 'his decision in all parliamentary matters is final' (Laxmikanth) are a statement about the House's own procedure. NCERT says similar things: the presiding officer of the legislature has the final power in deciding matters of breach of privilege and is the final authority in regulating the business of the legislature, and the presiding officer decides anti-defection cases. They are not statements that no court may ever look at a Speaker's decision. Module 5 shows the limit.

## A useful grouping for answers

If a Mains question asks 'Discuss the role of the Speaker', organise your points as **(1) order and procedure, (2) tie-breaking and special constitutional functions, (3) guardian of privileges and committees, (4) the Tenth Schedule**. That four-heading structure covers all ten items in a clean way.

### 5. The Speaker and the Tenth Schedule: Kihoto Hollohan (1992) and judicial review

*Objective:* Explain how the Speaker acts as the deciding authority under the Tenth Schedule, state what the Supreme Court held in 1992, and identify the criticisms that Mains questions raise.

## The Tenth Schedule in three lines

The **Tenth Schedule** (added by the 52nd Amendment, 1985; NCERT cites it as the anti-defection amendment) contains the anti-defection law. The anti-defection lesson itself is a later one and is not yet written; this module covers only the **Speaker's part**. Laxmikanth, on membership of Parliament, says a member incurs disqualification under the defection law in four cases:

1. if he **voluntarily gives up** membership of the party on whose ticket he was elected;
2. if he **votes or abstains** from voting in the House contrary to a direction given by his party;
3. if an **independently elected** member joins any political party; and
4. if a **nominated** member joins a party after six months.

## Who decides? The Speaker

The question of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule is decided by the **Chairman** in the Rajya Sabha and the **Speaker** in the Lok Sabha, **not by the President**. This contrasts with ordinary disqualifications (office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, citizenship), on which the **President** decides after obtaining the opinion of the **Election Commission** (Laxmikanth). NCERT notes that the presiding officer of the House is the authority who takes final decisions on such cases, and that after the **91st Amendment** the disqualified person is also barred from holding a ministerial post (compare GS-176, Article 75(1B)).

## Kihoto Hollohan (1992)

Laxmikanth says: in 1992 the Supreme Court ruled that the decision of the Speaker/Chairman in this regard is **subject to judicial review**. The case is **Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu**, decided in 1992 (the case name is standard knowledge; verify). From standard knowledge (verify with the judgment): a Constitution Bench upheld the Tenth Schedule as a whole, but struck down paragraph 7, which barred courts from reviewing the Speaker's decisions, because an amendment affecting the courts needed ratification by States. The Court treated the Speaker as acting as a **tribunal** in this role, whose decisions can be reviewed on limited grounds (for example violation of constitutional mandate, mala fides, natural justice, perversity) but not at an interlocutory stage before he has decided.

## What happened later (verify before use)

Standard knowledge, flagged: in a 2020 ruling (Keisham Meghachandra Singh) the Supreme Court is reported to have urged Parliament to consider an independent tribunal in place of the Speaker and to have said that Speakers should ordinarily decide disqualification petitions within three months. Courts have also been approached over delay by Speakers in several State Assemblies and in Parliament. These items are reported from memory of public sources; check the judgments before using them in an answer.

## NCERT's verdict

NCERT says that in about twenty years, the anti-defection amendment has not been able to curb defections, but has given **additional powers to party leaders and to the presiding officers** over members. This is the textbook source of the Mains argument that the law **concentrates power in the Speaker**.

## The criticism, in two columns

| Case for the Speaker deciding | Case against |
|---|---|
| He knows the House and Rules; quick resolution; keeps the matter inside Parliament | He belongs to a party and was elected by the majority; the decision can be politically timed; delay can nullify the law |
| Judicial review is available after the decision (Kihoto) | Review comes late; the interim damage is done |

Reform ideas that appear in the Mains answers (to be attributed carefully): decision by the President or Governor on the Election Commission's advice; a time limit for deciding; an independent tribunal; and the idea that the Speaker should be non-partisan (module 6).

### 6. Independence and impartiality: the safeguards, the party question and 'Once a Speaker, always a Speaker'

*Objective:* List the safeguards of the Speaker's independence, explain the British convention against India's practice, and weigh the proposal that the Speaker should leave his party.

## Six safeguards (Laxmikanth)

As the office has great prestige and authority, independence and impartiality are indispensable. Laxmikanth lists these provisions:

1. **Security of tenure**: he can be removed only by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members, not by an ordinary majority; the motion can be discussed only with the support of at least 50 members.
2. **Salary charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**: fixed by Parliament (Article 97); charged on the Consolidated Fund (Article 112(3)(b); verify), so not subject to the annual vote of Parliament. Laxmikanth says the salary and allowances are set under the Salaries and Allowances of Officers of Parliament Act, 1953; the sumptuary allowance and other sums quoted are dated; verify.
3. **His work and conduct cannot be discussed or criticised** in the Lok Sabha except on a **substantive motion**.
4. His powers of regulating procedure, conducting business and maintaining order are **not subject to the jurisdiction of any court** (module 5 limits what that means for the Tenth Schedule role; Article 122, standard knowledge, protects proceedings from being questioned on grounds of irregularity of procedure).
5. He **cannot vote in the first instance**; only a casting vote in a tie.
6. A **very high position in the order of precedence**: Laxmikanth prints 'seventh, along with the Chief Justice of India', higher than all Cabinet ministers except the Prime Minister and Deputy Prime Minister. GS-175 flagged that the official Table of Precedence is stated differently (No. 6; verify).

## The British convention and the Indian practice

In Britain the Speaker is strictly **non-party**: by convention he resigns from his party and remains politically neutral. Laxmikanth says this convention is **not fully established in India**; the Speaker does **not** resign from his party membership on election. The Constitution does not require it either. The 2025 Prelims question stated that the Constitution requires the Speaker to resign from his party immediately; module 9 treats this as a trap. From standard knowledge (verify): the Tenth Schedule exempts a person elected Speaker or Deputy Speaker who voluntarily gives up his party membership, and who rejoins after leaving office, from disqualification; so the law anticipates, but does not require, party-leaving. Examples reported (verify): N. Sanjiva Reddy resigned from his party on being elected Speaker in 1967, and Somnath Chatterjee was expelled from his party in 2008.

## Speaker's conference and 'Once a Speaker, always a Speaker'

The Speaker chairs the **conference of presiding officers** of legislative bodies, a forum of the presiding officers of India's legislative bodies (Laxmikanth). The 2020 Mains question asked whether the practice 'Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker' should be adopted to impart objectivity to the office. The idea, drawn from British practice (standard knowledge; verify), is that a Speaker, once elected, is not opposed by other parties at later elections and stays above party politics.

- **For**: lessens the incentive for partisan behaviour; a Speaker who knows he will not need a party's ticket can act with greater independence; strengthens the institution; supports smooth conduct of business.
- **Against**: it depends on **convention**, which cannot be enforced; in India a Speaker is a member elected on a party ticket from a particular constituency, so asking him to give up his political career may deter able members from taking the post; a party-less Speaker could lose touch with his constituency; and in a multi-party House the convention would need the agreement of every party to work.
- **Middle path** (suggested in discussions): a convention of resigning from the party plus a legal protection, a fixed time-limit for deciding Tenth Schedule petitions, and an independent tribunal for the Tenth Schedule decisions.

## Implications for the functioning of the House

An impartial Speaker helps Opposition members trust the chair, reduces disruptions and strengthens the House's control over the executive (GS-176 module 8). A perceived partial Speaker invites disruptions and weakens that control. This is the link to examine in a Mains answer.

### 7. The Deputy Speaker and the Panel of Chairpersons; the convention on the Opposition

*Objective:* State how the Deputy Speaker is elected and what he does, the Panel of Chairpersons, and the convention that the post goes to the Opposition with the 2019-24 flag.

## Election and term

Like the Speaker, the **Deputy Speaker** is elected by the Lok Sabha itself from amongst its members, after the election of the Speaker. The **date of election of the Deputy Speaker is fixed by the Speaker** (Laxmikanth); contrast the Speaker's election date, fixed by the President. Whenever the post falls vacant the Lok Sabha elects another member. He normally continues during the life of the Lok Sabha and vacates if he **ceases to be a member**, **resigns** (writing to the Speaker), or is **removed** by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members, with 14 days' notice.

## Functions

- He performs the duties of the **Speaker's office when it is vacant**, and acts as Speaker when the Speaker is **absent** from a sitting; in both cases he has **all the powers** of the Speaker. (The Article is 95; standard; verify.)
- He presides over a **joint sitting** if the Speaker is absent.
- He is **not subordinate** to the Speaker: he is **directly responsible to the House**.
- Special privilege: whenever he is **appointed a member of a parliamentary committee**, he automatically becomes its **chairman**.
- While presiding he cannot vote in the first instance; only a casting vote in a tie. When the Speaker presides he is an **ordinary member**, who may speak, take part and vote on any question.
- When a resolution for his own removal is under consideration, he cannot preside though he may be present.
- He receives a salary and allowances fixed by Parliament and charged on the Consolidated Fund.

The 2022 Prelims question included the phrase 'no appeal lies against his rulings'. Standard knowledge (verify): a ruling given from the chair by the Deputy Speaker, when he presides, has the same authority as the Speaker's.

## Panel of Chairpersons

Under the Rules the Speaker nominates a **panel of not more than ten chairpersons**. Any of them can preside in the absence of the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker, with the same powers. A panel member holds office until a new panel is nominated. If no panel member is present, another person determined by the House acts as Speaker. A panel member **cannot preside when the offices of both the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker are vacant**; then the **President** appoints a member to perform the Speaker's duties, and elections are held as soon as possible.

## The convention that the post goes to the Opposition

Laxmikanth: up to the 10th Lok Sabha both the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker were usually from the ruling party; since the 11th Lok Sabha there has been a **consensus** that the Speaker comes from the ruling party (or alliance) and the Deputy Speaker from the main Opposition party. The word is **consensus**, not law. Neither Article 93 nor the Rules requires the Deputy Speaker to come from the Opposition, as the 2017 and 2022 Prelims questions tested. The first, 2017, put this wrongly as a provision of the Constitution. Flag: the claim 'up to the 10th Lok Sabha' is Laxmikanth's generalisation; the practice has not been uniform (verify).

## The 17th Lok Sabha flag

Standard knowledge, reported and to be verified from a current source: no Deputy Speaker was elected during the 17th Lok Sabha (2019-24), which prompted public criticism and litigation, since Article 93 requires the House to choose the Deputy Speaker as soon as may be. The position in the 18th Lok Sabha should be checked. The Constitution makes the Deputy Speaker's election a duty of the House; the party from which he comes is a convention.

## Speaker against Deputy Speaker in one line

Both elected by the House from its members; both removable by the same resolution; the Speaker is first in authority, but the Deputy Speaker is **not his subordinate**; the Deputy Speaker's date of election is fixed by the Speaker; the Speaker's by the President.

### 8. Master layer: State legislatures, history, and Speaker against Chairman

*Objective:* Outline the Speaker in State Assemblies, recall the origin of the office and compare it with the Chairman with a link to GS-175.

## The Speaker in the State legislatures (outline)

A State Legislative Assembly has a **Speaker** and a **Deputy Speaker**; a Legislative Council (in States that have one) has a **Chairman** and a **Deputy Chairman**. The numbers below are standard knowledge (the extract for this lesson does not cover State legislatures; verify against the official text):

- **Article 178**: every Legislative Assembly chooses two members as Speaker and Deputy Speaker.
- **Article 179**: vacation of office, resignation and **removal** (a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Assembly, with 14 days' notice).
- **Article 180**: the Deputy Speaker (or another person) performs the Speaker's duties when the office is vacant.
- **Article 181**: neither presides while a resolution for his own removal is under consideration; the Speaker may speak and vote in the first instance only.

The pattern mirrors Articles 93-96. A State Speaker's role in the anti-defection law is similar to the Lok Sabha Speaker's (the Tenth Schedule applies to State legislatures too; the later anti-defection lesson will treat it). The Council's Chairman and Deputy Chairman are in Articles 182 onwards; they are elected by the Council from its own members. Do not memorise those unless a question demands.

## History of the office (Laxmikanth)

- Before 1921, the **Governor-General** presided over the Central Legislative Council.
- The offices of Speaker and Deputy Speaker originated in India in **1921** under the **Government of India Act of 1919** (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms). They were then called **President** and **Deputy President**, a nomenclature that continued until 1947.
- In 1921 Frederick Whyte and Sachidanand Sinha were appointed by the Governor-General as the first Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly.
- In **1925** Vithalbhai J. Patel became the first Indian and the first elected Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly.
- The Government of India Act of **1935** changed the names to Speaker and Deputy Speaker, but the old names stayed until 1947 since the federal part of the 1935 Act was not implemented.
- **G.V. Mavalankar** and **Ananthasayanam Ayyangar** were the first Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha. Mavalankar had also been Speaker in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) and the provisional Parliament, holding the post continuously from 1946 to 1956 (Laxmikanth).

## Speaker against Chairman (link, not repetition)

The full table is in GS-175 (master notes: 'Speaker versus Chairman of the Rajya Sabha'; module 7). In three lines: the Speaker is **elected by and from** the House, while the Chairman is the **Vice-President, not a member**; only the Speaker certifies **Money Bills** and presides over **joint sittings**; and while his removal is pending the Speaker may **vote in the first instance**, while the Chairman may **not vote at all**.

## Leaders in the House (context)

Laxmikanth adds, after the presiding officers, the **Leader of the House** (the PM if a Lok Sabha member, or a minister nominated by him), the **Leader of the Opposition** (leader of the largest Opposition party with not less than one-tenth of the House; statutory recognition in 1977) and the **Whip** (a convention, in neither the Constitution, Rules nor a statute). They work with the Speaker but are not presiding officers.

## Prelims analysis (no official key is asserted)

**2017 Q76** has two statements. The first says the winning candidate in a Lok Sabha or Assembly election must get at least 50 per cent of the votes polled. Laxmikanth (pp. 495-496) describes the first-past-the-post system as a **simple majority** system: the candidate with the **most votes** among the candidates wins, so 50 per cent is not required (standard knowledge: a plurality). The second says that **according to the Constitution** the Speaker's post goes to the majority party and the Deputy Speaker's to the Opposition. Module 7: it is a convention, a consensus since the 11th Lok Sabha, not a constitutional provision. On this analysis both statements conflict with the lesson.

**2022 Q20** has four statements on the Deputy Speaker. (1) The election date is fixed by the Speaker: matches module 7. (2) A mandatory provision that the Deputy Speaker must be from the principal Opposition or the ruling party: conflicts with module 7 (convention only). (3) The Deputy Speaker has the same power as the Speaker when presiding and no appeal lies against his rulings: Laxmikanth says he assumes all the powers of the Speaker when acting as Speaker; the 'no appeal' wording needs verification. (4) The practice is that the motion is moved by the Speaker and seconded by the Prime Minister: the extract gives no such practice, and the positive reason is that the Deputy Speaker is elected by the House from among its members on a motion moved by a member under the Rules of Procedure, not by a Speaker-mover and Prime Minister-seconder (verify Rule 8; if unsure, treat statement 4 as uncertain on the source alone and rely on statements 1 and 3). The pattern points to statements 1 and 3; this is analysis, not an official key.

**2024 Q93** tests the removal-pending position: cannot preside (true); no right to speak (false, he can speak); not entitled to vote in the first instance (false, he can vote in the first instance, not on a tie). The pattern points to statement 1 only; analysis, not a key.

**2025 Q87** has three statements: (I) on dissolution the Speaker does not vacate until immediately before the first meeting of the new House: matches module 2. (II) A member elected Speaker shall resign from his party immediately according to the Constitution: no such constitutional duty; it is the British convention (module 6). (III) Removal by resolution with a majority of all then members and 14 days' notice: matches module 3. The pattern points to statements I and III.

## Mains frames

**2020 Q5 (10 marks): 'Once a Speaker, always a Speaker'.** Introduction: Speaker's role and British practice. Body: for and against (module 6); implications for parliamentary business (trust of the Opposition, fewer disruptions, institutional stability); alternatives (convention, time-limit for Tenth Schedule decisions). Conclusion: neutrality needs both convention and law.

**2026 Q3 (10 marks): Speaker, partisan politics and the anti-defection regime.** Introduction: Tenth Schedule and the Speaker's tribunal role (Kihoto 1992; module 5). Body: where vulnerability arises (party affiliation, delay, selective decisions; NCERT's remark that the law empowers presiding officers and party leaders); institutional changes (time-bound decisions, independent tribunal, post-election resignation convention, Speaker's conference, review by the Election Commission or the courts). ## Traps

- Speaker 'must resign from his party' (convention, not law).
- Speaker vacates on dissolution (he does not, until just before the first meeting).
- Majority of members present (it is a majority of all the then members).
- The removal-pending Speaker 'cannot speak' or 'cannot vote' at all.
- The Deputy Speaker 'must be from the Opposition' or is 'subordinate to the Speaker'.
- Mixing the Speaker with the Chairman (Money Bill certificate, joint sitting, removal rule).

## MCQ thought aloud

**Question.** With reference to the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, consider: (1) he can be removed by a resolution passed by a simple majority of the members present and voting; (2) he continues in office after dissolution until immediately before the first meeting of the new House; (3) he must be a member of the party that has the majority. Which are correct?

**Thinking.** Take each statement and ask 'which source?'.

1. Removal: the rule is a majority of **all the then members** with 14 days' notice. 'Simple majority of those present and voting' conflicts with that (module 3). Cross out statement 1.
2. Dissolution: module 2 says he does not vacate; he continues until immediately before the first meeting of the new House. Statement 2 matches.
3. Party: the Speaker is elected by the House from among its members. There is a convention that the Speaker comes from the ruling party or alliance (module 7), but no requirement; in particular nothing makes him a member of the majority party by law. Cross out statement 3.

The answer is statement 2 only. Notice that the elimination used **positive reasons**, not the absence of a statement in a book.

## Mains skeleton: 'The Deputy Speaker's post should go to the Opposition. Do you agree?' (10 marks, 150 words)

1. **Introduction (25 words)**: the Deputy Speaker is elected by the House; the Constitution requires the House to elect him, not that he be from a party.
2. **Convention (30 words)**: since the 11th Lok Sabha a consensus that the post goes to the main Opposition (Laxmikanth); a convention.
3. **Arguments for (30 words)**: balances the ruling-party Speaker; the Deputy presides in the Speaker's absence with full powers; builds trust.
4. **Arguments against (30 words)**: a convention can be ignored (verify the 2019-24 experience); the Deputy is directly responsible to the House, and the Constitution only requires election.
5. **Conclusion (35 words)**: honour the convention and fill the post promptly as the Constitution requires.

### Notes: Definitions in one place

- Presiding officer: the person who conducts the sitting of a House (Speaker, Chairman and their deputies).
- Speaker: elected by the Lok Sabha from its members; head and spokesman of the House and guardian of its privileges (Laxmikanth).
- Deputy Speaker: elected by the Lok Sabha after the Speaker; performs the Speaker's duties when the post is vacant or he is absent; not subordinate to the Speaker.
- Speaker Pro Tem: member appointed by the President to preside over the first sitting of a new Lok Sabha, administer the oath and enable the election of the Speaker; office ceases then.
- Casting vote: the presiding officer's vote used only to break a tie. First instance: the first round of voting on a question.
- Majority of all the then members: more than half of all sitting members, excluding vacancies.
- Panel of Chairpersons: up to ten members nominated by the Speaker to preside in the absence of the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker.
- Quorum: the minimum number of members needed to transact business; one-tenth of the total strength.

### Notes: Articles and provisions (standard Article numbers: verify)

- Article 93: Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the House of the People.
- Article 94: vacation, resignation and removal (94(c): resolution of a majority of all the then members, 14 days' notice; Speaker stays after dissolution until immediately before the first meeting of the new House).
- Article 95: Deputy Speaker or another person performs the Speaker's duties.
- Article 96: neither presides while his own removal resolution is under consideration; the Speaker may speak and vote in the first instance only.
- Article 97: salaries and allowances fixed by Parliament; charged on the Consolidated Fund (Article 112(3)(b); verify); Article 98: Secretariat of Parliament.
- Article 100: voting, the casting vote and the quorum (one-tenth).
- Articles 108 and 118(4): joint sitting, presided by the Speaker. Article 110(3): the Speaker's decision on a Money Bill is final (GS-179).
- Tenth Schedule: the Speaker (Chairman in the Rajya Sabha) decides disqualification on ground of defection; decision subject to judicial review (Laxmikanth, 1992).
- Articles 178-181: Speaker and Deputy Speaker of a State Legislative Assembly (outline).

### Notes: Cause and effect: why Speaker neutrality matters

- The Speaker is elected from the majority on a party ticket and decides disputes between the majority and the Opposition.
- If he is seen as partisan, the Opposition loses trust in the chair; disruptions increase; scrutiny of the government weakens.
- The Tenth Schedule role puts a partisan-looking decision within his hands; judicial review comes after the event (Kihoto).
- Remedies discussed: convention of leaving the party, time-bound decisions, an independent tribunal, strengthening the Speaker's conference.

### Last-week summary

- Speaker elected by the Lok Sabha, date fixed by the President; Deputy Speaker's date fixed by the Speaker; each resigns by writing to the other.
- Removal: majority of all the then members, 14 days' notice; while pending he cannot preside but may speak and vote in the first instance, no casting vote.
- Speaker stays until immediately before the first meeting of the new House; Pro Tem appointed by the President administers the oath; quorum one-tenth; Panel of Chairpersons up to ten.
- Speaker alone certifies Money Bills and presides over joint sittings; decides Tenth Schedule disqualification, reviewable by courts (1992); no constitutional duty to leave his party.
