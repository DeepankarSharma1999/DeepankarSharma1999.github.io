### Bridge: Integration as adding up thin slices (shell-by-shell thinking)

## Why you need this

Every derivation in this lesson adds up a quantity over a body: the pull of all the rings of a shell, the energy of all the shells of a sphere. Mathematics writes that addition as an **integral**. You do not need to be fluent in calculus today; you need to read an integral as a sentence.

## The sentence an integral says

$$ ∫₀ᴿ f(r) dr $$

reads: "cut the range from r = 0 to r = R into tiny pieces of width dr; on each piece the quantity is f(r) times dr; add all the pieces." The symbol ∫ is a stretched S for *sum*. The numbers at the bottom and top (0 and R) are the **limits**: where the adding starts and where it stops. The letter after d tells you *what* is being sliced (here the radius r).

## The two rules you will actually use

1. **Power rule.** The sum of rⁿ dr from 0 to R is Rⁿ⁺¹/(n+1). Example: ∫₀ᴿ r⁴ dr = R⁵/5 and ∫₀ᴿ r² dr = R³/3. Check by differentiating backwards: d/dr of r⁵/5 is r⁴.
2. **Constants come out.** ∫ 16π²Gρ² r⁴ dr = 16π²Gρ² ∫ r⁴ dr. Anything that does not change as r changes can be pulled in front.

## The physical picture used in module 7

Imagine building a planet like an onion in reverse: start with a tiny core, add a thin shell of thickness dr, add another, and keep going until the radius is R. Each shell has mass 4πr²ρ dr (surface area × thickness × density). The energy released when a shell lands is small; the integral adds all those small releases. The lower limit is 0 (no core yet) and the upper limit is R (finished sphere). Getting these limits right is half the derivation.

### Bridge: Scientific notation, significant figures and the units of G

## Powers of ten

Physics numbers are written as a × 10ᵇ with a between 1 and 10 (NCERT Class 11, Units and Measurement, section 1.3). 6 × 10²⁴ kg means 6 followed by 24 zeros kilograms. Multiplying two such numbers: multiply the front parts and **add** the exponents. Dividing: divide the front parts and **subtract** the exponents. Squaring: square the front part and **double** the exponent. Example: (6 × 10²⁴)² = 36 × 10⁴⁸ = 3.6 × 10⁴⁹.

The exponent b is the **order of magnitude**: the Earth's diameter 1.28 × 10⁷ m is "of the order of 10⁷ m". In an examination, quoting the correct order of magnitude is the first thing a checker looks at.

## Significant figures

The data in the 2023 UPSC question (M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m) have one and two significant figures. Your final answer should therefore be quoted to about two significant figures (for example 2.3 × 10³² J), not to eight digits. Keep one extra digit in the middle of the calculation and round at the end.

## Units of G and a dimensional check

Newton's law F = G m₁m₂/r² gives G = F r²/(m₁m₂), so G has units N m² kg⁻². Since 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻², this is m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², and the dimensional formula is [M⁻¹ L³ T⁻²]. A **dimensional check** means confirming both sides of an equation have the same units. For gravitational energy, G M²/R has units (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg²)/m = kg m² s⁻² = joule. Whenever you finish a derivation, do this check in one line; it catches most algebra slips.

### Bridge: Vector field, scalar potential and the word gradient

## Two kinds of field

A **vector field** gives an arrow at every point: a magnitude and a direction. The gravitational field g is one: at every point near the Earth there is an arrow pointing roughly towards the Earth's centre, of length about 9.8 m s⁻² at the surface. A **scalar field** gives only a number at every point: temperature in a room, or the gravitational potential V (a number in joule per kilogram).

## Why physicists prefer the scalar

Adding arrows requires components and trigonometry; adding numbers is just addition. So the usual strategy is: compute the potential V (a sum of numbers), then recover the field g from it. The tool that does the recovery is the **gradient**.

## Gradient in one dimension first

If V depends only on the distance r from a centre, the gradient is just the ordinary derivative dV/dr: how many joule per kilogram the potential changes per metre moved outward. The field is

$$ g_r = −dV/dr $$

The minus sign says the field points the way the potential *decreases*: a ball rolls downhill. In three dimensions the symbol ∇V (read "grad V" or "del V") collects the three slopes ∂V/∂x, ∂V/∂y, ∂V/∂z into one arrow pointing in the direction of steepest increase. For spherically symmetric problems you only ever need the radial form above.

## Reading the sign

For a point mass, V = −GM/r is negative and becomes less negative (increases) as r grows. So dV/dr is positive, and g_r = −dV/dr is negative: the field points towards decreasing r, that is, towards the mass. The mathematics reproduces attraction automatically; you never have to insert the direction by hand.

### 1. Orientation: what 'gravitational self energy' means and why UPSC asks it

*Objective:* Place the topic inside Physics Paper I section 1(a), learn the examination vocabulary, and see the two actual UPSC questions you are preparing for.

## The one-sentence meaning

The **gravitational self energy** of a body is the total gravitational potential energy stored in the body because of the attraction of its own parts for each other. Equivalently, it is the work that would have to be done against gravity to pull the body apart into infinitely separated pieces, with a minus sign. For any real body it is a **negative** number, and for a uniform solid sphere of mass M and radius R it equals −(3/5) G M²/R.

## Where it sits in the syllabus

UPSC Physics Optional Paper I, section 1(a) "Mechanics of Particles", contains the phrase: *Fields and potentials; Gravitational field and potential due to spherical bodies, Gauss and Poisson equations, gravitational self-energy*. This lesson covers exactly that chain, in that order, because the self-energy derivation needs the potential of a sphere, and the potential of a sphere needs the field and potential of a shell.

## Examination vocabulary you will meet

- **Paper I, Q1 (Section A) and Q5 (Section B)** are compulsory, each with several 10-mark short parts drawn from that section. Both verified past questions on this topic (2023 Q1(b) and 2025 Q1(b)) were 10-mark compulsory parts.
- **Derive** means: start from a stated law, show every mathematical step, state assumptions, and finish with the boxed result. **Calculate** means: write the formula, substitute numbers with units, give the answer with units and sign.
- **Uniform** means the density ρ is the same everywhere inside the body. **Thin shell** means all the mass sits at one radius.

## The two real questions

2023 Q1(b): *Calculate the gravitational self-energy of the Earth* with M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m. 2025 Q1(b): *Derive the expression for the gravitational self-energy of a uniform solid sphere of mass M and radius R.* The derivation and the numerical are two faces of one piece of physics; you will learn to write both in about eight minutes each.

### 2. Foundation: Newton's law, G, gravitational field and potential

*Objective:* Define the gravitational field g and potential V from Newton's law, fix the zero-at-infinity convention and derive g = −dV/dr for a point mass.

## Newton's law of gravitation

Every particle attracts every other particle with a force proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them (Mathur, section 12.2; NCERT Class 11, chapter 7):

$$ F = G m₁ m₂ / r² $$

G is the **universal gravitational constant**. Its accepted value is G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² (NCERT eq. 7.8), dimensions [M⁻¹ L³ T⁻²]. It was first measured by Cavendish in 1798 with a torsion balance. The force is always attractive and acts along the line joining the two particles; such a force is called a **central force**.

## Gravitational field (intensity)

The **gravitational field** g at a point is the force per unit mass that a small test mass would feel there. For a point mass M at the origin, a test mass m at distance r feels F = GMm/r² towards M, so

$$ g = F/m = GM/r² , directed towards M $$

Its unit is N kg⁻¹, which is the same as m s⁻². At the Earth's surface g = GM_E/R_E² ≈ 9.8 m s⁻² (NCERT eq. 7.12). "Field" is the language for the influence of M spread through space; it exists whether or not a test mass is present.

## Gravitational potential

The **gravitational potential** V at a point is the potential energy per unit mass of a test mass placed there, equivalently the work done by an external agent in bringing unit mass from infinity to that point (NCERT 7.7). Only differences of potential energy have physical meaning, so a zero must be chosen; the universal convention is **V = 0 at infinite distance**.

Derivation for a point mass. Bring unit mass slowly from infinity to distance r. The gravitational force on it is GM/x² towards M; the external agent pushes outward with the same magnitude. Over a step dx (dx negative, since we move inward) the agent does work dW = (GM/x²) dx. Adding from x = ∞ to x = r:

$$ V(r) = ∫_∞^r (GM/x²) dx = [−GM/x]_∞^r = −GM/r − 0 = −GM/r $$

The sign is physical: gravity does positive work as the mass falls in, so the external agent does negative work; the potential energy *decreases* from zero to a negative value. Unit: J kg⁻¹.

## Field from potential

Differentiate: dV/dr = +GM/r². The field is minus this slope:

$$ g_r = −dV/dr = −GM/r² $$

The negative radial component means "pointing towards decreasing r", towards the mass. In general g = −∇V (bridge module B3). Two quick checks: dimensions, [V] = J kg⁻¹ = m² s⁻², so dV/dr has units m s⁻², the unit of g. Limiting case: as r → ∞, both V and g go to zero, as the convention demands.

## Potential energy of a two-mass system

Put mass m at distance r from M. Its potential energy is U = mV = −GMm/r (NCERT eq. after 7.25). This is the energy of the *pair*, not of either mass alone; it is the building block of self-energy in module 6.

### 3. Core: potential and field of a thin uniform spherical shell

*Objective:* Derive V and g inside and outside a thin shell by integrating over rings, and state the shell theorem that every later result depends on.

## Setting up

A **thin spherical shell** of radius a and total mass M has uniform surface density σ = M/(4πa²). Take the centre O as origin and a field point P at distance r from O (Figure in your notes: sphere, point P on the axis OP). Assumptions: thin (all mass at radius a), uniform σ, Newtonian gravity.

## Slicing into rings

Cut the shell into thin rings by planes perpendicular to OP. A ring at polar angle θ (measured from OP) has radius a sin θ, width a dθ, so its area is 2π a sin θ · a dθ and its mass is

$$ dM = σ · 2πa² sin θ dθ = (M/2) sin θ dθ $$

Every point of this ring is at the same distance x from P, where by the cosine rule

$$ x² = a² + r² − 2 a r cos θ $$

Because all of the ring is at one distance, its potential at P is just that of a point mass: dV = −G dM/x. (This is why we integrate the *potential* and not the field: the field contributions have different directions and would need components; the potential contributions are plain numbers.)

## Changing the variable from θ to x

Differentiate the cosine rule at fixed a and r: 2x dx = 2ar sin θ dθ, so sin θ dθ = x dx/(ar). Then

$$ dV = −(GM/2) · (x dx/(ar)) / x = −(GM/(2ar)) dx $$

The integrand is now a constant times dx, which makes the integral trivial. Only the limits differ between the two cases.

## Case 1: P outside (r > a)

The nearest point of the shell is at x = r − a (θ = 0), the farthest at x = r + a (θ = π):

$$ V = −(GM/(2ar)) ∫_{r−a}^{r+a} dx = −(GM/(2ar)) · 2a = −GM/r $$

Outside, the shell acts exactly like a point mass M at its centre. Field: g_r = −dV/dr = −GM/r², magnitude GM/r² inward.

## Case 2: P inside (r < a)

Now the nearest point is at x = a − r and the farthest at x = a + r:

$$ V = −(GM/(2ar)) · [(a + r) − (a − r)] = −(GM/(2ar)) · 2r = −GM/a $$

Inside, V is a **constant**, independent of r. Its derivative is zero, so g = 0 everywhere inside the shell. This is the famous result NCERT states without proof in section 7.3: a shell exerts no force on a mass inside it. Physically, the nearer part of the shell is smaller but closer, the farther part larger but farther, and the inverse-square law makes the two pulls cancel exactly.

## Case 3: on the surface (r = a)

Both formulas give V = −GM/a; the potential is continuous. The field jumps from 0 just inside to GM/a² just outside.

## Summary table

| Region | Potential V | Field magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| r > a | −GM/r | GM/r² (inward) |
| r = a | −GM/a | GM/a² |
| r < a | −GM/a (constant) | 0 |

Dimensional check: GM/r has units (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg)/m = m² s⁻² = J kg⁻¹. Limiting case: as r → ∞ outside, V → 0.

### 4. Core: field and potential of a uniform solid sphere, inside and outside

*Objective:* Derive g and V for a uniform solid sphere, obtain the parabolic interior potential V = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³), and verify continuity and the centre-to-surface ratio of 3/2.

## The strategy

A uniform solid sphere (radius R, mass M, density ρ = 3M/(4πR³)) is a stack of concentric thin shells. Module 3 tells us what each shell does: like a point mass at the centre for points outside it, and a constant potential (zero field) for points inside it. Add the shells.

## Outside the sphere (r ≥ R)

Every shell has the field point outside it, so every shell behaves as a point mass at O. Adding, the whole sphere acts as a point mass M:

$$ V(r) = −GM/r ,  g = GM/r² inward  (r ≥ R) $$

This is why NCERT can use F = GM_E m/r² for the Earth (section 7.5). On the surface V = −GM/R and g = GM/R².

## Field inside the sphere (r < R)

Split the sphere at radius r. Shells with radius larger than r contain P inside them and contribute **zero** field. Shells with radius smaller than r form a smaller sphere of mass M_r = (4/3)πr³ρ = M r³/R³, which acts as a point mass at O. So

$$ g(r) = G M_r / r² = G M r / R³  (r < R) $$

The field grows **linearly** from zero at the centre to GM/R² at the surface (NCERT eq. 7.10 is the same result written as a force). Sign: g_r = −GMr/R³.

## Potential inside the sphere (r < R)

Integrate the field inward from the surface, or add the two groups of shells directly; the second way shows the physics.

Group A: inner sphere of mass M r³/R³, point P on its surface: V_A = −G (M r³/R³)/r = −GMr²/R³.

Group B: a shell of radius s (r < s < R), thickness ds, mass dm = 4πs²ρ ds, with P inside it, contributes −G dm/s = −4πGρ s ds. Adding from s = r to s = R:

$$ V_B = −4πGρ ∫_r^R s ds = −4πGρ (R² − r²)/2 = −2πGρ (R² − r²) $$

Substituting ρ = 3M/(4πR³): V_B = −(3GM/(2R³))(R² − r²).

Total:

$$ V(r) = −GMr²/R³ − (3GM/(2R³))(R² − r²) = −(GM/(2R³))(2r² + 3R² − 3r²) = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³) $$

Written out, V(r) = −3GM/(2R) + GM r²/(2R³), an upward-opening parabola in r (convex, with positive curvature as Poisson's equation requires where there is matter): the potential has its minimum (most negative value) at the centre and rises to the surface.

## Checks that examiners look for

1. **Continuity at r = R:** interior formula gives −GM(3R² − R²)/(2R³) = −GM/R, equal to the exterior value.
2. **Centre:** V(0) = −3GM/(2R), which is 3/2 times the surface potential (a classic short question, Mathur exercises 12 and 35).
3. **Field from potential:** g_r = −dV/dr = −(GM/(2R³))(2r) = −GMr/R³, matching the direct result; good sign that the algebra is right.
4. **Dimensions:** GM/R has units of J kg⁻¹; the bracket (3R² − r²)/R² is dimensionless.
5. **Limiting case:** letting all the mass sit at R (shell) would replace the parabola with the flat value −GM/R, module 3.

## Alternative route: Gauss's law

Module 5 gives Gauss's law for gravitation. For a sphere of symmetry it yields the field in two lines: 4πr² g = 4πG M_enc, with M_enc = M (outside) or M r³/R³ (inside). The potential then follows by integrating −g dr from infinity. Use whichever method the question wording suggests; if it says "using Gauss's theorem", you must use Gauss.

## Summary

| Region | g (magnitude) | V |
|---|---|---|
| r ≥ R | GM/r² | −GM/r |
| r = R | GM/R² | −GM/R |
| r < R | GMr/R³ | −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³) |
| r = 0 | 0 | −3GM/(2R) |

### 5. Core: Gauss's law for gravitation, Poisson's and Laplace's equations

*Objective:* State and interpret ∮ g·dA = −4πG M_enc and ∇²V = 4πGρ, use Gauss to rederive the sphere field, and verify Poisson's equation on the parabolic potential.

## Flux and Gauss's law

The **flux** of the field g through a closed surface S is ∮ g·dA: at each small patch dA take the component of g along the outward normal, multiply by the area, and add over the whole surface. For a point mass M at the centre of a sphere of radius r, g has magnitude GM/r² and points inward, so its outward component is −GM/r² everywhere on the sphere, and the flux is −(GM/r²)(4πr²) = −4πGM. The r cancels: the flux does not depend on the size of the sphere, and (by the same geometry used for electrostatics) not on its shape either, as long as M is inside. Masses outside contribute zero net flux. Hence **Gauss's law for gravitation** (Mathur section 12.19):

$$ ∮ g · dA = −4πG M_enc $$

M_enc is the total mass inside the surface. The minus sign records that gravitational field lines point *into* mass (gravity attracts), the opposite of the field of a positive charge. Compare electrostatics: ∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀; the role of 1/ε₀ is played by −4πG.

## Using Gauss's law for the sphere

Choose a concentric spherical **Gaussian surface** of radius r. By symmetry g is radial and of the same magnitude everywhere on it, so the flux is −g · 4πr². Then

$$ −g · 4πr² = −4πG M_enc  ⇒  g = G M_enc / r² $$

Outside (r ≥ R): M_enc = M, g = GM/r². Inside (r < R): M_enc = M r³/R³, g = GMr/R³. These are the module 4 results in two lines, which is why the syllabus lists Gauss's equation beside the spherical-body results. For a thin shell, M_enc = 0 inside, giving g = 0 at once.

## From the integral law to a differential law: Poisson's equation

Gauss's law speaks of a whole surface. Shrinking the surface to a point gives a statement about the field at each point. The divergence theorem converts ∮ g·dA into ∫ (∇·g) dV, and M_enc = ∫ ρ dV; since the two integrals agree for every volume, the integrands agree:

$$ ∇ · g = −4πG ρ $$

Now insert g = −∇V. The operator ∇·∇ is written ∇² (the **Laplacian**), so

$$ ∇² V = 4πG ρ  (Poisson's equation) $$

In empty space ρ = 0 and it reduces to **Laplace's equation** ∇²V = 0. For spherical symmetry the Laplacian is

$$ ∇² V = (1/r²) d/dr ( r² dV/dr ) $$

Meaning in words: Poisson's equation says the *curvature* of the potential at a point is set by the density at that point. Where there is matter, the potential curves upward like a bowl; where there is none, it has no net curvature.

## Checking the sphere solution against Poisson

Inside: V = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³), so dV/dr = GMr/R³ and r² dV/dr = GMr³/R³; its derivative is 3GMr²/R³; dividing by r² gives ∇²V = 3GM/R³. Since ρ = 3M/(4πR³), 4πGρ = 3GM/R³. The two agree: the parabola satisfies Poisson's equation with the right constant, and this is the strongest consistency check you can offer in an answer. Outside: V = −GM/r, r² dV/dr = GM, a constant, so ∇²V = 0: Laplace's equation holds in the empty space outside, as it should.

## Why this matters for self-energy

Poisson's equation is the compact reason the potential inside the sphere is quadratic: a constant ρ forces a constant Laplacian, and the quadratic is the symmetric function with constant Laplacian. The ½∫ρV dV route to self-energy in module 7 uses exactly this V.

### 6. Core: potential energy of a system of masses and the definition of self energy

*Objective:* Build the pairwise sum for a system of point masses, understand the factor ½ in the double sum, and define the self energy of a continuous body.

## Two masses

The potential energy of two point masses m₁ and m₂ a distance r₁₂ apart is the work done in bringing them from infinite separation to r₁₂ (module 2):

$$ U₁₂ = −G m₁ m₂ / r₁₂ $$

## Three masses: assemble one at a time

Place m₁ first: no work, nothing is there yet. Bring m₂ to distance r₁₂: work U₁₂. Bring m₃ to distances r₁₃ and r₂₃ from the first two: work U₁₃ + U₂₃ (each existing mass pulls on it independently, by the superposition principle, Mathur section 12.25). Total (Mathur section 12.26):

$$ U = −G ( m₁m₂/r₁₂ + m₁m₃/r₁₃ + m₂m₃/r₂₃ ) $$

Three facts to remember. (1) The energy belongs to the **configuration**, not to any single mass; it is stored in the field. (2) Because gravity is a conservative force, the order of assembly and the paths taken do not matter. (3) Each **pair** appears exactly once.

## n masses and the factor ½

For n masses, add over all pairs:

$$ U = −G Σ_{i<j} m_i m_j / r_ij $$

The condition i < j (or j > i) counts each pair once. Many textbooks instead sum over *all* i and *all* j with i ≠ j; that counts every pair twice (as (2,3) and again as (3,2)), so the result must be halved:

$$ U = −(1/2) G Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} m_i m_j / r_ij $$

Both forms are equal. The ½ is bookkeeping, not physics; forgetting it (or applying it to the i<j form) is the most common factor error in this topic. The term i = j is excluded because a particle does not interact with itself.

A tidy rewriting: Σ_{j≠i} (−G m_j/r_ij) is the potential V_i at the position of m_i due to all the *other* masses. So

$$ U = (1/2) Σ_i m_i V_i $$

This "half the sum of mass times potential" form is the bridge to continuous bodies.

## Worked example: three 10 kg masses at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 1 m

Three pairs, each −G m²/a: U = −3 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (10)²/1 = −2.0 × 10⁻⁸ J. Using the double sum: each mass sees potential V_i = −2Gm/a; ½ Σ m V_i = ½ × 3 × m × (−2Gm/a) = −3Gm²/a, the same. The energy is tiny because G is tiny; gravity only matters for astronomical masses.

## From a system to a body: self energy defined

A continuous body is a system of an enormous number of infinitesimal mass elements. Its **gravitational self energy** U_s is the potential energy of that system, that is, the work done in assembling the body from elements initially infinitely far apart (Mathur section 12.27). Replacing sums by integrals in the ½ Σ m_i V_i form gives

$$ U_s = (1/2) ∫ ρ V dV $$

where V is the potential at the volume element due to the whole body (the self-interaction of a point element is negligible for a smooth density). The ½ survives because every pair of elements would otherwise be counted twice.

## The sign and its meaning

U_s is negative: energy is *released* as a body assembles under its own gravity. Reversed, |U_s| is the **binding energy**, the minimum work needed to disperse the body to infinity. A system of like charges has positive self energy (work must be supplied); gravity, being always attractive, gives negative self energy. For a galaxy of N equal stars of mass m at typical separation R, the same pairwise sum gives U ≈ −G N² m²/(2R) for large N (Mathur section 12.29), a result UPSC could set as a short derivation.

### 7. Master: self energy of a uniform solid sphere, two derivations, and the thin shell

*Objective:* Derive U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R by assembling shells, re-derive it from ½∫ρV dV, obtain −GM²/(2R) for a thin shell, and perform every check an examiner expects.

## Derivation 1: building the sphere shell by shell (the UPSC 2025 question)

**Assumptions and set-up.** Uniform density ρ, final radius R, final mass M = (4/3)πR³ρ. Newtonian gravity; potential zero at infinity. Coordinate: r is the current radius of the partly built core, 0 ≤ r ≤ R.

**Step 1: the partly built core.** When the core has radius r its mass is

$$ m(r) = (4/3) π r³ ρ $$

**Step 2: the next shell.** Bring mass from infinity and spread it as a thin shell of thickness dr on the core. Its mass is surface area × thickness × density:

$$ dm = 4π r² ρ dr $$

**Step 3: work done on the shell.** The shell lands on the surface of the core, where the potential due to the core is V(r) = −G m(r)/r (module 4, surface value; the shell itself is not yet part of the core, so only the core attracts it). The potential energy gained is dm × V(r):

$$ dU = −G m(r) dm / r = −G · (4/3)πr³ρ · 4πr²ρ dr / r = −(16/3) π² G ρ² r⁴ dr $$

Check the powers of r: r³ × r² / r = r⁴. This is exactly the quantity module B1 taught you to add up.

**Step 4: integrate from no core to full sphere.** Limits r = 0 (nothing built) to r = R (finished):

$$ U_s = −(16/3) π² G ρ² ∫₀ᴿ r⁴ dr = −(16/3) π² G ρ² · R⁵/5 = −(16/15) π² G ρ² R⁵ $$

**Step 5: express in M and R.** From M = (4/3)πR³ρ, ρ = 3M/(4πR³), so ρ² = 9M²/(16π²R⁶). Substitute:

$$ U_s = −(16/15) π² G · (9M²/(16π²R⁶)) · R⁵ = −(9/15) G M²/R = −(3/5) G M²/R $$

The 16π² cancels, R⁵/R⁶ leaves 1/R, and 9/15 reduces to 3/5. Box it:

$$ U_s = −(3/5) G M² / R $$

**Interpretation.** This much energy is released (as heat, radiation or kinetic energy) while the sphere forms; equally, (3/5)GM²/R is the work needed to blow it apart to infinity (Mathur section 12.28).

## Derivation 2: the ½∫ρV dV route (the check UPSC loves)

Use module 6's formula with the interior potential of module 4, V(r) = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³), and the volume element dV = 4πr² dr:

$$ U_s = (1/2) ∫₀ᴿ ρ · [−GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³)] · 4πr² dr = −(πρGM/R³) ∫₀ᴿ (3R² r² − r⁴) dr $$

The integral is 3R² · R³/3 − R⁵/5 = R⁵ − R⁵/5 = (4/5)R⁵. So

$$ U_s = −(πρGM/R³) · (4/5) R⁵ = −(4/5) π ρ G M R² $$

Put ρ = 3M/(4πR³): (4/5)π · 3M/(4πR³) · GMR² = (3/5) GM²/R. Hence U_s = −(3/5) GM²/R again. Two independent routes agreeing is strong evidence; if you drop the ½ you get −(6/5)GM²/R, the classic factor trap.

## The thin spherical shell: U_s = −GM²/(2R)

Build the shell by bringing mass in small amounts dm and spreading it over the fixed radius R. When mass m is already present, the potential on the shell is −Gm/R, so dU = −G m dm/R. Integrate m from 0 to M:

$$ U_s = −(G/R) ∫₀ᴹ m dm = −G M²/(2R) $$

Check with ½∫σV dA: V = −GM/R is constant on the shell, so ½ × M × (−GM/R) = −GM²/(2R). Same answer. For the same M and R, the shell is *less* tightly bound than the solid sphere (½ < 3/5) because its mass sits farther from the centre on average.

## Dimensional check and limiting cases

Units of GM²/R: (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg²)(m⁻¹) = kg m² s⁻² = J. The numerical factor 3/5 is dimensionless. Limiting cases: R → ∞ at fixed M gives U_s → 0 (dispersed matter, zero binding); R → 0 gives U_s → −∞ (the Newtonian hint that unlimited collapse releases unlimited energy). A sphere whose mass is pushed outward into a shell moves from 3/5 towards 1/2; a centrally concentrated star has a factor larger than 3/5. You may state (without proof) that for non-uniform spheres the result is −k GM²/R with k depending on the density profile.

## What a 10-mark answer must contain

Assumptions (uniform, solid, sphere, V = 0 at infinity); the expressions for m(r) and dm; the key physical statement that the shell feels only the core beneath it; dU with its sign; the integral with limits 0 to R; the substitution of ρ; the boxed result; the dimensional check; one sentence of interpretation. About twelve lines.

### 8. Exam: the Earth numerical, order of magnitude, meaning, and how to write it in the hall

*Objective:* Solve the 2023 UPSC numerical with units and significant figures, interpret the order of magnitude, connect self energy to binding energy and stellar contraction, and learn the traps.

## The 2023 question, solved step by step

*Calculate the gravitational self-energy of the Earth. M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m.*

**Step 1: model and formula.** Treat the Earth as a uniform solid sphere (state this: the real Earth is denser at the core, so the true value is somewhat more negative). Then U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R. The question does not give G; you must know G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².

**Step 2: M².** (6 × 10²⁴)² = 36 × 10⁴⁸ = 3.6 × 10⁴⁹ kg².

**Step 3: G M².** 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.6 × 10⁴⁹ = 24.0 × 10³⁸ = 2.40 × 10³⁹ N m² (that is, J m).

**Step 4: divide by R.** 2.40 × 10³⁹ / 6.4 × 10⁶ = 0.375 × 10³³ = 3.75 × 10³² J.

**Step 5: multiply by 3/5 and attach the sign.**

$$ U_s = −0.6 × 3.75 × 10³² J = −2.25 × 10³² J ≈ −2.3 × 10³² J $$

**Step 6: units and significant figures.** The data have one to two significant figures, so −2.3 × 10³² J (or −2.25 × 10³² J) is the right precision. Units: joule, as the dimensional check in module 7 guarantees.

**Step 7: one line of meaning.** About 2 × 10³² J would be needed to disperse the Earth to infinity; this is its gravitational binding energy.

## Feeling the size of 10³² J

The Sun radiates about 3.8 × 10²⁶ J every second, so it emits the Earth's binding energy in roughly 2.3 × 10³²/3.8 × 10²⁶ ≈ 6 × 10⁵ s, about a week. The Earth's orbital kinetic energy, ½ × 6 × 10²⁴ × (3 × 10⁴)² ≈ 2.7 × 10³³ J, is about ten times larger. If the Earth were a thin shell of the same mass and radius, U_s would be −GM²/(2R) = −1.9 × 10³² J, five-sixths of the solid-sphere value. (Solar luminosity and orbital speed are quoted from memory as standard values for context only.)

## Why self energy matters (context, not for reproduction)

Self energy is the *binding energy* of self-gravitating bodies, from planets to star clusters. When a star contracts, |U_s| grows; the released gravitational energy heats the gas and is partly radiated. This was the nineteenth-century Kelvin-Helmholtz proposal for the Sun's power: contraction of the Sun's radius by about a kilometre a year would release roughly 10²⁸ J s⁻¹ (worked in the numericals, following Mathur's Example 23), far more than the actual solar output, while the total reservoir (3/5)GM²/R ≈ 2 × 10⁴¹ J divided by the luminosity gives a lifetime of only tens of millions of years, too short for geology. Nuclear fusion resolved the puzzle. The virial theorem (Mathur Example 27) links the same |U_s| to the interior temperature of a star, of order 10⁷ K for the Sun. Treat this paragraph as background that enriches a conclusion, not as syllabus content to derive.

2. Second line: the value of G you are using.
3. Then the substitution written **once with all powers of ten visible**, then the arithmetic.
4. Box the answer with sign and unit.

## Traps, in order of frequency

- **Sign:** writing +2.25 × 10³² J. Self energy of a gravitating body is always negative.
- **Factor:** using ½ (the shell factor) or 1 instead of 3/5; or dropping the ½ in ½∫ρV dV and getting 6/5.
- **Powers of ten:** 6² = 36 and (10²⁴)² = 10⁴⁸, not 10⁵⁶. Write exponents separately.
- **Integration range:** integrating the shell-assembly integral from R to ∞ or from 0 to ∞; the sphere is built from 0 to R.
- **Using M instead of m(r)** for the core while assembling; it overcounts and gives −(3/2)GM²/R, not the sphere result. The shell value −GM²/(2R) comes from a different error, putting all the mass at the surface potential (conceptual question 2).
- **Unit slip:** quoting J kg⁻¹ (a potential) instead of J (an energy); or using R in kilometres with G in SI.
- **Forgetting the assumption:** not writing "uniform"; the 3/5 is specific to uniform density.

### Notes: Definitions

- Gravitational field g: force per unit mass on a small test mass at a point; unit N kg⁻¹ = m s⁻²; for a point mass, magnitude GM/r² towards the mass.
- Gravitational potential V: potential energy per unit mass at a point, taken as zero at infinity; unit J kg⁻¹; for a point mass V = −GM/r.
- Gradient relation: g = −∇V; for spherical symmetry g_r = −dV/dr.
- Gravitational self energy of a body: the potential energy of the body due to the mutual attraction of its own parts; the work done in assembling it from infinitely dispersed matter; always negative for gravity.
- Binding energy: the magnitude |U_s|, the minimum work needed to disperse the body to infinity.
- Gaussian surface: an imaginary closed surface chosen to exploit symmetry when applying Gauss's law.
- Uniform solid sphere: a sphere whose density ρ = 3M/(4πR³) is the same at every interior point.

### Notes: Key equations

- F = G m₁m₂/r², G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², [G] = M⁻¹ L³ T⁻².
- Point mass: V = −GM/r, g = GM/r² (inward).
- Thin shell (radius a): V = −GM/r and g = GM/r² outside; V = −GM/a and g = 0 inside.
- Uniform sphere outside: V = −GM/r, g = GM/r²; inside: g = GMr/R³, V = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³); centre V = −3GM/(2R).
- Gauss's law: ∮ g·dA = −4πG M_enc.
- Poisson: ∇²V = 4πGρ; Laplace (empty space): ∇²V = 0; spherical Laplacian (1/r²) d/dr (r² dV/dr).
- System of point masses: U = −G Σ_{i<j} m_i m_j/r_ij = −½ G Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} m_i m_j/r_ij = ½ Σ_i m_i V_i.
- Continuous body: U_s = ½ ∫ ρ V dV.
- Uniform solid sphere: U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R. Thin shell: U_s = −G M²/(2R).
- Earth (uniform model, M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m): U_s ≈ −2.3 × 10³² J.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: self energy of a uniform sphere by shell assembly

- Assume uniform density ρ, final radius R, M = (4/3)πR³ρ, V = 0 at infinity.
- Core of radius r has mass m(r) = (4/3)πr³ρ; the arriving shell has mass dm = 4πr²ρ dr.
- The shell feels only the core: potential at its landing radius is −G m(r)/r.
- dU = −G m(r) dm/r = −(16/3)π²Gρ² r⁴ dr.
- Integrate from r = 0 to r = R: U_s = −(16/15)π²Gρ²R⁵.
- Substitute ρ = 3M/(4πR³), ρ² = 9M²/(16π²R⁶): U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R.
- Check: units J; R → ∞ gives 0; agrees with ½∫ρV dV using V = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³).

### Notes: Shell versus solid sphere

- The table compares a thin shell and a uniform solid sphere of the same mass M and outer radius R.

### Notes: Why the self energy is negative and what it means

- Gravity is always attractive, so the force does positive work as matter falls together; the system's potential energy falls below the zero chosen at infinity, hence U_s < 0.
- The released energy appears as kinetic energy of the infalling matter and then as heat and radiation; this is the energy source in the Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction picture.
- Reversing the process, |U_s| is the energy needed to unbind the body; a more compact body (smaller R at fixed M) is more tightly bound.
- Like charges give positive self energy because work must be supplied against repulsion; the gravitational case has the opposite sign because there is no gravitational repulsion.

### Notes: Limiting cases and special values

- R → ∞ at fixed M: U_s → 0 (no binding).
- R → 0 at fixed M: U_s → −∞ in Newtonian gravity (unlimited release on collapse).
- U_s ∝ M² at fixed R and ∝ 1/R at fixed M; at fixed density U_s ∝ R⁵.
- Inside a shell the field is zero but the potential is not zero; it equals the surface value.
- The factor 3/5 holds only for uniform density; centrally condensed bodies have a larger numerical factor.
- The 3/5 and 1/2 factors are identical to those for a uniformly charged non-conducting sphere and a conducting sphere in electrostatics, with Q²/(4πε₀) replacing −GM².

### Notes: Worked examples at a glance

- Earth: U_s = −(3/5)(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(6 × 10²⁴)²/(6.4 × 10⁶) = −2.25 × 10³² J.
- Sun (M = 2 × 10³⁰ kg, R = 7 × 10⁸ m): U_s = −(3/5)(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(4 × 10⁶⁰)/(7 × 10⁸) ≈ −2.3 × 10⁴¹ J.
- Earth as a thin shell: −GM²/(2R) = −1.9 × 10³² J.
- Three 10 kg masses, equilateral triangle of side 1 m: U = −3Gm²/a = −2.0 × 10⁻⁸ J.
- Potential at Earth's centre (uniform model): −3GM/(2R) = −9.4 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ versus −6.25 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ at the surface.

### Notes: Concepts that are easily confused

- Potential V (J kg⁻¹, per unit mass) versus potential energy U (J, for a given mass): U = mV.
- Self energy of a body (its own parts) versus the mutual potential energy of two bodies (−GM₁M₂/d); the Earth-Sun pair energy is not the Earth's self energy.
- Pairwise sum with i<j (no ½) versus unrestricted double sum with i≠j (needs ½).
- Field zero does not imply potential zero: inside a shell g = 0 while V = −GM/a.
- Gauss's law for gravitation carries −4πG; Poisson's equation carries +4πGρ. The signs differ because g = −∇V.
- Using the full mass M instead of the core mass m(r) while assembling overcounts and gives −(3/2)GM²/R, not the sphere result. The shell value −GM²/(2R) comes from a different slip: putting all the mass at the surface potential −GM/R and halving.

  - Options with −GM²/(2R) for a solid sphere test shell-versus-sphere confusion.
  - Options with ∇²V = −4πGρ test the sign difference between Gauss's law and Poisson's equation.
  - Options claiming V = 0 inside a shell test the field-zero versus potential-zero confusion.
- **prelimsElimination:**
  - Eliminate any self-energy expression that is positive or not of the form (constant) × GM²/R.

  - Eliminate Poisson forms with a negative sign on the right-hand side.

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Define gravitational potential and give its SI unit.
    - **a:** Potential energy per unit mass at a point, taken as zero at infinity; unit J kg⁻¹.
  - - **q:** What is the gravitational field inside a thin uniform spherical shell?
    - **a:** Zero everywhere inside.
  - - **q:** Write the self energy of a uniform solid sphere of mass M and radius R.
    - **a:** U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R.
  - - **q:** What is the value and unit of G?
    - **a:** 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² (equivalently m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²).
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why can the whole sphere be treated as a point mass for exterior points?
    - **a:** Each concentric shell acts as a point mass at the centre for points outside it, and the sphere is a sum of such shells; the total mass therefore appears concentrated at the centre.
  - - **q:** Why is the self energy of a body negative while the self energy of a uniformly charged sphere is positive?
    - **a:** Gravity is attractive so assembling releases energy (negative work by the external agent); like charges repel so assembling requires positive work.
  - - **q:** A student computes the self energy of a sphere by placing the whole mass M in the potential −GM/R and halving: ½ M (−GM/R) = −GM²/(2R). What has gone wrong?
    - **a:** Using the surface potential for all the mass is correct only for a thin shell; the sphere's interior mass sits at more negative potential, and ½∫ρV dV with the parabolic V gives −(3/5)GM²/R.
  - - **q:** Explain why Poisson's equation implies the interior potential of a uniform sphere is quadratic in r.
    - **a:** Constant ρ forces a constant Laplacian; the spherically symmetric function with constant Laplacian and no singularity at the origin is a constant plus a multiple of r².
- **application:**
  - - **q:** Two identical uniform spheres of mass M and radius R merge into one uniform sphere of mass 2M (same density). Estimate the gravitational energy released, ignoring their initial mutual energy.
    - **a:** New radius R' = 2^(1/3) R. Final self energy −(3/5)G(2M)²/(2^(1/3)R) = −(3/5)(GM²/R) × 4/2^(1/3) ≈ −3.17 (3/5 GM²/R); initial total −2 (3/5 GM²/R). Released ≈ 1.17 × (3/5)GM²/R.
  - - **q:** The Sun's radius shrinks by a small amount a at fixed mass. Show the energy released is approximately (3/5)GM²a/R².
    - **a:** dU_s/dR = +(3/5)GM²/R²; a decrease a in R changes U_s by −(3/5)GM²a/R², i.e. that much energy is released (Mathur exercise 56 pattern).

- **derivations:**
  - - **id:** PH-008-DER-1
    - **task:** Derive U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R for a uniform solid sphere by assembling it from concentric shells.
    - **steps:**
      - Assume uniform density ρ, final radius R, M = (4/3)πR³ρ, V = 0 at infinity; let r be the current core radius.
      - Core mass m(r) = (4/3)πr³ρ; shell mass dm = 4πr²ρ dr.
      - The shell lands at radius r where the potential due to the core is −G m(r)/r (outer shells do not exist yet).
      - dU = −G m(r) dm / r = −(16/3)π² G ρ² r⁴ dr.
      - U_s = −(16/3)π²Gρ² ∫₀ᴿ r⁴ dr = −(16/15)π²Gρ²R⁵.
      - ρ² = 9M²/(16π²R⁶) ⇒ U_s = −(9/15)GM²/R = −(3/5)GM²/R.
    - **checks:**
      - Units: G M²/R = (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg²)/m = J.
      - Limit R → ∞ at fixed M gives U_s → 0.
      - Agrees with ½∫ρV dV using V = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³).
      - Sign negative: energy is released during assembly.
    - **traps:**
      - Using M instead of m(r)
      - Limits other than 0 to R
      - Forgetting the ρ substitution
      - Dropping the minus sign
  - - **id:** PH-008-DER-2
    - **task:** Derive the same result from U_s = ½∫ρV dV using the interior potential of a uniform sphere.
    - **steps:**
      - Interior potential V(r) = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³), volume element dV = 4πr² dr, constant ρ.
      - U_s = ½ ∫₀ᴿ ρ [−GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³)] 4πr² dr = −(πρGM/R³) ∫₀ᴿ (3R²r² − r⁴) dr.
      - ∫₀ᴿ 3R²r² dr = R⁵ and ∫₀ᴿ r⁴ dr = R⁵/5, so the bracket is (4/5)R⁵.
      - U_s = −(4/5)πρGMR².
      - Substitute ρ = 3M/(4πR³): U_s = −(3/5)GM²/R.
    - **checks:**
      - Without the ½ the result would be −(6/5)GM²/R, which disagrees with Derivation 1; the ½ is essential.
      - Dimensions: πρGMR² has units (kg m⁻³)(m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg)(m²) = kg m² s⁻² = J.
    - **traps:**
      - Dropping the ½
      - Using the exterior potential −GM/r inside the sphere
      - Forgetting 4πr² in dV
  - - **id:** PH-008-DER-3
    - **task:** Derive the potential of a thin uniform spherical shell at interior and exterior points by integrating over rings, and hence obtain the field.
    - **steps:**
      - Shell radius a, mass M, σ = M/(4πa²); field point P at distance r from the centre.
      - Ring at polar angle θ: mass dM = (M/2) sin θ dθ; distance to P: x² = a² + r² − 2ar cos θ.
      - dV = −G dM/x; from the cosine rule 2x dx = 2ar sin θ dθ, so dV = −(GM/(2ar)) dx.
      - Outside (r > a): integrate x from r − a to r + a, V = −GM/r.
      - Inside (r < a): integrate x from a − r to a + r, V = −GM/a (constant).
      - Field g_r = −dV/dr: −GM/r² outside, 0 inside; V continuous at r = a.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions of GM/r: J kg⁻¹.
      - Limit r → ∞: V → 0.
      - Gauss's law gives g = 0 inside (M_enc = 0) and GM/r² outside, consistent.
    - **traps:**
      - Swapping the inside and outside limits
      - Trying to integrate the field vector instead of the scalar potential
      - Claiming V = 0 inside

    - **task:** Show that the self energy of a thin spherical shell of mass M and radius R is −GM²/(2R).
    - **steps:**

      - Potential on the shell due to m is −Gm/R; dU = −G m dm/R.
      - U_s = −(G/R) ∫₀ᴹ m dm = −GM²/(2R).

    - **q:** Calculate the gravitational self energy of the Earth treated as a uniform solid sphere, with M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m (take G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²).
    - **solution:**
      - Model: uniform solid sphere, U_s = −(3/5) G M²/R.
      - M² = (6 × 10²⁴)² = 3.6 × 10⁴⁹ kg².
      - G M² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.6 × 10⁴⁹ = 2.40 × 10³⁹ J m.
      - G M²/R = 2.40 × 10³⁹ / 6.4 × 10⁶ = 3.75 × 10³² J.
      - U_s = −0.6 × 3.75 × 10³² = −2.25 × 10³² J ≈ −2.3 × 10³² J.
      - Check: units J; order of magnitude 10³²; negative sign for attraction. The real Earth is centrally condensed, so the true magnitude is somewhat larger.

    - **solution:**

      - G M²/R = 2.67 × 10⁵⁰ / 7 × 10⁸ = 3.81 × 10⁴¹ J; U_s = −0.6 × 3.81 × 10⁴¹ ≈ −2.3 × 10⁴¹ J.

### Memory skeleton

- Shell: point mass outside; g = 0 and V constant inside

- Self energy: assembly work, negative; U_s = ½ ∫ ρ V dV
- Uniform sphere by shell assembly: U_s = −(3/5) GM²/R; thin shell −GM²/(2R)

### Last-week summary

- Self energy = work of assembling the body from dispersed matter; negative because gravity attracts.
- Shell assembly: core m(r) = (4/3)πr³ρ, shell dm = 4πr²ρ dr, dU = −G m(r) dm/r, integrate 0 to R.
- Uniform sphere −(3/5)GM²/R; thin shell −GM²/(2R); 3/5 needs uniform density.
- ½ in ½∫ρV dV because every pair is counted twice; i<j sum needs no ½.
- Earth (M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m): U_s ≈ −2.3 × 10³² J.
