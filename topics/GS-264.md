## GS-264: Population ecology and adaptations

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Environment · Ecology fundamentals

*Goal:* Explain Population ecology and adaptations and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Population attributes and growth

Populations have properties individuals lack: birth and death rates expressed per capita, sex ratio and age distribution, shown as age pyramids that reveal whether a population is growing, stable or declining. Density may be measured by numbers, per cent cover or biomass; tiger counts use pug marks and faecal pellets. Under unlimited resources, dN/dt = rN produces exponential, J-shaped growth. In reality resources run short, competition sets in, and growth follows a logistic, S-shaped curve with lag, acceleration, deceleration and an asymptote at carrying capacity K. Life-history strategies, such as breeding once (Pacific salmon, bamboo) or many times, evolve to maximise fitness.

### Interactions between species

NCERT classifies interactions with +, − and 0 signs. Mutualism benefits both; competition harms both; predation and parasitism benefit one and harm the other; commensalism benefits one with no effect on the other; amensalism harms one without affecting the other. Predators transfer energy upward, control prey and maintain diversity — removing starfish Pisaster led to extinction of more than ten invertebrate species. Competitive exclusion is shown by goats outcompeting the Abingdon tortoise, and competitive release by Connell's barnacles. Parasites show adaptations like loss of sense organs, suckers and high reproductive capacity; cuckoos practise brood parasitism.

### Adaptations against enemies

Prey evolve defences. Some insects and frogs are camouflaged; the Monarch butterfly becomes distasteful by acquiring a chemical from a poisonous weed eaten as a caterpillar. Plants, unable to flee, use thorns, as in Acacia and cactus, and chemicals: Calotropis makes cardiac glycosides, so cattle avoid it, and nicotine, caffeine, quinine and opium are plant defences against grazers. Mutualists co-evolve: each fig species has a partner wasp, and the Ophrys orchid uses sexual deceit to get bees to pollinate it. These examples are favourites for statement-based questions.
