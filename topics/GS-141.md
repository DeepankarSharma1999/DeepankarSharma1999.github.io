## GS-141: Earth, oceans, flora/fauna change impacts

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Geography · Human/economic/world

*Goal:* Explain Earth, oceans, flora/fauna change impacts and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Climate change: natural record and human forcing

Glacial landforms, lake sediments, tree rings and historical accounts show that climate changes naturally and continuously. Rajasthan's desert was wet and cool around 8,000 BC, the last major glacial peak was about 18,000 years ago, and Europe passed through a Little Ice Age from 1550 to about 1850. Astronomical causes include sunspot cycles and Milankovitch orbital variations; volcanic aerosols can briefly cool the earth. The dominant human factor is the growing concentration of greenhouse gases, which let incoming short-wave radiation pass but absorb outgoing long-wave radiation. Carbon dioxide rises with fossil-fuel burning and deforestation, and the longer a gas stays aloft, the slower the atmosphere recovers.

### Impacts on ice, oceans and coasts

Warming is uneven but threatens life-support systems. Melting glaciers and ice caps, together with the thermal expansion of warmer sea water, raise sea level and can flood low coasts and islands, creating social problems. NCERT records about 0.4 C of warming in each of two 20th-century periods, 1901-44 and 1977-99. Coasts have natural defences: offshore bars take the first blow from storms and tsunamis, followed by barriers, beaches, dunes and mangroves. If sediment supply is disturbed or mangroves are cleared, these buffers erode and settlements face the full force of waves. The Kyoto Protocol, in force from 2005, obliged industrialised countries to cut emissions below 1990 levels.

### Flora and fauna: loss and conservation

Biodiversity operates at genetic, species and ecosystem levels, and more diverse ecosystems are more stable and better able to adapt to environmental change. Loss has accelerated as population growth raises consumption: deforestation and over-exploitation in the crowded tropics, natural calamities such as floods, fires and droughts, pesticides and heavy metals that kill sensitive species, exotic species that damage native communities, and poaching for horns, tusks and hides. IUCN groups threatened species as endangered, vulnerable or rare. Responses include protecting habitats where species feed and breed, regulating wildlife trade, India's Wildlife Act of 1972, the 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity, and focusing effort on megadiverse countries and biodiversity hotspots such as Madagascar.

### Worked example

Question: 'Discuss how changes in oceans and ice caps can affect coastal societies.' Frame: cause - greenhouse gases trap long-wave radiation; mechanism - glacier and ice-cap melt plus thermal expansion raise sea level; impacts - inundation of low coasts and islands, erosion where sediment budgets or mangroves are disturbed, stronger exposure to storms and tsunamis, habitat loss for coastal biodiversity; responses - emission cuts (Kyoto), mangrove and dune protection, habitat conservation.

### Recall

- - **q:** Name the five primary greenhouse gases listed by NCERT.
  - **a:** Carbon dioxide, CFCs, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone.
- - **q:** What two processes raise sea level in a warming world?
  - **a:** Melting of glaciers and ice caps, and thermal expansion of sea water.
- - **q:** What are exotic species and why do they matter?
  - **a:** Species introduced into a habitat where they are not native; they have caused extensive damage to native biotic communities.

### Common trap

Mixing up ozone depletion and global warming: CFCs destroy stratospheric ozone (creating the ozone hole over Antarctica and letting ultraviolet rays through), whereas warming comes from greenhouse gases trapping long-wave radiation; ozone itself acts as a greenhouse gas in the lower troposphere.
