### Bridge: Express limits and implied limits on a power

## Two kinds of limit

Every power has limits. An **express limitation** is written in the text: Article 368 says an amendment needs a special majority, and some amendments need ratification by half of the States. Anyone can point to the words.

An **implied limitation** is not written but follows from what the power is and what it is for. Think of a school that gives a class monitor the power to 'arrange the classroom'. Nothing says the monitor may not demolish the building, yet nobody would read the power that way. Arranging is not destroying. The limit is implied by the meaning of the power.

## Why this matters here

Article 368 says Parliament may amend 'by way of addition, variation or repeal' any provision. Read literally, 'repeal' of every provision would let Parliament abolish the Constitution. The doctrine of the basic structure answers that the power to amend is an implied-limit case: it is a power to amend a constitution, not to replace it with a different one. Laxmikanth quotes the Minerva Mills reasoning: the holder of a limited power cannot convert it into an unlimited one.

## Reading the word 'limited'

Do not confuse this with a ban on amendment. The doctrine still allows Parliament to amend every Part, including Fundamental Rights. The implied limit stops only changes that destroy the basic structure.

## Where the label comes from

The phrase 'implied limitations' is commonly linked to scholarly writing in the 1960s that influenced the Court [standard knowledge; verify]. The extracts used here do not use the phrase, so treat the term as a learning aid rather than a quotation.

### Bridge: The identity of a constitution

## A familiar puzzle

If every plank of a ship is replaced one at a time, is it the same ship? Most people say yes if the design, purpose and shape remain. They say no if the ship is rebuilt as something entirely different. A constitution works in a similar way.

## Identity in the Indian context

NCERT (chapter 9) says the Court looked at the basic structure as something without which the Constitution cannot be imagined at all. It describes the Court as balancing the **letter** of the Constitution (the exact words) with its **spirit** (the values and aspirations behind them). It adds that nowhere does the Constitution say that such and such features are part of the basic structure; the idea is the judiciary's own.

So the argument runs: Parliament has the power to amend the Constitution, which means to keep it up to date, and a constitution that has been changed beyond recognition has not been amended but replaced. Replacement belongs to the people through a new constituent process, not to a body created by the old Constitution.

## What counts as the core

The Court has not drawn a full list. Laxmikanth lists about twenty elements that emerged from judgments, such as supremacy of the Constitution, the democratic republic, secularism, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review and free and fair elections. These stand for the identity of the Indian Constitution as read by the Court.

## A caution

'Identity' is a reasoning tool, not a legal test with fixed steps. The Court decides case by case whether a given amendment destroys a feature. Keep two points apart: changing a feature in detail (allowed) and destroying it (not allowed).

## The question

Can Parliament, using Article 368, change anything in the Constitution, including its core? The doctrine of the **basic structure** says no: Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but not so as to alter its basic structure. ### 2. The logic of the doctrine: implied limits and the identity of the Constitution

*Objective:* Explain, in your own words, the two lines of reasoning behind the doctrine and use the Minerva Mills passage.

## Starting from the text

Article 368, as NCERT quotes it, lets Parliament in exercise of its constituent power amend 'by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision'. If the text is read literally, nothing is beyond Parliament. The Court found a limit that is not in the text. NCERT says plainly that the doctrine has no mention in the Constitution and is the invention of the judiciary, born from the spirit and not the letter.

## Reasoning 1: a limited power cannot make itself unlimited (implied limitation)

Laxmikanth quotes the Minerva Mills judgment (1980): since the Constitution conferred a limited amending power, Parliament cannot enlarge that very power into an absolute power; a limited amending power is itself one of the basic features, so the limitations on it cannot be destroyed; Parliament cannot expand its power so as to repeal or abrogate the Constitution or destroy its basic features; and the donee of a limited power cannot convert it into an unlimited one.

Read the argument in steps.

1. The Constitution is the source of Parliament's amending power.
2. Therefore the power is a **conferred** power, not an original one.
3. A conferred power carries the limits of what was conferred: the power to amend a constitution.
4. If Parliament could remove all limits, it would have turned a limited power into an unlimited one, which the donor never gave.

## Reasoning 2: identity

Amending means changing parts while the whole survives. Replacing the core is a different act. NCERT frames this as letter versus spirit and says the Court saw the basic structure as something without which the Constitution cannot be imagined. This is the 'identity' argument (bridge B2).

## What the doctrine does not do

NCERT lists three effects: it sets a specific limit on Parliament; it allows Parliament to amend any and all parts within that limit; and it makes the judiciary the final authority on whether an amendment violates the basic structure and on what the basic structure is. It also records that no list has been given: NCERT's own exercise warns that the statement 'the Supreme Court has given a clear list of basic features' is incorrect, and Laxmikanth says the Court is yet to define or clarify what constitutes the basic structure.

## Why the doctrine matters for balance

NCERT adds that the doctrine consolidates the balance between rigidity and flexibility: by saying that certain parts cannot be amended it underlines rigidity, and by permitting amendments to all others it underlines flexibility.

## Self-test

Try to state both reasonings without looking, then say which one the Minerva Mills passage expresses (the first).

### 3. 1951 to 1973: four cases and Parliament's replies

*Objective:* Read the early cases as a ladder of questions and state what Parliament did after each answer.

GS-173 gives the case cards. Here the same story is read as a ladder of questions, because each answer provoked the next question.

| Step | Question the Court faced | Answer | Parliament's reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shankari Prasad (1951) | Can Article 368 amend Fundamental Rights? Is an amendment a 'law' under Article 13? | Yes. 'Law' in Article 13 means ordinary law, not constituent law | Free hand; more laws added to the Ninth Schedule later |
| Sajjan Singh (1965) | Is the 17th Amendment (1964) valid; should Shankari Prasad stand? | Reaffirmed by a divided Court, with doubts [verify details; see GS-173] | None needed; Golak Nath (1967) challenged the 17th again |
| Golak Nath (1967) | Is the 17th Amendment valid? | Fundamental Rights are 'transcendental and immutable'; an amendment is a law under Article 13 | 24th Amendment (1971) amending Articles 13 and 368 |
| Kesavananda Bharati (1973) | Is the 24th Amendment valid? Is there any limit? | 24th valid; Golak Nath overruled; no power to alter the basic structure | See module 4 |

## Why the ladder matters

In the first case the answer was 'no limit but the procedure'. In the second, doubt appeared. In the third, the Court went to the other extreme: Fundamental Rights could not be touched at all. Parliament's reply was to restore its power by amending Articles 13 and 368 (the 24th Amendment). Only in the fourth case did the Court settle between the two extremes: Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights, but not the basic structure. The doctrine is therefore a **middle path** between 'Parliament can do anything' (1951) and 'Fundamental Rights cannot be touched' (1967).

## The compromise in one line

Kesavananda upheld the 24th Amendment, so Parliament won on **Fundamental Rights**, and it laid down the new doctrine, so the Court won on the **limit**. NCERT also notes that by 1973 the quarrel over property, over Fundamental Rights versus Directive Principles and over amending power had run for years, and the period 1970 to 1975 saw Parliament amend repeatedly to overcome adverse rulings.

## Details to hold with care

- The bench of thirteen and the 7 to 6 majority in Kesavananda, the bench of eleven and the narrow majority in Golak Nath, are widely reported [standard knowledge; verify; see GS-173].
- The Kesavananda judgment is dated 24 April 1973 (Laxmikanth, via Waman Rao). The judges did not agree on a single list; the judgment is a set of separate opinions, and a summary of the view of the majority was signed by a number of judges [standard knowledge; verify the details].
- The Court in Kesavananda also considered the 25th Amendment (Article 31C); that is covered in GS-165.

## Using this for a 15-mark answer

A strong answer uses the ladder as its spine: question, answer, reaction. It can be written in about six lines and forms the middle of the answer to the 2019 Mains question in module 10.

### 4. 1975 to 1981: the doctrine tested by the Emergency amendments

*Objective:* Describe how Indira Nehru Gandhi, the 42nd Amendment, Minerva Mills and Waman Rao fixed the doctrine.

## Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975)

Laxmikanth: the doctrine was reaffirmed and applied. The Court invalidated a provision of the 39th Amendment (1975) that kept election disputes involving the Prime Minister and the Speaker of the Lok Sabha outside the jurisdiction of all courts, because it affected the basic structure. The table in the chapter lists, for this case, sovereign democratic republic, equality of status and opportunity, secularism and freedom of conscience, rule of law, judicial review and free and fair elections.

Why does this matter? It was the first major use of the doctrine **to strike down** an amendment's provision. Before this, Kesavananda had only set the limit. Here the Court showed that the limit had teeth.

## The 42nd Amendment (1976)

Parliament reacted to what Laxmikanth calls the judicially innovated doctrine. The amendment changed Article 368 to declare that there is no limitation on the constituent power of Parliament and that no amendment can be questioned in any court on any ground, including contravention of Fundamental Rights. [Standard knowledge, verify: this was done by adding two clauses, 368(4) and 368(5).] NCERT describes the 38th, 39th and 42nd Amendments as the most controversial, made in the background of the internal emergency, and says the 42nd tried to override Kesavananda and restricted judicial review.

## Minerva Mills (1980)

The Court invalidated the provision because it excluded judicial review, which is a basic feature. The key reasoning is the passage in module 2: Parliament cannot enlarge its limited power into an absolute one. Two features are thus recognised in this case: a **limited power to amend** and **judicial review**; the table also lists harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles (see GS-165).

## Waman Rao (1981)

The Court adhered to the doctrine and clarified that it applies to constitutional amendments enacted **after 24 April 1973**, the date of the Kesavananda judgment. This is the cut-off used later in the Ninth Schedule cases (module 8).

## NCERT's reading of the period

After 1977 the 43rd and 44th Amendments cancelled most of the changes made by the 38th, 39th and 42nd Amendments. NCERT adds that after Kesavananda there were attempts to ask the Court to reconsider; when these failed, the 42nd Amendment asserted parliamentary supremacy; the Court repeated its stand in Minerva Mills; and for four decades all institutions accepted the doctrine. Even the Venkatachaliah commission (2000) kept within it.

## One chain to remember

39th Amendment (courts barred) leads to Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (struck down). 42nd Amendment (no limit, no court) leads to Minerva Mills (struck down). Each parliamentary attempt to remove judicial review was answered by judicial review.

## Common confusion

Waman Rao decides **from when** the doctrine applies; Minerva Mills decides **that the limited power is itself basic**. Do not swap them.

## What each case adds, in plain words

**Kihoto Hollohon.** The Tenth Schedule bars courts from examining anti-defection decisions of the Presiding Officer. [Standard knowledge, verify: the Court held that part to be invalid because it needed ratification by half the States, since it curtailed High Court and Supreme Court jurisdiction.] This is a **procedural** use of limits (GS-167). It was decided in 1992 and is printed as 1993 by Laxmikanth, who treats it as a basic-structure case on free and fair elections and democracy.

**S. R. Bommai.** NCERT chapter 7 records that the Court ruled the validity of President's Rule can be examined by the judiciary. Laxmikanth lists federalism, secularism and democracy as basic here. The practical result is that a State government cannot be dismissed at the Centre's pleasure without judicial scrutiny.

**L. Chandra Kumar.** The Court recognised that judicial review by the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure, so tribunals cannot exclude it [standard knowledge, verify the detail].

**I. R. Coelho.** Laws in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed on the effect on rights and the basic structure (module 8).

## The 2015 judges' appointment case (NJAC)

Parliament enacted the 99th Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act in 2014 to replace the collegium system. In 2015 a five-judge bench struck them down, holding that they damaged the independence of the judiciary, which is a basic feature [standard knowledge; verify the details such as the 4 to 1 majority]. This case is **not** in Laxmikanth's 6e table, which lists the 2014 Madras Bar Association case instead; the 2017 Mains question asked for a critical examination of this very judgment.

## The pattern

After 1980 the Court rarely asks whether a feature is basic from scratch. It uses features already listed (judicial review, equality, federalism, free elections) and applies them to new facts. When you meet a case, ask: which listed feature does it protect, and was the attack on the feature a law, an amendment or an executive act?

### 6. The basic features: what the Court has listed and how to learn it

*Objective:* Reproduce the groups of features Laxmikanth lists and the case that recognised the earliest ones, and state the meaning of an open list.

## Laxmikanth's list

Laxmikanth says that from various judgments the following have emerged as basic features: supremacy of the Constitution; sovereign, democratic and republican nature; secular character; separation of powers; federal character; unity and integrity of the nation; welfare State (socio-economic justice); judicial review; freedom and dignity of the individual; parliamentary system; rule of law; harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; principle of equality; free and fair elections; independence of the judiciary; limited power of Parliament to amend; effective access to justice; principles (or essence) underlying fundamental rights; powers of the Supreme Court under Articles 32, 136, 141 and 142; powers of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227. That is twenty.

## Learning it in four groups

1. **Nature of the State**: supremacy of the Constitution; sovereign, democratic, republican; secular; federal; unity and integrity; welfare State; parliamentary system.
2. **Rights and equality**: freedom and dignity of the individual; equality; essence of fundamental rights; harmony of Rights and Directives; effective access to justice.
3. **Courts and law**: judicial review; independence of the judiciary; rule of law; separation of powers; powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
4. **Democracy and amendment**: free and fair elections; limited amending power.

## Which case recognised which (the early ones, from the table)

- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**: supremacy of the Constitution; separation of powers; republic and democratic form; secular character; federal character; sovereignty and unity of India; freedom and dignity of the individual; mandate to build a welfare State; parliamentary system.
- **Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975)**: sovereign democratic republic; equality of status and opportunity; secularism and freedom of conscience; rule of law; judicial review; free and fair elections.
- **Minerva Mills (1980)**: limited power to amend; judicial review; harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
- **Central Coal Fields (1980)**: effective access to justice. **Bhim Singhji (1981)**: welfare State.
- **Sampath Kumar (1987)** and **P. Sambamurthy (1987)**: rule of law; judicial review.
- **Delhi Judicial Service Association (1991)**: powers of the Supreme Court. **Kumar Padma Prasad (1992)**: independence of the judiciary.

The full case-by-case table is in the Master notes.

## The list is open

Laxmikanth states that the Court is yet to define or clarify the basic structure. So the list shows what has emerged, not a final code. A feature absent from it is not proved amendable; a feature on it is not frozen in every detail.

## One feature recognised many times

Judicial review appears in Kesavananda's successors (Indira Gandhi, Minerva Mills, Sampath Kumar, Sambamurthy, Bommai, Coelho, Madras Bar). That repetition is why it is the most frequently cited basic feature in the table.

## What basic structure is not

NCERT's exercise warns against three mistakes: that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution after Kesavananda; that the Court has given a clear list; and that Kesavananda set no limits. The first is false (Parliament may amend), the second is false (no closed list), and the third is false (it set a limit).

### 7. For and against: judicial overreach, the counter-majoritarian difficulty and foreign comparison

*Objective:* Build a balanced argument on the doctrine and place it in a comparative frame with an appropriate caution.

## The case for the doctrine

1. **Protects the core from temporary majorities.** NCERT's discussion of amendments says a constitution too easily changed gives no security or identity. Without the doctrine, a party with a two-thirds majority could alter the democratic structure itself.
2. **Prevents misuse in a crisis.** The Emergency amendments (38th, 39th, 42nd) show how a majority can try to remove courts and rights; the doctrine and judicial review answered them (modules 3 and 4).
3. **Keeps the balance of rigidity and flexibility** (NCERT).
4. **Accepted over time.** NCERT notes that political parties, governments and Parliament accepted it for four decades, even in the Venkatachaliah review.
5. **Clear limits on the Court too.** The doctrine does not let judges amend anything; it only tests amendments, and Parliament can still amend all parts.

## The case against

1. **Judicial overreach.** The phrase is nowhere in the text; critics say judges have made a limit that elected representatives did not agree. NCERT records the objection that if amending power is with elected representatives, the judiciary should not decide validity.
2. **An open list.** The Court has not defined the structure (Laxmikanth), so Parliament cannot know in advance what is safe, and the Court decides each time.
3. **Unelected judges over elected representatives**, called the **counter-majoritarian difficulty**: a small group not chosen by voters sets aside the choice of the people's representatives.
4. **Judges deciding their own sphere.** In the judges' appointment case, the independence of the judiciary was used to strike an amendment about how judges are appointed, which critics say shows the Court judging its own cause [a view held by some commentators; the opposite view is that independence is exactly what the amendment threatened].

## How defenders answer the counter-majoritarian charge

A constitution is the people's higher law. Judicial review of amendments enforces that higher law against the temporary majority. NCERT adds that democracy is also about checks on arbitrary power and the rule of law, not only votes. The Court did not rule on the policy merits; it asked whether the amendment stayed inside the Constitution's framework.

## Foreign comparison (flagged)

Some constitutions contain an express 'eternity clause' that places certain principles beyond amendment; Germany's Basic Law is the usual example [standard knowledge; verify the Article and its content, as the Indian extracts do not discuss it]. The contrast is useful: Germany states the limits in the text (express limitation) whereas India's limit is implied by the Court (bridge B1). Do not go further than this without checking a source.

## Writing a balanced answer

Give two points for, two against, and a synthesis: the doctrine is a safeguard whose legitimacy depends on restraint, clarity and consensus. Anchor with the NCERT line that debates on what is basic are healthy in a democracy.

### 8. The Ninth Schedule after Coelho, and the current debate

*Objective:* Explain the Coelho position on Article 31B and write neutrally about the recent public debate.

## The problem Article 31B created

Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule were added by the 1st Amendment (1951) to protect listed laws (mainly land reform) from challenge for violating Fundamental Rights. Laxmikanth's Schedules table says the Supreme Court ruled in 2007 that laws included after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review. The case is **I. R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu (2007)**.

## Coelho in three steps

1. **Starting point.** Judicial review is a basic feature. Parliament cannot remove it by placing a law in a Schedule.
2. **Cut-off.** Waman Rao (module 4) fixed 24 April 1973 for amendments. Laws added after that date are open to review.
3. **Test.** Laxmikanth lists for Coelho: rule of law, separation of powers, essence of fundamental rights, judicial review and equality. GS-173 explains the 'effect' (rights) test: a law may be reviewed for damage to Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 [verify; usually cited as Articles 14, 19 and 21] and to the basic structure.

Laws added **before** 24 April 1973 are not reopened on these principles, according to GS-173's summary of the conclusions [verify].

## The Prelims fact trap

'Ninth Schedule laws are immune from judicial review' is the old statement. The correct modern statement has a date.

## The current debate (neutral, flagged)

In 2022 and 2023 senior constitutional functionaries, including the then Vice-President of India (who chaired the Rajya Sabha at the time), publicly questioned whether the Court's doctrine can limit Parliament's constituent power, and referred to the 2015 judges' appointment case. Judges, lawyers and scholars replied in defence of the doctrine. **Verify** the dates, speakers and wording before using any detail; treat everything here after 2024 as unchecked. The lesson takes no side.

## How to write about it

- Use verbs such as 'argued', 'questioned' and 'responded'.
- Present both sides: sovereignty of the elected Parliament, and constitutional supremacy with judicial review.
- Return to settled law: the Court has applied the doctrine since 1973 and Parliament still amends the Constitution under Article 368.
- Close with a constructive point: dialogue between institutions inside the Constitution (NCERT's tug-of-war and balance image).

### 9. Examination application: seven verified questions and the traps

## Seven questions, three shapes

1. **Limits on amending power** (Mains 2025 Q12; Mains 2019 Q12). Procedural and substantive limits; whether Parliament can destroy the basic structure.
2. **Institutions and the doctrine** (Mains 2017 Q2 on judges' appointments; Mains 2023 Q4 on parliamentary sovereignty; Mains 2021 Q1 on constitutional morality).
3. **Prelims statements** (2019 Q81; 2020 Q13) testing the existence and content of basic structure and of judicial review.

## Worked example: 2020 Prelims Q13 (thinking aloud)

Statement 1 says the Constitution defines the basic structure in terms of federalism, secularism, fundamental rights and democracy. NCERT states that nowhere does the Constitution mention the basic structure, so the Constitution does not define it; the Court has listed features across many cases. Statement 1 therefore conflicts with the source. Statement 2 says the Constitution provides for judicial review to safeguard liberties; judicial review is an established feature (Laxmikanth lists it as basic) and operates through Articles such as 13, 32 and 226 [verify Articles]. The analysis therefore points to statement 2 only. Trap: statement 1 sounds plausible because the four features are real basic features, but the Constitution does not define them.

## Worked example: 2019 Prelims Q81

Statement 1 says no High Court can declare a central law invalid. The Court recognised the powers of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 as basic (Laxmikanth's table), which includes testing laws against the Constitution, so the absolute claim conflicts with that. [Verify] Context: the 42nd Amendment's Article 226A barred High Courts from judging the validity of central laws, and the 43rd Amendment (1977) repealed it; that history is where the trap in this question comes from. Statement 2 says an amendment cannot be questioned by the Supreme Court. Kesavananda and Minerva Mills hold that amendments can be struck down for altering the basic structure. Both statements conflict with the sources, so the analysis points to neither. No official key is claimed.

## Traps

- **Which case recognised which feature.** Learn the early cases by name: Kesavananda (supremacy, federal, secular), Indira Gandhi (free elections, rule of law), Minerva Mills (limited amending power, balance of Rights and Directives), Waman Rao (cut-off).
- **Basic structure versus Fundamental Rights.** Fundamental Rights can be amended; only those parts that form part of the basic structure cannot be destroyed.
- **Defined versus listed.** The Constitution does not define it.
- **Absolute words**: 'cannot be questioned', 'no court', 'only'.
- **Dates**: 1973 (judgment and cut-off), 1975, 1980, 1981, 2007.

### 10. Guided practice: three 15-mark frameworks and a recall schedule

*Objective:* Plan three 15-mark answers and set up retrieval practice.

## Framework A: 'Can Parliament destroy the basic structure by expanding its amending power?' (2019 style)

1. **Opening (40 words)**: Article 368 gives a constituent power, but the doctrine says it is limited.
2. **Why limited (70 words)**: the Minerva Mills passage; implied limitation; identity.
3. **Path (80 words)**: Shankari Prasad, Golak Nath, 24th Amendment, Kesavananda, 42nd Amendment, Minerva Mills.
4. **Application (40 words)**: Waman Rao's cut-off; Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain.
5. **Conclusion (20 words)**: No; Parliament may amend widely but not enlarge into absolute power.

## Framework B: 'Critically examine the basic structure doctrine'

1. For: protects core; Emergency experience; balance of rigidity and flexibility.
2. Against: judicial overreach; counter-majoritarian difficulty; undefined list.
3. Synthesis: legitimacy rests on restraint, consistency and acceptance across institutions.

## Framework C: 'How have post-1980 cases extended the doctrine?'

1. Bommai (federalism, secularism), Chandra Kumar (High Court review), Coelho (Ninth Schedule), the 2015 judges' appointment case (judicial independence).
2. Pattern: listed features applied to new facts.
3. Assessment: strong protection, some uncertainty.

## Directive reminders

**Critically examine** needs both sides; **Examine** needs explanation with evidence; **Discuss** needs balanced coverage. Keep to 250 words for 15 marks.

### Notes: Key definitions

- Basic structure doctrine: Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution including Fundamental Rights, but not so as to alter the basic structure (Laxmikanth).
- Constituent power: the power to amend or make a constitution; legislative power makes ordinary law.
- Express limitation: written in the text (special majority, ratification). Implied limitation: follows from the nature of the power (basic structure).
- Judicial review: courts testing laws and amendments against the Constitution; recognised as a basic feature.
- Counter-majoritarian difficulty: unelected judges overriding elected majorities.
- Waman Rao cut-off: the doctrine applies to amendments enacted after 24 April 1973.

### Notes: The ladder of cases (question, answer, reaction)

- Shankari Prasad (1951): amendment is not a law under Article 13; Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights.
- Golak Nath (1967): amendment is a law under Article 13; Fundamental Rights 'transcendental and immutable'. Parliament replies with the 24th Amendment (1971).
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973): 24th upheld; Golak Nath overruled; basic structure limit.
- 39th Amendment (1975) bars courts from the PM and Speaker election disputes; Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975) strikes the provision.
- 42nd Amendment (1976): no limit on constituent power, no challenge in any court; Minerva Mills (1980) strikes it.
- Waman Rao (1981): doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973.
- I. R. Coelho (2007): Ninth Schedule laws after that date are reviewable.

### Notes: Express versus implied limits on amendment

- Basic structure is the implied limit; the procedure is the express one.

### Notes: Ninth Schedule and Coelho

- Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule: 1st Amendment (1951), to protect listed laws from challenge for violating Fundamental Rights.
- Original 13 Acts; Laxmikanth prints 282 (a dated count).
- I. R. Coelho (2007): no blanket immunity; laws placed after 24 April 1973 are open to review for rights and the basic structure.
- Earlier laws are not reopened on these principles (GS-173 summary; verify).

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Basic structure versus Fundamental Rights: rights can be amended unless they are part of the basic structure.
- Listed features versus a defined structure: the Court has listed features, the Constitution does not define them.
- Waman Rao (cut-off) versus Minerva Mills (limited power is basic).
- Kesavananda (limit) versus Golak Nath (no amendment of rights).
- Judicial review of amendments versus judicial review of ordinary laws.
- Procedural limits (Kihoto Hollohon ratification point) versus substantive limits (basic structure).

### Last-week summary

- Shankari Prasad 1951 (amendment not a law under Art 13) -> Golak Nath 1967 (it is; rights immutable) -> 24th Amendment 1971 -> Kesavananda 24 April 1973 (24th upheld, Golak Nath overruled, basic structure limit).
