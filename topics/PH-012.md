### Bridge: Integration of density over shapes: line, area and volume elements

To find a centre of mass of a continuous body you cut it into tiny pieces, write the mass of each piece, and add. That is what an integral does. The only new idea is how to write the mass of a piece, called the **mass element dm**.

- **Line (rod or wire)**: **linear density** λ = mass per unit length, in kg m⁻¹. A piece of length dℓ has dm = λ dℓ.
- **Area (plate or lamina)**: **surface density** σ = mass per unit area, in kg m⁻². A piece of area dA has dm = σ dA.
- **Volume (solid)**: **density** ρ = mass per unit volume, in kg m⁻³. A piece of volume dV has dm = ρ dV.

A body is **uniform** (homogeneous) when λ, σ or ρ is the same everywhere, so it can be pulled out of the integral. If the density depends on position, for example λ = λ₀ s/L along a rod, it stays inside.

Choosing the piece: use a **thin slice** whose points all have the same coordinate you need. For a rod along x, the slice is a small length dx. For a solid hemisphere standing on its flat face, thin discs parallel to the face are convenient because every point of a disc has the same height z. For circular shapes, polar coordinates (r, θ) make dA = r dr dθ, and spherical coordinates give dV = r² sin θ dr dθ dφ. The limits of integration are the first and last position of the slices: that choice is where most marks are lost.

Always check units: ∫ dm has units of mass, ∫ x dm has units of mass times length.

### Bridge: Symmetry: when an integral is zero without doing it

A function is **odd** if f(−x) = −f(x) (for example x, x³, sin θ about θ = 0); the integral of an odd function over limits −a to +a is zero, because the positive and negative parts cancel. A function is **even** if f(−x) = f(x) (x², cos θ); its integral over −a to +a is twice the integral from 0 to a.

For a centre of mass, ∫ x dm over a body is zero whenever the body has the same mass element at −x as at +x. This is the **reflection symmetry** argument that NCERT uses to show that the centre of mass of a uniform rod, ring, disc or sphere is at its geometric centre (Class XI, Systems of Particles, section 6.2). The same argument shows more: if a body is symmetric about a **plane**, its centre of mass lies in that plane; if symmetric about an **axis**, the centre of mass lies on the axis. A uniform semicircular plate is symmetric about its axis of symmetry, so only the distance along that axis has to be computed.

Symmetry fails if the density is not symmetric: a rod with λ = λ₀ s/L is not symmetric about its middle, so its centre of mass is not at L/2. Always check the density as well as the shape.

### 1. Orientation: what the centre of mass is and why it is a toolkit

*Objective:* State what the centre of mass does for a system, see the five past questions, and know what earlier lessons already give.

## The idea in one paragraph

A system of particles (a rod, a pair of atoms, an exploding shell) can twist, vibrate and break apart. Yet one point in it behaves as if the **whole mass were concentrated there** and **all the external forces acted there**: the **centre of mass (CM)**. NCERT Class XI and Mathur both introduce it this way. Because internal forces cannot move it, the CM turns a messy many-body problem into a one-particle problem for the translational motion.

## Vocabulary

**Moment of mass** (first moment): Σ mᵢ xᵢ. **Centroid**: the CM of a uniform body, which depends only on shape. **Lamina**: a thin flat plate. **Homogeneous**: uniform density. **Barycentre**: another name for the CM, common in astronomy (PH-009).

### 2. Foundation: the CM of discrete particles, shift of origin and two-body formulas

*Objective:* Compute R for particles in a plane or in space, prove its independence of the origin, and use the two-particle formulas.

## Definition

For particles of masses m₁, …, m_N at position vectors r₁, …, r_N from an origin O, the **centre of mass** is

$$ R = (Σ mᵢ rᵢ)/M,    M = Σ mᵢ $$

In components, X = Σ mᵢ xᵢ/M, Y = Σ mᵢ yᵢ/M, Z = Σ mᵢ zᵢ/M (NCERT eqs. 6.4; Mathur §6.2). It is a **mass-weighted average position**. Two equal masses have the midpoint as CM.

## Two particles on a line

For masses m₁ and m₂ a distance r apart, put the origin at m₁: x₁ = 0, x₂ = r. Then X = m₂ r/(m₁ + m₂). So the CM lies on the line joining them, at distance

$$ r₁ = m₂ r/(m₁ + m₂)  from m₁,    r₂ = m₁ r/(m₁ + m₂)  from m₂ $$

with r₁ + r₂ = r and m₁ r₁ = m₂ r₂ (the seesaw balance). The heavier particle is nearer to the CM. This is the formula asked in 2017 Q2(b); module 8 continues it to the moment of inertia.

## Properties

1. The CM lies within the smallest convex region containing the particles (between the extreme positions along any line).
2. **Origin independence.** Shift the origin to a point with position vector a: new positions rᵢ′ = rᵢ − a. Then R′ = Σ mᵢ (rᵢ − a)/M = R − a. The CM is the same physical point; only its coordinates change by the shift. (This needs M ≠ 0.)
3. **Zero moment about the CM.** With the origin at the CM, R = 0, so Σ mᵢ rᵢ′ = 0 where rᵢ′ = rᵢ − R. Differentiating, Σ mᵢ vᵢ′ = 0: the total momentum in the CM frame is zero (Mathur calls it the zero-momentum frame).
4. Dimensions: [R] = L. The first moment Σ m x has dimensions M L.

## Examples

**Three particles at the corners of an equilateral triangle** (NCERT Example 6.1): masses 100, 150 and 200 g at (0, 0), (0.5, 0) and (0.25, 0.433) m. Then X = (150 × 0.5 + 200 × 0.25)/450 = 0.278 m and Y = 200 × 0.433/450 = 0.192 m (verified with a script). The CM is not the geometric centre of the triangle, which would be the CM only for equal masses.

**Hydrogen atom** (part of 2019 Q2(a)): a proton is 1837 times heavier than an electron, so M = 1838 mₑ. The CM is at r mₑ/(M) = r/1838 from the proton: with r = 0.529 Å, 2.88 × 10⁻⁴ Å = 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁴ m from the proton centre, i.e. essentially at the proton. (The Bohr radius 0.529 Å is a standard value; verify.)

## Checks

Units: the answer is a length. Limiting case: m₂ → ∞ gives r₁ → r (the CM sits on the heavy particle); m₁ = m₂ gives r/2.

### 3. Core: continuous bodies and the rod, uniform and non-uniform

*Objective:* Write R = (1/M)∫ r dm for a continuous body and compute the CM of a uniform and a non-uniform rod with limits.

## From sums to integrals

Divide a body into elements dm at position r. The sum becomes an integral over the whole body:

$$ R = (1/M) ∫ r dm,    M = ∫ dm $$

Components: X = ∫ x dm/M, etc. (Mathur §6.2 uses ρ dV for volumes.) The mass element dm = λ dℓ, σ dA or ρ dV (bridge B1); the **limits** run over the whole body.

## Uniform rod

Rod from x = 0 to x = L, λ constant: M = λ L, ∫ x dm = λ ∫₀ᴸ x dx = λ L²/2. So

$$ X = (λ L²/2)/(λ L) = L/2 $$

By symmetry (bridge B2) this is the midpoint: for every dm at x there is one at L − x.

## Non-uniform rod: the 2018 question

A rod of length L has λ = λ₀ S/L, where S is the distance from the end O. (Taken from the question text; its figure is not reproduced here.) Take O as origin and s = S.

Total mass: M = ∫₀ᴸ λ₀ (s/L) ds = (λ₀/L)(L²/2) = λ₀ L/2.

First moment: ∫ s dm = ∫₀ᴸ s (λ₀ s/L) ds = (λ₀/L)(L³/3) = λ₀ L²/3.

$$ X = (λ₀ L²/3)/(λ₀ L/2) = 2L/3  measured from O $$

If the unseen figure of the question marks the other end as O, state the answer both ways: the CM is 2L/3 from the end where λ = 0 and therefore L/3 from the heavier end. Interpretation: the far end is denser, so the CM moves beyond the midpoint. Dimensions: λ₀ in kg m⁻¹; M in kg; X in m.

## General power-law density

For λ = λ₀ (s/L)ⁿ with n ≥ 0: M = λ₀ L/(n + 1), ∫ s dm = λ₀ L²/(n + 2), so X = (n + 1) L/(n + 2). Check: n = 0 (uniform) gives L/2; n = 1 gives 2L/3; n → ∞ gives L (all the mass at the far end). A different density λ = λ₀ (1 + s/L) gives M = 3λ₀ L/2 and ∫ s dm = 5λ₀ L²/6, so X = 5L/9 = 0.556 L, just beyond the midpoint (verified by sympy).

## Choosing the origin

The result is the same physical point from any origin. Measured from the **middle** of the rod instead of from the end O, the CM is at 2L/3 − L/2 = L/6 beyond the middle, consistent with R′ = R − a (module 2).

## Exam habit

State the density, the element, the limits, then both integrals. Give the result as "distance from end O" and not just a number.

### 4. Core: semicircular wire, semicircular plate, sector and triangle

*Objective:* Derive the centroids 2R/π, 4R/3π, 2R sin α/3α and 2h/3 with limits and symmetry.

Set O at the centre of the circle, the flat edge on the x-axis and the shape in the upper half-plane; symmetry gives X = 0, so only Y is computed.

## Semicircular wire (radius R, uniform λ)

An element at angle θ (0 ≤ θ ≤ π) has length ds = R dθ, mass dm = λ R dθ and height y = R sin θ. M = λ π R.

$$ Y = (1/M) ∫ y dm = (λ R² ∫₀^π sin θ dθ)/(λ π R) = (λ R² × 2)/(λ π R) = 2R/π $$

Check by a numerical average of sin θ over [0, π]: 0.63662. For an arc of half-angle α about the axis, Y = R sin α/α: α → π gives 0 (a full ring), α → 0 gives R (the arc shrinks to a point on the circle).

## Semicircular plate (radius R, uniform σ)

An element in polar form has dA = r dr dθ, y = r sin θ, M = σ π R²/2:

$$ Y = (σ ∫₀ᴿ r² dr ∫₀^π sin θ dθ)/(σ π R²/2) = (σ (R³/3)(2))/(σ π R²/2) = 4R/(3π) $$

Alternative by strips: a horizontal strip at height y has length 2√(R² − y²) and area 2√(R² − y²) dy; ∫₀ᴿ y 2√(R² − y²) dy = (2/3) R³ (substitute u = R² − y²), the same numerator. Value: 0.4244 R, lower than the wire's 0.6366 R because the plate has mass near the centre.

## Circular sector

A sector of half-angle α, radius R, with θ measured from its axis: M = σ α R² and ∫ x dm = σ ∫₀ᴿ r² dr ∫_{−α}^{α} cos θ dθ = σ (R³/3)(2 sin α). So

$$ X = 2R sin α/(3α)  from the apex $$

Limits: α = π/2 gives 4R/(3π), the semicircular plate; α → 0 gives 2R/3, the thin sector, i.e. the triangle-like result. For α = 60° (a 120° sector) X = 0.551 R.

## Triangle

Let the apex be at the top, base b at distance h. A thin strip at distance y from the apex has width b y/h, so dm = σ (b y/h) dy. M = σ b h/2 and ∫ y dm = σ (b/h)(h³/3):

$$ ȳ = (b h²/3)/(b h/2) = 2h/3  from the apex, that is h/3 above the base $$

This agrees with the median argument of NCERT Example 6.2: each strip's CM is at its midpoint, so the CM lies on a median, and three medians meet at the centroid. (A triangle made of three wires is a different problem and is not treated here.)

## Checks

All answers have the dimension of length and are proportional to R or h; each limit above is consistent.

### 5. Core: solid hemisphere, hemispherical shell, cone and a non-uniform hemisphere

*Objective:* Integrate to 3R/8, R/2 and H/4, and compute a non-uniform hemisphere.

The **2020 question** is the solid hemisphere. Take O at the centre of the flat base, the z-axis along the symmetry axis and the solid in z ≥ 0. By symmetry the CM lies on the axis.

## Solid hemisphere (density ρ), by discs

At height z the cross-section is a disc of radius √(R² − z²), area π (R² − z²). The slice of thickness dz has dm = ρ π (R² − z²) dz.

$$ M = ρ π ∫₀ᴿ (R² − z²) dz = ρ π (R³ − R³/3) = (2/3) ρ π R³ $$

$$ ∫ z dm = ρ π ∫₀ᴿ z (R² − z²) dz = ρ π (R⁴/2 − R⁴/4) = ρ π R⁴/4 $$

$$ Z = (ρ π R⁴/4)/((2/3) ρ π R³) = 3R/8 $$

Check with spherical coordinates: dV = r² sin θ dr dθ dφ, z = r cos θ, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2: ∫ z dV = ∫₀ᴿ r³ dr ∫ sin θ cos θ dθ ∫ dφ = (R⁴/4)(1/2)(2π) = π R⁴/4. Same. A random-point average over the hemisphere gives 0.375 R (numerical check).

## Hemispherical shell (surface density σ)

A zone of the shell between heights z and z + dz has area 2π R dz (Archimedes: equal heights give equal areas). So z is uniformly distributed from 0 to R and the mean height is R/2:

$$ Z = (∫₀ᴿ z 2π R dz)/(2π R × R) = R/2 $$

The spherical-coordinate version: dA = R² sin θ dθ dφ, ∫ z dA = R³(1/2)(2π) = π R³, M = 2π R² σ: Z = R/2.

## Solid cone (height H, base radius R)

Put the apex at x = 0. At distance x the radius is R x/H, so dm = ρ π R² x² dx/H². M = ρ π R² H/3 and ∫ x dm = ρ π R² H²/4:

$$ x̄ = (3/4) H from the apex,  that is H/4 above the base $$

For the **hollow cone** (lateral surface) the area element is proportional to x dx (circumference grows linearly), so x̄ = ∫ x² dx/∫ x dx = 2H/3 from the apex, that is H/3 above the base.

## A non-uniform hemisphere

Let ρ = ρ₀ r/R, where r is the distance from the centre of the flat face. M = ∫ ρ dV = ρ₀ (2π/R) ∫ r³ dr ∫₀^{π/2} sin θ dθ = π ρ₀ R³/2, and ∫ z dm = ρ₀ (2π/R) ∫ r⁴ dr ∫ sin θ cos θ dθ = π ρ₀ R⁴/5. So

$$ Z = (π ρ₀ R⁴/5)/(π ρ₀ R³/2) = 2R/5 = 0.4 R $$

which exceeds 3R/8 = 0.375 R: the heavier outer region moves the CM away from the flat face.

## Limits

Each result has the dimension of length. The shell's CM (R/2) is higher than the solid's (3R/8) because the shell's mass lies farther from the base.

### 6. Core: composite bodies, negative mass and the Pappus-Guldinus theorems

*Objective:* Combine or subtract shapes, and derive and use the two Pappus-Guldinus theorems.

## Method of addition

If a body is a union of pieces whose CMs and masses are known, the CM of the whole is the mass-weighted average of the piece centres: R = Σ Mᵢ Rᵢ/Σ Mᵢ. This is exactly the definition applied to the pieces (each treated as a point mass at its own CM). NCERT's Example 6.3, an L-shaped lamina of three 1 m squares of mass 1 kg each at (½, ½), (3/2, ½) and (½, 3/2) m, gives X = Y = 5/6 m (verified).

## Method of subtraction (negative mass)

A body with a hole is the full body plus a body of **negative mass** in the hole's place:

$$ R = (M_full R_full − M_hole R_hole)/(M_full − M_hole) $$

For a uniform disc of radius R with a circular hole of radius a whose centre is at distance d from the disc's centre (a + d ≤ R): masses ∝ R² and a², so, from the disc's centre,

$$ X = −d a²/(R² − a²) $$

(opposite side to the hole). For a = d = R/2: X = −(R/2)(1/4)/(3/4) = −R/6 (a random-point check gives −0.167 R). The same result holds for a solid cylinder with a cylindrical hole parallel to its axis (the mass per unit length factors out). **Sphere with a spherical hole (3-D).** For a solid sphere of radius R with a spherical hole of radius a whose centre is at distance d from the centre (a + d ≤ R) the masses are proportional to R³ and a³, so X = −d a³/(R³ − a³) from the sphere's centre, on the side opposite the hole. For a = d = R/2: X = −(R/2)(1/8)/(7/8) = −R/14 = −0.0714 R (random-point check −0.0714 R).

**Negative mass is a bookkeeping device only**; it needs the net mass M_full − M_hole to be positive.

**Cone on a hemisphere** (ice-cream shape, same density): cone of radius R = 3 cm, height 9 cm above the flat plane; hemisphere below the plane. The cone's CM is at +H/4 = +2.25 cm and the hemisphere's is at −3R/8 = −1.125 cm. Volumes: πR²H/3 = 27π and 2πR³/3 = 18π. The combined CM: (27π × 2.25 − 18π × 1.125)/(45π) = 0.9 cm above the plane.

## Pappus-Guldinus theorems (standard results; derived here)

Take a plane curve of length ℓ, or a plane region of area A, lying on one side of an axis in its plane at perpendicular distance ȳ of its centroid. Revolve it once about the axis.

**Theorem 1 (surface).** An element of the curve of length dℓ at distance y sweeps a ring of area 2π y dℓ. So

$$ S = 2π ∫ y dℓ = 2π ȳ ℓ $$

since ȳ ℓ = ∫ y dℓ is the definition of the centroid. **Theorem 2 (volume).** An element dA at distance y sweeps a ring of volume 2π y dA, so V = 2π ∫ y dA = 2π ȳ A. Conditions: the curve or region must not cross the axis and must not overlap itself during the revolution.

## Using the theorems

- Semicircular wire: sphere area 4π R² = (π R)(2π ȳ) gives ȳ = 2R/π.
- Semicircular plate: sphere volume 4π R³/3 = (π R²/2)(2π ȳ) gives ȳ = 4R/(3π).
- Cone: a right triangle with legs R and H about the leg H: A = RH/2, ȳ = R/3, so V = (RH/2)(2π R/3) = π R² H/3.
- Torus (ring radius a, tube radius r): V = 2π a × π r² = 2π² a r² and S = 2π a × 2π r = 4π² a r.

**Trap.** Pappus gives the centroid of the **plane region or curve** that was revolved, not of the solid produced. A quarter-disc revolved about its straight edge makes a solid hemisphere, but the quarter-disc's centroid distance is 4R/(3π), not 3R/8.

### 7. Core: the motion of the centre of mass, M R̈ = F_ext, and its consequences

*Objective:* Derive M R̈ = F_ext for n particles, and use it for projectiles that explode and for a man walking on a boat.

## Derivation (n particles)

Particle i has mass mᵢ (constant) and position rᵢ. The force on it is the external force Fᵢ^ext plus internal forces F_ij from the other particles j. Newton's second law:

$$ mᵢ r̈ᵢ = Fᵢ^ext + Σ_{j ≠ i} F_ij $$

Sum over all i. On the left Σ mᵢ r̈ᵢ = d²(Σ mᵢ rᵢ)/dt² = M R̈ (masses constant). On the right the internal sum Σᵢ Σ_{j≠i} F_ij contains each pair twice as F_ij + F_ji. Newton's third law (equal and opposite, **weak form**; the forces need not be central) makes each pair vanish. Therefore

$$ M R̈ = F_ext = Σ Fᵢ^ext $$

and with P = M Ṙ, dP/dt = F_ext. This is the CM motion theorem; NCERT eq. 6.11 and Upadhyaya (PDF p. 84) state it, and PH-001 module 5 gives the same derivation with the third law discussed.

Meaning: **only external forces move the CM; internal forces rearrange the parts about it.** If F_ext = 0 then Ṙ is constant: the CM stays at rest or moves uniformly. Dimensions: M R̈ is a force.

## A projectile that explodes

A shell moves on a parabola under gravity. At the top it explodes, but the forces of the explosion are internal, and gravity is the same before and after. The CM of the fragments therefore keeps following the original parabola until the first fragment lands (NCERT Fig. 6.12). Example: a 6 kg shell with range 400 m on level ground explodes at the top into 2 kg and 4 kg pieces; the 2 kg piece falls straight down. It lands under the top, at 200 m. The CM lands at 400 m, so (2 × 200 + 4 x)/6 = 400 gives x = 500 m for the other piece. Check by momentum: at the top 6u = 4v gives v = 1.5u, which moves a further 1.5 × 200 = 300 m (time of fall is half the flight time) beyond 200 m: 500 m.

## Man on a boat

There is no horizontal external force on boat + man (water resistance neglected), so the horizontal position of the CM cannot change. If the man walks a distance s relative to the boat, the boat moves back by D with m (s − D) = M D, so D = m s/(m + M). With m = 60 kg, M = 140 kg and s = 3 m: D = 0.9 m, and the man moves 2.1 m on the ground. Verify: 60 × 2.1 − 140 × 0.9 = 0.

## Other uses

- **Momentum conservation** and the CM are the same statement: F_ext = 0 gives P and Ṙ constant.
- A person cannot lift themselves by pulling their own hair: that force is internal.
- Once airborne, a jumping person can change shape, but the CM follows a parabola (during the push-off the ground force is external and does accelerate the CM).
- **Variable mass** (a rocket): apply the theorem to a fixed set of particles (rocket plus the exhaust that will leave); the CM of that set obeys M R̈ = F_ext, and PH-001 module 4 turns this into the rocket equation.

## Checks

Units of both sides: kg m s⁻². Limiting case n = 1: the single-particle law.

### 8. Core: the centre-of-mass frame, T = ½ M V² + T′, and the diatomic molecule

*Objective:* Derive the kinetic-energy theorem for n particles (the 2024 question) and the diatomic CM and moment of inertia (2017 and 2019).

## The CM frame

Let R be the CM position and V = Ṙ its velocity in an inertial lab frame. Write rᵢ = R + rᵢ′ and vᵢ = V + vᵢ′, where rᵢ′ and vᵢ′ are relative to the CM. By module 2, Σ mᵢ rᵢ′ = 0, so differentiating, Σ mᵢ vᵢ′ = 0. The total momentum in this frame is zero (Mathur §6.4-6.5), which is why it is called the zero-momentum frame. (The CM frame is inertial only if V is constant, that is if F_ext = 0, but the identities below do not need this.)

## Derivation: the 2024 question

Show that T = ½ M V² + ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ′².

$$ T = ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ² = ½ Σ mᵢ (V + vᵢ′)·(V + vᵢ′) $$

Expand the dot product: (V + vᵢ′)·(V + vᵢ′) = V² + 2 V·vᵢ′ + vᵢ′². Then

$$ T = ½ (Σ mᵢ) V² + V · (Σ mᵢ vᵢ′) + ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ′² $$

The middle term vanishes because Σ mᵢ vᵢ′ = 0, and Σ mᵢ = M:

$$ T = ½ M V² + ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ′² $$

Interpretation: the kinetic energy of the system is the kinetic energy of the CM motion plus the **internal** kinetic energy T′ in the CM frame. In a collision T′ alone can be changed (PH-011 module 3 uses this). Numerical check: m₁ = 2 kg with v₁ = (3, 0) m s⁻¹ and m₂ = 4 kg with v₂ = (−1, 2) m s⁻¹: V = (1/3, 4/3) m s⁻¹, T = 19 J, ½ M V² = 5.667 J, T′ = 13.333 J; the sum is 19 J.

## Two particles: T′ = ½ μ v²

With relative velocity v = v₁ − v₂, the CM-frame velocities are v₁′ = (m₂/M) v and v₂′ = −(m₁/M) v. Then T′ = ½ m₁ m₂² v²/M² + ½ m₂ m₁² v²/M² = ½ (m₁ m₂/M²)(m₁ + m₂) v² = ½ μ v² with μ = m₁ m₂/M (the reduced mass, PH-009). In the numbers above, v = v₁ − v₂ = (4, −2) m s⁻¹, v² = 20 m² s⁻², μ = 8/6 = 1.333 kg, so ½ μ v² = ½ × 1.333 × 20 = 13.33 J, equal to T′.

## The diatomic molecule: 2017 and 2019

Two atoms of masses m₁ and m₂ at fixed separation r. Origin at m₁: r₁ = m₂ r/(m₁ + m₂) from m₁ (module 2), and r₂ = r − r₁ = m₁ r/(m₁ + m₂) from m₂. The moment of inertia about an axis through the CM perpendicular to the line joining the atoms:

$$ I = m₁ r₁² + m₂ r₂² = (m₁ m₂² + m₂ m₁²) r²/(m₁ + m₂)² = m₁ m₂ r²/(m₁ + m₂) = μ r² $$

For 2019 Q2(a)(i): there are three axes of symmetry through the CM: the two perpendicular to the molecule (equal, μ r² each) and the one along the line of the atoms (I = 0 when the atoms are treated as point masses; this is the standard rigid-rotor idealisation, standard knowledge outside the extracts, flagged: verify. Upadhyaya's Example 25, PDF 187-188, covers only the perpendicular axis). Numerically, for HCl with m_H = 1 u, m_Cl = 35 u and r = 127.5 pm (illustrative data, verify): μ = 0.972 u = 1.614 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and I = 2.62 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m², with the CM 124.0 pm from the hydrogen atom.

### 9. Master: centre of gravity versus centre of mass, stability and frames

*Objective:* Say when the CM and the centre of gravity differ, apply the standing criterion for stability, and avoid CM mistakes with changing shape and frames.

This is a **deferred deep dive**. The centre-of-gravity and stability material is standard knowledge not set out in the extracts; verify it in a textbook.

## Centre of gravity

The **centre of gravity (CG)** is the point through which the resultant of the weights acts, defined by zero total gravitational torque about it: r_G = Σ mᵢ gᵢ rᵢ/Σ mᵢ gᵢ, where gᵢ is the field at particle i. **If g is the same everywhere** (uniform field) the gᵢ cancel and r_G = R: CG and CM coincide. In a non-uniform field they differ, and the centre of gravity of an extended body is then in general not unique (it can depend on the body's orientation); the rod result below is the point on the rod's line where the resultant weight acts.

Example: a thin vertical rod of uniform mass density extending from r = R_E to 2R_E above the Earth's centre. Its CM is at 1.5 R_E. With g ∝ 1/r²,

$$ r_G = ∫ r (1/r²) dr/∫ (1/r²) dr = ln 2/(1/2) R_E = 2 ln 2 R_E = 1.386 R_E $$

lower than the CM by 0.114 R_E, about 724 km (with R_E = 6371 km). For any body of ordinary size the difference is negligible, so one usually speaks of "CM" when g is uniform. A related fact: in a uniform field the net gravitational torque about the CM is zero, so gravity does not change the spin of a thrown rigid body about its CM.

## Stability (qualitative)

A body resting on a horizontal surface is in equilibrium if the vertical through its CM passes through the supporting base; it topples when the line leaves the base. It is stable if a small tilt raises the CM (the CM is at its lowest): a cone resting on its base is stable, a cone balanced on its apex is unstable, a cone on its slant side is neutral. Low CM and wide base help.

## Frames and time dependence

- The CM position is a **frame-dependent coordinate** but a frame-independent physical point (the relation R′ = R − a for a shift of origin; velocities in a moving frame obey V′ = V − u).
- For a system whose parts move, R(t) is still R = Σ mᵢ rᵢ(t)/M. The CM of a **closed** system does not accelerate: it moves with constant velocity, and stays at rest only if it starts at rest. The CM of a single part of it does accelerate.
- **Changing configuration**: a gymnast or a diver can change the body's shape and the CM position inside the body, but not the motion of the CM in flight.
- **Variable mass**: the theorem M R̈ = F_ext holds for a **fixed set of particles**. If particles enter or leave, include them in the system (module 7 and PH-001 module 4).

## Units and dimensions summary

[R] = L; [M R] = M L; [M R̈] = M L T⁻² (force). Always report the CM with a reference origin and direction.

## Common conceptual traps

- "CM is the geometric centre": true only for uniform bodies with symmetry.
- "CM must lie inside the body": false for a ring, a boomerang or a body with a cavity.
- "The CM moves because of the explosion": false; internal forces do not matter.
- Pappus gives the centroid of the generating plane figure, not of the solid.

### Notes: Definitions

- Centre of mass: R = (sum m_i r_i)/M for particles, R = (1/M) integral r dm for a continuous body.
- Mass elements: dm = lambda dl (line), sigma dA (area), rho dV (volume).
- Centroid: the centre of mass of a uniform body; it depends only on the shape.
- Centre of gravity: the point where the weight acts, r_G = sum m_i g_i r_i/sum m_i g_i; equal to the CM in a uniform field.

### Notes: Standard centres of mass (uniform bodies)

- Distances are measured from the stated reference; each result is derived in modules 3-5.

### Notes: Equations

- R = sum m_i r_i/M; shift of origin R' = R - a.
- Two particles: r_1 = m_2 r/(m_1 + m_2) from m_1; m_1 r_1 = m_2 r_2.
- M R'' = F_ext; P = M V; dP/dt = F_ext.
- T = (1/2) M V^2 + T', T' = (1/2) sum m_i v_i'^2; two particles T' = (1/2) mu v^2.
- Diatomic molecule: I = mu r^2 about an axis through the CM perpendicular to the bond.
- Pappus: S = 2 pi (ybar) l; V = 2 pi (ybar) A.
- Hole: X = -d a^2/(R^2 - a^2) from the disc's centre.

### Notes: Derivation roadmap

- Motion theorem: sum the n equations of motion; internal pairs cancel (module 7).
- Kinetic-energy theorem: expand (V + v')^2; the cross term vanishes since sum m v' = 0 (module 8).
- Rod, wire, plate, hemisphere, cone: choose the element, write dm and limits, integrate numerator and M (modules 3-5).
- Pappus: ring of area 2 pi y dl and volume 2 pi y dA (module 6).
- Diatomic: r_1, r_2 from m_1 r_1 = m_2 r_2, then I = m_1 r_1^2 + m_2 r_2^2 (module 8).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Centre of mass versus geometric centre: equal only for uniform symmetric bodies.
- Centre of mass versus centre of gravity: equal in a uniform field; differ for a body as large as the Earth's radius.
- Pappus gives the centroid of the revolved plane figure, not of the solid generated.
- Internal forces move the parts but never the CM; only external forces move the CM.
- The CM of a non-uniform rod is not at the midpoint; the CM of a ring lies in the empty centre.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- M R'' = F_ext is for a fixed set of particles; for variable mass include the ejected mass (PH-001 module 4).
- Negative-mass subtraction needs the net mass to be positive.
- The CM frame is inertial only when F_ext = 0 (V constant), though the kinetic-energy identity holds in any case.
- Reflection symmetry fails if the density is not symmetric.

  - - **id:** PH-012-D1
    - **task:** Derive the motion of the centre of mass of a system of n particles, M R'' = F_ext, showing why internal forces drop out.
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: inertial frame, constant masses, forces on particle i are an external force F_i^ext and internal forces F_ij from each other particle j, with F_ij = -F_ji (weak third law).
      - Equation of motion for particle i: m_i r_i'' = F_i^ext + sum over j not equal to i of F_ij.
      - Sum over all i: sum m_i r_i'' = sum F_i^ext + sum_i sum_{j not i} F_ij.
      - Left side: since masses are constant, sum m_i r_i'' = d^2(sum m_i r_i)/dt^2 = M R''.
      - Right side: in the double sum each pair (i, j) appears as F_ij + F_ji = 0, so the internal term is zero.
      - Hence M R'' = F_ext, the sum of external forces; also P = M V and dP/dt = F_ext.
      - Interpretation: the CM moves as a single particle of mass M acted on by F_ext.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions: M R'' is a force.
      - n = 1 gives m r'' = F.
      - F_ext = 0 gives V constant: conservation of linear momentum.
      - Practice target, not an official allotment: 12 minutes (10 marks).
    - **traps:**
      - Using a variable mass inside the sum.
      - Forgetting that only the weak form of the third law is needed.
  - - **id:** PH-012-D2
    - **task:** A rod of length L has linear density lambda = lambda0 S/L, where S is the distance from the end O. Find its centre of mass.
    - **steps:**
      - Take O as the origin and the x-axis along the rod; the density is lambda(s) = lambda0 s/L for 0 <= s <= L.
      - Mass element dm = lambda ds = (lambda0 s/L) ds.
      - Total mass M = integral from 0 to L of (lambda0 s/L) ds = (lambda0/L)(L^2/2) = lambda0 L/2.
      - First moment: integral of s dm = (lambda0/L) integral from 0 to L of s^2 ds = (lambda0/L)(L^3/3) = lambda0 L^2/3.
      - X = (lambda0 L^2/3)/(lambda0 L/2) = 2L/3 from O.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimension: length.
      - Uniform limit: lambda = lambda0 gives L/2; here the far end is denser, so X > L/2.
      - General power law (s/L)^n: X = (n + 1)L/(n + 2).

    - **traps:** - Not stating the end the distance is measured from.

    - **task:** State and prove the two theorems of Pappus-Guldinus (standard results) and use them to find the centroid of a semicircular plate.
    - **steps:**

  - - **id:** PH-012-D7
    - **task:** Show that the kinetic energy of a system of n particles is T = (1/2) M V_cm^2 + (1/2) sum m_i V_i'^2.
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: inertial lab frame; R is the CM position; V = R-dot; r_i = R + r_i', v_i = V + v_i'.
      - By definition sum m_i r_i' = 0, so sum m_i v_i' = 0.
      - T = (1/2) sum m_i v_i . v_i = (1/2) sum m_i (V + v_i') . (V + v_i').
      - Expand: (V + v_i') . (V + v_i') = V^2 + 2 V . v_i' + v_i'^2.
      - T = (1/2)(sum m_i) V^2 + V . (sum m_i v_i') + (1/2) sum m_i v_i'^2.
      - The middle term is zero and sum m_i = M, so T = (1/2) M V^2 + (1/2) sum m_i v_i'^2.
    - **checks:**

      - Not showing why the cross term vanishes.

    - **steps:**

### Memory skeleton

- R = Σmᵢrᵢ/M; continuous body R = (1/M)∫r dm; shift of origin R′ = R − a
- Elements: dm = λ dl, σ dA, ρ dV; symmetry removes coordinates
- Integration recipe: element, limits, numerator, mass, divide
- Composites: pieces at their own CMs; holes as negative mass
- Motion theorem: M R̈ = F_ext; internal forces cancel
- CM frame: T = ½MV² + T′; two bodies T′ = ½μv², diatomic I = μr²

- R = (1/M)∫r dm with dm = λ dl, σ dA or ρ dV; λ = λ₀s/L rod: 2L/3 from O; uniform solid cone H/4 above base.
