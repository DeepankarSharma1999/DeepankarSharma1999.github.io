## GS-192: Centre-state fiscal relations

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · States, local governance and federalism

### Bridge: Money vocabulary for fiscal federalism

A government has a **budget**: a plan of what it will receive and spend in a year. **Receipts** are tax revenue, non-tax revenue and borrowing. **Expenditure** is what it spends on salaries, services, defence, roads and so on. When expenditure is more than receipts other than borrowing, the gap is the **fiscal deficit** (standard usage; verify with the current Economic Survey), and the government fills it by **borrowing**.

A **transfer** is money moving from one government to another. A **share** of tax proceeds and a **grant** are both transfers. A transfer is **untied** when the receiving government may spend it as it likes, and **tied** when it must be spent on a stated scheme. **GSDP** (Gross State Domestic Product) is the total value of goods and services produced in a State in a year; economists express a State's borrowing or debt as a percentage of GSDP so that large and small States can be compared.

The **Consolidated Fund of India** is the main account of the Union government into which tax receipts and borrowings flow and from which spending is drawn; each State has its own **Consolidated Fund of the State**. **Expenditure assignment** means deciding which level of government is responsible for which services. **Revenue assignment** means deciding which level may impose which tax. A federation is stable only if these two assignments roughly match, and transfers exist to repair the mismatch.

### Bridge: Tax, duty, fee, cess and surcharge: five kinds of levy

A **tax** is a compulsory payment to government that is not tied to a specific service (income tax, GST). A **duty** is a tax on a transaction or movement of goods: **customs duty** on goods crossing the border, **excise duty** on goods manufactured, **stamp duty** on documents. A **fee** is a charge for a particular service or licence. Two further words decide most fiscal-federal quarrels.

A **cess** is a levy imposed for a specific purpose (for example, to fund a named programme). A **surcharge** is an extra charge added on top of an existing tax, calculated as a percentage of that tax; in everyday language it is a 'tax on tax'. In the Constitution, Article 271 lets Parliament levy a surcharge on the taxes of Articles 269 and 270 for the purposes of the Union, and Article 270 excludes cesses levied for specific purposes from the taxes that are shared.

Why does the label matter? Because the Constitution shares the **net proceeds of the taxes it lists**, not every rupee the Union collects. A rupee collected as a basic tax rate may enter the shareable pool; the same rupee collected as a surcharge or a cess does not. Keep this distinction in mind for module 5.

### 1. Orientation: fiscal federalism as four decisions

*Objective:* Define fiscal federalism, name its four decisions and see which earlier lessons already cover which part.

## What this topic is

**Fiscal** means 'to do with government money'; **fiscal federalism** is the study of how a federation divides money between national and regional governments: who raises it, who spends it, and how it moves between levels. NCERT states the Indian starting point plainly: the Constitution centralises economic and financial power, while the States carry immense responsibilities but have very meagre revenue sources.

## Four decisions every federation must make

1. **Expenditure assignment**: which level is responsible for which service (police, schools, defence, roads).
2. **Revenue assignment**: which level may impose which tax.
3. **Transfers**: how money flows between levels to repair the mismatch between decisions 1 and 2 (tax shares and grants).
4. **Borrowing and discipline**: how far each level may borrow, and who supervises.

In India decisions 1 and 2 sit mainly in the Seventh Schedule lists and Articles 246, 246A and 268-271; decision 3 in Articles 270, 275, 280 and 282; decision 4 in Articles 292 and 293. If 'Article', 'Part' or 'Schedule' feels unfamiliar, read bridge GS-193-B2 first.

### 2. The Seventh Schedule as a fiscal map: the tax entries

*Objective:* List the Union and State tax entries, say what the 101st Amendment removed, and test the rule 'the Concurrent List has no tax entries' against edge cases.

## The two lists of taxes

Every tax must trace its authority to an entry in a List of the Seventh Schedule or, for GST, to Article 246A. Laxmikanth counts **13 tax entries in the Union List** and **18 in the State List**; his notes 12 and 13 (p. 370) give the entry numbers and Appendix II prints most of the entries.

The entry-by-entry table, with each subject in plain words, is in the master notes ('Tax entries in plain words'). Note 12 and the count below are the part to remember.

**Count check.** Union: 82 to 91 is ten entries, plus 92A, 92B and 96 gives 13. State: 45 to 51 is seven, plus 53 and 54 makes nine, plus 56 to 59 makes 13, plus 60 to 63 makes 17, plus 66 makes 18. Residuary taxation sits in Union Entry 97 ('any tax not mentioned in either of those lists') and Article 248. Laxmikanth says gift tax, wealth tax and expenditure tax were imposed under it; wealth tax rests mainly on Union Entry 86, and the residuary power supported extending it to agricultural land (Union of India v H.S. Dhillon, 1971; verify). Gift tax ended in 1998 and wealth tax from 2015-16 (verify), so these are historical examples.

## What the 101st Amendment changed

Union Entries 92 and 92C and State Entries 52 (entry tax) and 55 (advertisements) are printed as 'Omitted'; Entry 84 shrank to petroleum products and tobacco, and State Entry 54 to petroleum products and alcohol. State Entry 62 (entertainment taxes) was substituted so that only local bodies levy them, and Union Entry 84, State Entry 54 and State Entry 62 are the entries that were rewritten. Article 246A, not an entry, now gives GST its own concurrent power.

## Testing the rule 'the Concurrent List has no tax entries'

Laxmikanth states the rule on p. 344. Test it on four entries that look fiscal:

- **Entry 44** (stamp duties other than judicial stamps) expressly excludes 'rates of stamp duty'; the rates are in Union Entry 91 and State Entry 63. So it does not authorise the tax itself.
- **Entry 35** (mechanically propelled vehicles) covers 'the principles on which taxes on such vehicles are to be levied'. The principles are concurrent; the tax is State Entry 57.
- **Entries 20 and 43** (economic and social planning; recovery of claims in respect of taxes) concern planning and recovery, not imposing a tax.
- **Entry 47** (fees on Concurrent List matters, not court fees) is a fee entry. Laxmikanth's 13 and 18 count the fee entries Union 96 and State 66, so read his rule as 'no entry for a tax proper'; a fee is not a tax.

Verdict: the rule holds in the form 'no Concurrent entry for a tax proper', with GST under Article 246A as the single exception, because each fiscal-looking entry fails to authorise a levy and a fee is not a tax.

### 3. Fences around taxation: Articles 274, 276, 285-289 and the immunities

*Objective:* State the Constitution's limits on State taxes, the protection for States inside Parliament, and the mutual immunities with their exceptions.

## Why a federation fences its own taxes

If each government could tax anything, goods would be taxed at every border and governments would tax one another. The Constitution therefore builds fences. Read each fence with its Article, because Prelims likes one-line facts.

## Fences on State taxing power

- **Profession tax (Article 276).** A State may tax professions, trades, callings and employments, but the total payable by any person cannot exceed **Rs 2,500 a year**. Laxmikanth's note 15 records that the limit was Rs 250 originally and the **60th Amendment Act, 1988** raised it to Rs 2,500. The Punchhi Commission (recommendation 29) wanted the ceiling done away with by a constitutional amendment, which tells you the ceiling is constitutional: a State law cannot lift it.
- **Supply outside the State, imports and exports (Article 286).** A State cannot tax a supply that takes place outside the State or in the course of import or export. Parliament lays down the principles for deciding where a supply takes place. Note 16 says the Article was amended by the 101st Amendment into this form.
- **Electricity (Article 287).** Opening with the words 'save in so far as Parliament may by law otherwise provide', the Article lets a State tax consumption or sale of electricity, but not electricity consumed by or sold to the Centre, or used in building, maintaining or operating a railway by the Centre or a railway company.
- **River-valley authorities (Article 288).** A State tax on water or electricity of an authority created by Parliament for an inter-State river or valley needs the **President's assent** to be effective.

## A fence inside Parliament (Article 274)

Four kinds of bill need the **President's recommendation** before introduction: a bill imposing or varying a tax in which States are interested; one changing the meaning of 'agricultural income'; one affecting the principles on which money is distributable to States; and one imposing a surcharge for the Centre. A tax 'in which States are interested' is one whose net proceeds are wholly or partly assigned to a State. **Net proceeds** means proceeds minus the cost of collection, certified by the **Comptroller and Auditor-General** (Article 279; GS-184, GS-193 module 2).

## Mutual immunities (Articles 285 and 289)

In short (full table in the master notes, 'Mutual immunities of the Union and the States'): the Union and a State do not tax each other's property and income, but a Union company is a separate legal entity and pays State tax, the Centre may tax a State's commercial operations if Parliament provides, and a State's immunity does not reach customs and excise duties (1963 advisory opinion).

## Testing 'governments do not tax each other'

Edge 1: a Union company pays State tax. Edge 2: the Centre can tax a State's commercial operation if Parliament provides. Edge 3: the Centre can impose customs duty on goods a State imports. Safer form of the rule: property and income of one government are exempt from the other's tax, subject to these three exceptions.

### 4. Borrowing, deficits and emergencies

*Objective:* State the four constitutional borrowing rules, read the consent rule carefully and recall how emergencies alter financial relations.

## Why borrowing is a fiscal-federal question

When a government spends more than it receives, it borrows (bridge GS-192-B1). Borrowing by one level can raise credit risk for all, so the Constitution regulates it in Articles 292 and 293.

## The four rules (Laxmikanth pp. 352-353)

1. **Union borrowing (Article 292).** The Centre may borrow within India or outside India on the security of the Consolidated Fund of India, or give guarantees, within limits fixed by Parliament. Laxmikanth adds that no such law had been enacted when he wrote. Flag: the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003 later set deficit targets; whether it counts as the Article 292 limit is for verification.
2. **State borrowing (Article 293).** A State may borrow only **within India**, on the security of its own Consolidated Fund, or give guarantees, within limits fixed by its own legislature.
3. **Union loans to States.** The Centre may lend to States or guarantee their loans; the sums are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
4. **The consent rule.** A State cannot raise any loan without the Centre's consent if any part of a Union loan to it, or of a loan guaranteed by the Centre, is still outstanding.

## Reading rule 4 slowly

The rule has a condition and an effect. Condition: a Union loan or guarantee is outstanding. Effect: the State needs consent for any new loan. If nothing is outstanding, the constitutional text itself does not require consent (other limits may still apply: verify). The rule gives the Union a lever over State borrowing. Article 293(4) lets the Union give its consent subject to such conditions as the Government of India thinks fit, which is why a recurring Mains point is that the lever can carry conditions. Because almost every State has outstanding Union loans, Article 293(3) in practice binds nearly all States (standard knowledge; verify). Flagged, post-source: State of Kerala v Union of India, an Article 131 suit about the Union's net borrowing ceiling, which counts off-budget borrowing by State entities; in April 2024 the Supreme Court is reported to have refused interim relief and referred Article 293 questions to a Constitution Bench (reported; verify current status). Flagged, post-source and to be verified before writing: in the pandemic year 2020-21 the Union was reported to allow additional State borrowing linked to specified reforms, which several States criticised as conditionality.

## Discipline beyond the Constitution (flagged)

Standard knowledge, not in the extracts: most States have their own fiscal responsibility laws. Do not quote targets without a dated source.

## Emergencies: the map redrawn (Laxmikanth p. 354)

- **National Emergency (Article 352):** the President may modify the distribution of revenues, reducing or cancelling tax sharing and grants till the end of the financial year in which the emergency ceases.
- **Financial Emergency (Article 360):** the Centre may direct States to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries and allowances, and reserve money and financial bills for the President. Full treatment: GS-170.

## Guided example

State P (Union loan outstanding, new loan in India): **consent needed**. State Q (nothing outstanding, loan in India): **no constitutional consent requirement** (verify other rules). State R (borrowing abroad): **barred** under Article 293.


### 5. Cesses and surcharges: why the headline share is not the real share

*Objective:* Explain why surcharges and cesses sit outside the divisible pool and compute how this lowers the real share of States.

## The constitutional facts

- **Article 270** shares the net proceeds of Union taxes **except** duties and taxes of Articles 268, 269 and 269A, surcharges under Article 271 and **any cess levied for specific purposes**.
- **Article 271** lets Parliament levy a **surcharge** on taxes under Articles 269 and 270 for the Centre, and its proceeds go to the Centre **exclusively**. The surcharge cannot be imposed on GST.
- The 101st Amendment subsumed **central surcharges and cesses relating to the supply of goods and services** into GST (Laxmikanth p. 347), and State surcharges and cesses on such supply as well.
- **Sarkaria (15):** the surcharge on income tax only for a specific purpose and a strictly limited period. **Punchhi (33):** review all cesses and surcharges to bring down their share in gross tax revenue.

## A worked example (illustrative numbers, not real data)

Let the Union's gross tax revenue be 100 and let the Finance Commission headline share for States be **40 per cent of the shareable pool**. Suppose some of the 100 comes through surcharges and cesses and so is outside the pool (cost of collection ignored for simplicity).

| Scenario | Gross Union tax revenue | Cess and surcharge | Shareable pool | State share at 40 per cent | Real share of gross |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 5 | 95 | 38.0 | 38 per cent |
| B | 100 | 15 | 85 | 34.0 | 34 per cent |
| C | 100 | 25 | 75 | 30.0 | 30 per cent |

Formula: real share of gross = headline share x (1 minus the cess-and-surcharge fraction). For scenario B, 40 x 0.85 = 34. The headline stays at 40 per cent in all three rows, yet States receive 38 in A and 30 in C: **8 less, about 21 per cent less than A** (8 divided by 38 is 0.21). A second check: with a headline of 41 per cent and a cess-and-surcharge fraction of 20 per cent, the real share is 41 x 0.80 = 32.8 per cent.

## What follows

1. The headline percentage is the Commission's recommendation, not the States' actual take; a balanced Mains answer says so in the first paragraph.
2. The Union argues that cesses fund specific national purposes (analysis; verify its current position); States reply that a rising cess share defeats the Commission's work.
3. GST changed one corner of this problem: the old central cesses on supply were merged into GST, and a surcharge cannot be put on GST, but the 2017 Compensation Act created a new GST Compensation Cess on selected goods, credited to a Compensation Fund and outside the divisible pool (flagged; reported wound down in 2025-26, verify). Cesses and surcharges on income tax, corporation tax and customs remain constitutionally possible.

For the qualitative table of pressure points, see GS-201 module 8. Put real percentages in an answer only with a dated source.


### 6. GST as a federal bargain: what was merged, who gave what

*Objective:* List the 15 levies GST subsumed, name the Articles of its machinery, and analyse what the Centre and States each surrendered and gained.

## Fifteen levies merged (Laxmikanth pp. 346-347)

**Seven Union levies:** Central Excise Duty; Additional Excise Duties; excise under the Medicinal and Toilet Preparations (Excise Duties) Act, 1955; Service Tax; Additional Customs Duty (Countervailing Duty); Special Additional Duty of Customs; and central surcharges and cesses relating to supply of goods and services. **Eight State levies:** State VAT or sales tax; entertainment tax other than that of local bodies; Central Sales Tax (levied by the Centre, collected by States); octroi and entry tax; purchase tax; luxury tax; taxes on lottery, betting and gambling; State surcharges and cesses on supply of goods and services. 7 + 8 = 15. A Mains answer to 'enumerate the taxes subsumed' (2019) is this list, grouped by level. For the amendments' context see GS-193 module 5.

## The machinery in five Articles

| Article | Role |
|---|---|
| 246A | Concurrent power of Parliament and State legislatures over GST; Parliament exclusive where supply is inter-State |
| 269A | Inter-State GST levied and collected by the Centre, divided as Parliament provides on the Council's recommendation |
| 279A | The GST Council (composition and voting: GS-194 module 8 and GS-201 module 4) |
| 286 | Restrictions on State tax on supply outside the State or in import and export, as amended |
| 268A and Entry 92C | Service tax provisions of the 88th Amendment, deleted by the 101st; Article 272 was deleted earlier by the 80th Amendment (note 17) |

Note: CGST forms part of the divisible pool under Article 270(1A), and the Union's portion of IGST enters it through Article 270(1B), so GST is not outside Finance Commission devolution (standard knowledge; verify).

## What each side gave and got (analysis)

| | Gave up | Got |
|---|---|---|
| Union | A free hand on its own indirect taxes and on central surcharges and cesses on supply | A single national base; a one-third weight that acts as a veto; States' acceptance of one law |
| States | Independent rate-setting on the merged State taxes | Two-thirds of the weight; a share of inter-State GST; a role in taxing services (before 2016 only the Union taxed them); compensation for five years |

## What stayed outside the bargain

Petroleum crude, high speed diesel, petrol, natural gas and aviation turbine fuel remain in Union Entry 84 and State Entry 54 until the Council recommends a date (p. 946); alcohol for human consumption stays in State Entries 51 and 54 and tobacco in Union Entry 84(f). Whether they remain outside GST today is a current-affairs question: verify.

## Compensation: the price of consent

The Council must recommend compensation to States for loss of revenue arising from GST for **five years**; Parliament determines it, and enacted the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 (pp. 946, note 5). Flagged, not in the extracts: the compensation was reported to be guaranteed at 14 per cent annual growth over a 2015-16 base, funded by a cess on selected goods; the five-year period ran from 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2022, and the cess continued after that to repay pandemic-era back-to-back loans (reported; verify); the pandemic shortfall led to borrowing arrangements and federal disputes (the 2020 Mains question); the cess was later reported to be wound down. Verify all of this before writing.

## Is the Council's word binding?

Persuasive, not binding, on the Supreme Court's 2022 holding in Mohit Minerals (verify): GS-201 module 4 and GS-194 module 8.


### 7. Transfers beyond the Finance Commission: schemes, performance and the Fiscal Health Index

*Objective:* Compare the three transfer channels on conditions and decision rights, use the Punchhi recommendations on transfers, and handle the Fiscal Health Index with flagged facts.

## Three channels, three questions

GS-193 module 6 taught Articles 270, 275 and 282. Ask three questions of each channel.

| Channel | Rule-based? | Conditions? | Who decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax devolution (Article 270) | Yes, by Finance Commission formula | None on spending; untied | President on Commission's advice |
| Statutory grants (Article 275) | Yes, need-based | Specific grants for tribal welfare and Scheduled Areas | Parliament on Commission's advice |
| Discretionary grants and schemes (Article 282) | No | Often tied; plan leverage | Union executive |

Discretionary grants help States meet plan targets and give the Centre leverage; NCERT notes charges of discrimination against opposition-ruled States. The full treatment of the Article 282 leverage, that discrimination point and Punchhi's recommendations 27 and 32 is in GS-201 module 8.

Punchhi on money, in brief: recommendation 27 (cost sharing in Central laws that need States' involvement), 31 (an annual independent assessment of every fiscal law, laid before the legislature) and 32 (even-handed Finance Commission terms of reference). Its terms of reference also covered linking assistance to State performance and positive discrimination for backward States (ToR e and f).

## Performance against need

A grant tied to performance rewards good management but can penalise poorer States that start behind; a grant tied to need can reward weak effort. Finance Commission formulas try both (GS-193 module 4). Good Mains answers state both dangers, then suggest a mix.

## Fiscal Health Index (flagged: not in the extracts; verify)

The 2025 GS III question asks how a Fiscal Health Index can assess States and encourage prudence. Standard reporting: NITI Aayog published a **Fiscal Health Index** for major States in early 2025, a composite score built from five sub-indices, commonly named as quality of expenditure, revenue mobilisation, fiscal prudence, debt index and debt sustainability. Treat names and coverage as unverified and check the report. Analysis you can write without numbers: (1) **benchmarking** on one scale; (2) **peer pressure** that supports competitive federalism (GS-201 module 4); (3) **policy hooks**, such as linking the index to borrowing space or incentive grants, as Punchhi suggested for performance-linked assistance; (4) **limits**: data lags, one composite hiding different causes, and States with heavy social-sector needs looking weak. Do not quote ranks without a dated source.


### 8. A reform ladder: what was demanded, what changed, 1950 to 2025

*Objective:* Use a dated chronology to say which fiscal demands were met, partly met or ignored, and to write the planning-to-GST narrative.

## The chronology

| Year | Event | Fiscal point |
|---|---|---|
| 1950-51 | Planning Commission set up by executive resolution (standard; verify the year); First Finance Commission (K.C. Neogy, appointed 1951) | Two channels for transfers begin |
| 1966-69 | Administrative Reforms Commission, report 1969 | Recommended transferring more financial resources to States; no action taken |
| 1969-71 | Rajamannar Committee (Tamil Nadu) | Finance Commission to be permanent; Planning Commission to be disbanded and replaced by a statutory body; residuary powers to States; ignored |
| 1977 | West Bengal Memorandum | 75 per cent of the Centre's total revenue to States; not accepted |
| 1983-88 | Sarkaria Commission | Corporation tax shareable (11); surcharge only for a specific purpose and limited period (15); the Finance Commission and Planning Commission division reasonable (16) |
| 1992 | 73rd and 74th Amendments | New Finance Commission function: supplement State funds for panchayats and municipalities |
| 2000 | 80th Amendment | 10th Commission's 29 per cent scheme from 1 April 1996; corporation tax and customs at par with income tax for sharing |
| 2007-10 | Punchhi Commission, report April 2010 | Recommendations 27 to 38 on cost sharing, cesses, terms of reference and secretariat |
| 2015 | NITI Aayog replaces Planning Commission | Finance Commission the main constitutional channel; 14th Commission's large devolution (flagged in GS-193 module 7) |
| 2016-17 | 101st Amendment; Compensation Act 2017 | GST, the Council, compensation for five years |
| 2020-21 | Pandemic revenue shock (flagged; verify) | Compensation dispute, borrowing arrangements |
| 2023-26 | 16th Finance Commission constituted 31 December 2023; award period 2026-27 to 2030-31 began on 1 April 2026; the report was reported as submitted in November 2025 and laid before Parliament in February 2026 (verify; do not quote the share until checked) (flagged in GS-193) | Check the report and the action taken memorandum |
| 2025 | GST rate rationalisation reported (flagged in GS-193) | Verify; rates change often |

## Reading the pattern

**Phase one, planning and centralisation (about 1950 to the 1990s):** the non-constitutional Planning Commission and Article 282 grants gave the Centre leverage; the Finance Commission's role was undermined till 2014 (Laxmikanth); autonomy demands came from Tamil Nadu, Punjab and West Bengal. **Phase two, rules and negotiation (1990s to 2014):** corporation tax and customs entered the sharing scheme (80th Amendment); Punchhi turned the debate to cost sharing and cesses. **Phase three, institutional reform (2014 onwards):** Planning Commission replaced; GST created a shared constitutional forum. These phases are an analytic device for writing, not an official periodisation.

## The 14th Finance Commission (flagged recap; GS-193 module 7)

Standard knowledge, to verify against the report: the share of the divisible pool rose from 32 to 42 per cent; the plan and non-plan distinction ended with the Planning Commission; forest cover and 2011 population entered the horizontal formula; some States received revenue-deficit grants; and basic and performance grants went to local bodies. Counterpoint (analysis): reduced central shares in schemes offset part of the gain.

## Demands met, partly met, not met

- **Met:** corporation tax shareable (Sarkaria 11, then the 80th Amendment); Planning Commission replaced (by NITI Aayog, not a statutory body as Rajamannar wished: verify).
- **Partly met:** a joint fiscal forum (GST Council), with the Centre holding a veto.
- **Not met in the sources read:** a permanent Finance Commission; 75 per cent of revenue to States.
- **Status unknown from the extracts:** Punchhi 29 (profession tax ceiling) and 33 (review of cesses): verify.

## Directive words and mark planning

**Examine** and **Explain**: set out features and causes, then weigh them. **How far**: a judgement with degrees, never a list alone. **Discuss**: two sides and a view. **Suggest measures**: a separate forward-looking part. A 10-mark answer allows about 150 words and a 15-mark answer about 250 (the project's practice convention); for 250 words split about 40 + 170 + 40.

## Four traps in this topic

1. **Quoting figures from memory** (devolution percentages, GST collection): use hedged wording or a dated source.
2. **Writing only about the Finance Commission** when the question asks about financial relations: add GST, cess and surcharge, borrowing, schemes.
3. **Listing reforms without judgement**, especially for 'how far'.
4. **Using the wrong Commission:** 'recently constituted' means the Commission current in the year the question was set.


### 10. Mains-writing progression: from a one-line stem to a 250-word answer

*Objective:* Build a 250-word answer in six guided steps and self-check it with a rubric.

## Six steps, thinking aloud, on the 2025 GS II question

Stem: *Examine the evolving pattern of Centre-State financial relations in the context of planned development in India. How far have recent reforms impacted fiscal federalism?*

**Step 1, decode.** Two commands: *examine the evolving pattern* (a description over time) and *how far* (a judgement). So the answer needs a timeline and a verdict.

**Step 2, retrieve.** Timeline from module 8; mechanisms from modules 5 and 6; transfer channels from module 7.

**Step 3, plan.** Introduction (40 words): the Constitution centralises money, States spend more than they raise, and the Finance Commission and grants correct it. Body one (about 70): the planning era, Article 282 grants, autonomy demands, Sarkaria and Punchhi. Body two (about 100): NITI Aayog replacing the Planning Commission, GST and its Council, compensation, larger untied devolution (flagged), with a verdict. Conclusion (40): a balanced view and one way forward.

**Step 4, draft (sample sentences).** 'The Planning Commission, a non-constitutional body, and discretionary grants under Article 282 gave the Centre leverage, drawing charges of discrimination.' 'GST pooled taxing power in a Council where States hold two-thirds of the weight but the Centre can block, which is cooperative in form yet limits States' rate autonomy.' 'Cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool can dilute the real share even when the headline share rises.'

**Step 5, count.** 40 + 70 + 100 + 40 = 250 words.

**Step 6, self-mark.** Rubric: structure (introduction, body, conclusion); correct Articles; dated evolution; judgement on 'how far'; a limitation; a forward measure. One mark for each of the six criteria; judge the conclusion under structure.

## A second skeleton: the Fiscal Health Index (flagged facts)

Introduction: a composite of sub-indices (verify names). Body: uses (benchmarking, peer comparison, link to incentives), how it encourages prudence, and limits (data lag, one composite hiding different causes). Conclusion: pair the index with State-specific context. Write no figures. Common errors: unsourced percentages, treating the Finance Commission as binding, confusing Article 269 with Article 270, and ending without a view.


### Notes: Core definitions

- Fiscal federalism: the division of money powers (spending, taxing, transfers, borrowing) in a federation.
- Expenditure assignment and revenue assignment: which level is responsible for which service and which level may impose which tax.
- Transfer: money moving between governments, either a share of taxes or a grant; tied transfers carry conditions, untied transfers do not.
- Surcharge: an extra percentage charged on an existing tax; under Article 271 its proceeds go to the Centre alone.
- Cess: a levy for a specific purpose; excluded from the Article 270 shareable taxes.
- Net proceeds: proceeds of a tax minus the cost of collection, certified by the Comptroller and Auditor-General (Article 279).
- Fiscal deficit: in standard usage, expenditure less receipts excluding borrowing, that is, the year's borrowing requirement (verify with the current Economic Survey).

### Notes: Articles at a glance for fiscal relations

- Article 246A: concurrent power of Parliament and State legislatures over GST (101st Amendment); Parliament exclusive for inter-State supply.
- Articles 268, 269, 269A, 270, 271: tax allocation categories (see GS-193 module 5).
- Article 274: President's recommendation for bills affecting taxation in which States are interested.
- Article 275 and Article 282: statutory and discretionary grants (see GS-193 module 6).
- Article 276: profession tax ceiling of Rs 2,500 a year, raised from Rs 250 by the 60th Amendment 1988.
- Article 279: net proceeds, certified by the Comptroller and Auditor-General; Article 279A: GST Council.
- Articles 285 and 289: immunity of Union property and of State property and income from the other's tax.
- Article 286: restrictions on State tax on supply outside the State or in import and export, as amended in 2016.
- Articles 287 and 288: electricity and river-valley authority taxes.
- Article 292: Union borrowing; Article 293: State borrowing and the consent rule.
- Articles 352 and 360: effects of National and Financial Emergencies on financial relations.

### Notes: Amendments that touched fiscal relations

- 60th Amendment Act, 1988: raised the Article 276 ceiling from Rs 250 to Rs 2,500.
- 73rd and 74th Amendment Acts, 1992: added the local-bodies function to Article 280.
- 80th Amendment Act, 2000: the 10th Commission's 29 per cent scheme from 1 April 1996; corporation tax and customs at par with income tax for sharing; deleted Article 272 (note 17).
- 88th Amendment Act, 2003: inserted Article 268A and Entry 92C (service tax), later deleted.
- 101st Amendment Act, 2016: GST; Article 246A, Article 269A, Article 279A; amended Article 286; deleted Article 268A and Entry 92C; omitted Union Entry 92 (newspapers and advertisements) and State Entries 52 and 55; substituted Union Entry 84, State Entry 54 and State Entry 62 (entertainment taxes now only by local bodies).

### Notes: What GST subsumed

- Seven Union levies and eight State levies merged (Laxmikanth pp. 346-347).

### Notes: Reports and fiscal recommendations

- ARC 1969 (Morarji Desai, then K. Hanumanthayya): 22 recommendations, including more financial resources to States; no action.
- Rajamannar Committee 1971: permanent Finance Commission; Planning Commission disbanded; residuary powers to States; ignored.
- West Bengal Memorandum 1977: 75 per cent of the Centre's total revenue to States; not accepted.
- Sarkaria 1988 (247 recommendations): net proceeds of corporation tax shareable (11); surcharge on income tax only for a specific purpose and limited period (15); division of functions between Finance and Planning Commissions reasonable (16).
- Punchhi 2010 (over 310 recommendations): cost sharing in Central laws as in RTE (27); royalty on major minerals revised at least every three years (28); profession tax ceiling removed by amendment (29); revisit Article 268 (30); annual independent assessment of fiscal laws (31); even-handed Finance Commission terms of reference (32); review of cesses and surcharges (33).

### Notes: Cause and effect chains

- States spend on police, health, agriculture and local services but hold narrower tax sources -> vertical imbalance -> transfers needed.
- Cesses and surcharges are excluded from the shareable pool -> a rising cess share shrinks the real share of States even at an unchanged headline percentage.
- GST merges State taxes -> States lose independent rate-setting -> the Council and compensation for five years were the price of consent.
- A State has an outstanding Union loan -> Article 293 consent rule -> the Union gains a lever over State borrowing.
- Discretionary grants under Article 282 carry conditions -> charges of discrimination against opposition-ruled States (NCERT) -> demands for formula-based transfers.

### Notes: Exceptions and fine print

- The Concurrent List has no tax entry, but GST has concurrent power through Article 246A.
- The Article 276 ceiling is constitutional; only a constitutional amendment can change it.
- Union property is exempt from State tax, but Parliament may remove the ban; Union companies are not exempt.
- State property and income are exempt from Central tax, but commercial operations can be taxed if Parliament so provides, and customs and excise can still apply to State goods (1963 advisory opinion).
- A State may borrow only within India, and needs consent if a Union loan or guarantee is outstanding.
- Surcharge under Article 271 cannot be levied on GST.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Cess versus surcharge: purpose-based versus base-based; both are outside the Article 270 pool.
- Article 268 (levied by Union, collected and kept by States) versus Article 269 (collected by Union, assigned to States).
- Article 269A (inter-State GST divided on Council recommendation) versus Article 270 (divisible pool on Finance Commission recommendation).
- Headline devolution share versus real share after cesses and surcharges.
- GST Council (recommends on GST) versus Finance Commission (recommends on devolution and grants) versus NITI Aayog (think-tank).
- Union borrowing (Article 292, within or outside India) versus State borrowing (Article 293, within India only).

### Core concepts

- Fiscal federalism is four decisions: expenditure assignment, revenue assignment, transfers, borrowing.
- The Seventh Schedule gives 13 Union and 18 State tax entries and no Concurrent tax entry except GST by Article 246A.
- The Constitution fences taxation (Articles 274, 276, 286-289) and sets mutual immunities with exceptions.
- Cesses and surcharges sit outside the divisible pool, so the real share is below the headline share.
- GST is a federal bargain: a Council with a Union veto and State two-thirds weight, plus compensation for five years.

### Practice

**Q.** In your own words, define fiscal federalism and name its four decisions.

**A.** It is how a federation divides money powers: expenditure assignment, revenue assignment, transfers, and borrowing and discipline.

**Q.** What is the difference between a cess and a surcharge?

**A.** A cess is a levy for a specific purpose; a surcharge is an extra percentage charged on an existing tax.

**Q.** How many tax entries does Laxmikanth count in the Union and State Lists?

**A.** 13 in the Union List and 18 in the State List.

**Q.** Which Articles regulate borrowing by the Union and by States?

**A.** Article 292 for the Union and Article 293 for States.

**Q.** Why does the Concurrent List have no tax entries, and what is the exception?

**A.** Taxing power is divided between the Union and the States so that each knows its own base; the exception is GST, for which Article 246A gives concurrent power.

**Q.** Why can a headline Finance Commission share overstate what States receive?

**A.** Because cesses for specific purposes and surcharges are outside the shareable pool, a rising share of Union revenue through them reduces the real share.

**Q.** Why can the Centre block a GST Council decision but not pass one alone?

**A.** Its one-third weight exceeds the 25 per cent that blocks a three-fourths rule but is far below the 75 per cent needed to pass; see GS-201 module 4.

**Q.** Why are Union companies not immune from State taxation although Union property is?

**A.** A company or corporation is a separate legal entity, not the Union's property.

**Q.** A State proposes to tax electricity consumed by the Centre's railway department. Can it, and why?

**A.** No; under Article 287 (unless Parliament by law otherwise provides) no State tax can be imposed on electricity consumed by the Centre or used for railways by the Centre or a railway company.

**Q.** Headline share 41 per cent, cess-and-surcharge fraction 20 per cent. Compute the real share of gross revenue.

**A.** 41 x 0.80 = 32.8 per cent of gross revenue.

**Q.** Classify a stamp duty on cheques: which Union entry and which Article for sharing?

**A.** Union Entry 91 (rates of stamp duty on bills of exchange, cheques and so on); Article 268, levied by the Union and collected and appropriated by the States.
