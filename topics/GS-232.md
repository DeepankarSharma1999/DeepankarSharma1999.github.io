## GS-232: FRBM framework and fiscal consolidation

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain FRBM framework and fiscal consolidation and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### What the 2003 Act required

NCERT presents the FRBM Act as a legislative commitment meant to bind every future government, because electoral pressure tends to push spending up. The Act asked the Centre to cut the fiscal deficit to no more than 3 per cent of GDP and to wipe out the revenue deficit, moving in fixed annual steps of 0.3 and 0.5 per cent of GDP. Targets could be breached only for national security, natural calamity or other exceptional grounds the Centre specifies. The Centre could not borrow from the RBI except through temporary advances, and from 2006-07 the RBI was barred from buying primary issues of central securities. Three statements must accompany the budget: a medium-term fiscal policy statement, a strategy statement on fiscal policy, and a macroeconomic framework statement; receipts and spending are also reviewed quarterly in Parliament.

### From deficit target to debt anchor

The 2026 FRBM statements show how the rule now works in practice. The legal targets are a 3 per cent fiscal deficit by March 2021 and endeavour targets to hold debt at 40 per cent (Centre) and 60 per cent (general government) of GDP by March 2025. Because these were not met, the Finance Minister lays a statement explaining the deviation, as Section 7(3)(b) demands. Budget 2026-27 states its fiscal anchor as a debt glide path: the fiscal deficit has come down to 4.4 per cent of GDP in RE 2025-26 from 6.7 per cent in 2021-22 and is budgeted at 4.3 per cent, while Central Government debt is projected to dip to 55.6 per cent of GDP. The medium-term aim is debt of 50 plus or minus 1 per cent of GDP by 2030-31, with the fiscal deficit as the operational target; rolling targets for the next two years were not given, citing global uncertainty.

### Quality of consolidation and the debate

Consolidation is judged not only by the size of the deficit but by what borrowing finances. The 2026 statement reports the ratio of capital expenditure to fiscal deficit rising to 72.1 per cent in BE 2026-27, meaning most borrowing funds asset creation. NCERT adds two cautions. Critics feared that hard targets would squeeze welfare spending, and a recession automatically widens the deficit as tax receipts fall, so a larger deficit need not signal looser policy. NCERT also notes that 26 states have passed their own fiscal responsibility laws and that a Review Committee was asked to revamp the 2003 framework rather than abandon it. A balanced answer weighs credibility and lower interest costs against reduced room for counter-cyclical and social spending.

### Worked example

Mains frame (150 words): define FRBM as a rule-based commitment; list the original targets and escape clause; show the current position (4.4 per cent RE 2025-26, 4.3 per cent BE 2026-27, debt 56.1 to 55.6 per cent); note the deviation statement and the shift to a debt anchor; evaluate using the capex-to-deficit ratio and the welfare-squeeze criticism; conclude that credibility comes from transparent glide paths rather than one fixed number.

### Recall

- - **q:** Name the three policy statements mandated by the FRBM Act.
  - **a:** Medium-term Fiscal Policy Statement, Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement and Macroeconomic Framework Statement (now presented as a combined MTFP-cum-FPS statement plus the MEF statement).
- - **q:** On what grounds can the Centre exceed FRBM targets?
  - **a:** National security, natural calamity or other exceptional grounds specified by the Central Government; the reasons must be explained to Parliament.
- - **q:** What debt ceilings does the amended FRBM framework set?
  - **a:** Endeavour to limit Central Government debt to 40 per cent and General Government debt to 60 per cent of GDP by 31 March 2025.

### Common trap

Assuming the 3 per cent fiscal deficit target has been met: the 2026 statements record a deficit above 3 per cent and a deviation statement, and the stated fiscal anchor is now a declining debt-to-GDP glide path, with the fiscal deficit as the operational target.
