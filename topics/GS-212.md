## GS-212: Regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Institutions, law and politics

*Goal:* Explain Regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### What makes a body quasi-judicial

A quasi-judicial body acts somewhat like a court: it hears parties, weighs evidence and records reasons, yet stands outside the regular court hierarchy. One family comes from Part XIVA of the Constitution, added in 1976: Article 323A for public-service disputes and Article 323B for listed subjects such as taxation, foreign exchange, labour disputes, land reform, elections and rent. As the text reads, the enabling law may shut out other courts except the Supreme Court's special leave power, though later judicial interpretation has shaped how far that goes. Other bodies acquire court-like powers from ordinary statutes, as the NHRC does under the Protection of Human Rights Act and Information Commissions under the RTI Act. Statutory regulators go further, combining rule-making, investigation and penalty powers within one body.

### A regulator at work: SEBI

The Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992 makes SEBI a body corporate headquartered in Bombay. Its board combines a Chairman, two officials from the Union ministry handling finance and company law, one Reserve Bank official and five more members, at least three of them whole-time, chosen for expertise in securities, law, finance or allied fields. Section 11 charges it with protecting investors and developing and regulating the securities market: registering intermediaries, curbing insider trading and unfair trade practices, regulating takeovers and carrying out inspections. It can issue directions after an enquiry, and its adjudicating officers may summon witnesses and impose penalties after hearing the person concerned; penalty money goes to the Consolidated Fund. The Centre retains power to give binding policy directions and, in defined emergencies or persistent default, to supersede the board.

### Outputs, appeals and accountability compared

Bodies differ in what they produce and how they are checked. The NHRC uses civil-court powers to inquire but ends with recommendations on compensation, prosecution or interim relief. Information Commissions issue binding decisions and can fine an information officer ₹250 a day up to ₹25,000; commissioners are chosen by a committee that includes the Leader of Opposition and can be removed for misbehaviour or incapacity only after a Supreme Court inquiry. SEBI's orders go on appeal to the Securities Appellate Tribunal within 45 days, its benches need a judicial and a technical member, civil courts are barred, and a further appeal on points of law lies to the Supreme Court. PIB lists SAT and the telecom appellate tribunal among the 16 tribunals covered by the 2026 reform Bill. The RTI Act also obliges authorities to give reasons for quasi-judicial decisions.

### Worked example

Classify: Administrative tribunal (Article 323A; adjudicates service disputes); NHRC (PHRA; civil-court powers during inquiry, outcome is a recommendation); Central Information Commission (RTI Act; civil-court powers, binding decisions and penalties on information officers); SEBI (SEBI Act 1992; regulations, inspections, directions and penalties, appeal to SAT within 45 days, then Supreme Court on law). Conclude that quasi-judicial status is a spectrum whose content is set by the enabling law.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which section gives the NHRC civil-court powers?
  - **a:** Section 13 of the PHRA, 1993.
- - **q:** Can a law under Article 323A exclude the Supreme Court?
  - **a:** No — the Supreme Court's jurisdiction under Article 136 is expressly preserved.
- - **q:** Which RTI provision requires reasons for quasi-judicial decisions?
  - **a:** Section 4(1)(d).
- - **q:** Who recommends appointment of the Chief Information Commissioner?
  - **a:** A committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM.
- - **q:** Where does an appeal against a SEBI penalty order go first?
  - **a:** The Securities Appellate Tribunal, within 45 days (SEBI Act s.15T); then the Supreme Court on questions of law (s.15Z).
- - **q:** Who sits on SEBI's board besides the Chairman?
  - **a:** Two Union ministry officials (finance/company law), one RBI official and five other members, at least three whole-time (s.4).

### Common trap

Having civil-court powers does not make a body a court: the NHRC uses them to inquire but ends with recommendations, while Information Commissions can actually impose penalties and issue binding decisions. Likewise, a regulator's independence is bounded by statute: SEBI must follow the Centre's written policy directions and can be superseded in defined situations.
