### Bridge: Union, federation, State and 'territory of India': four words kept apart

Four words carry this whole topic, and they are not synonyms.

A **State** (with a capital S, in Indian constitutional usage) is one of the units named in the First Schedule, such as Kerala or Punjab, with its own elected legislature and government that share power with the Centre. Do not confuse it with 'the State' in the abstract sense of the political community, which you met in GS-165.

A **federation** is a system in which two levels of government, national and regional, each have their own area of powers laid down in a written constitution. NCERT (Federalism chapter) describes it as an institutional mechanism to accommodate two sets of polities. The word does not appear in the Constitution of India; Article 1 uses the word **Union**.

A **Union** is the whole that the States form. Laxmikanth points out that 'Union of India' is narrower than 'Territory of India': the Union covers only the States, while the **territory of India** also covers Union Territories and any territory India may acquire.

Quick picture: Territory of India = States + Union Territories + acquired territories. Union of India = States only. Federation is a word for the *kind of system*, not a territorial unit. A Union Territory is governed directly by the Centre (GS-200 module 6).

### Bridge: What 'reorganisation' of States means: merge, split, elevate, alter, rename

The word **reorganisation** is used loosely, so fix its kinds before reading the history. Each kind is a different way of redrawing the map, and UPSC pairs often test which kind happened to which State.

1. **Merger**: two or more units combined into one (for example Travancore-Cochin joined with part of Madras to make Kerala).
2. **Bifurcation** (splitting): one unit divided into two (Bombay into Maharashtra and Gujarat; Andhra Pradesh into Andhra Pradesh and Telangana).
3. **Carving out**: a new unit created from a part of an existing one while the parent continues (Chhattisgarh from Madhya Pradesh).
4. **Elevation**: a Union Territory upgraded to a State (Himachal Pradesh, Goa). The reverse, turning a State into Union Territories, happened with Jammu and Kashmir in 2019 (see GS-200); in 1956 the former Part C States also became Union Territories.
5. **Alteration of boundaries**: a strip moved from one State to another without creating a new State.
6. **Renaming**: the name changes while the territory does not (Madras to Tamil Nadu).

Article 3 of the Constitution covers all of these. A hint for dates: statehood for a place that was a **Part C State** or a **Union Territory** is usually an *elevation*; a split of a bilingual State is a *bifurcation*.

### 1. Orientation: 'India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States'

*Objective:* State what Article 1 says, why the word 'Union' was chosen, and what this lesson adds to GS-161 and GS-200.

## One sentence

**Article 1** says: India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States; the States and territories are as specified in the First Schedule. Articles 1 to 4 (Part I) deal with the **Union and its territory**. (Vocabulary: bridge GS-169-B1.)

## Two things in one Article

Laxmikanth says Article 1 does two jobs: it gives the **name** of the country and the **type of polity**. The Constituent Assembly had no unanimity on the name: some wanted the traditional name (Bharat), others the modern name (India), so it adopted both ('India, that is, Bharat').

## Why 'Union' and not 'Federation'?

NCERT notes that the Constitution does not even mention the word federation. According to Dr B. R. Ambedkar (as Laxmikanth reports), two reasons: first, the Indian federation is **not the result of an agreement among the States**, unlike the American Federation; second, the States have **no right to secede**. The federation is a Union because it is **indestructible**: the country is an integral whole divided into States only for administrative convenience.

### 2. The territory of India: States, Union Territories and acquired territories

*Objective:* Name the three categories of territory and explain how India can acquire foreign territory.

## Three categories under Article 1

1. **Territories of the States**;
2. **Union Territories**;
3. **Territories that may be acquired** by the Government of India at any time.

The **First Schedule** lists the names of States and Union Territories and their extent. At present (as Laxmikanth's edition counts) there are 28 States and 9 Union Territories; the later change in the number of Union Territories is flagged in module 7.

## 'Territory of India' is wider than 'Union of India'

The Union includes only the **States**, the members of the federal system that share power with the Centre. Union Territories and acquired territories are **directly administered by the Central Government** (see GS-200 module 6 for how Union Territories are run).

## Uniform rules, with exceptions

The provisions on States apply to all States in the same way. The exceptions are the **special provisions of Part XXI** for Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa and Karnataka (GS-200 modules 3 and 4), and the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules** for scheduled and tribal areas inside States. Do not re-learn these here.

## How India can acquire territory

Being a sovereign State, India may acquire foreign territory by the modes recognised in international law (Laxmikanth):
- **Cession**: by treaty, purchase, gift, lease or plebiscite;
- **Occupation**: of territory not previously held by a recognised ruler;
- **Conquest or subjugation**.

Examples since 1950: Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Goa, Daman and Diu, Puducherry and Sikkim. Their stories are in module 6.

## Acquired territory is different from admission of a State

Article 2 lets Parliament **admit into the Union** an existing State or **establish** a new State. Article 3 concerns **internal readjustment** among the constituent States. Be careful: Article 2 is for States not yet part of the Union; Article 3 is for the States already in it (module 3).

## Hold this classroom picture

States are the full members of the school with their own rooms. Union Territories are classes run by the principal. Acquired territory is a new building that may later become either.

### 3. Articles 2, 3 and 4: Parliament's power over States and the procedure

*Objective:* List the five powers in Article 3, the two conditions, and why laws under Articles 2 and 3 are not amendments under Article 368.

## Article 2: admission and establishment

Article 2 empowers Parliament to **admit into the Union of India, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit**. 'Admit' means an already existing State; 'establish' means one that did not exist before. Article 2 relates to States not yet part of the Union.

## Article 3: the five powers

Article 3 authorises Parliament to:
(a) **form a new State** by separating territory from a State, by uniting two or more States or parts of States, or by uniting any territory to part of a State;
(b) **increase** the area of a State;
(c) **diminish** the area of a State;
(d) **alter the boundaries** of a State;
(e) **alter the name** of a State.

The power also covers forming a new State or Union Territory by uniting a part of a State or Union Territory to another (the source records this clarification as added by the **18th Amendment, 1966**). Revisit bridge B2 for the vocabulary of merger, bifurcation and renaming.

## The two conditions

1. A Bill under Article 3 may be introduced in Parliament **only with the prior recommendation of the President**.
2. Before recommending, the President must **refer the Bill to the legislature of the State concerned** to express its views within a specified period.

## What the conditions do not do

- The President and Parliament are **not bound** by the State legislature's views; they may accept or reject them, even if received in time.
- A **fresh reference** is not needed each time an amendment to the Bill is moved and accepted in Parliament (the source cites Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay, 1960).
- For a **Union Territory** no reference is needed; Parliament may act as it deems fit.
- The 'specified period' proviso (the reference to the State legislature with a time limit) came from the 5th Amendment (1955), and the 18th Amendment's Explanation I says 'State' includes a Union Territory for clauses (a) to (e), but the reference proviso does not apply to Union Territories (verify the Article text).

## Article 4: not an amendment

Article 4 itself declares that laws under Articles 2 and 3 are **not to be considered amendments of the Constitution under Article 368**, though they may amend the **First and Fourth Schedules** and contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions. So such laws pass by **simple majority** through the ordinary legislative process, without the special majority or ratification by States (compare GS-167 module 5 on the three modes of amendment, and GS-161 module 4 for the earlier remark).

## The procedure as a flow

Draft Bill, President refers it to the State legislature, views received or time expires, President recommends, Bill introduced, simple majority in both Houses, assent, First Schedule changed. Remember: where Article 3 changes require a text change in the Schedule, it is done by this law, not by Article 368.

### 4. Consent, cession and the Berubari and Bangladesh cases

*Objective:* Explain why Parliament can reshape States without their consent, yet cannot cede Indian territory to a foreign country by an Article 3 law.

## Parliament versus State consent

The Constitution authorises Parliament to form new States, or alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing ones, **without their consent**. In Laxmikanth's words, Parliament can redraw the political map of India according to its will; the territorial integrity or continued existence of a State is not guaranteed by the Constitution. NCERT agrees: the very existence of a State, including its territorial integrity, is in the hands of Parliament, with a safeguard of securing the State legislature's views. (The source does not give a case; standard knowledge records that the Andhra Pradesh Assembly's resolution against the 2014 Telangana Bill did not bar Parliament; verify.)

## Can Article 3 be used to give land to another country? Berubari (1960)

Laxmikanth reports a **Presidential reference in 1960**. The Central Government's decision to cede part of the Berubari Union (West Bengal) to Pakistan led to agitation. The **Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to diminish the area of a State under Article 3 does not cover cession of Indian territory to a foreign country**. Such cession can happen only by **amending the Constitution under Article 368**. So the **9th Amendment Act, 1960** was enacted to transfer the territory.

A teachable wrinkle: the same chapter's Table 5.5 lists an **Assam (Alteration of Boundaries) Act, 1951** that ceded a strip of Assam to Bhutan, which came before the 1960 opinion. Note it as listed in the source, not as a rule.

## The 1969 distinction

In **1969**, the Supreme Court ruled that **settlement of a boundary dispute** with another country does not need a constitutional amendment; it can be done by executive action because it is not cession. (This is the Rann of Kutch Maganbhai Patel case in standard knowledge; verify the name.)

## The Bangladesh Land Boundary: 100th Amendment (2015)

- Background: the India-East Pakistan boundary was set by the **Radcliffe Award (1947)**; disputes led to the **Bagge Award (1950)** and the **Nehru-Noon Agreement (1958)**; the **9th Amendment (1960)** could not be notified for territories in East Pakistan because of litigation and political events.
- **16 May 1974:** the Land Boundary Agreement was signed but could not be ratified because transferring territory needs a constitutional amendment.
- **6 September 2011:** a Protocol resolved undemarcated boundary, adverse possessions and enclaves, prepared with the concurrence of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and West Bengal.
- **100th Amendment (2015):** gave effect to the exchange: India transferred **111 enclaves** to Bangladesh and received **51**, plus adverse possessions and a **6.1 km** undemarcated stretch; it modified the First Schedule for **Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Tripura**. The land boundary is about **4,096.7 km**.

## The rule to hold

| Action | Tool |
|---|---|
| Form, merge, split, rename or alter boundaries of States | Article 3 law, simple majority |
| Cede Indian territory to a foreign State | Constitutional amendment under Article 368 |
| Settle a boundary dispute without cession | Executive action

### 5. From princely states to the 1956 map: Part A to D, Dhar, JVP, Fazl Ali

*Objective:* Narrate the reorganisation story to 1956 in order and with dates.

## 1947: two kinds of units

At independence India had **British provinces** (under direct rule) and **princely states** (ruled by native princes, subject to British paramountcy). The Indian Independence Act (1947) created two dominions and gave princely states three options: join India, join Pakistan or stay independent. Laxmikanth says **552** princely states lay within India's geographical boundaries; **549 joined India** and three (**Hyderabad, Junagarh, Kashmir**) refused. They were integrated later: Hyderabad by **police action**, Junagarh by **referendum**, Kashmir by the **Instrument of Accession**. (Other books give other totals for the number of princely states; use the figure your source gives and verify.) NCERT adds that more than 500 princely states had to be integrated into existing or new States.

## 1950: four-fold classification

The Constitution (1950) listed 29 units: **Part A** (nine former governors' provinces), **Part B** (nine princely states with legislatures), **Part C** (ten, centrally administered chief commissioners' provinces and some princely states) and **Part D** (the Andaman and Nicobar Islands alone). The original Parts VI to IX dealt with these.

## 1948 to 1949: first attempts

- **June 1948:** Linguistic Provinces Commission under **S. K. Dhar**; report **December 1948**; recommended reorganisation on **administrative convenience** rather than language, which caused resentment.
- **December 1948:** the Congress appointed the **JVP Committee** (Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya); **April 1949** report formally rejected language as the basis.

## 1953: Andhra State

After a prolonged agitation and the death of **Potti Sriramulu** after a hunger strike, the Government created **Andhra State** in **October 1953**, the first linguistic State, from Telugu-speaking areas of Madras. (The extract says his fast lasted 56 days; other books say 58; verify.)

## 1953 to 1956: the Fazl Ali Commission

The States Reorganisation Commission was appointed in **December 1953** (chair **Fazl Ali**, members **K. M. Panikkar** and **H. N. Kunzru**) and reported in **September 1955**. It broadly accepted language as a basis but rejected 'one language-one State', saying the unity of India should be the primary consideration. Its four factors: unity and security; linguistic and cultural homogeneity; financial, economic and administrative considerations; welfare planning. It suggested abolishing the four-fold classification and creating **16 States and 3 centrally administered territories**.

## 1956: the result

The Government accepted with minor modifications. The **States Reorganisation Act (1956)** and the **7th Amendment (1956)** abolished the Part A and B distinction and Part C. **14 States and 6 Union Territories** came into being on **1 November 1956**. Examples: Kerala from Travancore-Cochin plus Malabar and Kasaragod; Andhra Pradesh by adding Telugu areas of Hyderabad; Madhya Pradesh from Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh and Bhopal; Saurashtra and Kutch merged into Bombay; Coorg into Mysore; Pepsu into Punjab; Ajmer into Rajasthan; and a new Union Territory of Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands.

### 6. After 1956: Maharashtra to Telangana, State by State

*Objective:* Place each new State or Union Territory on the timeline with its instrument and state number.

## The narrative in steps

Even after 1956 the map kept changing under pressure of agitations and politics. Read the story in clusters.

**1960 to 1966: language and 'hostile' pressures.** In **1960** bilingual Bombay split into **Maharashtra** and **Gujarat** (the 15th State) by the Bombay Reorganisation Act. **Dadra and Nagar Haveli**, liberated from Portuguese rule in 1954, became a Union Territory by the **10th Amendment (1961)**. India acquired **Goa, Daman and Diu** in **1961** by police action; the **12th Amendment (1962)** made them a Union Territory. **Puducherry** (former French establishments, handed over in 1954) was an 'acquired territory' until it became a Union Territory by the **14th Amendment (1962)**. **Nagaland** was formed by the State of Nagaland Act, 1962, effective **1 December 1963**, as the 16th State, to satisfy the hostile Nagas, after being placed under the Assam Governor's control in 1961. In **1966** Punjab was divided into **Haryana** (17th) and Union Territory **Chandigarh** following the Punjabi Suba demand and the Shah Commission.

**1969 to 1975.** **Meghalaya** began as an autonomous sub-state within Assam by the 22nd Amendment (1969). **Himachal Pradesh** became the 18th State (Act 1970, effective **25 January 1971**). On **21 January 1972** Manipur (19th), Tripura (20th) and Meghalaya (21st) became States and Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh (NEFA) became Union Territories. **Sikkim**, a protectorate, became an 'associate State' by the **35th Amendment (1974)** (Article 2A and the Tenth Schedule), and after the 1975 referendum a full State (22nd) by the **36th Amendment (1975)**, which added Article 371F and repealed Article 2A and the Tenth Schedule.

**1986 to 1987.** After the **Mizoram Peace Accord (1986)**, Mizoram (23rd) and Arunachal Pradesh (24th) became States effective **20 February 1987**; **Goa** (25th) by the Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987, leaving Daman and Diu a separate Union Territory.

**2000.** **Chhattisgarh** (26th), **Uttarakhand** (then Uttaranchal; 27th) and **Jharkhand** (28th) from Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. **2014.** **Telangana** (29th) from Andhra Pradesh by the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014.

## The Andhra story in one line

Andhra State (1953, Telugu areas of Madras) became enlarged Andhra Pradesh in 1956 with Telugu areas of Hyderabad; in 2014 it was bifurcated into residuary Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. The 1953 first State and the 2014 29th State are the two bookends.

## Demands for new States (neutral)

NCERT records that after 2000 and 2014 some regions and linguistic groups are still struggling for separate Statehood, naming **Vidarbha** in Maharashtra. Other demands commonly mentioned in news (standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify) include Gorkhaland, Bodoland and divisions of Uttar Pradesh. Arguments that are made on each side: **for**: administrative closeness to people, recognition of cultural identity, focused regional development; **against**: duplicated institutions and costs, dependence of smaller States on the Centre, disputes over capitals, rivers and revenue, and the fear of repeated fragmentation. NCERT calls these changes the story of how federalism has functioned: **boundaries, names and the number of States** have all changed, largely in response to people's wishes, with Parliament holding the legal power.

### 8. Master view: 'an indestructible union of destructible States'

*Objective:* Explain the phrase, compare India with the USA, and link it to the unitary bias of the federal system.

## The phrase and its logic

Laxmikanth says that since Parliament can form new States or change the areas, boundaries or names of existing States without their consent, the territorial integrity or continued existence of any State is not guaranteed by the Constitution. Therefore India is rightly an **'indestructible union of destructible States'**: the Union Government can destroy States, whereas State governments cannot destroy the Union. In the USA the continued existence of a State is guaranteed, and the Federal Government cannot form new States or alter borders without the consent of the States; the USA is an **'indestructible union of indestructible States'**.

## Putting the two models side by side

| Feature | India | USA (as the source describes) |
|---|---|---|
| Origin of the federation | Not an agreement among States | Agreement among States |
| Territorial integrity of a State | Not guaranteed | Guaranteed |
| Borders changed without the State's consent | Yes, by Parliament | No |
| Citizenship | Single | Dual (see GS-162 module 5) |

## Why did the framers choose this?

1. **Integration after Partition.** NCERT says India was divided into British provinces and had more than 500 princely states, which had to be integrated into existing or new States.
2. **Unity and development.** NCERT: concern for unity and development led the framers to create a strong Centre; the Constitution provides that the very existence of a State, including its territorial integrity, is in the hands of Parliament.
3. **Language and identity.** The national movement had committed to linguistic States as far as possible, a demand that produced the 1953 to 1956 process.

## The safeguard

The Constitution requires the President to refer the Bill to the State legislature for its views; the practice of consulting people through agitation and commissions has mattered more than law. Remember that this is a **political** safeguard, not a legal veto.

## Traps

### Notes: Core definitions

- Union of India: the States as members of the federal system.
- Territory of India: States, Union Territories and territories that may be acquired.
- Reorganisation: any change in the map of States: merger, bifurcation, carving out, elevation, boundary alteration, renaming.
- Elevation: raising a Union Territory (or sub-state) to a full State.
- Cession: handing over territory to another State; different from settling a boundary dispute.
- Indestructible union of destructible States: Parliament can reshape States without their consent, but no State can leave the Union.

### Notes: Articles 1 to 4 at a glance (Table 5.6)

- Article 1: name and territory of the Union; India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States; territory in three categories; First Schedule lists States and Union Territories.
- Article 2: Parliament may admit into the Union or establish new States on such terms as it thinks fit.
- Article 2A: Sikkim associated with the Union (inserted by the 35th Amendment 1974; repealed by the 36th Amendment 1975).
- Article 3: formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States; President's recommendation; reference to the State legislature.
- Article 4: laws under Articles 2 and 3 amend the First and Fourth Schedules and are not Article 368 amendments; simple majority.
- Article 371F and Tenth Schedule: the Sikkim story (Article 371F added in 1975; Tenth Schedule of the associate-State arrangement repealed).

### Notes: Article 3: powers and conditions

- Power (a): form a new State by separation, by uniting States or parts, or by uniting territory to part of a State.
- Power (b): increase the area of a State.
- Power (c): diminish the area of a State.
- Power (d): alter the boundaries of a State.
- Power (e): alter the name of a State.
- Condition 1: Bill only on the prior recommendation of the President.
- Condition 2: President refers the Bill to the State legislature for its views within a specified period.
- Views are not binding; no fresh reference for amendments to the Bill; no reference for Union Territories.
- Passed by simple majority; not an amendment under Article 368 (Article 4).

### Notes: Territory rulings and agreements

- Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1960): no fresh reference to the State legislature for each amendment to the Bill (as cited in the source's footnote).
- Berubari Union reference (1960): Article 3 does not permit cession of Indian territory to a foreign country; that needs an amendment under Article 368; the 9th Amendment (1960) followed.
- 1969 Supreme Court ruling: settlement of a boundary dispute with another country does not need an amendment; executive action suffices (case name not in the extract; Maganbhai Patel in standard knowledge; verify).
- 100th Amendment (2015): implemented the Land Boundary Agreement 1974 and the Protocol of 6 September 2011; 111 enclaves to Bangladesh, 51 to India; 6.1 km undemarcated stretch; First Schedule changed for Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Tripura.

### Notes: Why reorganisation happened: cause and effect

- Ad hoc integration of princely states left an uneven map; regions (especially the South) demanded linguistic States.
- The Dhar report and JVP rejection of language caused resentment; the Andhra agitation forced the first linguistic State in 1953.
- The Fazl Ali Commission balanced language with unity and administration; the 1956 Act followed.
- Later splits responded to language (Bombay), identity and security (Nagaland, Punjab), tribal and North-Eastern aspirations (1972) and administrative size (2000, 2014).

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Article 2 (new States outside the Union) versus Article 3 (existing States).
- Union of India versus Territory of India.
- Alteration of boundaries (Article 3 law) versus cession (Article 368).
- Settling a boundary dispute (executive action, 1969) versus transferring territory (amendment).
- Dhar Commission (administrative convenience) versus JVP (rejected language) versus Fazl Ali (language with unity).
- State of Nagaland Act 1962 versus the 13th Amendment (Article 371A; see GS-200).

### Last-week summary

- Art 1 India, that is Bharat, a Union of States; territory = States + UTs + acquired territory. Art 2 admits/establishes new States; Art 3 forms, increases, diminishes, alters boundaries or names; Art 4 such laws are not Art 368 amendments, simple majority.
