## PH-020: Precession, spinning top and gyroscope

Physics Paper I · Physics · Mechanics: rigid bodies

### Bridge: Euler angles: three turns that fix the orientation of a top

A rigid body with one fixed point has three degrees of freedom (PH-015), so three numbers fix its orientation. **Euler angles** are one standard choice (the z-x-z convention is used here; books differ, so always state yours).

Start with body axes e₁, e₂, e₃ lying on the space axes x, y, z. Then make three turns in this order:

1. Turn by **φ** about the space z-axis. The x-axis moves to a new position called the **line of nodes** N (the line where the body's e₁-e₂ plane cuts the space x-y plane).
2. Turn by **θ** about N. The z-axis moves to the figure axis e₃, now tilted by θ from the vertical.
3. Turn by **ψ** about e₃ itself: this is the spin; it moves N to e₁.

**Ranges:** 0 ≤ φ < 2π, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π, 0 ≤ ψ < 2π. For a top, θ is the tilt of the axle from the upward vertical, φ̇ is the precession rate (the axle's own azimuth is φ − 90° for this convention, so it advances at φ̇), and ψ̇ is the spin relative to the turning frame. At θ = 0 or π the axle is vertical, φ and ψ turn about the same line, and only their sum is defined (a singular case; it matters for the sleeping top in Module 8).

**Which spin is which.** The total angular velocity component along the figure axis is ω₃ = ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ, not ψ̇. In PH-018 Module 8 the symbol ω₃ is this total. Do not mix the two.

**Components.** The three turn rates are along z, N and e₃, so the angular velocity is the vector sum φ̇ ẑ + θ̇ N̂ + ψ̇ e₃ (rates add as vectors, PH-016). Module 6 writes these in body axes.

### Bridge: The Lagrangian method in two pages

Newton's law needs forces, including constraint forces such as the tip of a top pressing on the floor. The **Lagrangian method** avoids them by working with energies.

**Generalised coordinates** q₁, q₂, ... are the smallest set of independent numbers that fix the configuration. For a bead on a wire it is one number; for the top with tip fixed it is the three Euler angles (θ, φ, ψ). **Lagrangian:** ℒ = T − V, written with the coordinates and their rates q̇ᵢ. **Euler-Lagrange equation** for each coordinate:

$$ d/dt (∂ℒ/∂q̇ᵢ) − ∂ℒ/∂qᵢ = 0 $$

**Check on one particle.** In one dimension ℒ = ½mẋ² − V(x). Then ∂ℒ/∂ẋ = mẋ, ∂ℒ/∂x = −V′(x), and the equation gives mẍ = −V′(x), which is Newton's second law, F = −dV/dx. For a spring V = ½kx² it gives mẍ = −kx.

**Momentum conjugate to q:** pᵢ = ∂ℒ/∂q̇ᵢ. For x it is mẋ. For an angle it is an angular momentum.

**Cyclic coordinates.** If ℒ does not contain a coordinate (only its rate), then ∂ℒ/∂qᵢ = 0 and the equation says d pᵢ/dt = 0: **the conjugate momentum is constant.** Example: a particle in a central potential, in plane polar coordinates, has ℒ = ½m(ṙ² + r²φ̇²) − V(r). The angle φ is absent, so p_φ = mr²φ̇ is constant, the angular momentum of PH-004. The method turns symmetry (the angle does not appear) into conservation.

**Energy.** If ℒ has no explicit time, the energy T + V (for velocity-independent V and coordinates that do not depend on time) is constant. For the top this gives E.

**Why the tip does not enter.** The force at a fixed tip does no work and is not needed, so only the weight, through V = Mgl cosθ, appears in ℒ.

### 2. Foundation: the gyroscope, the right-hand rule and a sign check

*Objective:* Describe the gyroscope and its three axes, apply the cross-product rule to a horizontal gyroscope, and predict the sense of precession.

## The device

Mathur (Section 11.16) describes a **gyrostat** as a heavy circular disc spinning fast on an axle through its centre of mass, mounted so that it can turn freely about three mutually perpendicular axes: the disc turns in a ring R₁, R₁ turns inside a second ring R₂, and R₂ turns in an outer frame F, all axes meeting at the centre. Whatever the frame does, the axle keeps its direction in space, because no torque reaches the disc. This is the **free gyroscope** (gimbal-mounted). In the **laboratory gyroscope** the axle rests on a pivot at one end, the wheel hangs out at distance l, and gravity supplies a torque. NCERT Class XI Chapter 6 introduces the top the same way: fixed tip, axis sweeping a cone, called precession.

## Vocabulary and symbols

- **Spin** ω₃: angular speed about the figure axis **e**₃ (axle).
- **I₃**: moment of inertia about the figure axis. **I₁ = I₂**: about a perpendicular axis through the fixed point (use the parallel-axis theorem from PH-017 to refer it to the tip).
- **L** = I₃ω₃ **e**₃ when the spin is fast.
- **M**, **l**: mass and distance from the fixed point to the centre of mass; **θ**: angle between **e**₃ and the upward vertical.

## The right-hand rule for A × B

Point the fingers of your right hand along **A**, curl them toward **B** through the smaller angle, and the thumb gives the direction of **A** × **B**; the length is |A||B| sin(angle). With x to the right, y away from you, z up: x × y = z, y × z = x, z × x = y, and reversing the order flips the sign.

## One rule drives everything

τ = d**L**/dt. If a force acts on a moving body at right angles to its velocity, the speed stays and the direction turns (circular motion). In exactly the same way, a torque at right angles to **L** leaves |L| unchanged and turns its direction. Mathur says the axis precesses 'in the direction of the torque'; this means the tip of **L** moves toward where **τ** points.

## Sign check on a horizontal gyroscope (guided)

Take the pivot O at the origin, the axle along +x, the wheel centre at **r** = l **x̂**, spin vector along +x so **L** points from the pivot toward the wheel, weight −Mg **ẑ**.

1. Torque: **τ** = l **x̂** × (−Mg **ẑ**) = −Mgl (x̂ × ẑ) = −Mgl(−ŷ) = **+Mgl ŷ**.
2. d**L** = **τ** dt points along +y, perpendicular to **L**, so the tip of **L** moves toward +y.
3. Seen from above (z toward you, x right, y up the page), a tip moving from +x toward +y is **anticlockwise**.
4. Check by the compact law: **Ω** × **L** = **τ** with **Ω** = Ω ẑ gives Ω ẑ × I₃ω₃ x̂ = Ω I₃ω₃ ŷ, which equals +Mgl ŷ, so Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃) is positive.

The cross products were also evaluated numerically in Python; they give +ŷ for both.

**Reverse the spin** (L along −x) and the torque, which depends only on weight and position, does not change, so the axle swings the other way. The rule to keep: for a top standing above its tip (θ < 90°), **the precession has the same sense as the spin seen from above** (both anticlockwise or both clockwise), because Ω carries the sign of ω₃ in Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃).

**Source slip to watch.** Upadhyaya's Example 9 (PDF pp. 210-211) gives a clockwise-from-above spin and states an anticlockwise precession. The relation Ω × L = τ (here and in PH-018 Module 8) gives the same sense as the spin, clockwise; the value 2.08 rad/s is unaffected. Verify against your edition.

### 3. Core: vector derivation of the precession rate, and why the gyroscope does not fall

*Objective:* Derive Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃) from τ = dL/dt for a fast-spinning gyroscope, state its limits, and explain the 'paradox' of not falling.

## Setting and assumptions

One point O (the pivot or tip) is fixed in an inertial frame, so τ = dL/dt about O holds (PH-004 Module 3). The body is symmetric (I₁ = I₂). Coordinates: z vertical upward, **e**₃ along the figure axis making angle θ with z. Gravity −Mg ẑ acts at the centre of mass at **r** = l **e**₃. **Assumption (fast spin):** L ≈ I₃ω₃ **e**₃, meaning the part of L due to the precession itself, of size about I₁Ω, is negligible. Sign convention: ω₃ > 0 means the spin vector points along +**e**₃.

## Derivation (the 10-mark form; the first derivation is in PH-004 Module 9)

1. Torque about O: **τ** = l **e**₃ × (−Mg ẑ) = Mgl (ẑ × **e**₃). Magnitude Mgl sinθ. It is horizontal, perpendicular to ẑ and to **e**₃.
2. Law: d**L** = **τ** dt. Since **L** ∥ **e**₃, **τ** ⟂ **L**, so **L**·d**L** = 0 and |L| is constant: only the direction changes.
3. The tip of **L** therefore moves on a horizontal circle of radius L sinθ about the vertical. In time dt the azimuth advances by dφ, so |d**L**| = L sinθ dφ.
4. Equate magnitudes: L sinθ dφ = Mgl sinθ dt. Cancel sinθ:

$$ Ω = dφ/dt = Mgl / L = Mgl / (I₃ω₃) $$

5. Vector form: d**L**/dt = Ω ẑ × **L**, hence Ω ẑ × **L** = **τ**. Taking components gives the signed result: Ω has the sign of ω₃, as checked in Module 2.

**Interpretation.** The bigger L is, the less its direction shifts in a given time. **Dimension:** [Mgl] = kg m² s⁻², [I₃ω₃] = kg m² s⁻¹, ratio s⁻¹ ✓. **Limits:** ω₃ → ∞ gives Ω → 0, so the axle holds its direction (gyroscopic stability, Mathur Section 11.16); ω₃ → 0 makes Ω large, and the derivation stops being valid because L ≈ I₃ω₃ fails (see below). The result does not contain θ and, with I₃ = Mk², not the mass: Ω = gl/(k²ω₃) (a standard exam statement).

## Numbers (checked by Python)

For the wheel in the diagram, Ω = 0.9358 rad/s. Its I₁ about the pivot (used for the exact roots below and in Modules 7 and 8) is I_cm,⊥ + Ml² = 1.0 × 10⁻³ + 0.50 × 0.12² = 8.2 × 10⁻³ kg m² (thin disc, I_cm,⊥ = I₃/2). The exact slow root of PH-018 Module 8 at θ = 60° is 0.9416 rad/s, so the approximation is about 0.6 per cent low. The ratio spin/precession is 336, which is why the fast-spin approximation is good. A numerical integration of the full rigid-body equations reproduces the exact value.

## The exact condition (state and link, do not re-derive)

PH-018 Module 8 proves I₁ cosθ Ω² − I₃ω₃Ω + Mgl = 0, with real roots only when ω₃ ≥ 2√(I₁Mgl cosθ)/I₃ (for θ < 90°, cosθ > 0; for θ ≥ 90° real roots always exist). Here the limit is 49.1 rad/s at 60°, about 469 rpm. At θ = 90° (horizontal axle) the quadratic becomes linear and Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃) is exact for steady precession (ω₃ the total spin component); a release from rest still nutates (Module 8). Module 6 below re-obtains the quadratic from the Lagrangian as a cross-check.

## The paradox: why does it not fall?

Three answers, in order of importance.

- **Direction, not size.** A torque along the horizontal changes L sideways. For a spinning wheel the sideways direction is the precession; for a non-spinning one, L starts at zero and grows along **τ**, which is the toppling. Spin means L is already large and perpendicular to the torque.
- **The support does the rest.** The pivot supplies an upward force Mg, so the centre of mass has no vertical acceleration; it moves on a horizontal circle of radius l sinθ, needing a horizontal pivot force MΩ² l sinθ (0.0455 N here).
- **It does dip a little.** Released from rest (θ̇ = φ̇ = 0 at θ = 60°), integration shows the axle sinks to 61.23°, a dip of 1.23°. Potential energy lost, Mgl(cos60° − cosθ₁) = 0.01104 J, equals the kinetic energy of precession at the lowest point, ½I₁φ̇² sin²θ₁ = 0.01104 J with φ̇ = 1.872 rad/s. So 'not falling' means 'falling only to the depth that pays for the precession'. Module 7 and Module 8 explain this as nutation.

### 4. Core: the gyroscopic couple τ = IωΩ on ships, aircraft, cars and bicycles

*Objective:* Derive the gyroscopic couple, find its direction by the cross-product rule, and compute it for a ship, an aircraft propeller and a car.

## Where this differs from the top

In the top, gravity gives the torque and the axis turns. Here the **frame** is forced to turn (a ship changing course, an aircraft pulling out of a dive) and the rotor reacts on the frame. Same law, other way round: the turn is given, the torque is found.

## Derivation

Rotor: moment of inertia I about its axle, spin ω along the unit vector **ê**, so **L** = Iω **ê**. The frame, and so the axle, turns with angular velocity **Ω** (a vector along the axis of the turn). The magnitude Iω is fixed, so **L** changes only because **ê** turns.

1. For a vector fixed in a frame turning with **Ω**, d**ê**/dt = **Ω** × **ê** (PH-002).
2. So d**L**/dt = Iω (**Ω** × **ê**) = **Ω** × **L**.
3. By τ = dL/dt, the bearings must give the rotor the torque **τ**_rotor = **Ω** × **L**, of size IωΩ sinα, where α is the angle between **Ω** and the axle. For **Ω** ⟂ axle (the usual case) this is

$$ τ = I ω Ω $$

4. Newton's third law: the rotor presses back on the frame with **τ**_frame = −**Ω** × **L** = **L** × **Ω**. That equal and opposite couple is what the ship, aircraft or car feels.
5. A component of **Ω** along the axle produces no couple (**Ω** ∥ **L**, cross product zero).

**Dimension:** [Iω] = kg m² s⁻¹, times s⁻¹ gives kg m² s⁻² ✓. **Limit:** Ω → 0 or ω → 0 gives no couple. **Sign convention:** x forward, y to the left, z up, right-handed (diagram below).

## Worked numbers (Python-checked)

**Ship turbine.** I = 750 kg m², 3000 rpm = 314.16 rad/s. The ship turns on a circle of radius 250 m at 15 m/s, so Ω = 15/250 = 0.060 rad/s. τ = 750 × 314.16 × 0.06 = **14137 N m**. With the rotor clockwise seen from the stern and a turn to starboard, the reaction on the ship tends to **lower the bow** (vector working in the diagram; books quote different conventions, so always use the cross product and state your axes).

**Pitching of the ship.** Pitch amplitude 6° with period 20 s, simple harmonic: peak pitching rate = (6° in rad)(2π/20) = 0.0329 rad/s, peak couple = 750 × 314.16 × 0.0329 = **7752 N m**, acting about the vertical (a yawing couple, since **L** is along the length and **Ω** is transverse).

**Aircraft propeller.** I = 15 kg m², 2400 rpm = 251.3 rad/s, pulling up at 0.20 rad/s: τ = **754 N m**. For a clockwise propeller seen from the cockpit, pitching the nose up yaws the aircraft to the right; pitching the nose down yaws it left (a known effect when a tail-wheel aircraft lifts its tail; verify in a flight text).

**Car.** Four wheels, I = 0.8 kg m² each, radius 0.3 m, speed 20 m/s: ω = 66.67 rad/s. Left turn of radius 50 m: Ω = 0.40 rad/s. Total wheel couple 4 × 0.8 × 66.67 × 0.4 = **85.3 N m**, lifting the inner side. On a 1.5 m track the load shift is 56.9 N. Compare the centrifugal overturning couple for 1000 kg with the centre of mass 0.5 m high, 1000 × (20²/50) × 0.5 = 4000 N m: the wheel effect is only 2.1 per cent. It matters for light vehicles with heavy fast rotors, not for an ordinary car.

## Bicycles and motorcycles

Mathur Section 11.17(iii): a lean gives a torque about the forward axis on the front wheel; the wheel turns its axle about the vertical, steering into the lean at Ω_steer = τ_lean/(Iω), the same rule as for the top. Bicycle stability also involves steering geometry, and how essential the gyroscopic term is remains debated (flagged; verify).

### 5. Core: gyroscope applications, the gyrocompass and the precession of the equinoxes

*Objective:* Explain, by the same law τ = dL/dt, how the gyrocompass, the ship stabiliser and the Earth's slow precession work, with the equinox period estimated from Sun and Moon.

## Directional stability (Mathur Section 11.17)

A fast rotor holds its axis because turning a large L needs a large torque: ΔL = ∫ τ dt. Mathur's uses: **rifling** (a spinning bullet resists small air forces); bicycles and hoops (Module 4); the ship **gyro-stabiliser** (controlled precession gives a torque against the roll); aircraft instruments (artificial horizon, turn and bank indicator, automatic pilot).

## The gyrocompass (principle only)

Mathur 11.17(i) describes a gimbal-mounted gyrostat, spun up with its axle in the meridian, whose axle stays on the meridian despite ship motion and magnetic disturbance. **Flag (verify): this is a simplification.** A truly free gyroscope keeps its direction relative to the stars, so relative to the Earth it drifts off the meridian (see the Foucault paragraph below). A real gyrocompass is meridian-seeking only because a pendulous (gravity) constraint is added, which converts the Earth-rotation tilt into a precession toward α = 0, with damping. The mechanism: a free rotor keeps **L** fixed *in space* while the Earth turns beneath it. Let the spin vector point at azimuth α east of north on the horizontal at latitude λ. Seen from the Earth, d**L**/dt = −**Ω**_E × **L**, whose vertical component (worked in east-north-up components) is +LΩ_E cosλ sinα. So the end where **L** points rises whenever α ≠ 0; a pendulous weight then supplies a torque that precesses the axle about the vertical toward α = 0, the meridian. Damping details are outside the extracts (verify).

**Foucault gyroscope (flagged).** A free gyroscope keeps its direction relative to the stars, so a horizontal axle appears to turn about the vertical at Ω_E sinλ: 15.04 degrees per hour at a pole, 7.20 degrees per hour at 28.6°N, zero at the equator.

## The Earth as a gyroscope: precession of the equinoxes

The Earth is slightly oblate (polar moment C greater than equatorial moment A). The Sun and Moon lie near the ecliptic, not the equator, and pull the near side of the bulge harder than the far side. The average torque is in the equatorial plane, perpendicular to **L** = Cω ẑ, so by Module 3 it swings the axis round the ecliptic pole (Mathur 11.17(v)).

1. Standard result (flagged; not derived here): an external mass M_x at distance a exerts a torque of size (3GM_x/a³)(C − A) sinδ cosδ, δ being the angle between the spin axis and the direction to M_x.
2. Average over a circular orbit in the ecliptic, inclined ε = 23.44° to the equator (the mean of the squared sine of the orbital angle is ½): τ̄ = (3/2)(GM_x/a³)(C − A) sinε cosε.
3. L's tip circles the ecliptic pole at polar angle ε, so τ̄ = LΩ_p sinε with L = Cω:

$$ Ω_p = (3/2) · [(C − A)/C] · (cosε/ω) · (GM_s/a_s³ + GM_m/a_m³) $$

4. Numbers (standard constants; verify): (C − A)/C = 0.003273, ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s, GM_s/a_s³ = 3.96 × 10⁻¹⁴ s⁻², GM_m/a_m³ = 8.63 × 10⁻¹⁴ s⁻². Result Ω_p = 7.78 × 10⁻¹² rad/s, a period of **25,598 years**. The Sun alone would give about 81,296 years and the Moon alone about 37,363 years (the Moon's pull is 2.18 times the Sun's). Mathur quotes 25,800 years; astronomy tables give about 25,770 (verify). That is 360 × 3600/25,800 = 50.2 arcseconds per year.
5. Torque, with C = 8.04 × 10³⁷ kg m² (standard; verify): τ = CωΩ_p sinε = 1.8 × 10²² N m. Spin exceeds precession by a factor ω/Ω_p = 9.4 × 10⁶ (9.36 × 10⁶ with the computed Ω_p above; 9.45 × 10⁶ with Mathur's 25,800-year rate), so Ω = τ/(L sinε) is accurate.

**Flag (verify):** the Moon's orbit is inclined about 5.1° to the ecliptic and its nodes regress with a period of about 18.6 years. The resulting periodic change in the torque makes the Earth's axis nod (astronomical nutation, amplitude of order 9 arcseconds), a forced oscillation distinct from the free nutation of a top in Module 8. The 25,598-year figure above is the mean (lunisolar) precession.

**Sign.** The cross product in equatorial axes gives Ω_p negative: the axis precesses **opposite** to the spin (retrograde), because the bulge torque tends to reduce the tilt, unlike a top above its tip (standard; verify). **Source slip:** Mathur says Vega will be the pole star in 'about 1200 years'; with a 25,800-year cycle the usual figure is about 12,000 years (verify), so a zero looks dropped.

## Larmor precession (forward link; flagged)

A magnetic moment **μ** = γ**L** in a field **B** feels **μ** × **B**, so d**L**/dt = −γ **B** × **L**: **L** precesses about **B** at ω_L = −γB, as in the top. For an orbiting electron ω_L = eB/(2m_e) = 8.79 × 10¹⁰ rad/s per tesla (about 14.0 GHz per tesla). Standard; not in the extracts; belongs to atomic physics.

### 6. Core: Euler angles, the Lagrangian of the symmetric top and its constants of motion

*Objective:* Derive the Lagrangian L = ½I₁(θ̇² + φ̇² sin²θ) + ½I₃(ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ)² − Mgl cosθ, find the conserved momenta p_φ and p_ψ, and answer the 2026 question.

## Setting (assumptions, coordinates, conventions)

Rigid symmetric top, tip fixed at the origin, so the body axes and the space axes share an origin. Body axes **e**₁, **e**₂, **e**₃ are principal axes about the tip with I₁ = I₂ and I₃; **e**₃ is the figure axis. The centre of mass is at **r** = l **e**₃. The Euler angles (bridge module) use the z-x-z convention: φ about the space z-axis, then θ about the line of nodes **N̂**, then ψ about **e**₃. Books differ in convention, so state yours. Potential energy V = Mgl cosθ (zero at the tip level).

## Step 1: the angular velocity from the three rates

The angular velocity is the sum of three turns, each along a simple axis (composition of angular velocities, PH-016 Module 8): **ω** = φ̇ ẑ + θ̇ **N̂** + ψ̇ **e**₃. In body axes (geometry of the bridge module):

- ẑ = (sinθ sinψ, sinθ cosψ, cosθ),
- **N̂** = (cosψ, −sinψ, 0),
- **e**₃ = (0, 0, 1).

$$ ω₁ = φ̇ sinθ sinψ + θ̇ cosψ,   ω₂ = φ̇ sinθ cosψ − θ̇ sinψ,   ω₃ = φ̇ cosθ + ψ̇ $$

These were checked symbolically (sympy) from Rᵀ Ṙ with R = R_z(φ) R_x(θ) R_z(ψ).

## Step 2: kinetic energy

Principal axes: T = ½I₁(ω₁² + ω₂²) + ½I₃ω₃². Expanding, the cross terms in ψ cancel: ω₁² + ω₂² = φ̇² sin²θ + θ̇². So

$$ T = ½ I₁ (θ̇² + φ̇² sin²θ) + ½ I₃ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ)² $$

## Step 3: the Lagrangian

$$ ℒ = T − V = ½ I₁ (θ̇² + φ̇² sin²θ) + ½ I₃ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ)² − Mgl cosθ $$

Dimension: every term is an energy. Limit: g → 0 gives the torque-free symmetric top (PH-018 Module 6).

## Step 4: constants of motion

φ and ψ do not appear in ℒ, only φ̇ and ψ̇, so ∂ℒ/∂φ = ∂ℒ/∂ψ = 0 and the Euler-Lagrange equations d(∂ℒ/∂q̇)/dt = ∂ℒ/∂q say the conjugate momenta are constant:

$$ p_ψ = ∂ℒ/∂ψ̇ = I₃ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ) = I₃ ω₃ $$
$$ p_φ = ∂ℒ/∂φ̇ = I₁ sin²θ φ̇ + I₃ cosθ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ) = I₁ sin²θ φ̇ + I₃ω₃ cosθ $$

**Meaning (sympy-checked):** p_ψ = **L**·**e**₃, the angular momentum along the figure axis, and p_φ = **L**·ẑ, the vertical component of angular momentum about the tip. Physically: gravity's torque about the tip is horizontal, so it has no z-component, and it is perpendicular to **e**₃ (for I₁ = I₂ this keeps ω₃ constant, as in PH-004 Module 9 and PH-018 Module 8). The momentum p_θ = I₁θ̇ is not conserved because ℒ depends on θ. The total energy E = T + V is conserved (ℒ has no explicit time). The vector **L** itself is not.

## Step 5: the θ equation and the steady-precession cross-check

∂ℒ/∂θ = I₁φ̇² sinθ cosθ − I₃ω₃ φ̇ sinθ + Mgl sinθ, and ∂ℒ/∂θ̇ = I₁θ̇, so

$$ I₁ θ̈ = I₁ φ̇² sinθ cosθ − I₃ω₃ φ̇ sinθ + Mgl sinθ $$

For steady precession θ̈ = 0 and φ̇ = Ω; dividing by sinθ gives I₁ cosθ Ω² − I₃ω₃ Ω + Mgl = 0, the exact condition of PH-018 Module 8. The two derivations (torque method there, Lagrangian here) agree.

**Numbers.** For the wheel (I₁ = 8.2 × 10⁻³ kg m² about the pivot) at θ = 60°, φ̇ = 0.9416 rad/s: p_ψ = 0.6283 kg m² s⁻¹ and p_φ = 0.3200 kg m² s⁻¹. These stay constant to machine accuracy in a numerical integration of Euler's equations, which also reproduces θ = 60° steady.

### 7. Core: the effective potential for θ, energy, nutation and the paths of the axis

*Objective:* Reduce the top to a one-dimensional problem in θ, read steady precession and nutation from the effective potential, and classify the paths of the axis.

## Reduction to one coordinate

From Module 6, p_ψ and p_φ are constants, so ω₃ = p_ψ/I₃ and φ̇ = (p_φ − p_ψ cosθ)/(I₁ sin²θ). The energy is

E = ½I₁θ̇² + ½I₁φ̇² sin²θ + ½I₃ω₃² + Mgl cosθ.

Substituting φ̇, the term ½I₁φ̇² sin²θ becomes (p_φ − p_ψ cosθ)²/(2I₁ sin²θ) (sympy-checked). Define E′ = E − p_ψ²/(2I₃) and the **effective potential**

$$ E′ = ½ I₁ θ̇² + V_eff(θ),   V_eff(θ) = (p_φ − p_ψ cosθ)² / (2 I₁ sin²θ) + Mgl cosθ $$

This is the energy of a particle of 'mass' I₁ moving in one dimension θ. The equation of motion from Module 6 is I₁θ̈ = −dV_eff/dθ (the same equation). So the three-angle problem is reduced to a particle in a well, and its solution is an integral (an elliptic integral, standard; not carried out here).

## Reading the potential

- V_eff → ∞ as θ → 0 or π unless p_φ = ±p_ψ (the first term blows up), so the axis never reaches the vertical, except in the special sleeping-top case of Module 8.
- The minimum of V_eff is **steady precession**: V_eff′ = 0 reproduces I₁ cosθ Ω² − I₃ω₃Ω + Mgl = 0.
- Any E′ above the minimum gives θ oscillating between two **turning points** θ₁ and θ₂ where V_eff = E′: this oscillation is **nutation**.

## Which motion? The sign of φ̇

φ̇ = (p_φ − p_ψ cosθ)/(I₁ sin²θ) is zero where cosθ* = p_φ/p_ψ. The nodding axis traces one of three patterns on a sphere around the tip.

| Case | Condition | Path of the axle tip |
|---|---|---|
| Wavy | cosθ* outside the nutation range | φ̇ keeps one sign; scalloped curve |
| Cusps | cosθ* equal to a turning point | φ̇ = 0 at that turning point; cusped curve |
| Loops | cosθ* inside the range | φ̇ changes sign; looped curve |

**Numbers for the wheel** (I₁ = 8.2 × 10⁻³ kg m² about the pivot, ω₃ = 314.2 rad/s, tip released with θ̇ = 0 at θ₀ = 60°; computed from V_eff = E′). Case wavy: φ̇₀ = 0.50 rad/s gives θ between 60° and 60.58°, and φ̇ = 0 would need θ = 59.68° (outside). Case cusps: φ̇₀ = 0, range 60° to 61.23°, φ̇ = 0 at θ = 60.00° (a turning point). Case loops: φ̇₀ = −1.0 rad/s, range 60° to 62.54°, φ̇ = 0 at 60.65° (inside).

## Energy of the top (the accounting)

E = ½I₁(θ̇² + φ̇² sin²θ) + ½I₃ω₃² + Mgl cosθ: nutation and precession kinetic energy, spin energy (constant since ω₃ is constant), and gravitational energy. In the released-from-rest case the starting θ is the upper turning point (φ̇₀ = 0), the axle dips to θ₁ = 61.23°, and at the bottom φ̇ = 1.872 rad/s. Energy conservation says ½I₁φ̇₁² sin²θ₁ = Mgl(cos60° − cosθ₁): both sides are 0.01104 J in the numerical integration. So the 'fall' pays for the precession (Module 3).

**Effective-potential limits:** large p_ψ makes the well deep and narrow, so nutation is small and fast (Module 8). As p_ψ falls the well widens and its minimum moves. For given p_φ and p_ψ a minimum always exists somewhere in 0 < θ < π, because V_eff → +∞ at θ → 0 and θ → π unless p_φ = ±p_ψ. What PH-018's minimum spin says is different: a steady precession at a chosen tilt θ < 90° is possible only if ω₃ ≥ 2√(I₁Mgl cosθ)/I₃. For a slower spin, the steady cone must sit at another (larger) tilt.

### 8. Master: the sleeping top, its critical spin, and nutation frequency and amplitude for a fast top

*Objective:* Derive the sleeping-top condition ω₃² > 4I₁Mgl/I₃² (marginal at equality) from the double root of the θ equation, and the fast-top nutation frequency and dip.

## Sleeping top: the top spinning upright (θ = 0)

Use u = cosθ. From E′ = ½I₁θ̇² + V_eff with θ̇² = u̇²/(1 − u²):

$$ u̇² = (1 − u²)(2E′ − 2Mgl u)/I₁ − (p_φ − p_ψ u)²/I₁² $$

The right-hand side is a cubic in u (standard; its roots give the turning points). For a top that is exactly upright, θ = 0 and u = 1, with θ̇ = 0 there. Let u = 1 be a root:

1. Put u = 1: the right-hand side is −(p_φ − p_ψ)²/I₁², so u = 1 is a root only if p_φ = p_ψ. (Consistent with Module 7: V_eff is finite at θ = 0 only if p_φ = p_ψ.)
2. Then E′ = Mgl (value at θ = 0).
3. Substitute: 2E′ − 2Mgl u = 2Mgl(1 − u), and (p_ψ − p_ψ u)² = p_ψ²(1 − u)². So

$$ u̇² = (1 − u)² [ 2Mgl (1 + u)/I₁ − p_ψ²/I₁² ] $$

4. u = 1 is a double root. A real small displacement requires u̇² ≥ 0 for u slightly below 1. The bracket at u = 1 is 4Mgl/I₁ − p_ψ²/I₁². If it is **negative**, no real motion exists near u = 1 (u̇² < 0 for u < 1), so the top **stays upright: stable**. This needs p_ψ² > 4I₁Mgl:

$$ ω₃² > 4 I₁ M g l / I₃²    i.e.    ω₃ > ω_c = 2√(I₁ M g l) / I₃ $$

5. If the bracket is positive (slow spin), the axle can leave the vertical. The lowest point reached is the third root, 1 + u₃ = p_ψ²/(2I₁Mgl), that is u₃ = 2(ω₃/ω_c)² − 1.

**Checks.** Dimension: [I₃ω₃] = [I₁Mgl]^½ ✓ (both kg m² s⁻¹). Consistency: this is a different question from PH-018's. PH-018's discriminant asks whether a steady cone at tilt θ exists (minimum spin 2√(I₁Mgl cosθ)/I₃); the double root at u = 1 asks whether the vertical is stable. The two thresholds coincide only in the limit θ → 0, where 2√(I₁Mgl cosθ)/I₃ → ω_c, which is a useful check, not the same condition. At exact equality ω₃ = ω_c the case is marginal (the bracket is 2Mgl(u − 1)/I₁ < 0 for u < 1, so no departure at this order); this lesson states stability as the strict inequality ω₃ > ω_c. **Numbers:** for the wheel (I₁ = 8.2 × 10⁻³ kg m² about the pivot), ω_c = 69.44 rad/s = 663 rpm. At ω₃ = 0.80 ω_c, u₃ = 0.28, θ = 73.74°; at 0.95 ω_c, θ = 36.39°. A numerical integration of the θ equation started at θ = 0.02 rad with p_φ = p_ψ gives 73.73° and 36.37°; above ω_c (1.05, 1.2, 2, 4.5 ω_c) θ never exceeded 0.02 rad (1.15°).

**Physical story.** Friction at the tip slowly lowers ω₃. While ω₃ > ω_c the sleeping top is stable; as it falls below ω_c a tiny disturbance grows and the top 'wakes up' into a wobbling precession.

## Fast-top nutation (derivation)

Take a fast top released from rest at θ₀ (θ̇ = φ̇ = 0). Then p_φ = I₃ω₃ cosθ₀, so φ̇ = I₃ω₃ (cosθ₀ − cosθ)/(I₁ sin²θ). Let θ = θ₀ + δ with δ small. Then cosθ₀ − cosθ ≈ δ sinθ₀, so φ̇ ≈ I₃ω₃δ/(I₁ sinθ₀). In the θ equation drop the φ̇² term (second order) and keep the other two:

I₁δ̈ ≈ −(I₃ω₃)² δ/I₁ + Mgl sinθ₀.

This is simple harmonic motion about δ_eq = I₁Mgl sinθ₀/(I₃ω₃)² with angular frequency

$$ ω_nut = I₃ω₃ / I₁ $$

The axle starts at δ = 0, so it swings between 0 and 2δ_eq: the total dip is **Δθ = 2 I₁ Mgl sinθ₀/(I₃ω₃)²**. The average precession is ⟨φ̇⟩ = I₃ω₃δ_eq/(I₁ sinθ₀) = Mgl/(I₃ω₃), the Module 3 result. Note that ω_nut is approximately L/I₁ (equal when L ≈ I₃ω₃), the rate at which the axis circles **L** in the torque-free top (PH-018 Module 6).

**Numbers.** (Wheel, I₁ = 8.2 × 10⁻³ kg m².) ω_nut = 76.62 rad/s (period 0.0820 s) against 75.71 rad/s from the integration, within about 1.2 per cent. Dip: formula 1.212°, integration 1.234° (about 1.8 per cent apart; the difference is the dropped φ̇² term). Both scale as expected: faster spin means a smaller and quicker nutation, and halving ω₃ makes the dip four times larger.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Spin versus precession versus nutation: spin turns about the figure axis, precession turns the axis about the vertical, nutation is the nodding of θ.
- ω₃ = ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ (total component along the axis) versus ψ̇ (spin relative to the turning frame).
- I₃ (figure axis, about the centre of mass or tip) versus I₁ (perpendicular axis, about the pivot, with the parallel-axis term).
- p_φ (L·ẑ, constant) versus p_θ (not constant) versus the vector L (not constant).
- Chandler wobble of the torque-free Earth (PH-018 Module 6) versus the equinox precession (Sun and Moon torque).
- Gyroscopic torque on the rotor (Ω × L) versus its reaction on the frame (−Ω × L).
- Precession sense for a top above its tip (same as the spin) versus the Earth (opposite, retrograde).
- Astronomical nutation (forced, period about 18.6 years from the regression of the Moon's nodes; verify) versus the free nutation of a top (ω_nut ≈ I₃ω₃/I₁, Module 8).

### Notes: Where each formula fails

- Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃) needs ω₃ ≫ Ω; for ω/Ω of about 10 the exact quadratic differs by several per cent (Module 9).
- For θ < 90°, at ω₃ below 2√(I₁Mgl cosθ)/I₃ there is no steady precession at that θ (for θ ≥ 90° real roots always exist).
- τ = IωΩ needs Ω ⟂ axle (otherwise IωΩ sinα) and a rigid rotor on frictionless bearings.
- The nutation formulas assume a fast top released from rest; the dip formula neglects the φ̇² term.
- The sleeping-top result assumes I₁ = I₂ and an ideal tip; friction slowly lowers ω₃ in practice.
- The Euler-angle forms are singular at θ = 0 and π (gimbal-lock-like): φ and ψ are not separately defined.

### Core concepts

- A torque perpendicular to L turns L without changing its length: precession.
- Gyroscope precession: Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃), slower for faster spin, independent of θ and (for fixed radius of gyration) of the mass.
- Gyroscopic couple: τ = IωΩ, equal and opposite on the frame: τ_frame = L × Ω.
- Heavy symmetric top: ℒ in Euler angles has φ and ψ cyclic, so p_φ = L·ẑ and p_ψ = I₃ω₃ are constant.
- Effective potential V_eff(θ): minimum = steady precession; oscillation = nutation; double root at θ = 0 = sleeping top.

### Practice

**Q.** State τ = dL/dt in words and say what a torque perpendicular to L does to L.

**A.** Torque is the rate of change of angular momentum; a perpendicular torque turns L without changing its length.

**Q.** What are spin, precession and nutation?

**A.** Spin: rotation about the figure axis; precession: turning of the axis about the vertical; nutation: nodding of θ.

**Q.** Using the right-hand rule, what is x̂ × ẑ?

**A.** −ŷ (z × x = +y, and reversing the order flips the sign).

**Q.** Which moment of inertia appears in Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃)?

**A.** I₃, about the figure axis.

**Q.** Explain why the precession rate falls when the spin rises.

**A.** Ω = τ/L: the same torque on a larger L turns the direction more slowly.

**Q.** Why is ψ̇ not the total spin component along the figure axis?

**A.** The frame turning at φ̇ adds φ̇ cosθ: ω₃ = ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ.

**Q.** Why is the vector L of the heavy top not conserved while p_φ and p_ψ are?

**A.** Gravity exerts a horizontal torque turning L, but that torque has no z-component and none along e₃, so those two components stay fixed.

**Q.** Derive Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃) from τ = dL/dt and state the assumption.

**A.** τ = Mgl ẑ × e₃ ⟂ L; |L| constant; tip of L on a circle of radius L sinθ; L sinθ dφ = Mgl sinθ dt; Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃); assumption fast spin L ≈ I₃ω₃ e₃.

**Q.** State the sleeping-top condition and the gyroscopic couple formula with the reaction direction rule.

**A.** ω₃² > 4I₁Mgl/I₃²; τ = IωΩ, reaction on the frame L × Ω.

**Q.** Give the effective potential and say what its minimum and its turning points mean.

**A.** V_eff = (p_φ − p_ψ cosθ)²/(2I₁ sin²θ) + Mgl cosθ; minimum steady precession; turning points the limits of nutation.

### Memory skeleton

- Setting: tip O fixed, I₁ = I₂, I₃ about figure axis e₃, centre of mass at l e₃, θ from upward vertical
- Gravity torque Mgl sinθ, horizontal, ⟂ L: |L| fixed, only its direction turns
- Tip of L on a horizontal circle: Ω = Mgl/(I₃ω₃), fast-spin approximation
- Exact steady precession: I₁ cosθ Ω² − I₃ω₃Ω + Mgl = 0, minimum spin for θ < 90°
- Gyroscopic couple: τ = Ω × L = IωΩ sinα; frame feels −Ω × L
- Applications: direction-holding of large L; ship stabiliser τ = IωΩ; rifling
