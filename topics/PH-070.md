### Bridge: Two waves adding: intensity, phase difference and fringes

A light wave at a point can be described by a **complex amplitude** A = a e^(iφ), where a is the amplitude and φ the phase (the stage of the oscillation). The **intensity** (brightness) is I = |A|² = a². When two **coherent** waves with complex amplitudes A₁ and A₂ arrive together, their amplitudes add before the intensity is taken:

$$ I = |A₁ + A₂|² = a₁² + a₂² + 2 a₁ a₂ cos δ $$

where δ is the phase difference. The last term is the **interference term**. Where δ = 0, 2π, 4π ... the waves are in step and I is largest (a₁ + a₂)²; where δ = π, 3π ... they are out of step and I is smallest (a₁ − a₂)². Moving across a screen, δ changes steadily, so bright and dark bands appear: **fringes**. A path difference Δ gives δ = 2πΔ/λ, so one fringe period corresponds to a path change of one wavelength. An ordinary photograph records only I, which loses the phase of each wave; the whole idea of holography is to turn the phase into something that survives in I through the interference term.

### Bridge: Coherence length and spatial frequency

Real light is not an endless sine wave but a jumble of finite **wave trains**. The length over which the phase of the wave can be predicted is the **coherence length** l_coh = λ²/Δλ, where Δλ is the spread of wavelengths (Brij Lal, PDF pp. 404 to 406; full treatment in Day 21 lesson PH-068). Two parts of a beam can show interference only if the path difference between them is smaller than l_coh. Brij Lal quotes about 1 mm for a sodium lamp and about 15 m for a commercial He-Ne laser (PDF p. 408, Chapter 16; Chapter 22 gives about 0.3 mm and about 100 m, see Day 21 lesson PH-068), with some lasers reaching kilometres. A fringe pattern is described by its **spacing** Λ (distance between neighbouring bright fringes) or by its **spatial frequency** 1/Λ, counted in **lines per millimetre**. A pattern with Λ = 2 µm has 1/Λ = 500 lines per mm. Fine patterns need a recording medium that can resolve that many lines per millimetre; the finer the fringes, the more demanding the plate and the more easily vibration blurs them.

### 1. Orientation: why a photograph loses the phase and what a hologram keeps

*Objective:* State in plain words what a hologram is, how it differs in idea from a photograph, who invented it, and the two steps of the process.

## The problem with a photograph
A camera lens focuses light from a three-dimensional object on to film, producing a flat picture. The film responds to the **intensity** of the light (the square of the amplitude); it does not record the **phase** (Brij Lal, PDF p. 654). That lost phase is exactly the information that tells how far away each point of the object is. A photograph therefore has no depth, and cutting it up destroys the picture.

## The idea
In 1948 Dennis Gabor outlined a two-step, lensless method he called **wave-front reconstruction**; it is also called **holography**, from the Greek *holos* (whole) and *graphein* (to write): writing the whole wave, amplitude and phase (PDF pp. 654 to 655). The trick is to add a second, plain **reference wave** to the light from the object. The two waves interfere (bridge B1) and the interference pattern carries the phase in the **positions and spacing of its fringes**. A photographic plate can record that pattern, because the pattern is an intensity pattern. The developed plate is the **hologram**.

## The two steps
1. **Recording**: object wave plus reference wave fall on the plate together; the plate keeps the fringes.
2. **Reconstruction**: the hologram is lit with the reference wave alone. The fringes act like a very elaborate diffraction grating and rebuild the original object wave, so the viewer sees a three-dimensional image of an object that is no longer there. No lens is needed in either step (PDF p. 655).

## History in three lines
Gabor's idea, 1948, used a mercury lamp (module 4). Holography became practical only after the **laser** gave intense coherent light. Leith and Upatnieks made the first laser holograms and introduced the **off-axis** reference beam (the book dates this to 1962), which solved the twin-image problem (module 4).

### 2. Foundation: recording geometry, fringe spacing (derivation 1) and the requirements for recording

*Objective:* Describe the off-axis recording arrangement, derive the fringe spacing of two crossing beams, and list the requirements for recording with numbers.

## The off-axis recording set-up (PDF pp. 655 to 656)
A broad laser beam is split by a beam splitter into two beams. The **reference beam** goes straight to the photographic plate. The **object beam** lights the object; every point of the object scatters it as spherical waves, and part of this scattered light reaches the plate. At the plate the reference wave and the countless spherical waves from the object overlap. Because they come from one laser they are coherent, so they interfere. The developed plate shows rings and swirls, with no recognisable picture.

## Derivation 1: fringe spacing of two crossing plane waves
*Assumptions:* two plane waves of the same wavelength λ in air and equal frequency, falling on a plate in the plane z = 0; wave 1 travels at angle θ₁ to the z-axis, wave 2 at angle θ₂ (angles on opposite sides of the axis taken as opposite in sign); k = 2π/λ.
1. Wave 1 at the plate has phase k x sin θ₁ (plus a constant); wave 2 has phase k x sin θ₂.
2. Phase difference at position x: δ(x) = k x (sin θ₁ − sin θ₂).
3. Bright fringes (bridge B1) occur where δ = 2π m, so the spacing is

$$ Λ = λ / |sin θ₁ − sin θ₂| $$

4. Symmetric case, angle θ between the beams (θ₁ = θ/2, θ₂ = −θ/2): Λ = λ / (2 sin(θ/2)).
5. In a medium of index n let α be half the angle between the beams inside it: Λ = λ / (2 n sin α) (the book's Eqs. 23.9 and 23.10). *Transmission* (symmetric about the normal): n sin α = sin(θ/2), so Λ = λ/(2 sin(θ/2)), the air value. *Reflection* (beams from opposite faces at β′ to the normal inside): α = 90° − β′, planes parallel to the surface, Λ = λ/(2n cos β′); head-on Λ = λ/(2n).

*Checks:* Λ is a length; θ → 0 gives Λ → ∞ (the same wave); head-on beams in air (θ = 180°) give Λ = λ/2, a standing wave.

|---|---|---|

| 30° | 1.22 µm | 818 |

## Requirements for recording
1. **A coherent source**: the path difference between any object ray and the reference ray must be smaller than l_coh (bridge B2). For an object 20 cm deep the path differences can reach about 0.4 m (front and back, out and return), so l_coh should exceed that; a laser with l_coh of metres (15 m for a commercial He-Ne, Chapter 16; Chapter 22 gives about 100 m, see PH-068) is ample, a sodium lamp (1 mm) hopeless.
2. **A second coherent beam, the reference**, from the same source, overlapping the object beam on the plate.
3. **A high-resolution recording medium** that resolves the finest fringes: several hundred lines per mm for moderate angles, several thousand for reflection holograms (table).
4. **Mechanical stability**: a shift of the fringes by a fraction of Λ during the exposure washes the pattern out (standard criterion: about a quarter of a period; verify). The book says holography is confined to vibration-isolated laboratories (PDF p. 660).
5. **A reference stronger than the object wave** (standard; verify): with intensity ratio K = I_ref/I_obj, fringe visibility is 2√K/(1+K) (0.80 for K = 4; more values in the master notes). A strong reference keeps the |O|² term small (module 3).

### 3. Core: reconstruction in four terms: the virtual image, the real image and the twin (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive the four terms of the intensity recorded on a plate and the three waves produced when the hologram is lit by the reference, and identify the virtual and real images.

## Set-up and assumptions
Use complex amplitudes (bridge B1) at the plate: R(x,y) for the reference wave, O(x,y) for the object wave, with the time factor e^(−iωt) omitted from both. Assumptions: the two waves are coherent and fully polarised alike; the plate responds linearly, so that after development its **amplitude transmittance** is t = t₀ − βI, where I is the exposure, t₀ and β are constants (β > 0 for a negative); the reference is much stronger than the object wave, |R| ≫ |O|. The book treats a point object in the coaxial case (PDF pp. 657 to 659); the argument below is general and reproduces its conclusion.

## Derivation 2: recording and reconstruction
1. **Recording.** The total field at the plate is R + O, so the intensity is

$$ I = |R + O|² = |R|² + |O|² + R*O + RO* $$

where * means complex conjugate. The first two terms carry no phase information; the last two are the interference term (bridge B1) and contain O and its conjugate.
2. **Development.** t = t₀ − β|R|² − β|O|² − βR*O − βRO*.
3. **Reconstruction.** Illuminate with the same R. The transmitted wave is U = R t:

$$ U = (t₀ − β|R|² − β|O|²) R − β|R|² O − β R² O* $$

(using R R* = |R|²).
4. **Reading the three terms:**
- **Term 1** is the reference wave itself, slightly reduced and modulated: the directly transmitted beam (zero order).
- **Term 2**, −β|R|² O, is the original object wave multiplied by a constant. Light that looks exactly like the light from the object continues beyond the hologram: the viewer sees the **virtual image** at the original position of the object, with true parallax (PDF p. 656). The minus sign is only a contrast reversal.
- **Term 3**, −βR²O*, contains the **conjugate** of the object wave. Conjugation reverses the curvature of wave fronts: a diverging object wave becomes a converging one, which comes to a focus in front of the hologram to form the **real image**, which can be photographed without a lens (PDF pp. 656 and 659). Because the real image is the conjugate, its depth is inverted (module 5).

*Dimension check:* each term has the dimensions of a field. *Limit:* if β → 0 nothing is recorded and no image appears. *Consistency check:* by python, for random band-limited O and a tilted plane R, U equals the sum of the three terms to 10⁻¹⁵.

## The point-object picture
For a point object a distance z₀ behind the plate, O is a diverging spherical wave exp(ikr)/r. The conjugate exp(−ikr)/r is a converging spherical wave that focuses at a distance z₀ in front of the plate: for a plane reference at normal incidence (the book's coaxial case) the real image is the mirror image of the virtual one in the plane of the hologram. A tilted reference adds a carrier 2f_R to term 3 (module 4), which deviates the real image to about arcsin(2 sin θ_R). The book's Eq. 23.8 finds the same three terms: the reference, a wave "identical with that emitted by the object" and a converging wave (PDF pp. 658 to 659).

## Why the coaxial scheme gives a twin image
In Gabor's coaxial arrangement (PDF pp. 656 to 657) the reference and object waves share an axis, so all three outputs travel in nearly the same direction. An observer who focuses on the virtual image sees it accompanied by the out-of-focus real image (the **twin image**) and the direct beam. The off-axis scheme, with the reference tilted by an angle, sends the three waves in different directions; how large the angle must be is the subject of module 4.

### 4. Core: separating the beams (derivation 3) and the requirements for reading a hologram

*Objective:* Derive the condition on the reference-beam angle that keeps the virtual image, real image and direct beam apart, and state what is needed to read a hologram.

## The question
Module 3 gave three outgoing waves. In Gabor's coaxial scheme they overlap (the twin-image problem). Leith and Upatnieks (1962, PDF p. 657) tilted the reference beam so that the images are separated angularly from each other and from the direct beam. How big must the tilt be?

## Derivation 3: the off-axis condition
*Assumptions:* one transverse direction x; the object wave on the plate contains spatial frequencies (bridge B2) only in the band −B to +B (lines per mm); the reference is a tilted plane wave R = |R| e^(i2π f_R x) with f_R = sin θ_R / λ; the transmittance is linear (module 3). Spatial frequency is also the direction of travel: a component of frequency f leaves the plate at sin θ = λ f.
1. The object term (term 2, ∝ O) occupies −B to +B: it travels near the axis.
2. Term 1 is R times (1 − β|O|²-type factor). The product |O|² has a band twice as wide (−2B to +2B), so term 1 occupies f_R − 2B to f_R + 2B.
3. Term 3 ∝ R²O*: the carrier is doubled and O* has the band −B to +B, so it occupies 2f_R − B to 2f_R + B.
4. Virtual image and direct beam do not overlap if f_R − 2B ≥ B, i.e. f_R ≥ 3B.
5. Direct beam and real image do not overlap if 2f_R − B ≥ f_R + 2B, i.e. again f_R ≥ 3B.
6. Hence the condition is

$$ sin θ_R ≥ 3 B λ $$

(the Leith-Upatnieks result; not in the extract; verify).

*Numbers (recomputed):* B = 100 lines/mm. Then f_R ≥ 300 lines/mm, sin θ_R ≥ 0.190, θ_R ≥ 10.9°; the highest frequency written on the plate is f_R + B = 400 lines/mm, a finest fringe of 2.5 µm. *Numerical check:* in a one-dimensional simulation the energy of the other terms leaking into the virtual-image band is about 4 × 10⁻⁵ at f_R = 2B and about 10⁻⁹ at 3B, and falls further beyond. *Propagation limit:* every spatial frequency must satisfy λf < 1, so 2f_R + B < 1/λ; with f_R = 3B this is 7Bλ < 1 (B = 200 lines/mm: 0.886, allowed). The condition f_R ≥ 3B is conservative when |R| ≫ |O|, because the |O|² halo is weak (the leakage numbers above).

*Cost:* a bigger angle means finer fringes (module 2 table), a more demanding plate and less tolerance of vibration.

## Requirements for reading
1. **The reference wave again**: a coherent wave of the same wavelength and the same wave-front shape (direction and curvature) as the recording reference. The book stresses that the information "can be decoded only by a coherent wave identical" to the reference, so without knowing the reference shape the pattern cannot be deciphered (PDF p. 660).
2. **Image choice**: R rebuilds the virtual image (term 2); the conjugate wave R* rebuilds a real image in the original object position: lit by R*, the term ∝ |R|² O* emerges along the axis (check).
3. **Source**: a thin hologram acts as a grating, so white light would send each wavelength to a different angle and smear the image into colours; a laser or a narrow-band source gives a sharp image. Thick (volume, reflection) holograms select a wavelength by the Bragg condition and can be read in white light (module 6).
4. **A different wavelength** changes the diffraction angles, and the image changes size and position. The book says that reconstruction with a longer wavelength gives a magnified image, the magnification proportional to the wavelength ratio (PDF p. 660). That holds for particular geometries only (standard; verify), so use it only as the book states it.
5. **Position**: the plate must be put back in the recorded position; a mis-angled reference gives a dim, distorted or shifted image.

### 5. Core: properties of a hologram and the hologram versus photograph table

*Objective:* State and explain the salient features of a hologram, contrast holography with photography in a table, and distinguish orthoscopic and pseudoscopic images.

## Salient features (PDF pp. 659 to 660)
1. **Three-dimensional image with parallax.** Moving the head shows the scene from different angles; hidden parts come into view (the book's chess-pawn example, Fig. 23.7). The virtual image looks as if seen through a window the size of the hologram.
2. **Every part holds the whole.** Light from every object point reaches every point of the plate, so a broken fragment still reconstructs the **entire scene**, only with reduced clarity and a narrower viewing range. In a photograph each region holds only its own part of the object.
3. **High storage capacity.** Several different images can be recorded on one hologram and told apart (for instance with different reference beams or angles); the book says a 6 × 9 mm photograph holds one printed page while a hologram of the same size can store up to 300 such pages (as printed).
4. **Key to decoding.** The pattern can be read only with a coherent wave matching the recording reference.
5. **Another wavelength can be used** for reading, with a changed image size (module 4).

*Why a fragment works (a window argument; standard, verify).* A hologram acts like a window. A window of width w seen from distance d shows the scene within angle 2 arctan(w/2d), and no detail finer than about λd/w can be resolved. For w = 10 cm and d = 30 cm the viewing range is 18.9° and the smallest detail about 1.9 µm; a 1 cm fragment keeps the whole scene but shows it over only 1.9° and with detail ten times coarser (19 µm).

## Hologram versus photograph
| Feature | Photograph | Hologram |
|---|---|---|
| What is recorded | Intensity only | Amplitude and phase, through an interference pattern |
| Light needed | Ordinary light | Coherent light (laser) and a second coherent reference beam |
| Lens | Required | Not required in recording or reconstruction |
| Point-to-point relation | Each object point maps to one image point | Each object point spreads over the whole plate |
| Appearance | Two-dimensional | Three-dimensional with parallax |
| If cut into pieces | Each piece holds only part of the picture | Each piece reconstructs the whole scene, dimmer |
| Several exposures on one film | Mixed and useless | Separable images can be stored |
| Viewing | Daylight | Reference-type wave (laser, or white light for thick holograms) |
| Recording sensitivity | Moderate | Very sensitive to vibration; fringes below a micron |
| Film | Ordinary | High-resolution medium |

## Orthoscopic and pseudoscopic images (PDF p. 659)
The hologram forms two images at equal distances from the plate. The **virtual** image sits at the old object position and has normal depth and parallax: it is **orthoscopic**. The **real** image forms in front of the plate; in it the depth is **inverted** (near points appear far and far points near), so it is **pseudoscopic** and looks unnatural. The inversion follows from the conjugation in module 3 (term 3).

## One limit to remember
Because the lines are fine and the exposure long compared with a flash, any movement of the object or of the optics larger than a fraction of a wavelength blurs the record. That is why holographic recording stays in a vibration-isolated laboratory (PDF p. 660), and why pulsed lasers are used for moving objects (standard; verify).

### 6. Master: types of hologram: thin and volume, transmission and reflection, white-light and rainbow

*Objective:* Classify holograms by thickness, geometry and read-out, explain the Bragg condition, and say which types are viewed in white light and why.

## By thickness (PDF pp. 660 to 661)
- **Thin (plane) hologram**: emulsion thickness much smaller than the fringe spacing. It diffracts into several orders: the direct beam (zero order), the first order (virtual image), the minus-first order (conjugate image, equal intensity) and weaker higher orders.
- **Thick (volume) hologram**: emulsion thicker than the fringe spacing, so the fringes form planes throughout the depth, a three-dimensional grating. Scattering obeys **Bragg's law**, 2d sin θ = mλ, where d is the spacing of the fringe planes. For a given hologram only one wavelength (at a given angle) is strongly diffracted, so the conjugate image and higher orders are absent. By changing the angle or the wavelength between exposures, several holograms can be stored in the same volume.

## By geometry
- **Transmission hologram**: object and reference beams arrive from the same side of the plate; the fringe planes stand roughly across the plate; the image is seen by looking through it. Fringe spacing Λ = λ/(2n sin α) (module 2).
- **Reflection hologram** (Denisyuk, 1962, PDF p. 661): the object wave comes back from the object and meets the incoming reference wave head-on, forming a standing-wave pattern; the fringe planes lie parallel to the surface, a stack of layers about half a wavelength (in the medium) apart: for 632.8 nm in n = 1.5, Λ = 211 nm. Light reflected from successive layers adds when the Bragg condition holds, so the hologram acts as a **wavelength filter** and can be read in ordinary white light.

## By what is modulated (PDF p. 661)
An **amplitude (absorption) hologram** changes the amplitude of the reading beam; a **phase hologram** changes its phase through refractive-index or thickness variation. A phase hologram wastes no energy in absorption and has higher diffraction efficiency. The maximum first-order efficiencies are about 6.25 % for a thin sinusoidal amplitude hologram, 33.9 % for a thin phase hologram and up to 100 % for a volume phase hologram (standard; verify; recomputed: J₁² peak 0.3386 at argument 1.84).

## White-light and special types (PDF pp. 662 to 664)
- **White-light reflection hologram** (Stroke and Labeyrie): recorded with coherent light, read with ordinary white light. At different angles the Bragg condition selects different wavelengths, so the colour changes with viewing angle. In air, normal incidence on a layer spacing of 211 nm in n = 1.5 selects 632.8 nm; at 30° incidence the selected wavelength is 597 nm (original numerical illustration). The book explains why many novelty holograms look green though made with a red laser by the Bragg effect (PDF p. 662); the usual full explanation also includes shrinkage of the emulsion in processing, which lowers the layer spacing (a 14.7 % shrinkage turns 632.8 nm into 540 nm; standard; verify).
- **Colour hologram**: recorded with three wavelengths (multiplexing); thin media suffer crosstalk, thick media remove it by the Bragg effect.
- **Rainbow hologram**: made by a two-stage process. A first transmission hologram is lit with the conjugate of its reference to project a real image; a horizontal slit is placed over it, removing vertical parallax; a second hologram is recorded through the slit. It gives a bright, sharp, nearly monochromatic image in white light, with the colour changing as the eye moves vertically. The price is the loss of vertical parallax.
- **360° hologram**: film on a cylinder around the object; the virtual image is seen from all sides.
- **Copying**: a master can be contact-copied with laser light, or embossed: a phase hologram with a surface relief is metallised and pressed into plastic sheet (PDF p. 664). Embossing is cheap and is the basis of security holograms (module 8).

### 7. Core: holographic interferometry: real-time, double-exposure and time-average fringes (derivations 4 and 5)

*Objective:* Explain the three methods of holographic interferometry and derive the fringe rules for a displacement (one fringe per half wavelength) and a vibration (the J₀² law).

## The idea (PDF pp. 665 to 666)
A hologram stores the whole object wave. Later that stored wave can interfere with the wave from the **same object in a different state**, so a rough, irregular surface can be compared with itself and tiny changes show up as fringes. The book gives the range of deformation as about 200 nm to 100 µm and lists three methods:
- **Real-time**: record the undisturbed object, develop the plate, replace it exactly and look through it at the object while stress is applied; the stored and live waves interfere and the fringes change with the stress.
- **Double (multi) exposure**: two exposures on one plate with the same reference, the second after stressing; the two stored waves appear together on reconstruction.
- **Time-average**: expose a vibrating object for many cycles; the plate records all its vibration states superposed.

## Derivation 4: double-exposure fringes
*Assumptions:* plane wave lighting along the surface normal, viewing along the normal; a point of the surface moves a distance d along the normal between the exposures; no rotation; the same reference; equal exposure strengths; wavelength λ.
1. The light travels to the surface and back, so the path changes by 2d and the object wave's phase changes by Δφ = 2π (2d)/λ = 4π d/λ.
2. The plate stores O₁ and O₂ = O₁ e^(iΔφ). On reconstruction the two waves coexist and add: I = |O₁|² |1 + e^(iΔφ)|² = 2|O₁|² (1 + cos Δφ) = 4|O₁|² cos²(Δφ/2).
3. **Bright** fringes where Δφ = 2π m, i.e. d = m λ/2; **dark** where d = (2m + 1) λ/4.
4. Neighbouring fringes therefore differ by Δd = λ/2: **one fringe per half wavelength** of normal displacement. This is the normal-incidence result; for oblique lighting or viewing the sensitivity changes (standard; verify).

*Checks:* Δφ is dimensionless; d = 0 gives I = 4|O₁|², bright everywhere: no fringes for an undeformed object.

*Numbers (He-Ne 632.8 nm):* one fringe is 316.4 nm of displacement. The book's lower limit of 200 nm is 0.63 of a fringe; 100 µm would be about 316 fringes, too crowded to count (original comment).

## Derivation 5: time-average fringes
*Assumptions:* a point of the surface vibrates along the normal as z(t) = a cos ωt, with a varying over the surface; the exposure lasts many periods; normal lighting and viewing.
1. The phase of the object wave at the plate varies as φ(t) = M cos ωt, with M = 4π a/λ (as in step 1 above, with the displacement a cos ωt).
2. The plate records the time-averaged intensity; its interference term R*O(t) is linear in O, so it averages to R*O₀⟨e^(i M cos ωt)⟩ and reconstruction gives a wave proportional to O₀⟨e^(i M cos ωt)⟩. Over one period, with u = ωt, (1/2π) ∫₀^(2π) e^(i M cos u) du = J₀(M), the zero-order Bessel function.
3. Hence the reconstructed intensity is

$$ I ∝ J₀²(4π a / λ) $$

4. At a **node** (a = 0), J₀(0) = 1: a bright region, as the book says: bright areas are the undeflected regions. Dark fringes appear where J₀ = 0, at M = 2.405, 5.520, 8.654 ..., that is a = 121.1 nm, 278.0 nm, 435.8 nm at 632.8 nm. The fringes are contour lines of equal amplitude (the zebra patterns of the book's Fig. 23.15).

*Checks:* M is dimensionless; a → 0 gives full brightness; the contrast falls for large a because J₀² decays. The number 2.405 is the first zero of J₀, the same number that appears in the single-mode fibre condition V < 2.405 (Day 18 lesson PH-065).

### 8. Master: the other applications: security, storage, microscopy, recognition, acoustical, optical elements and medicine

*Objective:* Explain the principle and limit of each simple application of holography listed in the extract, in two or three lines each, with the numbers it supports.

The book lists ten applications and four medical ones (PDF pp. 665 to 669). Each short answer needs a **principle** and a **reason it works**.

## Security and authentication
Holograms on documents, cards and products are hard to forge because making one needs a laser set-up and a master, and an ordinary copy cannot reproduce the changing colour and depth. Mass production uses **embossing**: a surface-relief phase hologram is metallised and stamped into thin plastic (module 6).

## Three-dimensional photography and projection
The record keeps the distance and orientation of each object point. Holographic projection can place flight data at the pilot's eye level in a cockpit (PDF p. 665).

## Microscopy
Record with a shorter wavelength and read with a longer one, and the image is magnified; small objects such as blood cells or amoebae can then be examined (PDF p. 665). The proportionality to the wavelength ratio is the book's simple statement; the actual magnification depends on geometry (module 4), and aberrations grow with the ratio (standard; verify).

## Character and pattern recognition
The process is reversible: the hologram of a pattern, lit by the pattern's own wave, regenerates the reference wave, in effect a bright spot where a match is found. This is the basis of fingerprint-type recognition (PDF p. 665).

## Data storage
Many page-sized images can be stored in one volume hologram by changing the reference angle or wavelength (Bragg selectivity, module 6). The book gives 300 printed pages on a 6 × 9 mm hologram and about 10¹² letters or digits per cm³ (as printed). A useful comparison: a volume of one cm³ can hold at most about 1/λ³ distinguishable bits, 8 × 10¹² at λ = 500 nm (standard estimate; verify), so the book's figure is about an eighth of that bound. Because every part holds the whole record, a scratch erases no data (PDF p. 665).

## Photolithography
Holography helps produce masks for microelectronic circuits.

## Acoustical holography
Sound is easy to make coherent and passes through dense liquids and solids where light does not. Two submerged coherent sound sources, a reference and one scattered by the object, form a ripple pattern on a calm water surface; the ripples are photographed and read with light, giving a view of an opaque object's interior, useful for submarines and body organs (PDF pp. 666 to 667). Scale problem: at 1 MHz in water (speed about 1,480 m/s, standard) the sound wavelength is 1.48 mm, about 2,340 times that of He-Ne light, so the pattern must be rescaled (standard; verify).

## Holographic optical elements
A **diffractive optical element** works by diffraction, not refraction, and can act as a grating, lens, beam splitter or filter, even several at once on one substrate. Features listed in the book: thin and light (a few micrometres), cheap to replicate, any substrate shape, narrow wavelength band only (PDF p. 667), for example car instrument readings shown on the windscreen.

## Medical applications (PDF pp. 667 to 669)
- **Holographic endoscope**: either an internal reflection hologram recorded with a miniature set-up using a single-mode fibre of 4 µm core (lateral resolution of 7 µm reported, enough to see cells), or an external recording using a thin conventional endoscope with graded-index rod lenses. The book says it has been used for early detection of cancerous hardening in the bladder wall.
- **Ophthalmology**: holograms of the eye, corneal shape to sub-micrometre precision, corneal expansion by holographic interferometry.
- **Diffractive bifocal intraocular lens**: a refractive lens plus a diffractive lens set at 50 % efficiency to give distance and near foci; the unwanted blurred image is mostly ignored by the visual system.
- **Orthopaedics**: contact-free strain measurement on fixation pins and rods.

## One caution
The book notes that holography is still a relatively expensive procedure. Current commercial status is not in the extract and is not stated here.

- *Formation:* a highly coherent source with l_coh greater than the object-reference path differences (laser: metres to kilometres; lamp: about a millimetre); a reference beam from the same source; a high-resolution plate that resolves 1/Λ with Λ = λ/(2 sin(θ/2)) (hundreds to thousands of lines per mm); vibration isolation, since a fringe shift of a fraction of a micrometre blurs the record; a reference stronger than the object wave; an off-axis reference with sin θ_R ≥ 3Bλ to separate the images.

### Notes: Definitions

- Hologram: the recorded interference pattern of an object wave and a coherent reference wave, which stores amplitude and phase; the word means 'whole writing'.
- Reference wave R: coherent plain wave sent directly to the plate; object wave O: light scattered from the object.
- Virtual image: formed by the term ∝ O at the original object position (orthoscopic). Real image: formed by the conjugate term ∝ R²O* in front of the hologram (pseudoscopic).
- Twin image: the unwanted second image of coaxial (Gabor) holography.
- Off-axis holography: reference beam at an angle (Leith and Upatnieks, 1962), separating the virtual image, real image and direct beam.
- Thin hologram: emulsion thickness much less than the fringe spacing; thick (volume) hologram: fringe planes through the depth, Bragg diffraction.
- Transmission hologram: beams from the same side; reflection hologram (Denisyuk): beams from opposite sides, fringe layers parallel to the surface.
- Holographic interferometry: comparing the stored object wave with the wave from the object in another state.

### Notes: Equations to know

- I = |R + O|² = |R|² + |O|² + R*O + RO*.
- Transmitted wave on reading with R: U = (t₀ − β|R|² − β|O|²) R − β|R|² O − β R² O*.
- Fringe spacing: Λ = λ/|sin θ₁ − sin θ₂|; in a medium Λ = λ/(2n sin α), α = half the angle between the beams inside. Transmission (symmetric): n sin α = sin(θ/2), so Λ = λ/(2 sin(θ/2)), the air value. Reflection: Λ = λ/(2n cos β′), head-on λ/(2n).
- Off-axis condition (standard; verify): sin θ_R ≥ 3 B λ, with B the highest object spatial frequency.
- Propagation limit (standard; verify): 2f_R + B < 1/λ; with f_R = 3B this is 7Bλ < 1.
- Bragg condition for a volume hologram: 2d sin θ = mλ (in the medium, wavelength λ/n).
- Visibility with intensity ratio K = I_ref/I_obj: V = 2√K/(1 + K).
- Double exposure (normal geometry): Δφ = 4πd/λ, I = 4|O₁|² cos²(2πd/λ), one fringe per λ/2.
- Time-average: I ∝ J₀²(4πa/λ); dark fringes at 4πa/λ = 2.405, 5.520, 8.654.
- Coherence condition: path difference ≪ l_coh = λ²/Δλ.

    - **task:** Derive the fringe spacing formed by two crossing plane waves (10 marks; practice target about 12 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Set-up: two plane waves of wavelength λ fall on a plate in the plane z = 0, travelling at angles θ₁ and θ₂ to the z-axis (angles on opposite sides of the axis opposite in sign); k = 2π/λ. Draw the beams and the plate.

      - Phase difference: δ(x) = k x (sin θ₁ − sin θ₂).
      - Bright fringes at δ = 2π m, so x_m = mλ/(sin θ₁ − sin θ₂), spacing Λ = λ/|sin θ₁ − sin θ₂|.

      - Λ has the dimension of length.
      - θ → 0 gives Λ → ∞.

      - Omitting the factor 2.
      - Dividing by n as well as using the outside angle for a transmission hologram (the air formula with the outside angle is already exact), or using the air wavelength for the layer spacing of a reflection hologram.

### Last-week summary

- Hologram = interference pattern of object wave O and coherent reference R; stores amplitude and phase; no lens in either step.
- I = |R|² + |O|² + R*O + RO*; read with R: direct beam, −β|R|²O virtual (orthoscopic, object position), −βR²O* real (pseudoscopic, in front).
- Λ = λ/|sin θ1 − sin θ2| = λ/(2 sin(θ/2)); He-Ne at 60°: 0.633 µm, 1,580 lines/mm.
- Form: laser, reference from same source, high-resolution plate, vibration isolation, off-axis tilt. Read: wave matching the reference, laser for thin, white light for thick reflection.
- Gabor 1948 coaxial (twin image); Leith and Upatnieks 1962 off-axis; Denisyuk 1962 reflection; Bragg 2d sin θ = mλ for volume holograms.
