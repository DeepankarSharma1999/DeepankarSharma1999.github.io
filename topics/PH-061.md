## PH-061: Double refraction and quarter-wave plate

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Optical path, phase difference and fractions of a wavelength

A wave plate works by delaying one wave behind another, so you need the link between thickness, index and phase.

**Optical path.** Light of vacuum wavelength lambda travelling a thickness d of a material of refractive index mu behaves, for phase purposes, as if it had travelled mu d in vacuum. This product mu d is the **optical path**. A wave in a medium with a larger index is slower and its phase accumulates faster.

**Path difference and phase difference.** If two waves cover the same thickness d but with indices mu_1 and mu_2, their optical paths differ by (mu_1 - mu_2) d. A path difference of one wavelength is a phase difference of 2 pi (360 degrees), so

$$ δ = (2π/λ) × (path difference) $$

A path difference of lambda/4 is 90 degrees (pi/2), lambda/2 is 180 degrees (pi), lambda is 360 degrees.

**Units.** 1 micrometre = 10^-6 m, 1 nanometre = 10^-9 m, 1 Angstrom = 10^-10 m. 6000 Angstrom = 600 nm = 0.6 micrometre. Keep every length in metres before you divide.

**Mod 360.** A phase of 450 degrees behaves as 90 degrees, because 360 degrees is a full cycle. This is why thick plates also work as quarter-wave plates (module 6).

**Trap.** Using the thickness d without multiplying by the index difference, or subtracting indices of different waves with different wavelengths.

### Bridge: Vectors in a plane and the tangent to an ellipse

Two small mathematical facts are used in module 3.

**Fact 1: a unit vector s makes an angle theta with the z axis**, s = (cos theta along z, sin theta across). For a point r with components (z, rho), the **dot product** s . r = z cos theta + rho sin theta is the distance of that point from the origin measured along s. A flat wave front (a straight line in a plane drawing) perpendicular to s at distance h from the origin is the set of points with s . r = h.

**Fact 2: the maximum of a cosine-plus-sine.** The function f(phi) = A cos phi + B sin phi has maximum sqrt(A^2 + B^2). Reason: write f = R cos(phi - phi0) with R cos phi0 = A and R sin phi0 = B; then R^2 = A^2 + B^2 and the maximum of a cosine is 1. Example: A = 3, B = 4 gives maximum 5, reached at tan phi = B/A.

**An ellipse in parametric form.** The points (a cos phi, b sin phi), phi from 0 to 360 degrees, trace an ellipse with semi-axes a (along the first axis) and b (along the second). For a = b it is a circle. A **tangent line** to the ellipse in a given direction is the outermost line of that direction that still touches the ellipse, which is why the maximum of s . r over the ellipse gives the distance of the tangent line from the origin.

**Trap.** The tangent point is not the point in the direction of s; it lies elsewhere on the ellipse, and this offset is exactly the double-refraction walk-off.

### 1. Orientation: what is new in this lesson and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Know the two big ideas (two wave surfaces; a plate as a delay), what Day 17 and Day 20 already taught, and the seven verified questions.

## The picture first

Look at a printed word through a clear calcite crystal (Iceland spar). You see **two** copies of the word. Rotate the crystal and one copy stays put while the other circles round it. That is **double refraction** (or **birefringence**): in some crystals one incoming ray splits into two linearly polarised rays that travel at different speeds. If the crystal is cut into a flat plate of the right thickness, the two rays come out with a quarter-wavelength delay between them, and plane-polarised light becomes circularly polarised: a **quarter-wave plate**.

## Two ideas to carry through the lesson

1. **Two wave surfaces.** From a point inside a uniaxial crystal, the ordinary wave spreads as a **sphere**, the extraordinary wave as an **ellipsoid of revolution** about the optic axis; they touch along the axis (Brij Lal, PDF pp. 515 to 516). Everything about directions of rays follows from this picture (modules 3 and 4).
2. **A plate is a delay.** For light normally incident on a plate with its axis in the faces, the two waves keep the same direction but one lags: phase difference δ = 2π |μ_o − μ_e| d / λ (PDF p. 532 to 533). Everything about thickness follows from this (modules 6 and 7).

### 2. Foundation: the scoreboard - symbols, speeds, numbers and the two surfaces

*Objective:* Fix the notation μ_o, μ_e, v_o, v_e, optic axis z, positive and negative crystals, and write the equations of the two wave surfaces.

## Notation

In an **isotropic** material (glass, water) light has the same speed in every direction. In an **anisotropic** crystal the atoms are arranged differently in different directions, so the speed depends on direction (PDF p. 513). A **uniaxial** crystal (calcite, quartz) has one special direction, the **optic axis**, along which both waves travel at the same speed. We write the optic axis as the **z axis**. As in the extract, μ_o and μ_e are **refractive indices** (not permeabilities); v_o = c/μ_o and v_e = c/μ_e.

- **o-wave (ordinary):** speed v_o in every direction, so one constant index μ_o. Its E vibrates perpendicular to the principal plane of the ray (the plane containing the ray and the optic axis), which is the principal section when the plane of incidence is a principal section (PDF pp. 514 to 515).
- **e-wave (extraordinary):** speed depends on direction: equal to v_o along the axis, equal to v_e across the axis. Its index μ_e is the value for travel perpendicular to the axis. E vibrates in that principal plane.
- **Birefringence:** Δμ = μ_e − μ_o. Positive crystal: μ_e > μ_o (e slower across the axis), quartz. Negative crystal: μ_e < μ_o (e faster across the axis), calcite (PDF pp. 517 to 518; extract eq. 20.13 prints μ_o where μ_e is meant).

## Numbers to hold

| Crystal | Type | μ_o | μ_e | Δμ = μ_e − μ_o |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calcite | negative | 1.658 | 1.486 | −0.172 |
| Quartz | positive | 1.544 | 1.553 | +0.009 |

(The extract's Nicol prism passage uses 1.66 and 1.486; Example 20.15 uses 1.658.) Speeds with c = 3.00 x 10⁸ m/s: calcite v_o = 1.81 x 10⁸ m/s, v_e (across) = 2.02 x 10⁸ m/s. The e-wave is faster than the o-wave in a negative crystal everywhere except along the axis.

## The two wave surfaces

From a point at the origin, after time t the o-wave has reached the **sphere** of radius v_o t. The e-wave has reached an **ellipsoid of revolution**. Take coordinates (z along the axis, ρ across it). The ellipsoid has semi-axis v_o t along z (the wave touches the sphere there) and semi-axis v_e t along ρ:

$$ z² / (v_o t)² + ρ² / (v_e t)² = 1 $$

Negative crystal: v_e > v_o, so the ellipsoid is stretched across the axis and lies **outside** the sphere (except at the two poles). Positive crystal: v_e < v_o, ellipsoid **inside** the sphere (PDF p. 518, Fig. 20.28).

## Why the axis is special

At the poles of the sphere and ellipsoid (the points on the z axis) the surfaces touch: both waves travel at v_o. So light along the axis shows no splitting; a wave travelling across the axis shows the largest difference, with speeds v_o and v_e.

## Text figure

See the diagram: the circle and ellipse in the (z, ρ) plane for a negative crystal.

## Trap

Do not confuse the **optic axis** (a direction in the crystal) with a **ray**. Light can travel in any direction; only for travel along the axis do the two waves coincide.

### 3. Core derivation 1: wave-normal index n(θ) and ray direction from the Huygens ellipsoid

*Objective:* Derive the e-wave speed for any wave-front direction and the direction of the ray, and show the ray is not perpendicular to the wave front.

## Setting and assumptions

Uniaxial crystal, optic axis along z, uniform and lossless. Take one plane of the figure containing the axis, with coordinates (z, ρ). Light starts as a plane wave front whose **normal** s makes angle θ with the optic axis: s = (cos θ, sin θ) in (z, ρ). Huygens' principle: each point of the front emits wavelets; the new front is the common tangent to the wavelets (extract, PDF pp. 515 to 516 and 521 to 522). Sign convention: θ is measured from the +z axis toward +ρ; all angles are in the plane of the figure. Let t be the time after emission and let the wavelet from the origin be the e-ellipsoid of module 2.

## Step 1: the wave-front distance

The point of the ellipsoid at parameter φ is r = (v_o t cos φ, v_e t sin φ). The distance of that point from the origin measured along s is

$$ s · r = t (v_o cos θ cos φ + v_e sin θ sin φ) $$

The new wave front is the straight line (plane) perpendicular to s that just touches the ellipsoid, that is, it passes through the point with the **largest** s · r. By bridge B2 the maximum over φ is the square root of the sum of squares of the two coefficients:

$$ v_n t = t √(v_o² cos² θ + v_e² sin² θ) $$

So the wave front moves with normal speed v_n = √(v_o² cos² θ + v_e² sin² θ).

## Step 2: the index n(θ)

With v_o = c/μ_o, v_e = c/μ_e and n(θ) = c/v_n:

$$ 1/n(θ)² = cos² θ / μ_o² + sin² θ / μ_e² $$

Checks: θ = 0 gives n = μ_o (along the axis, no difference); θ = 90 degrees gives n = μ_e. For calcite at θ = 30 degrees: 1/n² = 0.75/2.7490 + 0.25/2.2082 = 0.27283 + 0.11321 = 0.38604, n = 1.6095 (recomputed).

## Step 3: the ray direction

The ray joins the origin to the **point of tangency**. The maximum in step 1 occurs where d(s · r)/dφ = 0, that is −v_o cos θ sin φ* + v_e sin θ cos φ* = 0, so tan φ* = v_e sin θ / (v_o cos θ). The tangency point is (v_o t cos φ*, v_e t sin φ*), and the angle ψ of the ray from the axis satisfies tan ψ = ρ/z = (v_e/v_o) tan φ*, hence

$$ tan ψ = (v_e/v_o)² tan θ = (μ_o/μ_e)² tan θ $$

## Step 4: walk-off

The ray direction ψ differs from the wave-normal direction θ by the **walk-off angle** ψ − θ. Put k = (μ_o/μ_e)². Then tan(ψ − θ) = (k − 1) tan θ / (1 + k tan² θ), which is zero at θ = 0 and 90 degrees (axis and across) and has its largest value (k − 1)/(2√k) at tan θ = 1/√k. For calcite (k = 1.2449) the maximum walk-off is **6.26 degrees**, at θ = 41.9 degrees (recomputed). For θ = 30 degrees: ψ = 35.71 degrees, walk-off 5.71 degrees. A numerical tangent-line check gives the same ψ.

## Interpretation and limits

- The ray is not perpendicular to the wave front (the extract notes this on PDF p. 516). Energy goes along the ray, the phase moves along s.
- Isotropic limit μ_o = μ_e: k = 1, ψ = θ.
- Negative crystal: k > 1, the ray is tilted **away** from the axis relative to s. Positive crystal: k < 1, toward the axis.
- The o-wave has a sphere: ray and normal coincide, no walk-off.

Time target for this derivation: about 15 minutes (practice target, not an official allotment).

### 4. Core: the four cuts at normal incidence, walk-off numbers and the two-image experiment

*Objective:* Predict, for each orientation of the optic axis, what an incident plane wave becomes inside the crystal, and compute the sideways separation of the two beams.

## Set-up

A thin slab of a negative crystal is cut so that the optic axis is in a chosen position relative to the face. A parallel beam falls **normally** on the face (the extract's Huygens constructions, PDF pp. 521 to 524, use normal incidence). Normal incidence means the wave front is parallel to the face, so the wave-front normal s along the beam is perpendicular to the face. Then both waves have the same s, and the angle θ between s and the axis is the angle between the beam and the axis.

## The four cuts

| Cut | Angle θ between the beam and the axis | o-ray | e-ray | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Axis inclined to the face (extract case 1) | between 0 and 90 degrees | straight on | tilted by walk-off, ψ − θ | two beams separate, different speeds |
| 2a. Axis in the face, in the plane of incidence (case 2a) | 90 degrees | straight on, speed v_o | straight on, speed v_e | same direction, different speeds |
| 2b. Axis in the face, perpendicular to the plane of incidence (case 2b) | 90 degrees | straight on, speed v_o | straight on, speed v_e | same direction, different speeds |
| 3. Axis perpendicular to the face (along the beam) | 0 | straight on | straight on | same direction, same speed |

Cut 1 follows from module 3. For θ = 90 degrees, tan ψ = k tan θ is infinite, so ψ = 90 degrees measured from the axis: the ray runs along s with no walk-off, but the speeds differ (v_o against v_e). This is the geometry of a **wave plate** (cut 2): the waves stay together but one lags; the plate thickness controls the lag (module 6). In cut 3 the two surfaces touch on the axis, so there is no splitting and no lag (the extract's case 3 wording is loose; read 'axis along the beam', check).

## Numbers for cut 1 (recomputed)

Calcite (μ_o 1.658, μ_e 1.486; k = 1.2449) with the axis at 45 degrees to the beam: ψ = arctan 1.2449 = 51.23 degrees, so the e-ray runs 6.23 degrees away from the beam while the o-ray goes straight. The e-ray makes the angle ψ − θ = 6.23 degrees with the beam (the normal), so after thickness d the sideways separation is d tan(ψ − θ) = 0.1091 d, that is **1.09 mm for a 1 cm slab** (recomputed; both angles must be measured from the same reference line). Both rays leave the second face parallel to the incident beam if the faces are parallel (PDF p. 515), displaced by 1.09 mm and polarised at right angles. The images of a dot seen through the slab are separated by this amount in the principal section.

## The rotation experiment (PDF p. 515)

Put the crystal on a dot and rotate it about the o-image axis: the o-image stays still, the e-image circles round it. This shows the o-ray speed is the same in all directions (its image does not move), while the e-ray's depends on direction. A polaroid over the images extinguishes each in turn when rotated by 90 degrees, so the rays are linearly polarised at right angles.

## Oblique incidence (standard; verify)

The o-ray obeys Snell's law with the constant index μ_o. The e-ray does not obey it in its simple form, because its index n(θ) depends on direction (PDF p. 515); only the tangential part of the wave vector stays continuous across the face.

## Traps

- 'Normal incidence means no splitting' is false for cut 1.
- Do not mix up the beam direction (s) and the e-ray direction (S): they differ by the walk-off in cut 1.

### 5. Core derivation 2: electromagnetic theory of double refraction (the 2020 question)

*Objective:* Derive from Maxwell's equations that a non-conducting uniaxial crystal supports an o-wave of constant index μ_o and an e-wave with 1/n² = cos²θ/μ_o² + sin²θ/μ_e², and explain why the e-ray walks off.

## Setting and assumptions

The crystal is **non-conducting** (a dielectric: the extract, PDF p. 525), non-magnetic (vacuum permeability, written μ_vac so that it is not confused with the refractive indices μ_o and μ_e), linear, lossless, with no free charge or current. Choose axes along the **principal axes** so that the permittivity is a diagonal tensor (⊥ and ∥ refer to the optic axis): D_x = ε_⊥ E_x, D_y = ε_⊥ E_y, D_z = ε_∥ E_z, with ε_⊥ = ε₀ μ_o² and ε_∥ = ε₀ μ_e² (index² = relative permittivity for a non-magnetic medium). The optic axis is z. A plane wave: all fields ∝ exp i(k · r − ωt), wave vector k = k s, s a unit vector, k = nω/c, n the unknown index.

## Step 1: Maxwell's equations for a plane wave

With no sources: k × E = ωB, k × H = −ωD, k · D = 0, B = μ_vac H. Then k × (k × E) = ω k × B = ω μ_vac k × H = −ω² μ_vac D. Expand the left side: k(k · E) − k² E. So

$$ D = (k²/ω² μ_vac) [E − s (s · E)] = ε₀ n² [E − s (s · E)] $$

using k²/(ω² μ_vac) = n²/(c² μ_vac) = n² ε₀. So **D is perpendicular to s**, and D equals E minus its component along s.

## Step 2: the o-wave

Choose s in the x–z plane: s = (sin θ, 0, cos θ), θ measured from the optic axis. Try E = (0, E_y, 0), perpendicular to the plane containing s and the axis (the principal plane of s). Then s · E = 0, so D = ε₀ n² E. Also D_y = ε_⊥ E_y = ε₀ μ_o² E_y. Equating: **n = μ_o for every θ**. D ∥ E ∥ y, E ⟂ the principal plane. 

## Step 3: the e-wave

Now E, D lie in the x–z plane. Let t = (cos θ, 0, −sin θ) be the unit vector in that plane perpendicular to s. Since D ⟂ s, write D = D_t t. From the tensor relations E_x = D_x/ε_⊥ = D_t cos θ/ε_⊥ and E_z = D_z/ε_∥ = −D_t sin θ/ε_∥. Then

$$ E · t = E_x cos θ − E_z sin θ = D_t (cos² θ/ε_⊥ + sin² θ/ε_∥) $$

Step 1 dotted with t (s · t = 0) gives D_t = ε₀ n² E · t. Substituting and cancelling D_t:

$$ 1 = ε₀ n² (cos² θ/ε_⊥ + sin² θ/ε_∥) , so 1/n² = cos² θ/μ_o² + sin² θ/μ_e² $$

This is **exactly** the module 3 result obtained from Huygens' principle. At θ = 0, n = μ_o; at θ = 90 degrees, n = μ_e. The e-wave E has a component along s: E · s = D_t sin θ cos θ (1/ε_⊥ − 1/ε_∥), nonzero unless θ = 0 or 90 degrees, so only D is transverse.

## Step 4: the e-ray direction

The energy flows along the Poynting vector S = E × H. H is along y, so S lies in the x–z plane perpendicular to E. E ∝ (cos θ/μ_o², 0, −sin θ/μ_e²), so S ∝ (sin θ/μ_e², 0, cos θ/μ_o²) (their dot product is zero; S · s > 0). The ray angle from the axis satisfies

$$ tan ψ = S_x / S_z = (μ_o²/μ_e²) tan θ $$

the same as module 3. The two polarisations see different indices because the electrons are bound differently along and across the axis.

## Check

For calcite at θ = 30 degrees, solving the Maxwell system numerically gives n² = 2.59037 (e) and 2.74896 (o); the formula gives 1/(0.75/2.74896 + 0.25/2.20820) = 2.59037. Time target: about 18 minutes (practice target, not an official allotment).

### 6. Core: the wave plate - phase, thickness, orders and which wavelength it suits

*Objective:* Derive the retardation of a plate, the thicknesses of quarter-wave and half-wave plates of any order, and the dependence on wavelength.

## Setting

A plate of uniaxial crystal has its optic axis **in** the faces (module 4, cut 2). Take the beam along x at normal incidence, the optic axis along z, thickness d. Inside, the o-wave (E along y) and the e-wave (E along z) travel in the same direction with speeds c/μ_o and c/μ_e (PDF p. 532). Sign convention: Δ is the optical path of the o-wave minus that of the e-wave; Δ > 0 means the o-wave lags (calcite).

## Derivation 3: retardation

1. Optical paths: μ_o d for the o-wave and μ_e d for the e-wave (bridge B1).
2. Path difference Δ = (μ_o − μ_e) d (extract eq. 20.35, PDF p. 528).
3. Phase difference δ = 2π Δ/λ = 2π (μ_o − μ_e) d / λ.
4. **Quarter-wave plate**, least thickness: |δ| = π/2, so |Δ| = λ/4:

$$ d = λ / (4 |μ_o − μ_e|) $$

5. **Half-wave plate**, least thickness: |δ| = π, |Δ| = λ/2: d = λ / (2 |μ_o − μ_e|), twice as thick (extract eqs 20.48 to 20.51, PDF p. 533).

## Higher orders

A phase larger by 2π is the same state of polarisation. A plate with

$$ |Δ| = (m + 1/4) λ or (m + 3/4) λ , m = 0, 1, 2, ... $$

gives |δ| = π/2 or 3π/2 (mod 2π) and so produces circular light from an input at 45 degrees. Here m counts whole waves (the "order" of Day 17 lesson PH-207). Together these are **odd multiples of λ/4**, with j = 0, 1, 2, ... counting quarter-wave steps (j = 2m or 2m + 1):

$$ d = (2j + 1) λ / (4 |μ_o − μ_e|) $$

The case |δ| = π/2 + 2π m, d = (4m + 1) λ/(4|Δμ|) gives one hand and |δ| = 3π/2 + 2π m, d = (4m + 3) λ/(4|Δμ|) gives the opposite hand (standard; compare Day 17 lesson PH-207, module 8). The thinnest plate, m = 0, is the **zero-order** plate. A half-wave plate has d = (2m + 1) λ/(2|Δμ|), m = 0, 1, 2, ... (|Δ| = (m + 1/2) λ; (m + 1) λ/2 would include full-wave plates). A full-wave plate (|Δ| = λ, 2λ, ...) leaves the state unchanged.

For a given plate, d is fixed, so the condition picks wavelengths:

$$ λ = 4 d |Δμ| / (2j + 1) $$

(indices assumed constant over the range; in reality they vary slowly with wavelength). The 2022 question uses exactly this form (module 7).

## Handedness bookkeeping

For plane-polarised input at +45 degrees to the axis, which hand the output has depends on (a) which axis is fast, (b) the sign of β and (c) whether the order is 4m + 1 or 4m + 3. Day 17 lesson PH-207 (module 9) gives the table for the zero-order case; keep that convention and name the viewpoint. Example (Brij Lal Ch. 20 naming, Day 20 lesson PH-060 module 4; verify): for the 2022 quartz plate with input at +45 degrees from z toward y and constant indices, the output is left at 574.4 nm (2.25 waves) and right at 470.0 nm (2.75 waves), matching the PH-207 zero-order row for positive crystals.

## Checks

- Dimensions: |Δμ| is dimensionless, so d has the unit of λ.
- Calcite is practical only in high order; 0.86 micrometre (5890 Å) is too thin to handle. Quartz needs 16.4 micrometre at 589 nm, a thin but manageable sheet.

## Trap

Writing d = λ/(μ_e − μ_o) with a negative result for calcite: always use the absolute value, and state which crystal sign you have.

### 7. Core: numerical set - the four numeric PYQs and the extract's examples, recomputed

*Objective:* Solve the thickness and wavelength problems of 2017, 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2026 with correct orders, units and remarks.

All values were recomputed with python. Each is solved as analysis, not an official key. Formula: |Δμ| d = (2j + 1) λ/4.

## 2026 Q1(d): calcite, 6000 Å, μ_O = 1.658, μ_E = 1.486

|Δμ| = 0.172. Least thickness d = λ/(4|Δμ|) = 6.0 x 10⁻⁷/(4 x 0.172) = 8.72 x 10⁻⁷ m = **0.872 μm**. Thicker plates also work: d = (2j + 1) x 0.872 μm = 2.62, 4.36, 6.10 μm. Plane-polarised input must make 45 degrees with the axis. Remark: the plate is very thin, so real calcite plates are of higher order.

## 2019 Q3(b)(ii): 589 nm, μ_O = 1.544, μ_E = 1.553 (quartz)

|Δμ| = 0.009; d = 589 x 10⁻⁹/(4 x 0.009) = 1.636 x 10⁻⁵ m = **16.4 μm** (Day 17 lesson PH-207, module 12 gives the same).

## 2020 Q4(c) last part: λ = 5890 Å, μ_O = 1.53, μ_E = 1.54

The question fixes the path difference at exactly λ/4 = 1.4725 x 10⁻⁷ m, so d = (λ/4)/|μ_E − μ_O| = 1.4725 x 10⁻⁷/0.01 = 1.4725 x 10⁻⁵ m = **14.7 μm**. (A plate with 3λ/4, 5λ/4, ... would be 3, 5, ... times thicker.)

## 2017 Q3(c): d = 40 μm, birefringence 0.00004, circular output

Circular output needs |Δμ| d = (2j + 1) λ/4, so λ = 4 d |Δμ|/(2j + 1) = 4 x 40 x 10⁻⁶ x 4 x 10⁻⁵/(2j + 1) = 6.4 x 10⁻⁹/(2j + 1) m. For j = 0 that is **6.4 nm = 64 Å**, an X-ray wavelength, not visible light. ### 8. Master: using plates - circular, elliptical and reversed light, zero-order plates and limits

*Objective:* Apply the plate to production and reversal of polarisation states, and know why zero-order and compound plates are used.

## Production, in short (link, do not re-derive)

Polariser, then a quarter-wave plate with the input vibration at angle β to the axis: output elliptical with semi-axes proportional to cos β and sin β along and across the axis; β = 45 degrees gives a circle. The full derivation is Day 17 lesson PH-207, module 9 (derivation 7). Day 20 lesson PH-060, module 8 shows the plate as a shift of the phase difference by Δ for any state. The extract (PDF p. 534) gives the same results: elliptical output for β not 45 degrees, circular for 45 degrees.

## The half-wave plate in this lesson's terms

Retardation δ = π turns a vibration at β to the axis into one at −β, a rotation by 2β (PDF p. 534), and reverses the hand of circular and elliptical light. Reason in terms of the two waves: the plate delays one by half a wave, flipping the sign of that component; reflection of the state in the optic axis follows.

Three ways to reverse the hand: a half-wave plate, two quarter-wave plates in series (total Δ = λ/2), or reflection at a mirror at normal incidence (Day 17 lesson PH-207, section "Core: producing circular and elliptical light, handedness and left-right conversion"; PDF p. 545).

**Left to right circular (2019):** use a half-wave plate with the axis anywhere (circular light has equal components along any axes). Alternatively two parallel quarter-wave plates, a total of Δ = λ/2.

## Zero-order and compound plates

The retardation of a plate in waves is N = |Δμ| d/λ. A small fractional change ε of the wavelength changes N by about N ε. For the 2022 plate at 574.4 nm, N = 2.25 waves, so a 1 per cent change of wavelength changes the retardation by about 0.0225 wave = 8.1 degrees; for a zero-order quarter-wave plate (N = 0.25) the change is 0.0025 wave = 0.9 degrees. Higher order therefore means higher sensitivity to wavelength and temperature.

**Compound zero-order plate (standard; verify):** two plates of the same crystal with **crossed** axes. Their retardations subtract (module 8 of Day 20 lesson PH-060: crossed plates cancel), so the net thickness is d₁ − d₂. For quartz at 589 nm two plates differing in thickness by 16.36 μm act as one zero-order quarter-wave plate, but each can be a robust 0.5 mm. (Compound plates are made as sheets cemented with crossed axes.)

## Why the quarter-wave plate cannot work for all colours

For constant Δμ, δ ∝ 1/λ, so a plate that gives 90 degrees at 589 nm gives 117.8 degrees at 450 nm and 75.7 degrees at 700 nm (recomputed). Achromatic wave plates and Fresnel's rhomb (Day 17 lesson PH-207) reduce this.

## Testing a plate

Put it between crossed polarisers and rotate it. When its axes are parallel to the polarisers the field stays dark (extinction); at 45 degrees it passes light. The detection of circular and elliptical light with a quarter-wave plate and an analyser is Day 17 lesson PH-207, module 10.

## Biaxial crystals and the Nicol prism

The extract notes that **biaxial** crystals (mica, topaz, aragonite) have two optic axes and two extraordinary rays (PDF p. 513); the Nicol prism turns a calcite rhomb into a polariser by total internal reflection of the o-ray at the Canada balsam layer (PDF p. 519). Both are named here only.

## Limits of this treatment

- Normal incidence and negligible reflection losses and multiple reflections inside the plate.
- Constant indices over the band used.
- Plate cut with the axis in the faces and uniform thickness; absorption (dichroism) ignored.

### Notes: Definitions

- Double refraction (birefringence) is the splitting of a ray in an anisotropic crystal into an ordinary (o) and an extraordinary (e) ray, linearly polarised at right angles.
- The optic axis is the direction along which the o- and e-waves have the same speed; a uniaxial crystal has one, a biaxial crystal two.
- The principal section is the plane through the optic axis perpendicular to a pair of opposite faces; the principal plane of a ray contains the ray and the axis (PDF pp. 514 to 515). They coincide when the plane of incidence is a principal section; the e-ray vibrates in the principal plane and the o-ray perpendicular to it.
- The wave-normal index n(θ) is the index for a wave whose normal makes angle θ with the axis; the ray (energy) direction differs by the walk-off angle.
- A quarter-wave plate is a plate with its optic axis in the faces that gives a path difference (2j + 1) λ/4 (least thickness d = λ/(4|Δμ|)); a half-wave plate gives (m + 1/2) λ.

### Notes: Equations to know

- o-sphere radius v_o t = c t/μ_o; e-ellipsoid z²/(v_o t)² + ρ²/(v_e t)² = 1 with z along the axis, v_e = c/μ_e.
- Wave-normal speed v_n = √(v_o² cos²θ + v_e² sin²θ); 1/n² = cos²θ/μ_o² + sin²θ/μ_e².
- Ray direction tan ψ = (μ_o/μ_e)² tan θ; maximum walk-off tan = (k − 1)/(2√k) with k = (μ_o/μ_e)².
- EM theory: D = ε₀ n² [E − s (s · E)]; o-wave n = μ_o; e-wave as above; S ∝ (sin θ/μ_e², 0, cos θ/μ_o²).
- Retardation δ = 2π |μ_o − μ_e| d/λ; QWP d = (2j + 1) λ/(4|Δμ|); HWP d = (2m + 1) λ/(2|Δμ|).
- For a given plate λ = 4 d |Δμ|/(2j + 1) for circular output.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: Huygens ellipsoid

- Set-up: uniaxial crystal, axis z, wave-front normal s = (cos θ, sin θ) in (z, ρ), wavelets from the origin.
- Point on the ellipsoid (v_o t cos φ, v_e t sin φ); s · r = t (v_o cos θ cos φ + v_e sin θ sin φ).
- Tangent wave front: maximise over φ to get v_n = √(v_o² cos²θ + v_e² sin²θ).
- Tangency point tan φ* = (v_e sin θ)/(v_o cos θ); ray angle tan ψ = (v_e/v_o)² tan θ.
- Checks: θ = 0 and 90 degrees give no walk-off; μ_o = μ_e gives ψ = θ.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: electromagnetic theory

- Assumptions: non-conducting, non-magnetic, principal axes, ε = diag(ε_⊥, ε_⊥, ε_∥), plane wave exp i(k·r − ωt).
- Maxwell: k × (k × E) = −ω² μ_vac D, so D = ε₀ n² [E − s (s · E)].
- o-wave: E along y, s · E = 0, so n = μ_o for all θ.
- e-wave: D = D_t t, E from the tensor, dot with t to get 1/n² = cos²θ/μ_o² + sin²θ/μ_e².
- Poynting vector S ∝ (sin θ/μ_e², 0, cos θ/μ_o²), tan ψ = (μ_o/μ_e)² tan θ.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Optic axis (a direction) against a ray: light can travel in any direction, and only along the axis do the two waves coincide.
- Wave normal (phase direction) against ray (energy direction): equal for the o-wave, different for the e-wave by the walk-off.
- Plate with axis in the faces (no spatial separation, a lag) against a thick slab with the axis inclined (spatial separation).
- Quarter-wave plate against half-wave plate: δ = π/2 against π; circular production against rotation by 2β or reversal of hand.
- Zero-order against multiple-order plates: same state at the design wavelength, different sensitivity to wavelength.

### Core concepts

- Uniaxial crystals have an o-wave (sphere, constant index μ_o) and an e-wave (ellipsoid of revolution about the optic axis); they touch on the axis.
- The e-wave index depends on direction: 1/n² = cos²θ/μ_o² + sin²θ/μ_e²; its ray is tilted from the wave normal by the walk-off, tan ψ = (μ_o/μ_e)² tan θ.
- Electromagnetic theory: ε is a tensor, D = ε₀ n² [E − s (s · E)]; the o-wave has E perpendicular to the principal plane and n = μ_o.
- A wave plate has its axis in the faces; the o- and e-waves stay together and a phase δ = 2π |μ_o − μ_e| d/λ builds up; a quarter-wave plate has d = (2j + 1) λ/(4|Δμ|).
