## PH-005: Kepler laws and orbital dynamics

Physics Paper I · Physics · Mechanics: particles

### Bridge: Ellipse geometry: a, b, c, e, foci and the semi-latus rectum

An **ellipse** is a closed curve. Fix two pins F1 and F2 (the **foci**), loop a string longer than the gap between them, hold a pencil against the taut string and move it round: the pencil draws an ellipse (NCERT Class XI Gravitation, section 7.2, describes exactly this). For every point on the curve, distance to F1 plus distance to F2 is the same constant, and that constant equals 2a.

The vocabulary you need:

- **Semi-major axis a**: half of the longest diameter. It is also the average of the nearest and farthest distances measured from a focus.
- **Semi-minor axis b**: half of the shortest diameter.
- **Focal distance c**: the centre is midway between the foci, and each focus is at distance c from the centre.
- **Eccentricity e** = c/a. It measures how squashed the ellipse is: e = 0 is a circle (the two foci coincide at the centre), and e close to 1 is a long thin ellipse.
- **Semi-latus rectum l**: the distance from a focus to the curve measured at right angles to the major axis.

Relations, each checkable on a sketch: c = a e, b² = a² − c² = a²(1 − e²), and l = b²/a = a(1 − e²). The nearest point to a focus (the **perihelion** for the Sun, **perigee** for the Earth) is at r_p = a − c = a(1 − e); the farthest (**aphelion**, **apogee**) is at r_a = a + c = a(1 + e). So r_p + r_a = 2a and e = (r_a − r_p)/(r_a + r_p). The area of the ellipse is π a b. In polar form with the origin at a focus, r = l/(1 + e cos φ), derived in PH-003 bridge B2. Together the two extreme points are called **apsides** (singular apsis); the line joining them is the **line of apsides**.

### Bridge: Recap: energy, angular momentum and the AU-year-solar-mass units

This lesson reuses three results and does not re-derive them. (1) For any central force the angular momentum L = m r × v is constant, so the motion lies in a plane and the **areal velocity** dA/dt = L/(2m) is constant (PH-003 module 2; PH-004 module 4). (2) For the attractive inverse-square force F = −GMm/r² the potential energy is U = −GMm/r with U = 0 at infinity, and the total energy E = ½ m v² − GMm/r is constant (PH-001 module 6; PH-008 module 6 for the potential-energy definition). (3) The inverse-square orbit is a conic with eccentricity e = √(1 + 2 E L²/(m k²)), where k = GMm; E < 0 gives an ellipse, E = 0 a parabola and E > 0 a hyperbola (PH-003 module 6).

In this lesson **GM** (the product, called the standard gravitational parameter) appears so often that it is quoted directly. Standard values (not in the extracts; verify against a data table): GM of the Earth = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³ s⁻², GM of the Sun = 1.327 × 10²⁰ m³ s⁻². Using GM avoids needing G and the mass separately.

**Astronomical units.** One astronomical unit (AU) is the semi-major axis of the Earth's orbit, about 1.496 × 10¹¹ m. Measuring a in AU and T in years, with the Sun's mass as the unit of mass, turns Kepler's third law into the compact form T² = a³ (module 7 adds the mass). The Earth gives a = 1 and T = 1, so the constant is fixed by that one planet.

## The history in four lines

Tycho Brahe (1546-1601, Denmark) spent his life recording planetary positions by the naked eye. His assistant Johannes Kepler (1571-1640) analysed these data (NCERT Class XI Gravitation, p. 2). D. S. Mathur records that the work was done on Tycho's Mars data and took Kepler 22 years, with the first two laws published in 1609 and the third ten years later in 1619 (Mathur §12.40). Those years are the textbook's; the standard works of Kepler are usually cited as Astronomia Nova (1609) and Harmonices Mundi (1619); verify before quoting titles.

## The three laws, as an answer must state them

1. **Law of orbits.** Each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one **focus** (not at the centre).
2. **Law of areas.** The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time.
3. **Law of periods.** The square of the period of revolution is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis: T² ∝ a³, with the same constant for every planet of the same Sun.

## Reading the laws carefully

- Law 1 puts the Sun at a focus; only for e = 0 does the focus coincide with the centre.
- Law 2 says the planet **moves faster when nearer** the Sun. It holds for any central force (NCERT, Points to Ponder 2).
- Law 3 uses a, the **semi-major axis**, often called the "mean distance". Use a, not the time-averaged distance, which for an ellipse is a(1 + e²/2) (verified numerically for e = 0.6: 1.18a).
- The mass of the planet does not appear; nothing in the statement says the Sun is exactly fixed (module 7).

### 2. Foundation: the elements of an orbit and the link between a, e and E, L

*Objective:* Use a, e and the period as the language of an orbit, and convert between a, e and the dynamical constants E and h.

## Two descriptions of one orbit

Mathur (§12.39, book p. 674, PDF 686) separates the **geometrical constants** a and e, which give the shape and size, from the **dynamical constants** E and h (energy and angular momentum, both per unit mass), which a rocket engineer feels. The orientation of the ellipse in space is a further, separate piece of information. Only two of the four are independent, so any pair determines the other pair.

Work per unit mass: put ε = E/m (specific energy), h = L/m (specific angular momentum), and K = GM for gravity.

$$ ε = −GM/(2a),    h² = GM a(1 − e²) = GM l $$

and the inverse relations

$$ a = GM/(2|ε|),    e² = 1 + 2 ε h²/(GM)² $$

(For m, multiply ε by m to get E = −GMm/2a; and L = m h.) The first is PH-003 module 7; the second follows from l = h²/GM = a(1 − e²), which Mathur uses throughout.

## The period

Because dA/dt = h/2 is constant and the area is π a b, T = 2π a b/h. With b = a√(1 − e²) and h = √(GM a(1 − e²)),

$$ T = 2π a² √(1 − e²)/√(GM a (1 − e²)) = 2π √(a³/GM) $$

The factor (1 − e²) cancels: the period depends only on a, and e does not enter (PH-003 module 7).

## A table you can reuse

The apsidal speeds follow from vis-viva (module 4) and are tabulated in PH-003 module 8, so they are left out here.

| Quantity | Formula | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| Perihelion r_p | a(1 − e) | nearest |
| Aphelion r_a | a(1 + e) | farthest |
| v_p/v_a | (1 + e)/(1 − e) = r_a/r_p | from L conservation |
| Specific angular momentum h | √(GM a (1 − e²)) | constant |
| Period T | 2π√(a³/GM) | independent of e |

## Numbers for the Earth

The correct magnitude is 30.3 km s⁻¹ (module 4 and PH-003 module 8); the ratio 0.293/0.303 is correct, only the power of ten in the printed speeds is wrong. The method is sound.

## Which e values are typical

Planetary orbits are close to circles (the planets Venus and Neptune have e below 0.01, Earth 0.0167, Mars about 0.09, Mercury about 0.21; standard data, verify). Comets can reach e above 0.9 (module 10 uses a Halley-like comet with illustrative numbers).

### 3. Core: from Kepler's laws back to Newton's inverse-square law

*Objective:* Derive the force law from the three laws (the converse of PH-003), explain why each law is needed, and reproduce Newton's Moon test.

## Why the converse matters

PH-003 module 7 shows that an inverse-square force gives the three laws. History went the other way: Newton found the force from the laws. Both Mathur (§12.41) and Upadhyaya (§5.17) treat the converse, and it explains what each law contributes.

## Step 1: law 2 says the force is central

Equal areas in equal times means the areal velocity dA/dt = h/2 is constant, where h = r² dφ/dt is the angular momentum per unit mass. Then dh/dt = 0, i.e. r × a = 0, so the acceleration has no component perpendicular to r: the force is directed along the line to the Sun.

## Step 2: law 1 gives the r-dependence

For motion under a central force the Binet equation (PH-003 module 5), written per unit mass, gives the radial acceleration

$$ a_r = −h² u² (u'' + u),   u = 1/r,   u'' = d²u/dφ² $$

An ellipse with the Sun at a focus has u = (1 + e cos φ)/l, so u'' + u = 1/l (a constant). Then

$$ a_r = −(h²/l) u² = −K/r²,   K = h²/l $$

The acceleration of a given planet is therefore inverse-square, pointing to the Sun.

## Step 3: law 3 makes K the same for every planet

Laws 1 and 2 give a constant K for **each** planet, but K = h²/l could differ from planet to planet. Use the period T = 2π a b/h (area divided by areal velocity) and b² = a l:

$$ T² = 4π² a² b²/h² = 4π² a³ l/h² = 4π² a³/K $$

Kepler's third law says T²/a³ is the same for all planets, so K = 4π² a³/T² is the same for all planets: **the acceleration of any planet at a given distance is the same and depends only on that distance**.

## Step 4: Newton's third law fixes the mass dependence

Let the Sun (mass M) pull a planet (mass m) with F = −K m/r² (a = F/m). By the third law the planet pulls the Sun with an equal and opposite force, which by the same reasoning can be written k M/r², with k a constant of the planet's pull. Equating K m = k M gives K/M = k/m, a quantity that cannot depend on which body is which, so call it G. Then K = G M and

$$ F = −G M m / r² $$

## What each law contributed

| Law | What it gave Newton |
|---|---|
| 2 | direction: central force |
| 1 | distance dependence: 1/r² |
| 3 | universality: same K for every planet, hence the same law for all |

## Newton's Moon test

Compare the Moon's centripetal acceleration with g. The Moon's period is 27.3 days = 2.36 × 10⁶ s at distance R = 3.84 × 10⁸ m: a = 4π² R/T² = 2.72 × 10⁻³ m s⁻². Dividing into g = 9.81 m s⁻²: g/a = 3600, while (R/R_E)² = (60.3)² = 3633. The two agree within about 1 %, which supports the 1/r² law, because the Moon is about 60 Earth radii away. (Upadhyaya prints the Moon's acceleration as 2.7 × 10⁻³ **cm** s⁻²; the correct unit is m s⁻², as above.)

## Checks

Dimensions: K has units m³ s⁻² (acceleration × r²), and 4π² a³/T² has the same. Limiting case: for a circle of radius r the same argument gives K = 4π² r³/T², the centripetal relation v²/r = K/r² with v = 2π r/T.

### 4. Core: energy of an ellipse and the vis-viva relation derived from the apsides

*Objective:* Derive E = −GMm/2a and v² = GM(2/r − 1/a) using only conservation laws at the two apsides, and use them to find speeds.

## An independent method

PH-003 module 7 obtained E = −k/2a from the Binet solution. Here is a second route that uses **only** the definition a = (r_p + r_a)/2 and two conservation laws, so it also checks the first. Setting: attractive force −GMm/r², U = −GMm/r, inertial frame with the Sun (or Earth) fixed at the focus S.

## Derivation

At the apsides the radial velocity is zero (r is at a maximum or minimum), so the velocity is perpendicular to r. Then L = m r_p v_p = m r_a v_a, so v_a = v_p r_p/r_a (1). Conservation of energy between the two apsides:

$$ ½ v_p² − GM/r_p = ½ v_a² − GM/r_a $$

Substitute (1): ½ v_p² (1 − r_p²/r_a²) = GM (1/r_p − 1/r_a) = GM (r_a − r_p)/(r_p r_a). Write 1 − r_p²/r_a² = (r_a − r_p)(r_a + r_p)/r_a². Cancel (r_a − r_p) (non-zero unless the orbit is a circle):

$$ ½ v_p² (r_a + r_p)/r_a² = GM/(r_p r_a)  ⇒  v_p² = 2GM r_a/(r_p (r_a + r_p)) = GM r_a/(a r_p) $$

since r_a + r_p = 2a. The energy per unit mass:

$$ ε = ½ v_p² − GM/r_p = (GM/r_p)(r_a/2a − 1) = (GM/r_p)(r_a − 2a)/(2a) = −GM/(2a) $$

because r_a − 2a = −r_p. Thus **E = −GMm/(2a)**. At any point at distance r, ½ v² − GM/r = −GM/2a gives the **vis-viva relation**

$$ v² = GM (2/r − 1/a) $$

("vis viva" is Latin for living force; the name is historical.) Substituting r_p = a(1 − e) gives v_p² = (GM/a)(1 + e)/(1 − e), agreeing with module 2.

## Physical interpretation and limits

- Circle (r = a): v² = GM/a, the circular speed.
- r → a(1 ± e) recovers the apsidal speeds.
- a → ∞ (parabola): v² = 2GM/r, the escape speed.
- It gives speed only; the direction needs h: the tangential component is h/r and the radial component is v_r² = v² − h²/r².

Energy depends only on a: all orbits of the same a have the same ε and the same period, whatever their e.

## Numerical check: Earth about the Sun

Take Mathur's perihelion distance r_p = 1.47 × 10¹¹ m with a = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m and GM = 1.327 × 10²⁰ m³ s⁻². Then v_p = √[GM(2/r_p − 1/a)] = 30.3 km s⁻¹. The aphelion distance is r_a = 2a − r_p = 1.522 × 10¹¹ m and v_a = v_p r_p/r_a = 29.3 km s⁻¹. These are the values Mathur's Example 3 should print (module 2): the printed 0.303 and 0.293 km s⁻¹ are 30.3 and 29.3 km s⁻¹ with the power of ten lost.

## Satellite example

Perigee altitude 300 km, apogee altitude 3000 km above the Earth (R_E = 6371 km, GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴; standard values): r_p = 6671 km, r_a = 9371 km, so a = 8021 km, e = 0.1683. Vis-viva gives v_p = 8.355 km s⁻¹ and v_a = 5.948 km s⁻¹; check r_p v_p = r_a v_a = 5.574 × 10¹⁰ m² s⁻¹ and v_p/v_a = 1.405 = r_a/r_p. The period is 2π√(a³/GM) = 119.2 min. Specific energy −24.85 MJ kg⁻¹.

### 5. Core: circular satellite orbits, geostationary radius and orbit decay

*Objective:* Compute speed, period and energies of a circular satellite, find the geostationary radius, and explain why drag speeds a satellite up.

## Circular orbit relations

Gravity supplies the centripetal force: m v²/r = GMm/r², with r the distance from the **centre** (r = R_E + h, h the altitude; NCERT eq. 7.35-7.38). Then

$$ v = √(GM/r),   T = 2π r/v = 2π √(r³/GM) $$

Energies: K = ½ m v² = GMm/(2r), U = −GMm/r = −2K and E = K + U = −K = −GMm/(2r) (NCERT section 7.10; this is the 2017 question in PH-003 derivation D7). The angular momentum is L = m v r = m√(GM r). Note that raising r lowers v but **raises** the total energy (E becomes less negative).

## Numbers: low Earth orbit

At altitude 400 km, r = 6.771 × 10⁶ m: v = 7.673 km s⁻¹, T = 92.4 min (5545 s). Close to the surface NCERT estimates T = 2π√(R/g) ≈ 85 min (with g = 9.8 m s⁻² and R = 6400 km; recomputed 84.6 min).

## The 2024 energy-ratio question

Two satellites of the same mass at altitudes R and 5R have orbital radii r_A = 2R and r_B = 6R. Since K = GMm/2r and U = −GMm/r:

$$ K_A/K_B = r_B/r_A = 3,   U_A/U_B = 3 $$

so both energy ratios are 3 (and E_A/E_B = 3 too). If the question is read as the ratio of K to U for each satellite, K/U = −1/2 for both A and B, since U = −2K; state both readings. The trap is to put the altitude, not r = R + h, into the formulas, which would give 5, not 3.

## Geostationary orbit

A geostationary satellite stays above one point on the equator. It must (i) orbit in the equatorial plane: the point below the satellite must stay on one circle of latitude, and the orbit plane must contain the Earth's centre. Only the equator meets both conditions; (ii) go round in the same direction as the Earth spins; (iii) have period equal to the Earth's rotation period relative to the stars, the **sidereal day**, 86164 s (23 h 56 min 4 s; standard value, verify). Kepler's third law gives

$$ r³ = GM T²/(4π²)  ⇒  r = (3.986 × 10¹⁴ × 86164²/4π²)^(1/3) = 4.2164 × 10⁷ m $$

that is 42,164 km from the Earth's centre. The altitude is 42,164 − 6378 = 35,786 km above the equator (with R_E = 6371 km one gets 35,793 km; the difference is only the radius used). The speed is 2π r/T = 3.075 km s⁻¹. If the 24-hour solar day (86400 s) were used, the radius would be 42,241 km: a small but real error, the usual numerical trap.

## Orbit decay: the drag paradox

Air drag does negative work, so E = −GMm/2r decreases. Since E is more negative for smaller r, **r decreases**. But v = √(GM/r) then **increases**: the satellite speeds up as it loses energy. Reason: dE = −dK. While E falls by an amount ΔE, K rises by |ΔE| and U falls by 2|ΔE|, since U = −2K. Drag lowers the potential energy by twice the energy it removes, and the surplus appears as kinetic energy.

Numbers: lowering a 400 km orbit by 10 km raises the speed by 5.67 m s⁻¹ (dv = −½ v dr/r) and shortens the period by 12.3 s; per kilogram, E falls by 43.5 kJ, K rises by 43.5 kJ and U falls by 87.0 kJ. (The mass and drag rate of a particular station are not in the extracts; only the mechanics is taught.) The drag is strongest near perigee, so decaying elliptical orbits tend to become circular (qualitative; verify with an orbital-mechanics text).

### 6. Core: changing orbits with impulsive burns; the Hohmann transfer; escape and parabolic speeds

*Objective:* Derive the Hohmann transfer speeds and time, and apply them to LEO to GEO and Earth to Mars.

## Impulsive burns

Assume a burn is so brief that the position does not change, only the velocity: v → v + Δv. After the burn the new orbit passes through the same point, and its shape follows from vis-viva (new a) and from L = m r v_⊥ (new e). Where the velocity is perpendicular to r (on a circular orbit or at an apsis) the point stays an apsis after the burn: a prograde burn that takes the speed above the local circular speed makes it the new **perigee** and raises the opposite side of the orbit; a burn against the velocity lowers it. Elsewhere the burn also turns the velocity, and the point is in general not an apsis of the new orbit. Assume the transfers below are coplanar, the two circular orbits have the same centre of force, and the only force is the central attraction.

## Derivation: the Hohmann transfer

Take circular orbits of radii r₁ < r₂ about GM and an ellipse touching both at its apsides: perigee r₁ and apogee r₂, so the semi-major axis is a_t = (r₁ + r₂)/2.

Circular speeds: v_c1 = √(GM/r₁), v_c2 = √(GM/r₂). On the transfer ellipse, vis-viva at r₁:

$$ v_p = √(GM(2/r₁ − 1/a_t)) = √(GM(2/r₁ − 2/(r₁ + r₂))) = √(GM/r₁) √(2r₂/(r₁ + r₂)) $$

First burn: Δv₁ = v_c1 [√(2r₂/(r₁ + r₂)) − 1]. At r₂ the transfer speed is v_a = v_c2 √(2r₁/(r₁ + r₂)) (same algebra), so the second burn

$$ Δv₂ = v_c2 [1 − √(2r₁/(r₁ + r₂))] $$

Both burns are prograde. The transfer time is half the period of the ellipse, by Kepler's third law:

$$ t = π √(a_t³/GM) $$

Checks: if r₂ = r₁ both Δv vanish; for r₂ → ∞, Δv₁ → (√2 − 1) v_c1, the speed increment to escape. Hohmann's transfer is the lowest-energy two-burn transfer between coplanar circular orbits (stated, standard; not proved here; flag).

## Numbers: LEO to GEO

r₁ = 6.771 × 10⁶ m (400 km altitude) and r₂ = 4.2164 × 10⁷ m. a_t = 24,467 km, e_t = 0.7233. Speeds: v_c1 = 7.673 km s⁻¹, v_p = 10.072 km s⁻¹, so Δv₁ = 2.399 km s⁻¹; v_a = 1.617 km s⁻¹ and v_c2 = 3.075 km s⁻¹, so Δv₂ = 1.457 km s⁻¹. Total 3.857 km s⁻¹; time 19,044 s = 5.29 h. (The inclination change from a launch latitude is extra and is not included.)

## Numbers: Earth to Mars (heliocentric, a₁ = 1 AU, a₂ = 1.524 AU)

Transfer a_t = 1.262 AU, t = ½ × 1.262^1.5 yr = 0.709 yr = 259 days. Heliocentric speeds: Earth 29.78, Mars 24.13 km s⁻¹; transfer ellipse speeds 32.73 (perihelion), 21.48 (aphelion) km s⁻¹. Heliocentric increments 2.95 and 2.65 km s⁻¹. Mars's period is 1.881 yr, so it moves 360 × 0.709/1.881 = 135.7° during the transfer; at launch it must lead the Earth by 180° − 135.7° = 44.3°. (These ignore the planets' own gravity wells; patched-conic analysis is beyond the extracts.)

## Escape and parabolic speed (recap)

v_esc = √2 v_c (PH-003 module 8): the extra speed from a circular orbit is (√2 − 1) v_c, e.g. 3.18 km s⁻¹ from the 400 km LEO. A body leaving with v > v_esc keeps v_∞ = √(v² − v_esc²) at infinity (hyperbolic orbit). The speed at escape does not depend on the direction of launch provided the path misses the body.

### 7. Core: Kepler's third law with total mass, binary stars and the Moon

*Objective:* Apply T² = 4π²a³/G(m₁ + m₂), find total and individual masses of a binary, and judge when the Sun-fixed approximation fails.

## Recap with a link

PH-009 module 6 and derivation D5 show that two gravitating bodies orbit their common centre of mass, and that Kepler's third law holds with the **total mass**:

$$ T² = 4π² a³/(G(m₁ + m₂)) $$

where a is the semi-major axis of the **relative** orbit (the distance between the bodies, for a circle), and the relative motion obeys μ r̈ = −G m₁ m₂ r̂/r² with the reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). (Upadhyaya §5.16 and its Example 10 find the Moon's mass this way.) The two bodies move in similar ellipses about the centre of mass, with semi-major axes a₁ = (m₂/M) a and a₂ = (m₁/M) a, a₁ + a₂ = a, and m₁ a₁ = m₂ a₂.

## Solar units

With T in years, a in AU and masses in solar masses:

$$ T² = a³/(m₁ + m₂) $$

The Sun and the Earth (mass ratio about 3 × 10⁻⁶) give T = 1 yr. Jupiter's mass is about 9.5 × 10⁻⁴ of the Sun's, so Jupiter's value of T²/a³ differs from the Earth's by about 0.1 %. Rounded textbook data (a = 5.20, T = 11.86) hide this because their own rounding is about as large (a table below).

## A binary star problem

In a **visual binary** both stars are seen; the angular separation and the distance give a, the period is measured, and then Kepler's law gives the total mass. The ratio of the distances from the centre of mass gives the ratio of masses.

Example (illustrative values): relative orbit a = 16 AU, T = 40 yr. Then M = a³/T² = 4096/1600 = 2.56 solar masses. The centre of mass is 6 AU from star 1 and 10 AU from star 2, so m₁/m₂ = a₂/a₁ = 10/6 = 5/3, giving m₁ = 2.56 × 10/16 = 1.60 and m₂ = 0.96 solar masses. A measured example with standard data (verify): for Sirius A and B, a ≈ 19.8 AU and T ≈ 50.1 yr give M ≈ 3.1 solar masses.

## The Earth and the Moon

Using T = 27.32 days and a = 3.844 × 10⁸ m, the same relation returns a total mass of 6.03 × 10²⁴ kg, in agreement with M_E + m_Moon = 5.97 × 10²⁴ + 0.0735 × 10²⁴ kg to within rounding. The centre of mass lies about 4670 km from the Earth's centre, inside the Earth (PH-009 module 6). Treating the Earth as fixed would give the Moon's period about 0.6 % too long.

## The Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector

For F = −k r̂/r² (k = GMm) define A = p × L − m k r̂. Differentiate with L constant and ṗ = −k r̂/r²:

$$ d(p × L)/dt = ṗ × L = −(k/r²) r̂ × (m r × v) $$

Use the triple-product rule r̂ × (r × v) = r (r̂ · v) − v (r̂ · r) = ṙ **r** − r **v** (here **r** and **v** are vectors, r and ṙ scalars). Then ṗ × L = (k m/r²)(r **v** − ṙ **r**). Also d r̂/dt = (r **v** − ṙ **r**)/r², so ṗ × L = k m d r̂/dt. Hence dA/dt = 0: A is a constant vector in the plane of the orbit, pointing from the centre of force to the perigee. Dot with r: r · (p × L) = L · (r × p) = L², so A r cos φ = L² − m k r, i.e.

$$ r = (L²/mk)/(1 + (A/mk) cos φ) $$

This is the conic with e = A/(m k); a closed ellipse needs |A| < m k, i.e. E < 0 (|A| = m k is the parabola, |A| > m k the hyperbola): **Kepler's first law in two lines**, with the orientation of the line of apsides as the direction of A. A numerical integration confirms that A is constant and |A|/(mk) equals e = √(1 + 2EL²/mk²) to 11 digits. The conservation of A holds only for the inverse-square force, which is why a closed ellipse with fixed axes needs 1/r² (compare module 8 of PH-003 on apsidal precession).

## Kepler's equation (forward link)

To find where the planet is at a given **time** one uses the eccentric anomaly E (a geometrical angle) and the mean anomaly M = 2π (t − t_p)/T, with t_p the time of perihelion passage:

$$ M = E − e sin E,    r = a (1 − e cos E) $$

The equation is transcendental: E cannot be written as a closed formula in M and is found numerically. Example: e = 0.5 and M = 1 rad give E = 1.4987 rad. Its derivation is not in the extracts and is not required for the answers to the four past questions; it is the standard method of orbital tracking.

## Perturbations and precession

The planets also attract each other, so a real orbit is not an exact fixed ellipse: the perihelion slowly turns (precesses). For Mercury general relativity accounts for the ~43 arc-seconds per century that Newtonian perturbations leave unexplained (standard value; verify). The formula quoted by relativity textbooks is Δφ = 6π GM/(c² a (1 − e²)) per orbit; with Mercury's data (a = 5.79 × 10¹⁰ m, e = 0.2056) it gives 5.02 × 10⁻⁷ rad per orbit, and with 415.2 orbits per century 42.98″ per century. Newtonian planetary perturbations, and the turning of the reference axes, account for the rest of the observed total of about 5600″ per century.

## Gravity assist

In the frame of a moving planet (speed U about the Sun) a spacecraft's speed is unchanged by the encounter but its **direction** is turned; back in the Sun's frame this changes the speed. The largest gain, for a head-on approach turned through 180°, is v_out = v_in + 2U, like an elastic collision with a very heavy wall (PH-011). With Jupiter's orbital speed U = 13.06 km s⁻¹ (from the Kepler circular speed at 5.203 AU), a probe arriving at 10 km s⁻¹ could at most leave at 36.1 km s⁻¹; real flybys give less. The energy comes from the planet's orbital energy, which changes imperceptibly.

### Notes: The three laws at a glance

- Each law is a statement about a conic orbit of a body under the Sun's attraction.
- Law 2 needs only a central force; laws 1 and 3 need the inverse-square form.

### Notes: Core equations

- Energy of an elliptical orbit: E = -GMm/(2a); specific energy -GM/(2a).
- Vis-viva: v^2 = GM(2/r - 1/a).
- Apsidal speeds: v_p^2 = (GM/a)(1+e)/(1-e), v_a^2 = (GM/a)(1-e)/(1+e), v_p/v_a = (1+e)/(1-e).
- Specific angular momentum: h^2 = GM a (1 - e^2); period T = 2 pi sqrt(a^3/GM).
- Circular orbit: v = sqrt(GM/r), T = 2 pi sqrt(r^3/GM), K = GMm/2r, U = -2K, E = -K, L = m sqrt(GM r).
- Hohmann: dv1 = v_c1[sqrt(2 r2/(r1+r2)) - 1], dv2 = v_c2[1 - sqrt(2 r1/(r1+r2))], t = pi sqrt(a_t^3/GM), a_t = (r1+r2)/2.
- Two bodies: T^2 = 4 pi^2 a^3/(G(m1+m2)); a1 = m2 a/M, a2 = m1 a/M.

### Notes: Orbit shape from energy

- Circle: e = 0, E = -GMm/2r; ellipse: 0 < e < 1, E < 0; parabola: e = 1, E = 0; hyperbola: e > 1, E > 0 (PH-003 module 6).
- Apsides: closest and farthest points, where the velocity is perpendicular to r; perihelion/aphelion for the Sun, perigee/apogee for the Earth.
- Sidereal day (86164 s) is the Earth's rotation period relative to the stars and is the geostationary period (standard; verify).

### Notes: Derivation roadmap (what to memorise)

- Energy and vis-viva: L and E conservation at two apsides, substitute r_a + r_p = 2a (module 4).
- Kepler to Newton: central (law 2), 1/r^2 (law 1 via Binet), same K (law 3), K = GM (third law of motion) (module 3).
- Hohmann: vis-viva at r_1 and r_2 on the ellipse a_t = (r_1+r_2)/2; time is half the period (module 6).
- LRL vector: d(p x L)/dt = d(m k r-hat)/dt so A = p x L - m k r-hat is constant; r.A gives the conic (module 8).

### Notes: Confusions to separate

- Altitude h versus orbital radius r = R + h: all circular-orbit formulas use r.
- Solar day (24 h) versus sidereal day (23 h 56 min 4 s): the geostationary period is the sidereal day.
- Mean distance in Kepler III means the semi-major axis a, not the time-averaged distance a(1 + e^2/2).
- Total energy decreases under drag while the speed increases: E = -K, so K = -E rises.
- Equal areas (law 2) is angular momentum, not energy; the vis-viva relation is energy.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- Kepler's third law with a fixed Sun is exact only for negligible planetary mass; use the sum of the masses otherwise (PH-009).
- Perturbations by other planets and relativity make orbits precess; general relativity accounts for the ~43 arc-seconds per century of Mercury's perihelion shift that Newtonian perturbations leave unexplained (standard; verify).
- Hohmann is optimal among two-burn transfers between coplanar circular orbits; other transfers can be better for very large radius ratios (standard; verify).

### Core concepts

- Kepler's laws: ellipse with the Sun at a focus; equal areas in equal times; T^2 proportional to a^3.
- Law 2 is angular momentum conservation for any central force; laws 1 and 3 need 1/r^2.
- E = -GMm/2a and vis-viva v^2 = GM(2/r - 1/a) from conservation at the apsides.
- Kepler to Newton: central, inverse-square, universal K, K = GM.
- Hohmann transfer: two prograde burns and half an ellipse.
- Two bodies: Kepler III with total mass; both orbit the centre of mass.
