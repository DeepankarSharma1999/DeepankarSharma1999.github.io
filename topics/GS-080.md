## GS-080: Indo-Islamic architecture

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Indo-Islamic architecture and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### New structural logic

Islam reached India through merchants, holy men and conquerors over some six centuries, but monumental building took off with the Delhi Sultanate in the early thirteenth century. The key technical shift was from trabeate spans to arcuate ones: arches built of wedge-shaped voussoirs with a keystone could carry domes, and squinches or pendentives turned a square room into a base for a circular dome. New building types joined older ones: mosques and Jama Masjids, tombs, dargahs, minars, hammams, formal gardens, madrasas, sarais and kos minars. Walls were thick rubble masonry faced with lime plaster or dressed stone, and bricks also came into use from the early seventeenth century.

### Decoration and regional styles

Religious buildings avoided living forms, so surfaces carried arabesque, geometric patterns, Quranic calligraphy, jalis and floral motifs, including the lotus-bud fringe inside arches. Glazed tiles in blue, turquoise, green and yellow appeared in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries, later joined by tessellation and pietra dura. Arches evolved from squat or pointed forms to trefoil and multifoil shapes from the sixteenth century. Provincial courts adapted the idiom: Gujarat drew openly on temple carving, while Mandu under the Ghauri dynasty founded by Hoshang Shah built airy pavilions such as the Jahaz Mahal between two reservoirs and the buttressed Hindola Mahal, adapting to heat and terrain. Hoshang Shah's tomb combines Afghan robustness with jalis, brackets and toranas.

### Minars, forts and two-way exchange

Minars served the call to prayer while their height projected royal power; the Qutub Minar also became linked with the saint Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki. Forts such as Daulatabad used staggered gates and a labyrinthine approach, and Golconda had concentric walls. Exchange ran both ways. At Vijayanagara, the arched gateways and gate domes of the fortifications are treated by historians as Indo-Islamic features, while mosques and tombs in the city's Muslim quarter resemble Hampi temple mandapas. NCERT therefore describes Indo-Islamic architecture as a style that kept growing through interaction with local practice, and its four categories as aids to understanding rather than fixed boxes.

### Worked example

Q: 'The arch and the dome changed Indian architecture, but India changed the arch and the dome.' Comment. Frame: trabeate vs arcuate technique (voussoir, keystone, squinch, pendentive); new building types; decoration without figures; provincial adaptations (Gujarat temple motifs, Mandu's climate-sensitive pavilions, Bijapur's Gol Gumbaz); reverse borrowing at Vijayanagara. Conclude with synthesis, not substitution.

### Recall

- - **q:** What lets a dome sit on a square chamber?
  - **a:** Squinches or pendentives bridging the corners.
- - **q:** Name the four conventional categories of Indo-Islamic architecture.
  - **a:** Imperial (Delhi Sultanate), Provincial, Mughal and Deccani.
- - **q:** Which Mandu palace stands between two reservoirs like a ship?
  - **a:** The Jahaz Mahal, attributed to Sultan Ghiyasuddin Khilji.

### Common trap

Assuming Indians had no arches before the Sultanate. NCERT says arches were shaped in wood and stone earlier but could not carry heavy superstructures; what was new was the load-bearing true arch with voussoirs and keystone supporting domes.
