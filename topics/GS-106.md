## GS-106: Global circulation, monsoons and ENSO

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Reading a pressure map: isobars, highs, lows and a 'see-saw' number

**Pressure** is the weight of the column of air above a place. Weather maps show it with **isobars**, lines joining places of equal pressure, measured in millibars (mb). An area enclosed by isobars with the lowest value at the centre is a **low**; with the highest value at the centre, a **high** (NCERT kegy209 p2). Air flows from high to low, and the closer the isobars are, the stronger the flow.

Now a useful trick. Meteorologists often track not one place but the **difference** between two places. Suppose place X has 1,012 mb and place Y has 1,008 mb. The difference X minus Y is +4 mb. If next year X is 1,008 and Y is 1,012, the difference is -4 mb. The number has swung from positive to negative, like a see-saw: when one end goes up the other goes down. NCERT uses exactly this idea for the southern oscillation: the pressure difference between Tahiti (NCERT's 'east Pacific'; it lies in the central South Pacific, east of Darwin) and Port Darwin in northern Australia is used to measure the strength of the south-west monsoon winds (kegy104 p7).

Keep two habits. First, always say which place is subtracted from which, because the sign depends on it. Second, remember that a pressure difference drives wind, so a swing in the difference means a swing in the winds. The pressure figures here are made up to practise the idea; they are not data.

### Bridge: Sea-surface temperature, warm pools and 'anomalies'

**Sea-surface temperature (SST)** is the temperature of the top layer of the sea. In the tropics it is high, and the warmest water forms a pool in the west of the Pacific and Indian oceans in a normal year (standard knowledge; verify). Warm water matters for weather because warm sea evaporates more and warms the air above it, which then rises and forms cloud and rain.

An **anomaly** is how much a value differs from its long-term normal for the same place and season. If the normal SST at a place in December is 24 degrees C and this December it is 27 degrees C, the anomaly is +3 degrees C (warmer than normal). If it were 22 degrees C, the anomaly would be -2 degrees C. Climate signals such as El Nino and the Indian Ocean Dipole are described by anomalies: not 'the sea is warm' (it always is in the tropics) but 'the sea is warmer than normal here and cooler than normal there'.

The word **dipole** means two poles. A dipole signal compares two regions: one warmer than normal and the other cooler than normal. When you meet a signal in a question, ask three things: which regions are compared, which one is warmer, and what happens to the rising air above each.

### 1. Orientation: one idea at three scales

*Objective:* Say in one minute how unequal heating gives a global wind system, a seasonal monsoon and year-to-year signals such as El Nino.

## One chain, three scales

The whole lesson rests on one chain: **unequal heating makes unequal pressure, and unequal pressure makes wind** (NCERT kegy209 p1). Air expands when heated and is compressed when cooled, so heating differences create pressure differences, and air moves from high pressure to low. The wind in turn redistributes heat and moisture across the planet.

Apply the chain at three scales.

| Scale | Time | What changes | Example | Module |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Global circulation | Permanent pattern | Belts of high and low pressure with steady winds | Trade winds, westerlies | 2 to 3 |
| Seasonal | Half a year | Belts follow the sun; land and sea heat unequally, so winds reverse | The monsoon | 4 to 5 |
| Year to year | Every few years | Warm and cool patches in the oceans shift where air rises | El Nino, Indian Ocean Dipole | 6 to 7 |

## Three questions to ask of any climate signal

1. **Where** is the heating or cooling that started it?
2. **Which way** does the pressure difference point?
3. **Where** does air rise (cloud and rain) and where does it sink (dry weather)?

`heating contrast -> pressure contrast -> wind -> rising air (rain) and sinking air (dry)`

### 2. Foundation: pressure, the three forces and the belts they make

*Objective:* Recall how pressure changes with height, name the three forces on wind and state which way each hemisphere turns the wind.

## Pressure

**Atmospheric pressure** is the weight of the column of air above a place; the average at sea level is 1,013.2 millibars (mb) (NCERT kegy209 p1). It falls with height. NCERT says the fall is about 1 mb for each 10 m in the lower atmosphere, but not at a constant rate. Its Table 9.1 gives 1,013.25 mb at sea level, 898.76 at 1 km, 540.48 at 5 km and 265.00 at 10 km. Recomputed: the fall over the first kilometre is 1,013.25 - 898.76 = 114.49 mb, about 1 mb per 8.7 m, so the '1 mb per 10 m' rule is rough. The upward pressure force is balanced by gravity, so we do not feel strong upward winds (p1).

## Belts of pressure

Near the equator pressure is low (**equatorial low**); near 30 N and 30 S it is high (**subtropical highs**); near 60 N and 60 S it is low (**subpolar lows**); near the poles it is high (**polar highs**). The belts are not permanent: they oscillate with the apparent movement of the sun (p2-3; module 4).

## The three forces on horizontal wind (p3-4)

1. **Pressure gradient force**: pushes air from high to low, at right angles to the isobars. It is strong where isobars are close together.
2. **Frictional force**: slows wind; greatest at the surface and felt up to 1 to 3 km; least over the sea.
3. **Coriolis force**: the earth's rotation turns the wind to the **right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere**. It is absent at the equator and greatest at the poles; NCERT says it is 'directly proportional to the angle of latitude', which is loose: the force per unit mass is 2 x rotation rate x wind speed x sine of latitude (standard physics). It acts at right angles to the pressure gradient force and the turn is larger when the wind is faster.

Because the Coriolis force is zero at the equator, wind there blows straight across the isobars and a low is filled rather than deepened, which is why tropical cyclones do not form near the equator (p4; Day 22 lesson GS-107).

## Balanced wind and rotation around highs and lows

Above about 2 to 3 km the wind is free of surface friction. If isobars are straight, the pressure gradient force is balanced by the Coriolis force and the wind blows **parallel to the isobars**: the **geostrophic wind** (p4).

| System | Pressure | Northern hemisphere | Southern hemisphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclone | Low | Anticlockwise | Clockwise |
| Anticyclone | High | Clockwise | Anticlockwise |

Over a low the air converges and rises; over a high it subsides and spreads out at the surface (p4). Rising air gives cloud and rain, sinking air gives dry weather. This is why belts of low pressure are wet and belts of high pressure are dry.

`low: converge -> rise -> cloud and rain      high: sink -> diverge -> dry`

For detail see Day 19 lesson GS-104, sections on the forces and on balanced winds.

### 3. Core: the general circulation - belts, cells and surface winds

*Objective:* Describe the five factors, the three cells and the surface winds in each hemisphere, and explain what the winds do to rainfall.

## What shapes the planetary winds

NCERT (kegy209 p4) lists five factors: (i) latitudinal variation of atmospheric heating; (ii) emergence of pressure belts; (iii) migration of the belts with the apparent path of the sun; (iv) the distribution of continents and oceans; (v) the rotation of the earth. The pattern of the planetary winds is the **general circulation of the atmosphere**. It carries heat from lower to higher latitudes (p5).

## The belts (Leong p195)

- **Equatorial low** (within 5 degrees of the equator): intense heating, rising air, wind **convergence**; the doldrums.
- **Subtropical highs** (near 30 N and S): descending air, dry and calm, wind **divergence**; the horse latitudes.
- **Temperate (subpolar) lows** (near 60 N and S): zones of convergence with cyclonic activity.
- **Polar highs** (poles): permanently cold.

## The three cells (NCERT p5)

A **cell** is a circulation going up in one place and down in another.

1. **Hadley cell** (tropics): air rises at the **ITCZ** (Inter Tropical Convergence Zone) because of convection under high insolation, reaches the top of the troposphere (NCERT says up to 14 km; Day 18 lesson GS-103 gives about 18 km at the equator; check), moves towards the poles, and sinks at about 30 N and S to form the subtropical high. Near the ground it returns to the equator as the **easterlies** (trades).
2. **Ferrel cell** (middle latitudes): NCERT words this loosely; the standard reading is that air sinks near 30, flows poleward at the surface as the **westerlies**, rises near 60 and returns equatorward aloft (verify).
3. **Polar cell**: cold air sinks near the poles and flows out as the **polar easterlies**.

## The winds on the ground, per hemisphere

Winds blow from the high belts to the low belts, then the Coriolis force turns them: right in the north, left in the south.

| Wind | From -> to | Direction of origin |
|---|---|---|
| North-east trades | 30 N high -> equatorial low | From the north-east |
| South-east trades | 30 S high -> equatorial low | From the south-east |
| Westerlies, northern | 30 N high -> 60 N low | South-westerlies (Leong) |
| Westerlies, southern | 30 S high -> 60 S low | North-westerlies (standard; verify) |
| Polar easterlies | Poles -> 60 | From the east (north-east in the north, south-east in the south; verify) |

## What the winds do to rainfall (Leong pp195-196)

- **Trade winds** are regular and blow from cooler subtropical latitudes to the warm tropics, so they can hold much moisture. They bring heavy rain to the **east coasts** of the continents within the tropics. On the **west coasts** they blow off the land, so those regions are arid: the Sahara, Kalahari, Atacama and Great Australian deserts. NCERT adds that west coasts of continents in the tropics are bordered by cool waters (kegy213 p5).
- **Westerlies** carry warm water and air to the west coasts of temperate lands. In the southern hemisphere, where there is much ocean from 40 to 60 S, they blow with great force and regularity: the Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties and Stormy Sixties; they bring much precipitation to west coasts.

The general circulation also sets the ocean currents in motion (NCERT kegy209 p4): ocean circulation roughly matches the atmosphere's, and oceans give back energy and water vapour (kegy213 p4). Ocean currents are in Day 23 lesson GS-108.

`Hadley: rise at ITCZ -> poleward aloft -> sink at 30 -> return as trades at surface`

### 4. Core: belts that follow the sun, and the pressure map in July and January

*Objective:* Say how the belts shift in each season, which regions receive westerlies only in winter and how continents change the pressure pattern.

## The belts move with the sun

NCERT (kegy209 pp3, 5) says the pressure belts oscillate with the apparent movement of the sun: in the northern hemisphere they move **south in winter and north in summer**. Leong (p196) gives the size of the shift: when the sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer (June), all the belts move about 5 to 10 degrees **north** of their average position; when the sun is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn (December), they swing 5 to 10 degrees **south**. At the equinoxes (March and September) they sit in their normal position.

## Mediterranean-type regions

The shift decides who gets the westerlies. Regions between about 30 and 40 degrees N and S lie at the edge of the westerly belt. Leong names **California, Iberia, central Chile, southern Africa and south-western Australia** as places that receive the westerlies **only in winter**:

- In the northern winter (December) the belts move south, so the Mediterranean lands of Europe and California come under the westerlies and get their rain in December.
- In June, when it is winter in the southern hemisphere, the Mediterranean-type parts of the southern continents (central Chile, southern Africa, south-western Australia) come under the westerlies and get rain.
- In the summer of each hemisphere the subtropical high moves over them and they are dry.

`northern winter: belts south -> westerlies reach 30-40 N -> winter rain`

## The pressure map changes with the season (Leong p197)

**July** (sun overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, summer in the north):

- The doldrum low swings north (Leong says about 5 degrees).
- Intense heating of Asia, especially the Indian sub-continent, sharply lowers pressure and joins the equatorial low. The south-west United States shows this to a lesser extent.
- The northern subtropical high breaks into cells centred over the Pacific and Atlantic oceans, because the continental interiors are overheated; in the southern hemisphere (winter) the horse latitudes form an almost continuous high-pressure zone.
- The northern subpolar lows move near the Arctic Circle.

**January** (sun overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, summer in the south):

- The doldrums swing south, lagging behind the sun; the low is not well developed because the southern continents are smaller, but pressure falls over Africa and Australia.
- The southern subtropical high is weakened and breaks into cells over the South Pacific and South Atlantic.

## Two books, two numbers

Leong's doldrum shift of about 5 degrees describes an ocean-dominated belt. NCERT's ITCZ in July lies at about 20 to 25 N over the Gangetic plain (kegy104 p4), where a heated continent pulls the low far north. These are different places and not a slip, but do not write one for the other. The Indian side is Day 20 lesson GS-119.

## Why this matters

The same logic explains why a belt lies over a place in one season and not another: seasonal rain, seasonal deserts and, in module 5, the monsoon.

### 5. Core: the monsoon as a seasonal reversal over Asia and northern Australia

*Objective:* Explain the monsoon as a seasonal pressure and wind reversal, and give the direction of the winds in January and July over Asia and northern Australia.

## Breeze and monsoon

Land and sea heat unequally (NCERT kegy209 p6; Leong p197). By day the land warms faster than the sea; air over the land rises, pressure falls, and a **sea breeze** blows from sea to land. By night the land cools faster, pressure over it rises, and a **land breeze** blows to the sea. Leong says **monsoons are the same thing on a bigger scale**: both come from differential heating of land and sea, the breeze in a daily rhythm and the monsoon in a seasonal rhythm. The seasonal change in pressure brings a **complete reversal in wind direction** (Leong p197). NCERT's definition of the monsoon is the same: a climate with a seasonal reversal of wind (Day 20 lesson GS-119). The effect is most pronounced over south-east Asia (kegy209 p5) and is most notable in China, Japan, South-East Asia and the Indian sub-continent, with a slight effect in northern Australia and the Guinea coast of West Africa (Leong p197).

## January: winds blow out of Asia (Leong p198)

- A very intense **high** forms over **Mongolia**, and it exceeds the subtropical high.
- In the southern summer a very intense **low** forms over **northern Australia**, stronger than the equatorial low, so it becomes the focus of the Asian air.
- Winds blow out from Asia. Over **Japan** they are north-westerly; over **south China, the South China Sea and South-East Asia** north-easterly. On crossing the equator they are deflected to the **left**, because they are in the southern hemisphere, and become the **north-west monsoon of Australia**.

## July: winds blow into Asia (Leong p198)

- An intense **low** develops over the Asian land mass, and **high** pressure over northern Australia (winter there).
- South-easterly winds blow from the Australian high towards the equator and on across it. Crossing into the northern hemisphere they are deflected to the **right** and become **south-westerly**.
- Moving north they join the **anticlockwise** circulation around the Asian low and cross Japan and the China coast as the **south-east monsoon**.

`January:  Mongolia HIGH ->(NE over S China)-> equator ->(turn left)-> NW monsoon -> Australia LOW`
`July:     Australia HIGH ->(SE)-> equator ->(turn right)-> SW -> around Asian LOW ->(SE over China coast)`

Check by the left-right rule. A north-easterly blows towards the south-west; after crossing into the southern hemisphere it turns to its left, towards the south-east, so it now comes from the north-west. A south-easterly blows towards the north-west; after crossing into the northern hemisphere it turns to its right, towards the north-east, so it now comes from the south-west. Both agree with Leong.

## NCERT's global view

NCERT (kegy104 p4) records that the older land-sea heating account is only part of the story: a real breakthrough came when the monsoon was studied at the **global** level, taking in the shift of the ITCZ, the jet streams and the Pacific signals (modules 6 and 7). For India the detail is in Day 20 lesson GS-119 and the jets in Day 20 lesson GS-105.

## Two points to keep straight

- The **southern hemisphere** summer monsoon (January) over northern Australia is the mirror of the Asian summer (July).
- A monsoon is not 'a rainy season'; it is a reversal of wind. Rain is the usual result when the reversed wind blows from sea to a heated land.

### 6. Core: El Nino, the southern oscillation and ENSO

*Objective:* Describe El Nino, the southern oscillation and ENSO as the extracts present them, add the standard background with its verify flag, and state the reported effects.

## The ocean as the next actor

NCERT (kegy209 p5) says warming and cooling of the Pacific Ocean is the most important in terms of general atmospheric circulation. Oceans take energy and water vapour from the air and give it back, slowly (kegy209 p4-5; kegy213 p4).

## El Nino as NCERT defines it

- The **warm water of the central Pacific** slowly drifts towards the **South American coast** and replaces the cool **Peruvian current**. Such appearance of warm water off the coast of Peru is the **El Nino** (kegy209 p5).
- The word means 'Child Christ': the current appears around Christmas, a summer month in Peru (kegy104 p5).
- It appears about once in **three to seven years** and brings drought, floods and other extremes (kegy104 p5). NCERT says the water off the Peruvian coast warms by 10 degrees C (the book's figure; verify). Effects on the sea: disturbed equatorial circulation, irregular evaporation, fewer plankton and fish.
- A wording warning: kegy104 describes El Nino as an extension of the warm equatorial current which gets 'replaced' by the cold Peruvian current. That is worded the wrong way round (kegy104 p5 box) against kegy209. Teach kegy209's direction (warm replaces cool; check).

## The southern oscillation

The change in pressure over the Pacific and Australia closely associated with El Nino is the **southern oscillation**; El Nino plus southern oscillation is **ENSO** (kegy209 p5). Its strength can be measured by the **pressure difference between Tahiti (French Polynesia; NCERT says 'east Pacific', though it lies in the central South Pacific east of Darwin; NCERT rounds it to 20 S, 140 W and the usual value is about 17.5 S, 149.5 W; verify) and Port Darwin (northern Australia, 12 degrees 30 minutes S, 131 E)** (kegy104 p7). Think of the see-saw in bridge GS-106-B1: when pressure rises at one end it falls at the other. The positions are NCERT's rounded values (verify).

## The standard background (not in the extracts; verify)

In a normal year the trade winds blow from east to west along the equatorial Pacific, piling warm water in the west near Indonesia and northern Australia, with cool water rising off Peru. Air rises over the warm west and sinks over the cool east: the **Walker circulation**. In an El Nino the trades weaken, warm water spreads east, the rising air moves east, and the pressure see-saw swings. **La Nina** is the opposite phase, with a cooler than normal east Pacific. These are standard descriptions, not NCERT's.

`normal:   warm pool west, rising air west, cool Peru   |   El Nino: warm water east, rising air shifts east`

## Reported effects (kegy209 p5)

When ENSO is strong, large weather variations occur around the world: the arid west coast of South America gets **heavy rain**; **drought** in **Australia** and sometimes in **India**; **floods in China**. The phenomenon is closely monitored and used for long-range forecasting. In India the 1990-91 El Nino delayed the south-west monsoon over most parts by five to twelve days (kegy104 p5). Note 'sometimes': NCERT does not say every El Nino brings Indian drought.

## Why a Pacific event reaches India

The air that rises over the Pacific warm pool and sinks elsewhere is part of one connected global circulation; shifting the rising air in the Pacific shifts the sinking and rising air in other places. This is called a teleconnection (standard term; verify). The India-specific consequences are in Day 20 lesson GS-119.

### 7. Core: Indian Ocean signals, Ocean Mean Temperature and monsoon forecasting

*Objective:* Define the Indian Ocean Dipole and Ocean Mean Temperature with their verify flags, and say how signals are used and misused in forecasting.

## What the extracts give

The extracts on the Indian Ocean are short. NCERT kegy213 (p5) says that in regions of pronounced monsoonal flow the **monsoon winds influence the movement of ocean currents**, and that ocean circulation roughly follows the atmosphere's. NCERT kegy104 (pp5, 7) says El Nino is used in India for forecasting long-range monsoon rainfall, and that the Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) can forecast the monsoon's behaviour on the basis of **16 indicators** (the method has changed over time; treat 16 as the book's figure; verify). The **Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)** and the **Ocean Mean Temperature (OMT)** are not in the extracts, yet both were asked in official Prelims papers, so they are taught here as standard knowledge with a verify flag. Day 20 lesson GS-119 gives the same definitions (sections 'Core: the upper-air and ocean account - jet streams, El Nino and the southern oscillation' for the IOD and 'Exam: the three official questions, answer frames and traps' for OMT).

## Indian Ocean Dipole (standard knowledge; verify)

A dipole compares two regions (bridge GS-106-B2). The IOD is the **difference in sea-surface temperature between the western tropical Indian Ocean (Arabian Sea and east African side) and the eastern tropical Indian Ocean (south of Indonesia)**.

- **Positive phase**: the west is warmer than normal and the east cooler. This tends to favour monsoon rain over India.
- **Negative phase**: the east is warmer and the west cooler. This tends to work against it.
- A positive IOD can partly offset the drying effect of an El Nino (reported cases such as 1997; verify). So the IOD can influence an El Nino's impact on the monsoon.

The common error is to place one pole of the dipole in the **Pacific**. Both poles of the IOD are in the Indian Ocean.

## Ocean Mean Temperature (standard knowledge; verify)

OMT is the **average temperature from the surface down to the 26 degrees C isotherm** (the line joining points at 26 degrees C), which usually lies about 50-100 m deep. January to March OMT was reported as a predictor of above- or below-mean monsoon rain. The mean depth reported for the south-western Indian Ocean in January to March is about 59 m, not the 129 m printed in the 2020 question (reported; the source is unchecked; verify). The analysis points to '2 only'; not an official key.

## The family of signals

| Signal | Regions compared | Basis | Source in this lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| El Nino / southern oscillation (ENSO) | Central-east Pacific and Australia | Warm water off Peru; Tahiti-Darwin pressure | NCERT kegy209, kegy104 |
| Indian Ocean Dipole | West and east tropical Indian Ocean | Sea-surface temperature difference | Standard; verify |
| Ocean Mean Temperature | Upper ocean to the 26 degrees C isotherm | Layer-average temperature | Standard; verify |
| Jet-stream shift | Westerly to easterly jet over India | Withdrawal of westerly jet (Day 20 lesson GS-105) | NCERT kegy104 p5 |
| IMD indicators | Several land, ocean and air variables | 16 indicators (book's figure) | NCERT kegy104 p7 |

## How to use signals without over-claiming

- A signal changes the **odds**; it does not fix the result. NCERT says drought occurs 'sometimes' in India in strong ENSO years.
- Signals combine. An El Nino with a positive IOD can look different from an El Nino alone.
- A forecast is a statement about seasonal rainfall relative to a long-term mean; it does not say where or when within the season.

`signal in ocean -> shifts where air rises and sinks -> monsoon stronger or weaker (a tendency, not a certainty)`

### 8. Master: the Asian monsoon climate, and the Purvaiya wind

*Objective:* List the characteristics of the monsoon climate that support dense farming populations and explain the Purvaiya wind of Bhojpur.

## The 2017 Mains question: characteristics of the monsoon climate

The question says the monsoon climate feeds more than 50 per cent of the world population in Monsoon Asia. No extract gives the population share, so no figure is repeated here (verify before quoting any). The characteristics can be built from the extracts.

1. **Seasonal reversal of wind** caused by differential heating of land and sea (Leong p197; module 5).
2. **Rain concentrated in one warm season.** For India most parts get rainfall in June to September (NCERT kegy104 p3); the rainy season and the growing season coincide (standard; verify).
3. **Warmth through the year.** Except in the Himalayas, all parts of India have temperatures above the threshold level to grow crops or plants throughout the year (kegy104 p12). Warmth plus summer water together suit water-demanding crops such as rice (standard; verify).
4. **Large regional variation in amount.** Cherrapunji and Mawsynram in the Khasi Hills receive over 1,080 cm in a year, while Jaisalmer rarely gets more than 9 cm (kegy104 p3). Regional variation in the monsoon helps grow various types of crops (p12).
5. **Variability and uncertainty.** Onset, burst, break and failure vary from year to year; variability brings droughts or floods every year in some part of India (p12); El Nino and other signals add to it (modules 6 and 7). A sudden burst causes soil erosion (p12).
6. **Dependence of farming.** NCERT (p12) says about 64 per cent of Indian people depend on agriculture, which is based on the south-west monsoon (the book's figure; dated; verify).
7. **Relief controls the pattern.** The Himalayas trap the monsoon winds and force them to shed moisture within the subcontinent (p3); the windward Western Ghats and Assam are wet and the leeward plateau is dry (pp3-4).
8. **Culture and calendar.** The traditional six two-month seasons, including Varsha (July-August), and the variety of food, clothes and house types reflect regional climate (pp11-12).

Which countries? Leong (p197) names China, Japan, South-East Asia and the Indian sub-continent as the places where the monsoon is most notably developed.

## The 2023 Mains question: Purvaiya in Bhojpur

'Purv' means east, so Purvaiya is an easterly wind. The south-west monsoon is the same system bent by relief and the thermal low (full account in Day 20 lesson GS-119, modules 6 and 10).

- The Bay of Bengal branch is deflected by the Arakan Hills, so it enters West Bengal and Bangladesh from the **south and south-east** instead of the south-west. Under the influence of the Himalayas and the thermal low in the north-west it splits; one arm moves **westward along the Ganga plains** (kegy104 p11).
- NCERT p8 says the winds are southwesterly on the west coast and along West Bengal and Bangladesh but **easterly or south-easterly over north Bengal and Bihar**, as the ITCZ's trough extends from the Thar desert to Patna and the Chotanagpur plateau.
- Bhojpur (the Bhojpuri-speaking area of western Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh; verify on a map) lies on that path, so its rain-bearing wind comes from the east.
- **Cultural ethos** is not in the extracts. Commonly cited, to be verified: seasonal songs of the rains and of migrant men's separation, and the central place of the first rain for paddy planting. Write them as examples, not as figures.

### 9. Exam: the four official questions, answer frames and traps

*Objective:* Analyse the 2017 and 2020 Prelims items, plan the 2017 and 2023 Mains answers and avoid the common traps.

## Prelims 2017: the Indian Ocean Dipole

The question gives two statements. 1: the IOD is a difference in sea-surface temperature between the tropical **western Indian Ocean** and the tropical **eastern Pacific**. 2: the IOD can influence an El Nino's impact on the monsoon. By the definition in module 7 (standard; verify) statement 1 puts one pole in the wrong ocean, so it does not match. Statement 2 matches the reported role of a positive IOD offsetting an El Nino. The analysis therefore points to '2 only'. This is analysis, not an official key, and the IOD is not in the NCERT extracts.

## Prelims 2020: Ocean Mean Temperature

Statement 1: OMT is measured down to the 26 degrees C isotherm, which is stated as 129 m in the south-western Indian Ocean in January to March. Statement 2: OMT collected in January to March can be used to assess whether monsoon rainfall will be less or more than a long-term mean. Statement 2 is consistent with the reported use of OMT (module 7; verify). Statement 1 combines a correct idea (the 26 degrees C isotherm) with a depth that conflicts with the reported mean of about 59 m (module 7; reported, unchecked; verify). The analysis therefore points to '2 only', as in Day 20 lesson GS-119. This is analysis, not an official key; check the IMD or MoES source before relying on the figure.

## Mains 2017 and 2023

For the 2017 question (15 marks), use the eight characteristics in module 8, group them under physical (wind reversal, relief), climatic (seasonal rain, warmth), agricultural (crops, dependence) and risk (variability, ENSO), and avoid any population figure. For the 2023 question (10 marks), put the mechanism first (Bay of Bengal branch, westward arm, easterly winds over Bihar) and then two examples of cultural influence flagged as examples.

## Traps

- Placing a pole of the IOD in the Pacific.
- Reversing El Nino: warm water replaces the cool Peruvian current, not the opposite (kegy209).
- Saying El Nino always causes an Indian drought; NCERT says 'sometimes'.
- Turning the equator-crossing wind the wrong way (right in the north, left in the south).
- Quoting a precise depth, temperature or share of population with no source.
- Naming a wind by where it goes instead of where it comes from.

## Elimination checklist for ENSO, IOD and monsoon statements

1. **Place test**: are the regions in the right ocean?
2. **Direction test**: warm replaces cool or the reverse; which way does Coriolis turn?
3. **Absolute-word test**: 'always', 'only', 'all' are red flags.
4. **Source test**: is the precise figure in a source you have read? If not, flag it and do not rest the answer on it.
5. **Scope test**: is the question about the Indian monsoon mechanism (Day 20 lesson GS-119) or the global frame (this lesson)?

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Atmospheric pressure: weight of the air column above a place; average sea-level value 1,013.2 mb (NCERT kegy209 p1).
- Isobar: a line joining places of equal pressure; pressure gradient force acts at right angles to isobars (p2-4).
- Geostrophic wind: wind parallel to straight isobars above the friction layer, where the pressure gradient force is balanced by the Coriolis force (p4).
- General circulation of the atmosphere: the pattern of planetary winds, shaped by latitudinal heating, pressure belts, their migration with the sun, continents and oceans, and the earth's rotation (p4).
- ITCZ: Inter Tropical Convergence Zone, low pressure where tropical winds converge and air rises; it oscillates with the sun (kegy209 p5, kegy104 p4).
- Monsoon: a climate with seasonal reversal of wind, caused by seasonal change in pressure from differential heating of land and sea (NCERT, Leong p197).
- El Nino: appearance of warm water off Peru, replacing the cool Peruvian current (kegy209 p5); southern oscillation: the pressure change over the Pacific and Australia; ENSO: the two together.
- Indian Ocean Dipole: west-minus-east sea-surface temperature difference in the tropical Indian Ocean (standard; verify).
- Ocean Mean Temperature: layer-average temperature down to the 26 degrees C isotherm (standard; verify).
- Anomaly: a value minus its long-term normal (bridge GS-106-B2).

### Notes: Circulation cells and surface winds

- Directions of origin are stated per hemisphere; the southern westerly and polar-wind directions are standard knowledge (verify).

### Notes: The year in pressure and wind

- March and September: belts in normal position (Leong p196).
- June: sun overhead at the Tropic of Cancer; belts 5-10 degrees north; Asian low deepens; northern subtropical high breaks into ocean cells; southern winter highs; southern Mediterranean-type regions get westerly rain.
- July: Asian low, northern Australian high; south-east winds cross the equator, turn right, become south-west, circle the Asian low and cross the China coast as the south-east monsoon.
- December: sun overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn; belts 5-10 degrees south; northern Mediterranean lands and California get westerly rain.
- January: Mongolian high, northern Australian low; north-east winds cross the equator, turn left, become the north-west monsoon of Australia.
- Every three to seven years: an El Nino (NCERT kegy104 p5); the 1990-91 event delayed the Indian onset by five to twelve days.

### Notes: January and July over Asia and northern Australia

- Leong p198.

### Notes: ENSO: reported effects and cause chain

- Pacific trades weaken and warm water drifts east (standard background; verify) -> warm water off Peru replaces the cool current (NCERT kegy209).
- Pressure see-saw between Tahiti and Port Darwin changes -> the southern oscillation (kegy104 p7).
- Strong ENSO: heavy rain on the arid west coast of South America; drought in Australia and sometimes India; floods in China (kegy209 p5).
- Sea effects: disturbed equatorial circulation and evaporation, less plankton and fish (kegy104 p5).
- India: 1990-91 delay of five to twelve days in the south-west monsoon onset; El Nino used in long-range forecasts.
- Tendency, not law: NCERT says 'sometimes' for India.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- El Nino versus southern oscillation: the ocean warming versus the pressure see-saw; together ENSO.
- El Nino versus La Nina: warm versus cool east Pacific (La Nina is standard knowledge; verify).
- IOD versus ENSO: Indian Ocean west-east difference versus Pacific warming and pressure change.
- Monsoon versus sea breeze: seasonal reversal versus daily reversal.
- Doldrums versus horse latitudes: equatorial low with convergence versus subtropical high with descending air.
- Geostrophic wind versus surface wind: balanced and parallel to isobars versus friction-affected and crossing isobars.
- North-east trades versus north-east monsoon: a permanent planetary wind versus a seasonal winter wind of the Indian subcontinent.
- Hadley, Ferrel and polar cell: tropical, middle-latitude and polar circulations.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- Winds that cross the equator reverse their Coriolis turn: left-turn in the south, right-turn in the north.
- The Coriolis force is zero at the equator, so it cannot turn winds there; tropical cyclones do not form there (kegy209 p4).
- Not every El Nino brings an Indian drought (NCERT: 'sometimes').
- Westerlies are year-round in the southern oceans (40-60 S) but only winter winds for Mediterranean-type regions.
- The Doldrum shift (about 5 degrees) and the monsoon trough (20-25 N over India) are different statements for different settings.
- In July the northern subtropical high exists only as ocean cells (Leong p197).

### Notes: Source notes and slips

- kegy104 p5 describes El Nino as an extension of the warm equatorial current replaced by the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current, which is worded the wrong way round (same label as Day 20 lesson GS-119); kegy209 p5 says warm water replaces the cool Peruvian current. The latter direction is taught (check).
- NCERT's Tahiti (roughly 20 S, 140 W) and Darwin (12 degrees 30 minutes S, 131 E) positions are rounded; the usual Tahiti value is about 17.5 S, 149.5 W (verify), and NCERT's 'east Pacific' for Tahiti is loose: it lies in the central South Pacific, east of Darwin.
- NCERT says the Coriolis force is directly proportional to the angle of latitude, which is loose: the force per unit mass is 2 x rotation rate x wind speed x sine of latitude, zero at the equator and greatest at the poles (standard physics).
- NCERT kegy209 p5 gives the top of the Hadley cell as up to 14 km; Day 18 lesson GS-103 gives about 18 km at the equator (check).
- NCERT's '1 mb per 10 m' is rough: Table 9.1 gives 114.49 mb in the first km (8.73 m per mb) (recomputed).
- Leong p195 spells out the southern westerlies without the direction; north-westerly is the standard result of a left turn (verify).
- The 2020 question's 129 m depth for the 26 degrees C isotherm conflicts with the reported mean of about 59 m for the south-western Indian Ocean in January to March (the isotherm is usually about 50-100 m deep). Both figures are reported, not checked against IMD or MoES material (verify). The analysis points to '2 only', as in Day 20 lesson GS-119; not an official key.
- The population share of Monsoon Asia in the 2017 question is not in any extract and is not stated.

### Notes: Recomputed numbers

- Table 9.1 pressure drops: 0 to 1 km: 114.49 mb (114.49 per km); 1 to 5 km: 358.28 mb (89.57 per km); 5 to 10 km: 275.48 mb (55.10 per km). The rate falls with height, as NCERT says.
- Rule of thumb 1 mb per 10 m: 500 m gives a drop of 50 mb; 1,010 - 50 = 960 mb.
- Direction check for the January wind: north-easterly (blowing towards the south-west), turned left, becomes a north-westerly. July: south-easterly (towards the north-west), turned right, becomes a south-westerly.

### Core concepts

- Unequal heating makes pressure differences that drive wind; air rises at lows and sinks at highs.
- Three cells (Hadley, Ferrel, polar) and the surface winds they make, turned right in the north and left in the south.
- Pressure and wind belts shift 5-10 degrees with the sun; Mediterranean-type regions get westerlies only in winter.
- The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind: January out of Asia, July into Asia, with a turn after the equator.
- ENSO links Pacific warming to a pressure see-saw and to floods and droughts elsewhere.
- The IOD is a west-east temperature difference within the Indian Ocean.

### Practice

**Q.** Which way does the Coriolis force turn wind in each hemisphere?

**A.** To the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern.

**Q.** Name the three circulation cells.

**A.** Hadley, Ferrel and polar.

**Q.** What is the ITCZ?

**A.** The Inter Tropical Convergence Zone, a low-pressure zone where tropical winds converge and air rises.

**Q.** What is the southern oscillation?

**A.** The change in pressure over the Pacific and Australia associated with El Nino.

**Q.** Explain why trade-wind deserts lie on the west coasts of continents.

**A.** Trade winds blow from east to west and are offshore on west coasts; they carry no moisture from the sea there and the coasts are bordered by cool water, so they are arid (Leong, NCERT).

**Q.** Why is a monsoon described as a large-scale breeze?

**A.** Both come from differential heating of land and sea, the breeze daily and the monsoon seasonally, with the wind reversing as pressure reverses.

**Q.** Why does a north-easterly wind from Asia become a north-westerly over northern Australia?

**A.** It crosses into the southern hemisphere, where the Coriolis force turns it to the left.

**Q.** Why can El Nino change rainfall in places far from the Pacific?

**A.** It shifts where air rises and sinks over the Pacific, and the circulation is connected globally, so weather elsewhere changes (teleconnection; verify).

**Q.** A student draws the July wind from Australia crossing the equator and turning left. Correct the drawing and explain.

**A.** It must turn right because it has entered the northern hemisphere, becoming south-westerly and then circling the Asian low anticlockwise.
