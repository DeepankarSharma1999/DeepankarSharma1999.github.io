## GS-146: Women and gender equality

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Women and gender equality and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why gender inequality is social

Because men and women differ physically, inequality between them is often assumed to be natural. NCERT rejects this: biology cannot explain why few women hold public power or why women receive little or no family property. The strongest evidence comes from societies that differ from the usual pattern. Matrilineal communities such as the Nairs of Kerala in the past and the Khasis of Meghalaya today functioned for centuries with women as inheritors, and women have been successful farmers and traders in many African societies. Gender is therefore a form of social inequality and exclusion comparable to caste and class, with its own features. Since the nineteenth-century reformers, the focus has shifted from practices like sati to development's gendered effects and, more recently, to the declining child sex ratio.

### Sex ratio and literacy as indicators

Nature produces slightly more male births, but because girls resist infant disease better and women outlive men, sex ratios in most contexts favour females. India has bucked this for over a century: 972 in 1901, 927 in 1991 and 943 in 2011. The child sex ratio is more alarming, dropping from 976 in 1961 to 919 in 2011 and falling below the overall ratio for the first time in 2001. Since maternal mortality has been declining, NCERT attributes the drop to neglect of girl infants, sex-selective abortion and infanticide. Literacy shows both progress and gaps: in 2011 the male-female gap was 16.3 points, female literacy grew faster than male literacy, and SC and ST women had still lower rates, which tends to reproduce disadvantage across generations.

### Law, attitudes and the unpaid-work burden

Prosperous families wanting fewer children may also seek to choose the child's sex, which ultrasound made possible. The law banning this, in force since 1996 and tightened in 2003, imposes fines and imprisonment, but NCERT argues that lasting solutions depend more on how social attitudes evolve; the 'Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao' programme is presented as one policy response. Inequality persists into old age. MoSPI's Elderly in India 2021 reports that the share of elderly women doing unpaid domestic work for household members is more than double that of elderly men, and that women aged 60+ spend about 245 minutes a day on it versus 112 for men. Census 2011 literacy among elderly women (28%) was less than half that of elderly men (59%).
