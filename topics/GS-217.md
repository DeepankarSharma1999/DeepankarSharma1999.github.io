## GS-217: Nominal versus real growth and deflators

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Basic macro and monetary

*Goal:* Explain Nominal versus real growth and deflators and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Separating price from volume

When GDP for two years is measured at each year's own prices, a rise could come from producing more, from charging more, or both. To compare across time or across countries, economists revalue output at a fixed set of prices from a chosen base year. That figure is real GDP; any movement in it must reflect quantities. Nominal GDP, by contrast, simply multiplies current output by current prices. Because the quantities are identical in the nominal and real calculations for a given year, the gap between the two numbers can only come from prices having moved since the base year. This is why NCERT says comparisons over time or across countries should rely on real rather than current-price GDP.

### The GDP deflator versus CPI

Dividing nominal GDP by real GDP gives the GDP deflator, a price index for the whole of domestic production; expressed as a percentage it shows how much the price level of produced goods has changed relative to the base year. It is not the same as the consumer price index. CPI tracks a fixed basket bought by a representative consumer, so it includes imported items and ignores goods consumers do not buy, such as machinery. The deflator covers every good and service produced at home, leaves out imports, and its implicit weights shift with the pattern of production each year. So a rise in the price of imported goods enters CPI directly but not the deflator.

### Index-number basics behind any deflator

Any price index condenses many different price changes into one number measured against a base period fixed at 100. A simple aggregative index adds up prices but treats every item as equally important, which is misleading when food takes a much bigger share of spending than other items. Weighted indices fix this. Laspeyres' formula prices the base-period basket at current prices, answering how much the old basket now costs; Paasche's formula uses current-period quantities. Because the baskets differ, the two methods give different readings from the same price data. NCERT adds that base-period weights are usually preferred because recomputing weights every year is inconvenient, so the choice of base and weights matters to the reading.

### Worked example

Base year: 200 units at Rs 5 = Rs 1,000. Next year: 220 units at Rs 6. Nominal GDP = 220 × 6 = Rs 1,320; real GDP at base prices = 220 × 5 = Rs 1,100. Deflator = 1,320 ÷ 1,100 × 100 = 120. Nominal growth = 32%, real growth = 10%, and 1.32 ÷ 1.10 = 1.20 shows prices rose 20%.

### Recall

- - **q:** Does nominal GDP correct for price changes?
  - **a:** No. Only real GDP, valued at constant base-year prices, removes the price effect.
- - **q:** Give two ways the GDP deflator differs from CPI.
  - **a:** It covers all domestically produced goods (not just a consumer basket) and excludes imported goods; its weights also change with production while CPI weights are fixed.
- - **q:** Which quantities weight a Laspeyres price index?
  - **a:** Base-period quantities.

### Common trap

Assuming a doubling of nominal GDP means output doubled: NCERT notes the same result could arise purely from all prices doubling with unchanged production.
