### Bridge: The Ottoman sultan, the caliph and why Indian Muslims cared

The **Ottoman Empire** was a large Muslim-ruled empire centred on Turkey. Its ruler, the **sultan**, also held the title **caliph** (Khalifa), claimed as the religious head of Sunni Muslims worldwide. The Arabic word *khilafat* means the office of the caliph. Spectrum says Indian Muslims, like Muslims across the world, regarded the sultan of Turkey as their spiritual leader.

In the First World War Turkey fought on the side of Germany against Britain. When the war ended Britain, as a victor, was in a position to dismember the empire and to decide the fate of the sultan. Indian Muslims asked for three things (NCERT Class XII): that the Khalifa keep control of the Muslim sacred places of the former empire; that the *jazirat-ul-Arab* (Arabia, Syria, Iraq, Palestine) remain under Muslim sovereignty; and that the Khalifa be left enough territory to defend the faith. These sacred places were not in India, which is why Spectrum says the Khilafat issue was not directly linked to Indian politics.

The **Treaty of Sevres** (1920) was the peace treaty with Turkey; Spectrum says it completely dismembered Turkey (the date is a slip, see module 4). **Hijrat** means migration; in 1920 some Muslims left for Afghanistan in protest (standard knowledge; verify).

Keep the point straight: the Khilafat demand was religious and foreign in origin, but it became part of an Indian political movement.

### Bridge: Words of the movement: boycott, non-cooperation, swaraj, khadi

**Boycott** means refusing to use or deal with something: government schools, courts, councils, foreign cloth. **Non-cooperation** is the wider method of refusing to help the government or use its institutions, which breaks no law by itself. **Civil disobedience** is the deliberate breaking of a particular law, openly and without violence; GS-045-B1 gives the full difference. Non-Cooperation planned to move to civil disobedience (non-payment of taxes) only in a second stage (Spectrum; NCERT Class XII lists not paying taxes in its summary of the call).

**Swaraj** means self-rule. In 1920 Gandhi promised it within a year if the programme were fully carried out (Spectrum and NCERT). The Congress creed changed at Nagpur from self-government by constitutional means to swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means.

**Khadi** is hand-spun, hand-woven cloth; the **charkha** is the spinning wheel, a symbol of self-reliance. **Picketing** means standing outside a shop (foreign cloth, liquor) to persuade customers not to enter. A **panchayat** is a village council; the movement asked people to settle disputes in panchayats instead of government courts. **National schools and colleges** were institutions outside government control. **Constructive work** means building alternatives (khadi, national schools, temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity, removal of untouchability), as against the negative side of boycott.

Two test questions: does the act break a law (civil disobedience) or only refuse help (non-cooperation)? Is it destruction (boycott) or building (constructive work)?

### 1. Orientation: from Punjab wrongs to Chauri Chaura on one page

*Objective:* Place the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation movements on one timeline with their three demands.

### 2. Foundation: why 1919 and 1920 were ripe

*Objective:* Explain the four pressures of 1919-20 and the ideas Gandhi brought to them.

## Four pressures

Spectrum opens the chapter with the forces that belied the hope of British generosity after the war.

1. **Economic hardship.** Post-war prices rose, the output of Indian industry fell and rents and taxes were heavier. Almost every section had suffered, which strengthened anti-British feeling.
2. **Punjab.** The Rowlatt Act, martial law in Punjab and Jallianwala Bagh exposed what Spectrum calls the brutal face of foreign rule. The Hunter Committee was seen as an eyewash; Spectrum says the House of Lords endorsed Dyer's action and a Morning Post fund collected about 30,000 pounds for him (GS-042 teaches about 26,000 and notes the difference between chapters).
3. **The Montford disappointment.** Spectrum says the dyarchy scheme failed to satisfy the demand for self-government (GS-050 module 3).
4. **A ground for Hindu-Muslim action.** The Lucknow Pact of 1916 had stimulated Congress-League cooperation, the Rowlatt agitation had brought Hindus, Muslims and other sections together, and radical nationalist Muslims such as Muhammad Ali, Abul Kalam Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan and Hasan Imam had become more influential in the League than the conservative Aligarh group (GS-040 module 7). The younger men wanted militant nationalism and active participation.

In this atmosphere, Spectrum says, the Khilafat issue emerged, around which the Non-Cooperation Movement developed.

## What Gandhi brought

- **Satyagraha and non-violence** as political method, tested in Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda and Rowlatt (GS-041, GS-042). Spectrum's summary labels Kheda 'first non-cooperation'; GS-041-B2 explains how to read such 'first' labels.
- **Swaraj** as the aim, which NCERT lets peasants and workers read in their own ways (module 6).
- **A theory of refusal.** NCERT Class XII quotes Louis Fischer: non-cooperation was negative enough to be peaceful but positive enough to be effective, entailing denial, renunciation and self-discipline, a training for self-rule.
- **Gandhi's 1909 book Hind Swaraj** (standard; verify) argued that swaraj meant self-rule and moral self-discipline, not just replacing British rulers; the extracts do not cover it, so treat the link as background only.

## The leadership problem

Tilak was uneasy about an alliance with Muslim leaders over a religious issue and was sceptical of satyagraha (Spectrum). The whole plan therefore depended on persuading the Congress, which module 4 follows.

### 3. Core: the Khilafat issue and the Congress decision

*Objective:* Narrate the Khilafat agitation from 1919 to the Congress's decision to support non-cooperation.

## The grievance

Bridge B1 gave the background: Turkey sided with Germany, was dismembered after the war and the Khalifa was removed from power. Spectrum says this incensed Muslims worldwide, and in India they demanded that the Khalifa's control over Muslim sacred places be kept and that he be left sufficient territory.

## The Khilafat Committee and conference

In early 1919 a **Khilafat Committee** was formed, led by the Ali brothers (Shaukat Ali and Muhammad Ali), Maulana Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan and Hasrat Mohani (Spectrum; the exact place and date of founding, standard Bombay in 1919, verify). For some time the leaders limited themselves to meetings, petitions and deputations. Then a militant trend emerged. At the **All India Khilafat Conference in Delhi in November 1919** a call was made to boycott British goods, and the leaders said that unless the peace terms favoured Turkey they would stop all cooperation. Spectrum says Gandhi was president of the All India Khilafat Committee; standard accounts say he presided over the All India Khilafat Conference at Delhi in November 1919, while the Central Khilafat Committee was headed by Seth Mian Muhammad Chotani (verify). Either way, Gandhi saw in the issue a platform for mass, united non-cooperation. The notion of a 'Khilafat Day' on 17 October 1919 is standard, not in the extracts (verify).

## Why the Congress hesitated

Support from the Congress was essential. Gandhi favoured satyagraha and non-cooperation on the Khilafat question, but the Congress was divided. **Tilak** opposed an alliance over a religious issue and doubted satyagraha. Other parts of the programme, such as the boycott of councils, met opposition too. Spectrum, citing Ravinder Kumar, says Gandhi made a concerted effort to convince Tilak. He later won the Congress's approval.

## Why the Congress agreed

Spectrum gives three reasons:

1. A **golden opportunity to cement Hindu-Muslim unity** and bring the Muslim masses in; different sections (Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, peasants, artisans, capitalists, tribals, women, students) could enter the movement by fighting for their own rights.
2. The Congress was **losing faith in constitutional struggle** after the Punjab incidents and the partisan Hunter report.
3. It knew the **masses were eager** to express discontent.

The Muslim League also decided to give full support to the Congress and its agitation on political questions.

## A distinction to hold

The Khilafat leaders began non-cooperation on the Khilafat wrong; the Congress adopted non-cooperation on three grounds, Khilafat, Punjab and swaraj. The 'adoption by the Congress of the movement initiated earlier by the Khilafat Committee' (Spectrum) is why the movement is called Khilafat-Non-Cooperation.

### 4. Core: from February to December 1920, the launch, Calcutta and Nagpur

*Objective:* Follow the 1920 sequence to the Nagpur session and state what Nagpur changed.

## The road, month by month (Spectrum unless marked)

- **February 1920.** A joint Hindu-Muslim deputation to the Viceroy failed. Gandhi announced that Punjab wrongs and constitutional advance had been overshadowed by the Khilafat question, and that he would lead non-cooperation if the peace terms failed to satisfy Indian Muslims.
- **May 1920.** Spectrum says the Treaty of Sevres was signed in May and completely dismembered Turkey. **Slip to flag:** the terms were published in May but the treaty was signed on 10 August 1920 (standard; verify). NCERT Class VIII also puts the harsh treaty in 1920.
- **June 1920.** An all-party conference at Allahabad approved a programme of boycott of schools, colleges and law courts and asked Gandhi to lead it.
- **The launch.** Spectrum says that on **31 August 1920** the Khilafat Committee began a campaign of non-cooperation and the movement was formally launched, noting that Tilak had died on 1 August 1920. Standard books date the launch to **1 August 1920**, the day Gandhi returned his Kaiser-i-Hind medal (the 1919 versus 1920 slip is in GS-042). Teach 1 August as the usual date and know that Spectrum prints 31 August (verify).
- **September 1920, Calcutta special session.** The Congress approved a non-cooperation programme until the Punjab and Khilafat wrongs were removed and swaraj established. The presiding officer was Lala Lajpat Rai and the dates were about 4 to 9 September (standard; verify). Some leaders, notably C. R. Das, disliked a council boycott but bowed to discipline. The Congress boycotted the November 1920 elections, and the majority of voters stayed away.
- **December 1920, Nagpur session.** The programme was endorsed, and three changes were made.

## What Nagpur changed

| Change | Detail (Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| Creed | From attainment of self-government by constitutional means to **swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means**, committing the Congress to extra-constitutional mass struggle |
| Organisation | A Congress Working Committee of 15 members; provincial Congress committees on a linguistic basis; ward committees; entry fee cut to four annas |
| Promise | Gandhi said that if the programme were fully implemented swaraj would come within a year |

The Nagpur president was C. Vijayaraghavachariar (standard; verify). Several revolutionary groups, especially in Bengal, pledged support. Jinnah, Annie Besant, G. S. Kharpade and B. C. Pal left the Congress because they believed in a constitutional and lawful struggle, and Surendranath Banerjea founded the Indian National Liberal Federation (Spectrum). Spectrum says that the Congress's adoption of a movement the Khilafat Committee had begun gave it new energy, and 1921 and 1922 saw an unprecedented popular upsurge.

## A pair to hold apart

**Calcutta** approved the programme; **Nagpur** changed the creed and the organisation and confirmed the programme. A Prelims option that gives the change of creed to Calcutta, or the Working Committee to Calcutta, is a swap.

### 5. Core: the programme, boycott and constructive work

*Objective:* List the programme's items under boycott, constructive work and conditions, and state the planned stages.

## The programme as Spectrum gives it

The Congress approved non-cooperation until the Punjab and Khilafat wrongs were removed and swaraj established. The programme had two faces.

**The boycott side (negative).**

- Boycott of **government schools and colleges**.
- Boycott of **law courts**, with justice through **panchayats** instead.
- Boycott of the **legislative councils**, including the elections of November 1920.
- Boycott of **foreign cloth**, with khadi and hand-spinning in its place. The picketing of liquor and toddy shops grew out of this.
- **Renunciation of government honours and titles.**

**The constructive side.** National schools and colleges, panchayats, khadi and spinning, and, in the conditions for participants, work for **Hindu-Muslim unity** and the **removal of untouchability**. After the withdrawal in 1922 the Congress working committee gave the constructive items as the work of the hour: popularising khadi, national schools, campaigning for temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity and against untouchability.

**The condition.** All participants had to remain **non-violent** throughout.

## The stages

Spectrum says the **second phase could include mass civil disobedience**, including resignation from government service and non-payment of taxes. The Congress later called on local bodies to start civil disobedience if they thought people were ready (module 8). Gandhi's one-year promise of swaraj hung over the plan.

**Source difference:** NCERT Class XII summarises the call as including not paying taxes; Spectrum places non-payment of taxes in a second phase. Follow Spectrum for the plan adopted in 1920, and read the no-tax campaigns of 1921 in Midnapore and Guntur as local starts of that later stage.

## The summary list

Spectrum's summary names the techniques: boycott of government-run schools, colleges, law courts, municipality and government service, foreign cloth and liquor; setting up of national schools, colleges and panchayats and using khadi; and, in the second stage, civil disobedience through non-payment of taxes.

## Three traps

1. **Titles were renounced, not kept.** A statement that titles could be retained without using them in public contradicts the programme.
2. **Non-violence was a condition, not an option.**
3. **Civil disobedience was a later stage in this movement.** Do not confuse it with 1930 (GS-045), where law-breaking began from the start.

Two more points that often appear: the boycott of councils was contested, and C. R. Das later argued for entering them (GS-044, Day 15, not yet written).

### 6. Core: who took part, students, lawyers, women, workers, peasants, business, Muslims and princes

*Objective:* Describe each group's role and the limits of participation, with NCERT's point about different readings of swaraj.

## Gandhi's tour and the new institutions

Gandhi, with the Ali brothers, toured the country (Spectrum). In February 1921 he was received by adoring crowds in eastern Uttar Pradesh, and in 1921 during a South India tour he shaved his head and adopted the loincloth to identify with the poor (NCERT Class XII). New Congress branches were set up and provincial committees followed linguistic regions.

## Group by group

| Group | Role (Spectrum unless marked) | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Students** | Thousands left government schools for about 800 national institutions led by Narendra Dev, C. R. Das, Lajpat Rai, Zakir Hussain and Subhas Bose (principal of the National College, Calcutta): Jamia Millia, Kashi Vidyapeeth, Gujarat Vidyapeeth, Bihar Vidyapeeth | Many returned later (standard; verify) |
| **Lawyers** | Motilal and Jawaharlal Nehru, C. R. Das, C. Rajagopalachari, Kitchlew, Vallabhbhai Patel, Asaf Ali, T. Prakasam and Rajendra Prasad gave up practice | Response in the big cities was limited |
| **Middle class** | Led at the start; leaders like Rajendra Prasad and Patel found it a viable alternative to terrorism | Reservations later; Calcutta, Bombay and Madras gave a limited response; resignations from government service and surrender of titles were not taken seriously |
| **Business** | Supported the economic boycott as Swadeshi had helped them; NCERT notes G. D. Birla among open supporters | A section of big business feared labour unrest |
| **Peasants** | Participation was massive; the Congress opposed class war but the masses broke the restraint, turning against landlords and traders; in Bihar the sacred-thread conflict between lower and upper castes merged into the movement | Local aims differed from the leadership's |
| **Workers** | NCERT: official figures give 396 strikes in 1921 involving 600,000 workers and seven million lost workdays | Strikes sometimes defied local nationalist leaders |

Spectrum says the Tilak Swaraj Fund was oversubscribed with one crore rupees collected, imports of foreign cloth fell by half and the volunteer corps acted as a parallel police.

## Hindu-Muslim unity

Spectrum calls the massive Muslim participation and the maintenance of communal unity, despite events like the Moppila uprisings, great achievements. It says that in many places two-thirds of those arrested were Muslims, which it describes as unprecedented; treat the proportion as Spectrum's wording. Gandhi and others addressed Muslim masses from mosques.

## The princes

The extracts do not describe the princes' role. NCERT Class XII says Praja Mandals were established to spread the nationalist creed in the princely states. The princes themselves were not participants (standard; verify).

## Different readings of swaraj

NCERT: peasants, workers and others interpreted non-cooperation in ways that suited their own interests rather than the dictates from above. This is the point to quote when asked about the movement's mass character.

### 7. Core: local movements, Awadh, Eka, Mappila, Assam, Andhra, Punjab and others

Spectrum says the spirit of defiance gave rise to many local struggles: the Awadh Kisan Movement and the Eka Movement in Uttar Pradesh, the Mappila revolt in Malabar, and the Sikh agitation against mahants in Punjab. ## How to use the table

Spectrum notes that the Congress was against class war, yet the masses broke this restraint. NCERT Class XII adds that these movements were sometimes carried out in defiance of local nationalist leadership. So the locals were not mere branches of a central plan: they joined a local grievance to Gandhi's name. Some turned violent (Awadh in January 1921, Malabar in 1921), which fed Gandhi's worry that the movement could slip from non-violence (module 8).

## Identify, then link

For each movement in an exam, give three items: place, grievance and form (strike, no-tax, forest satyagraha, picketing). A fourth item, whether it stayed non-violent, links to the withdrawal.

### 8. Core: repression, the Prince of Wales, Chauri Chaura and the withdrawal

*Objective:* Narrate 1921 to March 1922 in order and state exactly what happened at Chauri Chaura and Bardoli.

## Government and movement in 1921

- **May 1921.** Talks between Gandhi and Viceroy **Reading** broke down because the government wanted Gandhi to urge the Ali brothers to remove passages suggesting violence. Gandhi saw an attempt to divide him from the Khilafat leaders and refused (Spectrum).
- **July 1921.** The Ali brothers called on Muslims to resign from the army as irreligious; they were **arrested in September**. Gandhi echoed the call.
- **Local civil disobedience.** The Congress told local bodies to start civil disobedience if they judged people ready; no-tax campaigns were already running in Midnapore and Guntur (module 7).
- **November 1921.** The visit of the **Prince of Wales** brought strikes and demonstrations (Spectrum). The landing at Bombay on 17 November and the riots there are standard details (verify).
- **December 1921.** The government came down hard: volunteer corps were declared illegal, public meetings banned, the press gagged and most leaders except Gandhi arrested. At the **Ahmedabad** session (C. R. Das presiding in jail, Hakim Ajmal Khan acting) the Congress made Gandhi the sole authority on civil disobedience.

## The Bardoli ultimatum

On **1 February 1922** Gandhi threatened to start civil disobedience from **Bardoli** (Gujarat) unless political prisoners were released and press controls lifted (Spectrum). The movement had hardly begun when it ended.

## Chauri Chaura, 5 February 1922

At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district of the United Provinces, the police beat the leader of a volunteer group campaigning against liquor and high food prices, then opened fire on the crowd that came to protest before the police station. Spectrum says the crowd burned the station with policemen inside and killed those who tried to flee; **twenty-two policemen died**. NCERT Class XII says several constables died and Class VIII says twenty-two, adding that the peasants were provoked because the police had fired on a peaceful demonstration. Source differences: Spectrum places the first provocation in the beating of the volunteer leader; NCERT in firing on a peaceful demonstration.

## The withdrawal

Gandhi at once announced the withdrawal. The Congress Working Committee at **Bardoli in February 1922** (12 February, standard; verify) resolved to stop all activity that led to breaking the law and turn to constructive work: khadi, national schools, temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity and removal of untouchability. C. R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Subhas Bose and Jawaharlal Nehru were bewildered; Lajpat Rai and others in prison shared the resentment. In March 1922 Gandhi was arrested and sentenced to six years (Spectrum); NCERT quotes Judge Broomfield's tribute to him as a patriot.

## After the incident

The Chauri Chaura trial and the legal defence are not in the extracts. Standard accounts name Madan Mohan Malaviya as leading the defence; verify. Gandhi was released on health grounds in February 1924 (Spectrum, ch17), when the Swarajist question (GS-044, Day 15, not yet written) was already live.

### 9. Master: why he withdrew, the Khilafat collapse and the evaluation

*Objective:* Give Gandhi's reasons and the critics' reading, the Khilafat collapse, and a balanced evaluation.

## Why Gandhi withdrew (Spectrum)

1. **Non-violence had not been learnt.** Incidents like Chauri Chaura could turn the movement violent.
2. **A violent movement is easily suppressed**, giving the state an excuse for armed force.
3. **Fatigue.** It is hard to hold a movement at a high pitch for long.
4. **No negotiating mood.** The government seemed unwilling to talk.
5. **The central issue faded.** Spectrum lists the fading of the Khilafat question, but the Turkish events came in November 1922, after the withdrawal, so treat it as the reason the movement could not be revived, not a cause of the decision (verify).

Gandhi's own words, quoted in Spectrum from *Young India* of 16 February 1922: he would suffer every humiliation, torture, ostracism and death to prevent the movement from becoming violent. NCERT Class XII quotes him that no provocation could justify the brutal murder of defenceless men.

## The critics

- **Subhas Bose**, as quoted in Spectrum, called the order of retreat, just when enthusiasm was at boiling point, nothing short of a national calamity, a view he says the jailed leaders Das, Motilal Nehru and Lajpat Rai shared.
- A **Marxist reading** (Spectrum) says a wave of revolutionary unrest in 1919 (strikes, peasant protests) forced the leadership to a radical stand, that Gandhi and the Congress leaders took over the leadership to keep it controlled and within safe channels, and that they called it off just when the masses were taking the initiative.
- Spectrum also prints, in a View box (PDF p386), Koenraad Elst calling the Khilafat movement retrograde and anti-nationalist, pitting Islamic interests against secular and non-Muslim interests. Treat this as one partisan reading, not a settled finding.

## The Khilafat collapse

In **November 1922** the Turkish people under Mustafa Kemal Pasha deprived the sultan of political power and Turkey became a secular state with a European-style legal system and rights for women; **in 1924 the caliphate was abolished** (Spectrum). The central theme lost its relevance. **Source slip:** NCERT Class XII speaks of the Caliphate as already abolished at the time of the 1920 movement; the abolition came in 1924 (standard dates: sultanate 1 November 1922, caliphate 3 March 1924; verify).

## The evaluation

| Gains | Limits |
|---|---|
| Nationalism reached every nook and corner and politicised artisans, peasants, students, urban poor, women and traders (Spectrum) | The movement 'communalised national politics, to an extent'; leaders failed to raise religious consciousness to secular political consciousness (Spectrum) |
| Brought urban Muslims into the movement | Middle-class and big-business reservations |
| Colonial rule's two myths, benevolence and invincibility, were challenged; people lost their fear (Spectrum) | Local violence, withdrawal, fatigue; swaraj in one year not achieved |
| NCERT Class XII: as a consequence of the movement the British Raj was shaken to its foundations for the first time since the Revolt of 1857 | The programme of boycott brought limited responses from elite centres |

## Balance

A fair answer joins both sides: the movement was a turning point in mass mobilisation and Hindu-Muslim cooperation, and its retreat left a political vacuum in which the Swarajist question rose (GS-044, Day 15, not yet written). Spectrum's own judgement is that politicisation of millions gave the national movement a revolutionary character.

### Notes: Definitions to hold

- Khalifa (caliph): the title held by the Ottoman sultan, claimed as the religious head of Sunni Muslims worldwide.
- Boycott: refusal to use or deal with an institution or product.
- Non-cooperation: refusing help to the government without breaking a law; civil disobedience is the deliberate breaking of a law.
- Swaraj: self-rule; at Nagpur defined as the goal to be won by peaceful and legitimate means.
- Constructive work: khadi, national schools, panchayats, temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity and removal of untouchability.
- Tilak Swaraj Fund: the fund collected during the movement; Spectrum says one crore rupees were collected.

### Notes: Who is who

- Shaukat Ali and Muhammad Ali: the Ali brothers, leaders of the Khilafat Committee.
- Maulana Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Hasrat Mohani: other Khilafat leaders; Ajmal Khan was acting Congress president at Ahmedabad 1921.
- Lord Reading: Viceroy; his talks with Gandhi broke down in May 1921.
- C. R. Das: lawyer, opposed council boycott but accepted discipline; presided at Ahmedabad 1921 while in jail.
- Tilak: hesitated over the Khilafat alliance; died on 1 August 1920.
- Mustafa Kemal Pasha: Turkish leader under whom the sultan lost political power (1922).
- J. M. Sengupta: leader of the Assam strikes of 1921.
- Judge Broomfield (NCERT prints C. N. Broomfield; standard name R. S. Broomfield, verify): sentenced Gandhi in March 1922.

### Notes: Cause and effect: why the movement rose and why it ended

- Rose: post-war hardship, Punjab events and the Hunter report, Montford disappointment, Hindu-Muslim base after Lucknow, the Khilafat issue.
- Spread: Gandhi's tours, new institutions, linguistic committees, peasants' own grievances.
- Ended: violence at Chauri Chaura (5 February 1922), Gandhi's fear of suppression, fatigue, repression.
- Afterwards: the Khilafat theme collapsed in 1922-24, the Congress divided over council entry (GS-044), and constructive work continued.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Calcutta special session (approved the programme) versus Nagpur (changed the creed and organisation).
- Non-Cooperation (1920-22; refuse help) versus Civil Disobedience (1930-34; break laws from the start).
- Khilafat Committee (1919, the Ali brothers) versus the Congress (adopted the method in 1920).
- Chauri Chaura (UP, 1922, violence) versus Jallianwala Bagh (Punjab, 1919, state violence).
- Bardoli ultimatum of 1922 versus the Bardoli peasant satyagraha of 1928 (GS-031, GS-045).
