## GS-166: Fundamental Duties

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: Rights, duties and directives; citizen versus person

## Three words that are easy to blur

Think of the Constitution as speaking in three different voices.

- **Fundamental Rights** (Part III) are things an individual can claim *against* the State. If the State violates them, the individual can approach a court. Example: freedom of speech.
- **Directive Principles of State Policy** (Part IV) are instructions to the *State* about the goals it should pursue when making laws and policies. Courts do not enforce them directly (see GS-165). Example: organising village panchayats.
- **Fundamental Duties** (Part IVA) are obligations the Constitution places on *citizens*. They are listed in Article 51A. Example: protecting public property.

A **right** is a claim; a **duty** is an obligation; a **directive** is guidance to the State. The first speaks from the individual to the State, the second from the Constitution to the citizen, the third from the Constitution to the government.

## Citizen versus person

A **citizen** is a member of the political community of India as defined by the Constitution and the citizenship law (for example, a person born in India under the rules of the time). A **person** is anybody on Indian soil, including foreigners. Some rights are available to all persons (Laxmikanth lists Articles 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 and 28), while others are limited to citizens. The Fundamental Duties are confined to **citizens**: a foreigner in India has no duty under Article 51A, though ordinary law of course applies to everyone.

## Why this matters for the topic

Almost every question on duties plays on these distinctions: Which Part? Who is bound? Can a court enforce it? You will rebuild the full comparison table in module 7.

### 1. Orientation: what Fundamental Duties are and where they sit

*Objective:* State in one sentence what Part IVA and Article 51A are, and name the two amendments that shaped the list.

## The core idea

The Constitution of India does two kinds of things for a citizen. It protects (Fundamental Rights, Part III) and it asks (Fundamental Duties, Part IVA). Laxmikanth opens the chapter with the idea that rights and duties of citizens are *correlative and inseparable*, meaning each makes sense only with the other. Yet the Constitution as it came into force in 1950 had **only rights**. It did include duties of the **State**, in the form of the Directive Principles of State Policy.

## The one-page summary

- **Where:** Part IVA of the Constitution, which has only **one Article: Article 51A**.
- **What:** a list of duties of every citizen of India, which in the present text runs from (a) to (k), eleven duties.
- **When:** ten duties were added in **1976** by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act**; the eleventh, on education of children, was added in **2002** by the **86th Constitutional Amendment Act**.
- **Why:** during the internal Emergency (1975-77) the Congress government wanted citizens to be made conscious of their duties; the Swaran Singh Committee recommended a chapter on duties.
- **Nature:** a mix of moral and civic duties. They are **non-justiciable**, so courts do not enforce them directly, and they apply only to **citizens**.

### 2. Origin: from the Emergency to the 42nd Amendment

*Objective:* Explain why the framers left duties out in 1950, what the Swaran Singh Committee recommended, what the government accepted, and what the 86th Amendment added.

## 1950: rights for citizens, duties for the State

The framers did not feel it necessary to include a list of citizens' duties in the original Constitution. They instead put the **duties of the State** in the Directive Principles. The idea of citizens' duties, Laxmikanth says, is closer to the thinking of socialist constitutions, and the Fundamental Duties in India are inspired by the Constitution of the erstwhile USSR.

## 1975-77: the immediate trigger

The need for stating duties was felt during the internal Emergency (1975-1977). In 1976 the Congress Party set up the **Sardar Swaran Singh Committee** to make recommendations. The committee proposed a separate chapter on fundamental duties and stressed that citizens must become conscious that, besides enjoying rights, they have duties.

## What the government accepted and left out

The Congress government at the Centre accepted the idea and passed the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**. It added Part IVA with **Article 51A**, which for the first time set out a code of **ten** fundamental duties. The Swaran Singh Committee had suggested **eight**; the amendment included ten.

Three recommendations were **not accepted**:

1. Parliament may impose a penalty or punishment for non-compliance with or refusal to observe any duty.
2. No law imposing such a penalty could be challenged in court on the ground of violating a Fundamental Right or any other provision of the Constitution.
3. The duty to **pay taxes** should be a Fundamental Duty.

The ruling party called the non-inclusion of duties in 1950 a historical mistake that was now being corrected.

## The Janata government did not undo it

In 1977 the Janata Government, led by Morarji Desai, set about undoing several changes made by the 42nd Amendment through the 43rd (1977) and 44th (1978) Amendments. It did **not** remove the Fundamental Duties. Laxmikanth reads this as an eventual consensus on including duties. NCERT also notes the 42nd Amendment as a wide-ranging, controversial amendment of the Emergency period, which added the duties among many other changes.

## 2002: the eleventh duty

The **86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002** added one more duty, now clause (k): to provide opportunities for education to one's child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years. (The same amendment also dealt with the right to education in Part III and Part IV; see GS-165.)

## Quick timeline

1950 no duties; 1975-77 Emergency; 1976 Swaran Singh Committee and 42nd Amendment (ten duties); 1977-78 Janata amendments, duties retained; 2002 86th Amendment (eleventh duty).

### 3. The list of duties in Article 51A

*Objective:* Recall the eleven duties by theme and identify which one was added in 2002.

## Reading Article 51A

Article 51A says it shall be the duty of every citizen of India to do the following. The table below paraphrases each clause and groups it by theme, because the order alone is hard to remember.

| Clause | Theme | What the citizen must do |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Constitution and symbols | Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem |
| (b) | Freedom struggle | Cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom |
| (c) | Sovereignty and integrity | Uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India |
| (d) | Defence and service | Defend the country and render national service when called upon |
| (e) | Harmony and dignity of women | Promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women |
| (f) | Culture | Value and preserve the rich heritage of the country's composite culture |
| (g) | Environment | Protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and have compassion for living creatures |
| (h) | Scientific outlook | Develop scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform |
| (i) | Public property and non-violence | Safeguard public property and abjure violence |
| (j) | Excellence | Strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement |
| (k) | Education of children | Provide opportunities for education to one's child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years (added by the 86th Amendment, 2002) |

## Grouping for memory

- **Nation:** (a), (b), (c), (d).
- **Society and culture:** (e), (f).
- **Nature and mind:** (g), (h).
- **Conduct and ambition:** (i), (j).
- **Family and learning:** (k), the only duty that names a specific age band and that was added later.

Clauses (a) to (j) are the **original ten** of 1976; (k) is the 2002 addition.

## Words that students find hard

- **Composite culture:** the shared heritage formed by the interaction of India's many communities.
- **Scientific temper:** the habit of questioning and testing claims instead of accepting them blindly.
- **Humanism:** respect for human beings and their dignity.
- **Abjure:** to give up, especially a practice, on principle.

## A tip for Prelims

The most frequently tested clauses are (f) composite culture, (g) environment, (h) scientific temper and (k) education. In pairing questions the examiner may place a clause under the wrong Part (for example, a Directive Principle presented as a duty), so link each clause to its theme.

### 4. Features: moral and civic, citizen-only, non-justiciable

*Objective:* State the four features given by Laxmikanth, explain what 'non-justiciable' means for duties, and reconcile 'non-enforceable' with 'Parliament may enforce'.

## The four features

1. **Moral and civic duties.** Some duties are moral precepts, such as cherishing the noble ideals of the freedom struggle. Others are civic duties, such as respecting the Constitution, the National Flag and the National Anthem.
2. **Rooted in tradition.** The duties codify tasks that are part of Indian tradition, mythology, religions and practices. They are values not newly invented by the Constitution.
3. **Only for citizens.** Some Fundamental Rights extend to all persons, including foreigners, but the Fundamental Duties are confined to citizens and do not extend to foreigners.
4. **Non-justiciable.** Like the Directive Principles, the duties are non-justiciable. The Constitution does not provide for their direct enforcement by the courts and there is no legal sanction against their violation in the Constitution itself.

## What non-justiciable means

A matter is **justiciable** when a court can hear it and give a binding order. So you cannot go to the Supreme Court or a High Court and say: "this citizen has violated Article 51A, punish him for that reason alone." That is the sense of "non-justiciable". NCERT states the same point: the Constitution does not say anything about enforcing these duties.

## Reconciling two statements

Laxmikanth says elsewhere in the chapter that the duties are "enforceable by law" because Parliament can provide for penalties. This looks like a contradiction, but it is not. Read the two together:

- The **Constitution** itself provides no machinery or penalty for duties.
- **Parliament** is free to make ordinary laws that punish conduct covered by a duty, and several such laws already exist (module 6).

So the duties are not self-executing like Fundamental Rights, but Parliament may give them teeth through legislation. Keep the phrase **"enforceable only through ordinary legislation"** in mind.

## The relationship with rights

NCERT adds an important clarification: the Constitution does not make the enjoyment of rights dependent on, or conditional upon, fulfilment of duties. Adding the duties did not change the status of Fundamental Rights. A citizen who fails a duty does not automatically lose a right under the Constitution.

## One-line summary for answers

Fundamental Duties are moral and civic obligations of citizens, placed in Part IVA by the 42nd Amendment, non-justiciable, enforceable only through ordinary law passed by Parliament.

### 5. Criticism, significance and the political debate

*Objective:* Give five criticisms and five points of significance, with the arguments of the government and the opposition in 1976.

## Five criticisms

1. **Not exhaustive.** The list leaves out duties such as voting, paying taxes and family planning. The Swaran Singh Committee itself had recommended the tax duty.
2. **Vague.** Phrases such as "noble ideals", "composite culture" and "scientific temper" can be interpreted in different ways, and are hard for the common person to understand.
3. **A code of moral precepts.** Being non-justiciable, they have been called mere moral advice, even though the committee had suggested penalties.
4. **Superfluous.** Critics said the duties would be performed by people even without constitutional inclusion.
5. **Placed as an appendage to Part IV.** The critics said the place reduces their value; they should have come after Part III to be on par with Fundamental Rights.

## Five points of significance

1. **A reminder** that while enjoying rights, citizens owe duties to the country, society and fellow citizens.
2. **A warning** against anti-national and anti-social acts such as burning the National Flag and destroying public property.
3. **A source of inspiration** that promotes discipline and commitment, making citizens active participants in national goals rather than spectators.
4. **A help to the courts** in deciding the constitutional validity of a law. In 1992 the Supreme Court ruled that if a law seeks to give effect to a fundamental duty, a court may consider it "reasonable" in relation to Article 14 (equality before law) or Article 19 (the six freedoms), and so save it from unconstitutionality. (The case name is not in the extract used here.)
5. **Enforceable by law**, in the sense that Parliament may provide for appropriate penalty or punishment for failure to fulfil them (see module 4).

## The 1976 political argument

- **Government:** H. R. Gokhale, the then Law Minister, said that on the eve of the Emergency in June 1975 a section of people showed no anxiety to respect the established legal order, and that the chapter on duties would have a sobering effect on such restless spirits. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi argued that the moral value of the duties would not smother rights but would establish a democratic balance by making people as conscious of duties as they are of rights.
- **Opposition:** The Opposition in Parliament strongly opposed including duties.
- **Aftermath:** The Janata Government that followed did not annul them (module 2), which suggests a broad consensus later on.

## How to use this in Mains

Present criticism and significance as a balanced scale and end with a judgment: the duties are mainly a moral and educational statement, valuable as constitutional values and as an aid to interpretation, but weak as a legal tool without supporting legislation.

### 6. Verma Committee and the judicial approach

*Objective:* List the eight laws identified by the Verma Committee and describe how courts have used the duties.

## The Verma Committee (1999)

The Verma Committee on Fundamental Duties of the Citizens (1999) identified existing legal provisions that implement some of the duties. This is the best proof that duties, though non-justiciable in the Constitution, are backed by ordinary law. The committee's list, as given by Laxmikanth:

| Law | What it does |
|---|---|
| Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 | Prevents disrespect to the Constitution, the National Flag and the National Anthem |
| Criminal laws on enmity between groups | Punish promotion of enmity on grounds of language, race, place of birth, religion and so on |
| Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 | Punishes offences related to caste and religion |
| Indian Penal Code | Makes imputations and assertions prejudicial to national integration punishable |
| Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 | Allows a communal organisation to be declared an unlawful association |
| Representation of the People Act, 1951 | Disqualifies legislators for corrupt practices such as seeking votes on religion or promoting enmity |
| Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 | Prohibits trade in rare and endangered species |
| Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 | Checks deforestation and diversion of forest land |

Note: the Indian Penal Code was replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which came into force in 2024; verify the current equivalents before quoting section numbers. This lesson reports the committee's 1999 list as Laxmikanth prints it.

## How the duties map to these laws

Clause (a) is supported by the Prevention of Insults Act; clauses (c) and (e) by the criminal laws on enmity and national integration, the Protection of Civil Rights Act and the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act; clause (g) by the Wildlife and Forest Acts. This mapping is our own reading of the list, a useful way to answer "how are duties enforced?" but not a table printed by the committee.

## The judicial approach

The extract supports one rule: the **1992 Supreme Court ruling** that a law giving effect to a duty may be treated as reasonable under Articles 14 and 19 (module 5). In general, courts use the duties as an **aid to interpreting** rights and laws, not as a stand-alone source of liability.

Two further decisions are often cited in textbooks, but they are **not in the extracts used here**, so verify them before relying on them: *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986), commonly cited on the National Anthem and Article 51A(a), and *AIIMS Students Union v. AIIMS* (2001), commonly cited when rights and duties are read together.

## Putting it together

- No court can enforce Article 51A directly.
- Parliament can and does legislate (the Verma list).
- Courts use the duties to test and support laws, especially under Articles 14 and 19.

### 7. Rights, directives and duties compared; the international angle

*Objective:* Build the comparison table of Parts III, IV and IVA from memory and recall what Laxmikanth says about duties in other constitutions.

## The comparison table

| Point | Fundamental Rights (Part III) | Directive Principles (Part IV) | Fundamental Duties (Part IVA) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addressed to | State; claims by individuals | State | Citizens |
| Nature | Legal claims | Guidelines and goals | Moral and civic obligations |
| Justiciable? | Yes, directly enforceable by courts | No (Article 37) | No |
| In the original Constitution? | Yes | Yes | No; added in 1976 |
| Source of inspiration | Largely the US Bill of Rights | Irish Constitution | Constitution of the erstwhile USSR |
| Who benefits or is bound | Some rights for all persons, some for citizens only | Society as a whole | Citizens only |
| Legal sanction | Courts can strike down violating laws | No court sanction; moral and political | No direct sanction; Parliament may legislate |
| Number of Articles | Articles 12-35 | Articles 36-51 | Article 51A only |

Note on 'Addressed to': some Fundamental Rights (Articles 15(2), 17, 23, 24) also bind private persons, not only the State.

Link: for the Directive Principles in detail see GS-165. For rights see the Fundamental Rights topics of the roadmap.

## How they relate

NCERT presents Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles as complementary: rights restrain the government, directives exhort it. Duties add a third voice: they ask citizens to act. NCERT stresses that duties do not make rights conditional.

## The international angle

Laxmikanth says:

- The Fundamental Duties in India are inspired by the Constitution of the erstwhile USSR.
- None of the Constitutions of major democratic countries such as the USA, Canada, France, Germany and Australia specifically contains a list of citizens' duties.
- The Japanese Constitution is, perhaps, the only democratic constitution in the world to list duties of citizens.
- Socialist constitutions gave rights and duties equal importance and declared that the exercise of rights and freedoms is inseparable from the performance of duties.

Use these as the only international claims you make unless you verify more. Note: the claim about Japan is Laxmikanth's assertion and is unverified here; and France's constitutional bloc includes the 2004 Charter for the Environment, which imposes duties (verify), so treat the claim about France cautiously.

## Exam-ready contrasts

- Rights: enforced by courts. Duties: not enforced by courts directly.
- Directives: State must apply. Duties: citizens ought to perform.
- Fundamental rights are in the original text; Part IVA came in 1976.

## Question shapes

## Worked example 1: 2017 (statements on duties)

The question asks which statements are true of the Fundamental Duties: (1) a legislative process has been provided to enforce these duties; (2) they are correlative to legal duties. Method: take each statement to a fact. For (1): the Constitution gives no enforcement machinery for Article 51A; Parliament may legislate if it chooses, as the Verma list shows (modules 4 and 6). The wording "has been provided" is the trap, since a reader may confuse *Parliament's power* with *a process in the Constitution*. For (2): the extracts discuss moral and civic duties and the correlation of rights and duties, but not a link to "legal duties" as a category, so this statement needs careful reading. On that analysis neither statement matches the module text; treat that as reasoning, not an official key.

## Worked example 2: 2017 (rights and duties)

The question asks the correct relationship between rights and duties. Laxmikanth's opening line says that rights and duties of citizens are correlative and inseparable. The other options set rights against duties or rank one above the other. Analysis points to the option stating that rights are correlative with duties. Again, this is analysis, not an official key.

## Worked example 3: 2025 (pairs)

Three pairs: (I) separation of the judiciary from the executive in public services, said to be under the Directive Principles; (II) valuing and preserving the composite culture, said to be under Fundamental Duties; (III) prohibition of employment of children below 14 in factories, said to be a Fundamental Right. Method: I is an Article 50 Directive (see GS-165); II is clause (f) of Article 51A; III is Article 24 in Part III. Article 24 is standard knowledge outside the extracts. On that analysis all three pairs appear correct, which would make the answer "all three"; this is not an official key.

## Traps

1. Treating duties as justiciable or as conditions for rights.
2. Saying the original Constitution had duties.
3. Giving ten as the present number (the present text has eleven clauses).
4. Putting a Directive Principle (Article 40, 44, 48A) under duties.
5. Saying duties apply to all persons.

## Elimination habit

Ask three questions of every option: Which Part? Who is bound? Can a court enforce it?

**Q.** Which duty was added in 2002 and by which amendment?

**A.** Clause (k), education of a child or ward aged six to fourteen, by the 86th Amendment.

**Q.** List the four features of Fundamental Duties.

**A.** Moral and civic; rooted in Indian tradition; confined to citizens; non-justiciable (Parliament may legislate).

**Q.** Name four laws from the Verma Committee list and the three Swaran Singh recommendations that were not accepted.
