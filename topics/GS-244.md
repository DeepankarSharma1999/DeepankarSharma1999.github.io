## GS-244: Public-private partnership models

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Public-private partnership models and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Models and risk allocation

The Economic Survey 2025-26 describes PPPs as a way for government to draw on private expertise and capital to close infrastructure gaps and improve service delivery. It lists four models used in India: Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT), the Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM), Toll-Operate-Transfer (TOT) and DBFOT, which is BOT with design and finance added to the name. It separates BOT (Toll) from BOT (Annuity) by who carries traffic risk: as the names indicate, the toll operator earns from users, while under annuity the authority pays. Box IX.2 wants investors who profited from de-risked brownfield assets to accept more greenfield risk, for instance through plain BOT concessions in mature road projects. Success depends on sound institutions, financial support and standard documents such as model RfQ, RfP and concession agreements.

### Institutional support

The PPPAC is the apex body that appraises central PPP projects. Chaired by the DEA Secretary, it includes the Secretaries of Expenditure and Legal Affairs, the sponsoring ministry and NITI Aayog's CEO; from 2014-15 to December 2025 it recommended 129 projects. Viability Gap Funding supports projects that are economically desirable but commercially unviable, with higher support for social sectors. The India Infrastructure Project Development Fund, notified in 2022 with ₹150 crore, pays transaction advisers to prepare bankable projects. A three-year pipeline of 852 projects worth over ₹17 lakh crore now exists. DEA has also issued risk-allocation and appraisal guides, sector toolkits and an index rating the PPP readiness of states and ministries.

### Lessons and the next phase

The Survey's Box IX.2 notes that early PPPs suffered stress from contract weaknesses and poor risk allocation, which led to better model concessions and guidelines. Roads, ports, power and renewables now draw steady private capital, but health, education, sanitation and urban services need a stronger partnership approach in which the public side absorbs early risks that investors cannot price. Outcomes are poorest when land, statutory clearances, demand estimates or shifting of utilities are left unsettled. States face capacity gaps, trust deficits and a perception that PPPs mean selling assets, even though concessions run 30–60 years. Brownfield assets attract pension, insurance and InvIT money, but greenfield projects still lean on bank lending.

### Worked example

Illustrative case: a highway with uncertain traffic. Under BOT (Toll) the developer's revenue depends on traffic, so it carries that risk and may bid cautiously; under BOT (Annuity) the authority carries it and pays fixed sums, which eases financing but raises fiscal commitments. A Mains answer should match the model to the risk each party can best bear, settle land and clearances before award, and mention VGF for economically desirable but commercially unviable projects.

### Recall

- - **q:** Who chairs the PPPAC?
  - **a:** The Secretary, Department of Economic Affairs.
- - **q:** What VGF support is available for social-sector PPPs?
  - **a:** Up to 80 per cent of capital cost and 50 per cent of operational expenditure for five years after commercial operation.
- - **q:** What distinguishes BOT (Toll) from BOT (Annuity)?
  - **a:** Allocation of traffic risk: the private operator bears it under toll; the authority bears it under annuity.

### Common trap

Treating a PPP as just an outsourced construction (EPC) contract: in a PPP the private partner shares long-term risks and operates the asset, whereas EPC is a vendor arrangement.
