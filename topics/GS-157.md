## GS-157: Education, health and inequalities

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Education, health and inequalities and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Education: progress with persistent gaps

Literacy empowers people by widening awareness of careers, enabling participation in the knowledge economy and improving health awareness. India's literacy rate rose from 18.3% in 1951 to 73.0% in 2011. The gender gap narrowed to 16.3 points in 2011 because female literacy, starting from a lower base, rose faster than male literacy over 2001–2011. Yet gaps remain wide by social group and region: Scheduled Castes and Tribes have lower literacy, women within these groups lower still, and Kerala nears universal literacy while Bihar trails. NCERT highlights why this matters: illiterate parents find it much harder to secure good education for their children, so educational inequality tends to pass from one generation to the next.

### Health: policy targets and deprivations

National Health Policy 2017, reproduced in NCERT's demography chapter, set targets including raising public health spending to 2.5% of GDP by 2025, lifting life expectancy to 70, cutting under-five mortality to 23 and neonatal mortality to 16, reducing TFR to 2.1, cutting stunting among under-fives by 40%, and lowering catastrophic household health spending by a quarter. It also aims to place doctors and paramedics according to Indian Public Health Standards in high-priority districts. NITI Aayog's MPI data show that between 2015–16 and 2019–21 all 12 indicators across health, education and living standards improved, with sanitation and cooking fuel deprivations falling most; nutrition and years of schooling also contributed significantly, though NITI notes further scope for improvement.

### Why inequality reproduces itself

Sociologists distinguish inequality produced by society from differences in individual talent or effort. Drawing on Bourdieu, NCERT identifies economic capital (assets and income), cultural capital (qualifications and status) and social capital (networks). These overlap and convert: a well-off family can buy expensive higher education, turning money into credentials, and influential contacts can help secure a well-paid job. Stratification persists over generations because families transmit these resources, and it is sustained by beliefs that portray inequality as fair or inevitable. Applied to education and health, this framework helps explain why children of poor, rural, SC, ST or illiterate households face compounded barriers even when schools and clinics exist.

### Worked example

Q: 'Inequalities in education and health reinforce each other in India.' Discuss. Frame: literacy trends and gaps (73.0% in 2011; gender gap 16.3; SC/ST and Kerala–Bihar differences); health targets (NHP 2017: 2.5% of GDP, U5MR 23, catastrophic spending cut); MPI evidence of improving deprivations; Bourdieu's capitals explaining intergenerational transmission; policy levers (public spending, targeted support for girls and SC/ST children, sanitation and nutrition); conclude on breaking the cycle.

### Recall

- - **q:** What was India's male–female literacy gap in Census 2011?
  - **a:** 16.3 percentage points (80.9% male vs 64.6% female).
- - **q:** What public health spending target did NHP 2017 set?
  - **a:** Raising government health expenditure from 1.15% to 2.5% of GDP by 2025.
- - **q:** Name Bourdieu's three forms of capital.
  - **a:** Economic, cultural and social capital.

### Common trap

Treating rising averages as falling inequality. NCERT stresses that literacy gains have to keep pace with population growth and that gaps by gender, caste and region persist, reproducing inequality across generations.
