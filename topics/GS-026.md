### Bridge: Words and dates for war chapters

A **nawab** governed a province under the Mughal system; the Nawab of the Carnatic ruled around Arcot. A **subahdar** governed a large province or 'subah'; the Nizam, ruler of the Deccan, was its subahdar. A **sepoy** is an Indian soldier in a European-style army. A **treaty** ends a war and lists terms; **restitution** means handing back conquests, an **indemnity** is money paid by the loser, and **hostages** are people held to guarantee that the payment will be made.

Dates in war chapters have two kinds. A war has a span (for example 1740-48 for the First Carnatic War) and a treaty has a single date (1748 for Aix-la-Chapelle). Do not use the treaty year as the war's start year, and note that books sometimes differ by a year at the start (Barnet seized French ships in 1745; Madras fell in 1746). A good habit is to write each war as 'span, trigger, treaty'.

When a question says 'war' it often wants the war's name, and when it says 'treaty' it wants the end. A table of seven rows (module 1) keeps this tidy.

### Bridge: How a European war becomes an Indian war

Europe's wars of the eighteenth century reached India because the English and French companies were part of their home countries' rivalry. When Britain and France went to war in Europe, the local commanders in India felt free, or were ordered, to attack each other's settlements.

| European war | India |
|---|---|
| War of the Austrian Succession | First Carnatic War, 1740-48 |
| Seven Years' War (1756-63) | Third Carnatic War, 1758-63 |
| American War of Independence (France helped the rebels) | Added to English suspicion of Haidar's French friendship before the Second Anglo-Mysore War |

The second Carnatic war and the Mysore wars were different. They grew out of Indian quarrels (succession, a border dispute with Travancore) that the Europeans decided to join. Remembering which wars were 'imported' and which were 'joined' will help you write the cause section of any answer.

### 1. Orientation: seven wars in south India, 1740 to 1799

*Objective:* State in one breath what the Carnatic Wars and the Anglo-Mysore Wars were, who fought whom, and how each series ended.

## The story in one page

Between 1740 and 1799 the English East India Company fought two series of wars in south India. The first series, the three **Carnatic Wars**, was against the French company. Its result was that France stopped being a political rival and kept only small trading enclaves. The second series, the four **Anglo-Mysore Wars**, was against the sultanate of Mysore, first under **Haidar Ali** and then under his son **Tipu Sultan**. Its result was that the last strong independent power of the south was conquered in 1799.

GS-024 module 7 introduced Mysore and GS-025 module 7 outlined the Carnatic Wars; this lesson adds causes, treaties, Dupleix and Clive, the four Mysore wars, Tipu's administration and the question 'why did the Company win?'. Plassey and Buxar belong to GS-027 (to come on Day 12, not yet written).

## The whole series on one table

| War | Years (Spectrum) | Who fought | How it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| First Carnatic | 1740-48 | English v French | Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, 1748: Madras returned to the English |
| Second Carnatic | 1749-54 | English v French, each backing Indian claimants | Dupleix recalled 1754; English-French agreement not to meddle in princes' quarrels |
| Third Carnatic | 1758-63 | English v French | Wandiwash 1760; Pondicherry fell 1761; Treaty of Paris 1763 |

## Three questions to carry through

Why did each war start (a European quarrel, a succession dispute, or fear of a rival's friend)? How did it end? What did the Company gain?

Two threads run through the lessons: **sea power and money**, and Indian rulers gradually becoming **clients** of European support. A verified Mains question (2022, Q2) asks why the Company's mostly Indian armies kept beating larger Indian armies; module 9 builds the answer.

### 2. Foundation: the south in 1740 and the vocabulary of war

*Objective:* Describe the political map of south India around 1740 and say why it invited foreign interference.

## Carnatic: a European name

**Carnatic** was the name Europeans gave to the Coromandel coast (the south-east coast) and its hinterland. It is not the same as the modern state of Karnataka. The French base **Pondicherry** and the English base **Madras** lay on this coast only a short distance apart, which is why the two rivals kept colliding.

## A region without a strong centre

Spectrum describes south India in 1740 as uncertain and confused. The Nizam of Hyderabad (Asaf Jah) was old and busy fighting the Marathas in the western Deccan, and his subordinates were already speculating about what would happen when he died. To the south of his kingdom the Coromandel coast had no strong ruler to keep a balance. There was the remnant of the old Vijayanagara empire in interior Mysore, Cochin and Travancore on the Malabar coast, and small states in the east: Madura (Madurai), Tanjore (Thanjavur) and Trichinopoly (Tiruchirappalli). The Maratha kingdom of Tanjore also gave the Peshwa an excuse to interfere. GS-024 module 6 explains Hyderabad and the Carnatic as successor states of the Mughal empire.


## Why the wars happen when they do

The words nawab, subahdar, sepoy, treaty and indemnity are explained in bridge GS-026-B1.

The English and French rivalry in India mirrored their wider rivalry in Europe. Spectrum says it began with the outbreak of the War of the Austrian Succession and ended with the end of the Seven Years' War. When Britain and France went to war in Europe, their companies in India were drawn in too. Bridge GS-026-B2 sets this out as a short table.

For the Mysore wars the cause was different. Mysore was a rising Indian power with control over the profitable trade of the Malabar coast, friendly to the French, and close to Madras; the English felt that both their political and commercial interests were threatened (Spectrum; see module 5).

### 3. Core: First and Second Carnatic Wars, Dupleix and Clive

*Objective:* Explain why the first two Carnatic Wars began, how they ended, and what Dupleix and Clive each contributed.

## First Carnatic War (1740-48)

This war was an extension of the Anglo-French war in Europe that arose from the Austrian Succession. France, conscious that it was weaker in India, did not want the war to spread. The English navy under Barnet seized some French ships, which provoked France. The French governor of Mauritius, **La Bourdonnais**, took **Madras in 1746** with a fleet from Mauritius. GS-025 module 7 gives the same facts in outline.

Two points deserve more attention here. First, Spectrum says Dupleix persuaded La Bourdonnais to break his promises to the English over Madras. Second, the **Battle of St. Thome** (on the river Adyar, Madras): the English had appealed to the Nawab Anwaruddin, and a small French army under Captain Paradise defeated a much larger Indian army under his son Mahfuz Khan. Spectrum calls this an eye-opener: a small disciplined army could defeat a much larger Indian one. The war also showed the importance of sea power. The **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)** ended the European war; Madras went back to the English, and the French got their territories in North America.

## Second Carnatic War (1749-54)

**Cause.** Dupleix, the French governor, wanted more power and more French political influence, and decided to use local dynastic quarrels against the English. Spectrum calls Dupleix the first European to interfere in the internal politics of Indian rulers. The occasion came in 1748. Nizam-ul-Mulk died, and in the same year the Marathas released **Chanda Sahib**. In Hyderabad Nasir Jung (the Nizam's son) was opposed by Muzaffar Jung (the Nizam's grandson). In the Carnatic Chanda Sahib resented Anwaruddin's appointment as Nawab. The French backed Muzaffar Jung and Chanda Sahib; the English backed Nasir Jung and Anwaruddin.

**Course.** At **Ambur** (near Vellore) in 1749 the combined armies of Muzaffar Jung, Chanda Sahib and the French killed Anwaruddin. Muzaffar Jung became subahdar of the Deccan, and Dupleix was named governor of the Mughal territories south of the Krishna. A French force under **Bussy** was stationed at Hyderabad, and territory near Pondicherry and on the Orissa coast, Masulipatnam included, was ceded to the French. Spectrum calls Dupleix the originator of subsidiary practice because the French army at Hyderabad was paid for by the subahdar (GS-028, Day 13, not yet written, covers Wellesley's system).

**The English recovery.** Robert Clive proposed a diversion when Muhammad Ali, the English candidate, was hard pressed at Trichinopoly: a sudden attack on **Arcot**, the Carnatic capital. In August 1751, with only 210 men, he captured it. Chanda Sahib sent a force of 4,000 from Trichinopoly but failed to retake the fort in a siege of 53 days (23 September to 14 November). Mysore, Tanjore and the Maratha chief Morari Rao then helped relieve Trichinopoly. Chanda Sahib and the French General Law were cooped up in Srirangam and surrendered in June 1752; Chanda Sahib was put to death (Spectrum: executed by Muhammad Ali; many accounts: by the Tanjore commander, verify).

**End.** The French company, annoyed at the heavy cost of Dupleix's policy, recalled him in 1754. His successor **Godeheu** negotiated with the English. They agreed not to interfere in the quarrels of Indian princes, and each side kept the territory it actually held. Spectrum names no treaty and gives no month here; the arrangement is usually called the Treaty of Pondicherry (concluded in December 1754, effective January 1755; verify). The long-term lesson is that Indian authority was no longer needed for European success; instead Muhammad Ali in the Carnatic and Salabat Jang in Hyderabad became clients.

### 4. Core: Third Carnatic War, Wandiwash and the Treaty of Paris

*Objective:* Narrate the Third Carnatic War with its battles and treaty, and weigh Wandiwash against Plassey as a turning point.

## Cause

In Europe the Seven Years' War (1756-63) began when Austria wanted to recover Silesia, and Britain and France were again on opposite sides. The Indian war was a branch of this larger conflict, just as the first had been.

## Course

In 1758 the French army under **Count de Lally** captured Fort St David in June 1758 (Spectrum also lists Vizianagaram, which was not an English fort, verify). The English then went on the offensive. Spectrum says they inflicted heavy losses on the French fleet under Admiral D'Ache. Spectrum places this 'at Masulipatnam', but the naval actions were off the Coromandel coast and Masulipatnam was taken on land by Forde in 1759 (verify). Keep the general point that English naval and military pressure grew.

A quotation in Spectrum from Dodwell explains why the tide turned: the English received food and money from Bengal, men from Europe, and grain from their northern settlements, while the French could receive nothing except what came laboriously by land. So the English were steadily strengthened and the French steadily weakened, and this let **Eyre Coote** establish military superiority over Lally and shut him up inside Pondicherry.

## Wandiwash and after

The decisive battle was fought at **Wandiwash (Vandavasi, Tamil Nadu) on 22 January 1760**. General Eyre Coote routed Lally and took Bussy prisoner. Lally defended **Pondicherry** for eight months before surrendering on **16 January 1761**. With the loss of Jinji (Gingee) and Mahe, French power in India fell to its lowest. Lally was taken to London as a prisoner of war, returned to France, was imprisoned and was executed in 1766.

## Treaty of Paris (1763)

The Treaty of Paris restored to the French their factories in India, but French political influence disappeared. Like the Portuguese and Dutch before them, the French now confined themselves to small enclaves and to commerce. Spectrum adds that the Dutch had already been defeated at Bidara (Biderra, near Chinsurah, also called the battle of Hooghly) in November 1759, leaving the English as the supreme European power.

## Plassey or Wandiwash?

Historians usually treat **Plassey (1757)** as the decisive event for British rule. Spectrum records the other view: the true turning point was Wandiwash, since it left the Company with no European rival in India. Plassey gave the Company a base in the richest province; Wandiwash removed its only European challenger. A safe examination sentence is: Plassey gave the Company a territorial base, and Wandiwash cleared the European field.
## Where Dupleix and Clive stand

Spectrum credits Dupleix as administrator, diplomat and planner, but records his weaknesses: an over-hopeful temper, an autocratic manner and the fact that he never led an army in the field as Lawrence or Clive did. Clive's own remark, quoted there, is that the English accomplished against the French exactly what the French had intended against the English.

### 5. Core: Mysore, Haidar Ali and the First Anglo-Mysore War

*Objective:* Explain how Haidar Ali rose, why Mysore alarmed the Company, and how the First Anglo-Mysore War ended in the Treaty of Madras.

## The kingdom and its dynasty

After the Battle of Talikota (1565) broke the Vijayanagara empire, small kingdoms emerged from its remains. In 1612 a Hindu kingdom under the **Wodeyars** arose around Mysore. Spectrum gives the ruler Chikka Krishnaraja Wodeyar II as ruling 1734-66; but two ministers, Nanjaraj (the sarvadhikari) and Devaraj (the dulwai), had reduced him to a puppet.

## Haidar Ali

Haidar Ali, born in an obscure family in 1721 (Spectrum's date; some books give 1720, verify), began as a horseman in the Mysore army under those ministers. Uneducated but shrewd and energetic, he found his chance because Mysore had been weakened by repeated Maratha and Nizam raids that demanded heavy payments. He became the de facto ruler in **1761**. NCERT, as summarised in GS-024 module 7, dates his rule 1761-82.

Spectrum credits Haidar with clear military reasoning. The Marathas were mobile, so they must be met with swift cavalry; the French-trained Nizami artillery must be met with effective artillery; and western arms must be matched by arms obtained from the same source or made with the same know-how. So he took French help to set up an **arms factory at Dindigul** and introduced western methods of training. In 1761-63 he captured Dod Ballapur, Sera, Bednur and Hoskote and subdued the Poligars (local chiefs). The Marathas under Madhavrao defeated him in 1764, 1766 and 1771, and he paid heavily for peace; after Madhavrao died in 1772 he raided them in 1774-76 and recovered all he had lost.

## Why the Company worried

Spectrum lists three anxieties: Mysore's closeness to the French, Haidar's and Tipu's control over the rich trade of the Malabar coast, and Mysore's power as a threat to English control of Madras.

## First Anglo-Mysore War (1767-69)

**Background.** After the easy success in Bengal, the English felt confident. In 1766 they made a treaty with the Nizam in which he gave them the Northern Circars in return for a promise to protect him from Haidar. Haidar already had land quarrels with the Nawab of Arcot and differences with the Marathas.

**Changing alliances.** The Nizam, the Marathas and the English allied against Haidar. He answered with diplomacy: he paid the Marathas to stay neutral, promised the Nizam a share of conquered land and made him an ally, and then joined him to attack the Nawab of Arcot.

**Course.** The war dragged for about eighteen months with no result. Haidar then changed strategy and suddenly appeared before Madras, causing panic and chaos. The English were forced to make what Spectrum calls a very humiliating treaty.

**Treaty of Madras (4 April 1769).** The terms were exchange of prisoners and mutual restitution of conquests, and a promise of English help to Haidar if another power attacked him. That last clause matters: in 1771 the Marathas attacked Mysore and the English did not help, and Haidar called this a breach of faith, which became a cause of the next war.

### 6. Core: Second and Third Anglo-Mysore Wars, Mangalore and Seringapatam

*Objective:* Give the causes, course and treaty of the Second and Third Anglo-Mysore Wars, including the terms of Seringapatam.

## Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780-84)

**Causes.** Spectrum gives four. The English did not help Haidar when the Marathas attacked in 1771, so he accused them of breaking the Treaty of Madras. The French were more useful in supplying guns, saltpetre and lead, and some French war material reached Mysore through **Mahe**, a French port on the Malabar coast. The American War of Independence had broken out and France supported the rebels against England, so Haidar's French friendship worried the English even more. Finally the English tried to capture Mahe, which Haidar regarded as under his protection, and he took this as a direct challenge to his authority.

**Course.** Haidar formed an anti-English alliance with the Marathas and the Nizam and attacked the Carnatic, capturing Arcot and defeating an English army under Colonel Baillie. Sir Eyre Coote detached the Marathas and the Nizam from him, and Haidar was defeated at **Porto Novo**. He regrouped and defeated the English again, taking their commander Braithwaite prisoner. Spectrum dates Baillie's defeat to 1781 and Porto Novo to November 1781; many standard books date Pollilur (Baillie's defeat) to September 1780 and Porto Novo to July 1781, so this is a flagged slip to verify. **Haidar Ali died of cancer on 7 December 1782.** His son Tipu carried on for a year without a clear result.

**Treaty of Mangalore (March 1784).** Both sides were tired of the inconclusive war, and each gave back the territories it had taken. This is the second treaty in a row with Mysore in which neither side gained territory.

## Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790-92)

**Cause.** Travancore bought the Dutch forts of Cranganore (Kodungallur) and Jaikottai (Ayakottai) in the Cochin state (Spectrum prints 'Jalkottal and Cannanore'; verify). Cochin was a feudatory of Tipu, so he treated the purchase as a violation of his sovereign rights, and in April 1790 he declared war on Travancore. The English sided with Travancore and attacked him.

**Course.** In 1790 Tipu defeated General Meadows. In 1791 **Cornwallis** took command, marched through Ambur and Vellore, captured Bangalore (March 1791) and went on to Seringapatam. Coimbatore fell to the English and was lost again. At last, with the help of the Marathas and the Nizam, the English attacked Seringapatam a second time. Tipu fought hard but the odds were against him.

**Treaty of Seringapatam (1792).** The victors took nearly half of Mysore's territory.

| Who | What they received (Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| English | Baramahal, Dindigul and Malabar |
| Marathas | The areas around the Tungabhadra and its tributaries |
| Nizam | The areas from the Krishna to beyond the Pennar |

In addition Tipu had to pay a war indemnity of three crore rupees, half at once and the rest in instalments, and his two sons were taken as hostages. Cornwallis's own words, which Spectrum quotes, are that he had crippled the enemy without making his friends too formidable. Many standard books add that Coorg (Kodagu) was also taken; the extract does not mention it, so verify.

### 7. Core: Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799, and Mysore after Tipu

*Objective:* Explain why the Fourth War was fought, how it ended and what happened to Mysore afterwards.

## Recovery, 1792 to 1799

Both sides used these years to recoup their losses. Tipu fulfilled every term of the Treaty of Seringapatam and got his sons released. In 1796, when the titular Wodeyar king died, Tipu did not install the minor heir (Spectrum: 'declared himself sultan'; Tipu had ruled as Sultan since 1782, verify). He also meant to avenge the humiliating treaty of 1792.

## Wellesley's reasons

In 1798 **Lord Wellesley** succeeded Sir John Shore as governor general. Spectrum calls him an imperialist to the core. He was worried by Tipu's growing friendship with the French. Spectrum says his aim was to annihilate Tipu's independent existence or force him into submission by the **subsidiary alliance** (the system explained in GS-028, to come on Day 13, not yet written; for now treat it as a treaty in which a ruler accepts a permanent English force at his own expense and gives up independent foreign dealings).

The 'charge-sheet' Wellesley drew up said that Tipu was plotting with the Nizam and the Marathas and had sent envoys to Arabia, Afghanistan and Kabul, to Zaman Shah, to Mauritius and even to Versailles. Spectrum says Tipu's explanation did not satisfy Wellesley.

## The war

The war began on **17 April 1799** and ended on **4 May 1799** with the fall of **Seringapatam**. Tipu was defeated first by General Stuart and then by General Harris; Arthur Wellesley, the governor general's brother, also took part. Spectrum says the Marathas and Nizam again helped; standard accounts say the Nizam fought alongside the Company while the Peshwa stayed out, consistent with the Marathas' refusal of Soonda and Harponelly (verify). Spectrum adds that the Marathas had been promised half of Tipu's territory, and the Nizam had already signed a subsidiary alliance. Tipu died fighting bravely. His family members were interned at Vellore and his treasures were seized.

## The new Mysore

Spectrum lists the settlement: Wellesley offered the districts of Soonda and Harponelly to the Marathas, who refused; the Nizam received Gooty and Gurramkonda; the English took Kanara, Wynad, Coimbatore, Dwaraporam and Seringapatam. A boy from the old Hindu royal family, **Krishnaraja III**, was made Maharaja of a reduced Mysore and had to accept the subsidiary alliance. Later, in 1831, Governor General William Bentinck took control of Mysore on the ground of misgovernance, and in 1881 Lord Ripon restored the state to its ruler.

### 8. Core: Tipu Sultan, administration, reforms and the verdict

*Objective:* List Tipu Sultan's military, naval, economic and cultural initiatives from Spectrum, and judge the debate over his place in history.

## The man

Spectrum says Tipu was born in November 1750 to Haidar Ali and Fatima, was well educated, and spoke Arabic, Persian, Kanarese and Urdu. He was known as the **Tiger of Mysore**.

## Military reform

Tipu gave great care to raising and maintaining an efficient force. He organised the army on the European model with Persian words of command. He used French officers to train soldiers but never let them become a pressure group. He is credited as a pioneer of **rocket technology** in India and wrote a military manual explaining how rockets are used.

## Naval plans

Like his father he saw the value of sea power. In 1796 he set up a **Board of Admiralty** and planned a fleet of 22 battleships and 20 large frigates, with three dockyards at Mangalore, Wajedabad and Molidabad. The plans did not materialise.

## Economic and cultural initiatives

He is credited as a pioneer in introducing **sericulture** (silk farming) in Mysore, and Spectrum says he is credited with starting capitalist development at a time when feudalism was prevalent. He is described as a patron of science and technology. Spectrum's extract gives no detail of his revenue system, trading depots or coinage; many standard books mention a state role in trade, but those points are outside the extract (verify).

## The French connection and political symbols

French soldiers in Seringapatam set up a **Jacobin Club** in 1797 with Tipu's support; he ordered a salute of 2,300 cannons and 500 rockets, became a member, and accepted being called Citizen Tipu. He planted a Tree of Liberty at Seringapatam. This closeness to France, a revolutionary power at war with Britain, gave Wellesley material for his charge-sheet in module 7.

## Religion and the debate over his image

Colonial historians depicted him as a bigot. Spectrum answers that this is not fully correct: he crushed the Hindu Coorgs and Nairs but also punished the Muslim Moplahs who defied him. He is reported to have demolished temples in Kerala when conquering it, yet protected temples within his own kingdom and paid for the repair of the **Sringeri temple** and the installation of the goddess Sarada's idol after a Maratha raid in 1791. The lesson is to judge past rulers not by modern yardsticks of secularism and democracy.

## First nationalist or defender of his own power?

Some writers called him the first Indian nationalist and a martyr of freedom. Spectrum quotes Mohibbul Hasan that this projects the present into the past: in his age there was no sense of nationalism, or awareness that Indians were a subject people, and Tipu fought to preserve his own power and independence. A contemporary English officer, Lieutenant Moore, described Tipu's country as cultivated, populous and flourishing. A balanced answer says Tipu was an able, modernising ruler and a determined opponent of the Company, but 'nationalist' is an anachronism.

### 9. Master: why the Company's armies won

*Objective:* Build a tested, evidence-based list of reasons for English victories, including why Indian soldiers in the Company's army fought for it.

## Spectrum's list of reasons

Spectrum's chapter 5 lists causes of British success. Each can be tested against the wars you have just studied.

1. **Better arms and strategy.** English muskets and cannons were faster and longer in range. Many Indian rulers imported arms and hired European officers, but their officers and ranks stayed imitators. Test: Haidar built an arms factory at Dindigul with French help, and Tipu used rockets, so this reason is least complete for Mysore.
2. **Discipline and regular pay.** The Company paid salaries regularly and enforced strict discipline. Many Indian rulers could not pay regularly; the Marathas sometimes diverted campaigns to collect revenue to pay troops, and relied on retinues or mercenaries who could rebel or switch sides.
3. **Civil discipline and merit.** Company officers and soldiers were placed on grounds of reliability and skill, not heredity or caste; Indian administrators were appointed by caste and personal ties.
4. **Leadership.** The English had great leaders (Clive, Hastings and others) and a bench of second-line officers such as Coote and Arthur Wellesley. Indian leaders such as Haidar and Tipu were brilliant but often lacked second-line trained men.
5. **Finance.** The Company's income paid shareholders and funded wars, and England had profits from world trade; sea power kept resources available.
6. **National pride and unity.** Spectrum says Indians lacked a unified political nationalism, so rulers often supported the English against neighbours. In the Mysore wars the Marathas and Nizam helped in 1790-92, and the Nizam in 1799 (Spectrum also names the Marathas; modules 6 and 7).
## The soldiers question

The 2022 Mains question stresses that the Company's armies were 'mostly comprising of Indian soldiers' and still outnumbered. The extracts tell us three things. Sepoys served in both armies at Wandiwash. The Company paid regular salaries and kept strict discipline. And Indian rulers' armies often used men who were not regularly paid. The extracts do not describe how the Company's sepoy regiments were recruited and organised (standard books describe the Presidency armies under European officers; verify). A good answer therefore separates the soldier from the system: the same Indian soldier fought better under a paymaster who paid on time, under drilled formations and commanders who were selected for ability.

## Testing the list with Mysore

- If better arms alone explained victory, Tipu's rockets and French-trained army should have won. They did not, because the English could combine military strength with allies, money and ships.
- The first war ended in a treaty on Haidar's terms after he split the coalition against him, and the second ended with no gain for either side; the third and fourth were won when the English had Cornwallis, Wellesley and allies.
- That shows that victory depended on coalition management, money and command, not merely on technology.

## Debates

Seeley called the conquest 'a fit of absent-mindedness'; the other school says it was planned. Spectrum finds both overstated and quotes Grover that from 1798 to 1818 British motives were consciously imperialistic, which fits Wellesley's attack on Tipu. The Carnatic reasons (sea power, bases, company structure) are in GS-025 module 9.

### 10. Exam: the 2022 Mains question, answer frames, traps and unverified facts

*Objective:* Plan a 10-mark answer, avoid the common slips in dates and treaties, and know which facts to verify outside the extracts.

## The verified question

**Mains GS I, 2022, Q2 (10 marks):** why the Company's armies, mostly Indian soldiers, won consistently against more numerous and better equipped armies of Indian rulers. A 10-mark answer is normally about 150 words (the standard word limit for 10 marks; check the paper instructions). The directive is 'give reasons', so it is a list of causes with evidence, not a narrative.

## A frame that fits

1. One-line introduction: the premise is partly true, since by 1800 the Company's sepoy-based army had defeated the French and then Mysore.
2. Organisation: pay, discipline, merit (module 9).
3. Military technology and training with the limit that Indian rulers imitated.
4. Finance and sea power (Dodwell passage in module 4; Spectrum on funds).
5. Divided opponents: allies of the English in the Mysore wars (the Nizam and the Marathas) and Indian sepoys in both armies at Wandiwash.
6. A conclusion: not one cause but a system (money, command, alliances, organisation).

## Traps

- **Date slips.** Learn Second Anglo-Mysore as 1780-84, the Spectrum summary prints 1779. Learn Wandiwash as 22 January 1760; Spectrum's summary box prints '1760-61', which merges it with the Pondicherry surrender (16 January 1761).
- **Treaty confusion.** Madras = First Anglo-Mysore (1769), Mangalore = Second (1784), Seringapatam = Third (1792). The Fourth ended with conquest, not a treaty. 'Treaty of Madras' of 1769 is not the same as the French capture of Madras in 1746 under La Bourdonnais.
- **Aix-la-Chapelle and Paris.** Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) ended the First war and returned Madras; the Treaty of Paris (1763) ended the Third.
- **Dupleix and subsidiary.** Dupleix is called the originator of the subsidiary practice by Spectrum; the formal Subsidiary Alliance system belongs to Wellesley (GS-028).

## Facts to verify outside the extracts

- The English-French agreement after Dupleix's recall is usually called the Treaty of Pondicherry (1754-55); Spectrum gives no name.
- Pollilur (Baillie's defeat) September 1780 and Porto Novo July 1781 are the dates in many standard books; Spectrum's text gives 1781 and November 1781.
- The cession of Coorg in 1792 and the details of Tipu's revenue and trade measures.
- The organisation of the Company's sepoy regiments.
- The 'prelude' of the Subsidiary Alliance: GS-028 (Day 13) is not yet written; GS-027 on Plassey and Buxar is to come on Day 12, not yet written.
