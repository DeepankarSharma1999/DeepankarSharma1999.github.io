## PH-039: Standing waves on string

Physics Paper I · Physics · Waves and oscillations

### Bridge: Waves from scratch: wavelength, frequency, wave number and phase

A **wave** is a pattern of disturbance that travels through a medium while the medium's own particles only oscillate about their rest positions. On a string the particles move up and down while the pattern moves along: a **transverse** wave (displacement perpendicular to the direction of travel). In sound in air the particles move back and forth along the direction of travel: a **longitudinal** wave (NCERT PDF p. 3).

A travelling sine wave on a string is written y(x, t) = A sin(kx − ωt). Read each symbol:

- **A** amplitude: the largest displacement (metres).
- **λ** wavelength: the distance over which the shape repeats; **k = 2π/λ** is the wave number (rad per metre) (NCERT PDF p. 6; Mathur PDF p. 494).
- **ω = 2πf** angular frequency (rad per second); **f** frequency in hertz; **τ = 1/f** period. This lesson writes τ for the period because T is the tension.
- **v = fλ = ω/k** wave speed.
- The whole bracket kx − ωt is the **phase**. A point of constant phase obeys kx = ωt + const, so x = (ω/k)t + const: it moves to the right at speed ω/k. For kx + ωt the pattern moves left. Two oscillations are **in phase** when their phases differ by 0 (or 2π) and in **opposite phase** when they differ by π (the idea of phase constant is in Upadhyaya PDF p. 379).

Example: y = 0.02 sin(10x − 2000t) in SI units has k = 10 rad/m, so λ = 2π/10 = 0.628 m; ω = 2000 rad/s, so f = 318.3 Hz and v = ω/k = 200 m/s (check: fλ = 318.3 × 0.628 = 200 m/s).

The earlier lessons used ω and phase for SHM (PH-035 modules 2 to 4); here a point of the string at fixed x simply performs SHM.

### Bridge: Partial derivatives, small angles and the sum-to-product identity

**Partial derivatives.** y depends on two variables, position x and time t. ∂y/∂x means: differentiate with respect to x while treating t as a fixed number. It is the **slope** of a photograph of the string. ∂y/∂t treats x as fixed: it is the **velocity** of one marked point of the string. Second derivatives repeat the step: ∂²y/∂x² measures how sharply the photograph bends (curvature); ∂²y/∂t² is the acceleration of a marked point. Example: for y = sin(3x) cos(5t), ∂y/∂x = 3 cos(3x) cos(5t) and ∂²y/∂t² = −25 sin(3x) cos(5t).

**Small angles.** If the string makes a small angle θ (in radians) with the x-axis, then sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ and cos θ ≈ 1 − θ²/2 ≈ 1. At θ = 0.05 rad: sin θ = 0.04998, tan θ = 0.05004, cos θ = 0.99875. Because tan θ is the slope, **sin θ ≈ ∂y/∂x** for a gently curved string. Dropping terms of order θ² is the small-amplitude assumption used in module 2.

**Two trigonometric facts.**

$$ sin(P + Q) + sin(P − Q) = 2 sin P cos Q $$

(expand both sines: the cos P sin Q terms cancel and the sin P cos Q terms add). Also the average of sin² over a whole number of half-waves is ½, so ∫ from 0 to L of sin²(nπx/L) dx = L/2 (module 5).

**Taylor step.** For small dx, f(x + dx) − f(x) ≈ f′(x) dx. With f = ∂y/∂x this gives ∂y/∂x|(x+dx) − ∂y/∂x|(x) ≈ (∂²y/∂x²) dx.

### 1. Orientation: what a standing wave is, what is asked and the notation

*Objective:* State in words what a standing wave is, list the symbols used and see how this topic links to the earlier oscillation lessons.

A **standing** (or stationary) **wave** is a vibration in which the shape stays in place and only its height changes with time. Pluck a guitar string: it vibrates in a fixed outline, some points never move (**nodes**), others swing the most (**antinodes**). The pattern does not travel along the string; that is the difference from the travelling waves of the bridge modules.

**What the examination asks.** The project maps this topic to Mains Physics Paper I clause 5 (waves and oscillations) and lists one verified past question: 2022 Q7(a), 20 marks, a guitar string vibrating in a magnetic field (module 9). The usual standard question types are: derive the wave equation or the normal modes of a string, find frequencies and node positions from L, T and μ, find the energy of a mode, compare standing with travelling waves, and find beats between two strings. Per the project brief (Q1 and Q5 compulsory, several 10-mark short parts); verify against the paper instructions.

**Symbols (used throughout).**

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| y(x, t) | sideways displacement | m |
| x | distance from the left end | m |
| T | tension | N |
| μ | linear density (mass per length) | kg/m |
| L, n | string length, mode number | m, 1, 2, 3 … |
| A, a | amplitude of each travelling wave; amplitude at an antinode, a = 2A | m |

### 2. Foundation: the wave equation on a stretched string and v = √(T/μ)

*Objective:* Derive ∂²y/∂t² = (T/μ) ∂²y/∂x² from Newton's second law for a small element, and check the wave speed by dimensions.

## Assumptions and coordinates
A string of uniform linear density μ is stretched along the x-axis to tension T and displaced sideways by y(x, t). Assumptions: (1) small amplitude, slopes ∂y/∂x ≪ 1; (2) μ uniform; T the same everywhere and constant in time (the extra stretch is second order in the slope); (3) gravity and air drag neglected (for the string S of module 4, T = 40 N, the standard sag estimate μgL²/8T is about 8 × 10⁻⁵ m; verify); (4) motion purely transverse. Coordinates: x to the right along the string, y upward; positive slope means the string rises to the right.

## The element
Take the piece between x and x + dx. Let θ be the angle between the string and the x-axis, so tan θ = ∂y/∂x. The rest of the string pulls the piece with a force of magnitude T along the string at each end.

**Horizontal.** T cos θ(x + dx) − T cos θ(x) differs from zero only at order θ², so the net horizontal force is negligible.

**Vertical.** F_y = T sin θ(x + dx) − T sin θ(x). With sin θ ≈ tan θ = ∂y/∂x (bridge B2):

F_y = T [∂y/∂x|(x+dx) − ∂y/∂x|(x)] = T (∂²y/∂x²) dx.

**Mass.** The piece has length ds = dx √(1 + (∂y/∂x)²) ≈ dx to first order, so its mass is μ dx.

**Newton's second law** (mass × acceleration = force): μ dx ∂²y/∂t² = T ∂²y/∂x² dx. Cancel dx:

$$ ∂²y/∂t² = (T/μ) ∂²y/∂x² $$

Meaning: a piece where the string curves upward (∂²y/∂x² > 0) is pulled up, and the acceleration is proportional to the curvature. Mathur PDF p. 493 shows the end of the same force argument and the same equation (its start lies before the supplied extract).

## Wave speed and checks
Write v² = T/μ, so

$$ v = √(T/μ) $$

**Dimensions:** [T] = M L T⁻², [μ] = M L⁻¹, so T/μ = L² T⁻² and v has the dimension of a velocity. NCERT (PDF p. 8) first finds v = C√(T/μ) by dimensions and says the full derivation gives C = 1. **Limits:** T → 0 gives v → 0 (a slack string cannot carry a wave); a heavier string (larger μ) is slower; v depends on the medium (T, μ), not on frequency.

## Solutions
Any y = f(x − vt) is a solution: ∂²y/∂t² = v² f″ and ∂²y/∂x² = f″. So is g(x + vt) (Mathur PDF pp. 493 to 494). For a sine wave y = A sin(kx − ωt): ∂²y/∂t² = −ω²y and ∂²y/∂x² = −k²y, so the equation holds if and only if ω = vk. The equation is linear, so a sum of solutions is a solution: this is the superposition principle used in PH-038.

**Number (string S).** L = 0.80 m, mass 3.2 g, so μ = 3.2 × 10⁻³/0.80 = 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg/m; with T = 40 N, v = √(40/0.0040) = √10⁴ = 100 m/s.

### 3. Core: two opposite waves make y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt)

*Objective:* Derive the standing wave by adding an incident and a reflected wave, and read nodes, antinodes, amplitude envelope and phase relations from it.

## Reflection supplies the second wave
Let the string occupy x ≥ 0 with a fixed end at x = 0. A wave arriving at the end travels toward −x: y_a = A sin(kx + ωt). The reflected wave travels toward +x: y_b = B sin(kx − ωt + φ). The end cannot move, so y(0, t) = 0 for all t:

A sin ωt + B sin(φ − ωt) = 0 for all t. This holds for every t only with B = A and φ = 0 (or the identical wave B = −A, φ = π), which gives A sin ωt − A sin ωt = 0. At the wall y_a = A sin ωt but y_b = −A sin ωt: the reflected wave is reversed relative to the incident one, a phase change of π (NCERT PDF p. 12; Mathur PDF p. 498 finds reflection coefficient −1 at a rigid end).

## Adding (superposition, PH-038)
y = A sin(kx + ωt) + A sin(kx − ωt). Expand each: sin kx cos ωt + cos kx sin ωt + sin kx cos ωt − cos kx sin ωt, so

$$ y(x, t) = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt) $$

(NCERT PDF p. 13; Mathur PDF p. 504 gets the same in complex form). Check: with ω = vk it satisfies the wave equation of module 2.

## Reading the result
- **No f(x ± vt) form.** kx and ωt are in separate factors, so the shape does not travel.
- **Envelope.** Each point oscillates with the same ω; the amplitude of the point at x is 2A|sin kx|, between 0 and a = 2A.
- **Nodes:** sin kx = 0 ⇒ x = mλ/2, m = 0, 1, 2 … (spacing λ/2).
- **Antinodes:** |sin kx| = 1 ⇒ x = (2m + 1)λ/4, amplitude a = 2A.
- **Phase.** sin kx changes sign at every node: all points inside one loop move together (in phase); points in neighbouring loops move in opposite phase (π). Every point passes y = 0 at the same instant (ωt = π/2, 3π/2 …) and reaches its extreme at the same instant. Velocity ∂y/∂t = −2Aω sin kx sin ωt, so an antinode has maximum speed 2Aω.

## Limiting case: imperfect reflection
If the reflected amplitude is B < A, then y = (A + B) sin kx cos ωt + (A − B) cos kx sin ωt. The second term does not vanish at the points where sin kx = 0, so the nodes have residual motion (A − B) sin ωt. Only for B = A are the nodes perfect (Mathur PDF p. 506 notes that nodal points exist only if reflection is complete).

**Alternative check (separation).** Try y = X(x) Θ(t): the wave equation gives X″/X = Θ″/(v²Θ) = −k², constant. Solutions X = sin kx, Θ = cos ωt with ω = vk: the same standing wave without mention of two travelling waves.

### 4. Core: boundary conditions, normal modes and the harmonic–overtone trap

*Objective:* Derive λ_n = 2L/n and f_n = nv/2L for a string fixed at both ends, count nodes and antinodes, and use harmonic and overtone numbering correctly.

## Boundary conditions
Fix both ends: y(0, t) = 0 and y(L, t) = 0 for all t. The first is built into y = a sin(kx) cos(ωt), a = 2A. The second needs a sin(kL) cos ωt = 0 for every t, hence

sin kL = 0 ⇒ kL = nπ, n = 1, 2, 3 …

(n = 0 means k = 0: no wave; negative n repeats a positive one). So L = n(λ/2): the string holds a whole number of half-wavelengths (NCERT PDF p. 14; Mathur PDF p. 505). Using k = 2π/λ = ω/v:

$$ λ_n = 2L/n,   f_n = v/λ_n = n v/(2L) = (n/2L) √(T/μ),   ω_n = nπv/L $$

Only these frequencies can exist as steady patterns: the boundary condition selects them (a travelling wave allows any frequency). The mode is y_n = a_n sin(nπx/L) cos(ω_n t + δ_n).

**Intuition (standard).** The fundamental period 2L/v is the time for a disturbance to run to one end and back.

## Nodes and antinodes in the nth mode
- Nodes at x = rL/n, r = 0, 1, …, n: that is **n + 1 nodes including the two ends**, n − 1 interior ones (Mathur PDF p. 506 counts the n − 1 interior).
- Antinodes at x = (2r − 1)L/(2n), r = 1 … n: **n antinodes**.
- Each loop has length L/n = λ_n/2.

## Terminology (a frequent confusion)
| Mode number n | Harmonic name | Overtone name (this string) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | fundamental = 1st harmonic | none |
| 2 | 2nd harmonic | 1st overtone |
| 3 | 3rd harmonic | 2nd overtone |
| n | nth harmonic | (n − 1)th overtone |

A **harmonic** is a frequency that is an integer multiple of the fundamental; an **overtone** is any natural frequency above the fundamental, counted from the first one above it. So the nth harmonic is the (n − 1)th overtone. Mathur PDF p. 520 uses 'overtone' loosely for the integral multiples and then names the second harmonic the first overtone; NCERT (PDF p. 14) uses harmonic numbers only. For a pipe closed at one end the overtones are 3rd, 5th … harmonics, so the first overtone is the 3rd harmonic: never convert without checking the system.

## Number (string S: L = 0.80 m, v = 100 m/s)
f₁ = 100/(2 × 0.80) = 62.5 Hz; f₂ = 125 Hz; f₃ = 187.5 Hz. For n = 3: nodes at 0, 0.267, 0.533, 0.800 m and antinodes at 0.133, 0.400, 0.667 m. A 500 Hz driver gives n = 500/62.5 = 8 (eight loops, nine nodes). **Dimensional check:** v/L has dimension T⁻¹. **Limit:** L → ∞ gives f_n → 0 and the modes crowd together into a continuum.

### 5. Core: energy in a standing wave, zero net flow and the energy of the nth mode

*Objective:* Show that no net energy crosses any section of a standing wave and derive E_n = ¼ m ω_n² a_n² = n²π²T a_n²/(4L).

**Set-up.** y = a sin(kx) cos(ωt) with a = 2A, k = nπ/L, ω = vk, v² = T/μ. Energy per unit length: kinetic ½ μ (∂y/∂t)². Potential: stretching the element by its extra length ½(∂y/∂x)² dx against tension T costs ½ T (∂y/∂x)² dx (Mathur PDF pp. 506 to 507).

## 1. Power across a section
The left part of the string pulls the right part with transverse force −T sin θ ≈ −T ∂y/∂x, so the power passing to the right is S = F_y ∂y/∂t = −T (∂y/∂x)(∂y/∂t).

∂y/∂x = a k cos kx cos ωt and ∂y/∂t = −a ω sin kx sin ωt, so

S = T a² k ω sin kx cos kx sin ωt cos ωt = ¼ T a² k ω sin(2kx) sin(2ωt).

Its average over one period τ vanishes because ⟨sin 2ωt⟩ = 0. **No net energy crosses any section.** At a node ∂y/∂t = 0 and at an antinode ∂y/∂x = 0, so S = 0 at all times there. Energy sloshes between neighbouring parts without net transfer (Mathur PDF p. 518 gets the same for sound). **Check on a travelling wave:** y = A sin(ωt − kx) gives S = T A² kω cos²(ωt − kx), average ½ T A² kω = ½ μ ω² A² v (using T = μv² and ω = vk), equal to Mathur's energy-flow result on PDF p. 499.

## 2. Energy of the mode
Kinetic: KE = ∫₀ᴸ ½ μ a² ω² sin²kx sin²ωt dx. Using ∫₀ᴸ sin²(nπx/L) dx = L/2 (B2): KE = ¼ μ L a² ω² sin²ωt.

Potential: PE = ∫₀ᴸ ½ T a² k² cos²kx cos²ωt dx = ¼ T k² L a² cos²ωt = ¼ μ L a² ω² cos²ωt, since Tk² = μω².

Sum with m = μL (mass of the string):

$$ E_n = ¼ m ω_n² a_n² = n²π² T a_n² /(4L) $$

constant in time because sin² + cos² = 1 (Mathur PDF p. 507 obtains ¼ m ω_n²(A_n² + B_n²)). In terms of the travelling-wave amplitude A (a = 2A): E = m ω² A², which is 2 × (½ μ ω² A² × L), the two travelling waves each carrying ½ μ ω² A² per unit length.

**Per loop.** Each of the n loops has L/n, and ∫ over a loop of sin² is L/2n, so each loop holds **E_n/n** and none crosses a node.

## Number and checks
String S, n = 2, a = 2.0 mm: E = 4π² × 40 × (2.0 × 10⁻³)²/(4 × 0.80) = 1.974 × 10⁻³ J (about 1.97 mJ); each loop 0.987 mJ. Cross-check ¼ m ω² a² with m = 3.2 g, ω = 2π × 125 = 785.4 rad/s gives the same. **Dimensions:** T a²/L is N·m²/m = J. **Limit:** a → 0 gives E → 0. At fixed a, E ∝ n²: higher modes cost more energy.

### 6. Core: general motion, plucked and struck strings, a free end and pipes

*Objective:* Write the general motion as a sum of normal modes, find which harmonics a plucked string lacks, and derive the modes of a string with one free end.

## General motion
The wave equation is linear and each y_n satisfies both boundary conditions, so any sum does too:

y(x, t) = Σ sin(nπx/L) [a_n cos ω_n t + b_n sin ω_n t].

Mathur PDF p. 506 says all normal modes may be present at once. **Fourier idea (standard knowledge; verify):** any shape that is zero at both ends can be written as a sum of the sin(nπx/L); the coefficients follow from a_n = (2/L)∫₀ᴸ y(x, 0) sin(nπx/L) dx, and b_n from the initial velocity. Mathur's Fourier section (PDF p. 526) treats the time version for periodic waves; the string version used here is the same idea in space. Sound quality (timbre) comes from which modes are present (NCERT PDF p. 14; Mathur PDF p. 520).

## Plucked string (standard; verify)
Pull the point x = pL aside by h and release from rest: all b_n = 0 and (checked by symbolic integration)

$$ a_n = 2h sin(nπp) / [π² n² p(1 − p)] $$

At the midpoint p = ½: a_n = (8h/π²n²) sin(nπ/2). Even n vanish (a₂ = a₄ = 0), a₁ = 0.8106h, a₃ = −a₁/9, a₅ = a₁/25. **Reason:** the midpoint is an antinode of odd modes (|sin(nπ/2)| = 1) and a node of even modes, and a mode cannot be excited at its own node. Plucking at L/3 removes the 3rd, 6th … harmonics (sin(nπ/3) = 0 when n is a multiple of 3). **Energy check:** E_n = n²π²T a_n²/4L = 16Th²/(π² n² L) for odd n; summing with Σ(odd) 1/n² = π²/8 gives 2Th²/L, the initial potential energy of the triangular shape ✓.

## Struck string (standard; verify)
A hammer giving velocity at x = pL: displacement amplitudes go as sin(nπp)/n, falling more slowly than the 1/n² of plucking, so a struck string is richer in high harmonics. The same node argument applies: no harmonic with a node at the strike point.

## String with one end fixed, one free (idealised)
Free end (a light ring on a smooth rod at x = L): no transverse force, so ∂y/∂x = 0 there, an antinode. For y = a sin kx cos ωt: ∂y/∂x|_L = a k cos kL cos ωt = 0 ⇒ cos kL = 0 ⇒ kL = (2n − 1)π/2, n = 1, 2 …

$$ λ = 4L/(2n − 1),   f_n = (2n − 1) v/(4L) $$

odd harmonics only, with fundamental v/4L: half that of the fixed–fixed string of the same L. Mathur PDF p. 498 shows reflection at a free end keeps the sign (no phase change); the rod cases (PDF pp. 521 to 522) and NCERT's pipe (PDF p. 15) give the same pattern; for a string this is standard knowledge, flagged.

## Pipes (contrast only)
The mathematics repeats for sound in an air column, with v the speed of sound: a closed end is a displacement node, an open end an antinode; a closed–open pipe gives odd harmonics f = (2n − 1)v/4L, an open–open pipe all harmonics f = nv/2L (NCERT PDF pp. 14 to 15, Example 14.5).

## Resonance link
Drive the string at f near f_n and mode n resonates (PH-037); a driver at a node of mode n cannot excite it.

### 7. Master: scaling laws, tuning, beats, Melde's experiment and the travelling-wave comparison

*Objective:* Predict how f_n changes with T, L and μ, tune strings with beats, and state the standard laboratory arrangements with their relations.

## Scaling
f_n = (n/2L)√(T/μ). So f ∝ 1/L (T, μ fixed), f ∝ √T, f ∝ 1/√μ; for wires of one material μ = ρπr², so f ∝ 1/r. Using string S (f₁ = 62.5 Hz, L = 0.80 m, T = 40 N):
- T × 1.21 gives f × √1.21 = 1.10: 68.75 Hz.
- Pressing at L/2 (L → 0.40 m): f₁ = 125 Hz.
- An octave (f × 2) by tension needs T × 4 = 160 N: v = 200 m/s, f₁ = 125 Hz ✓.
- A semitone in equal temperament is a ratio 2^(1/12) = 1.0595 (standard; verify): tension × 2^(1/6) = 1.1225, or length × 0.9439.

**Units trap:** 0.040 g/cm = 0.040 × 10⁻³ kg/10⁻² m = 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg/m. Mixing grams with newtons gives answers wrong by √1000 or more.

## Tuning with beats (PH-038 module 8)
Two strings at 440.0 Hz, one tension raised 2.0%: f′ = 440√1.02 = 444.38 Hz, giving 4.38 beats per second; first order: δf/f = ½ δT/T, so 0.5 × 0.020 × 440 = 4.4. Tighten or slacken until the beats vanish.

## Melde's experiment and the sonometer (standard; not in the supplied extracts; verify)
In Melde's arrangement a thread of length L is driven by a tuning fork and loaded with weights for tension T. **Transverse** arrangement (prong at right angles to the thread): the thread vibrates at the fork frequency f = (p/2L)√(T/μ) with p loops; hence T p² = 4f²L²μ is constant, a handy test. **Longitudinal** arrangement (prong along the thread): the thread vibrates at half the fork frequency: thread frequency f/2 = (p/2L)√(T/μ), so the fork frequency is f = (p/L)√(T/μ). In the sonometer a wire passes over two bridges at a variable distance L under weights; resonance with a fork of frequency f is shown by a paper rider falling off at the antinodes, and L f = const at fixed T and μ. A driven string also shows resonance (PH-037).

## Standing versus travelling
| Feature | Travelling wave | Standing wave on a fixed string |
|---|---|---|
| Form | f(x − vt) | 2A sin kx cos ωt |
| Amplitude | same for all points | 2A|sin kx|, zero at nodes |
| Phase | changes along the string | same within a loop, π between loops |
| Net energy flow | ½ μ ω² A² v | zero |
| Allowed frequencies | any | f_n = nv/2L only |

(compare Mathur PDF pp. 518 to 519).

### 9. Practice: the verified 2022 Q7(a) analysed

*Objective:* Set up a standing-wave mode inside an electromagnetic induction problem and finish it with the integral.

**The question (verified list, 2022 Physics Optional I, Q7(a), 20 marks):** a metal guitar string, L = 70 cm, fundamental 246.94 Hz, uniform B = 10 T perpendicular to the plane of vibration, sinusoidal mode shape, maximum displacement 3 mm at the centre. Find the maximum emf across the string and when it occurs; then the emf for the second harmonic. **This is analysis, not an official solution or key.**

## Set-up
Fundamental: y = A sin(πx/L) cos ωt, A = 3 mm, ω = 2π × 246.94 = 1551.6 rad/s. Velocity ∂y/∂t = −Aω sin(πx/L) sin ωt, in the plane of vibration (y direction). B is along z. The force per unit charge v × B = (∂y/∂t) B along x: **along the string**, so the emf of an element dx is dε = B (∂y/∂t) dx (sign convention: positive along +x).

## Fundamental
ε(t) = B ∫₀ᴸ ∂y/∂t dx = −B A ω sin ωt ∫₀ᴸ sin(πx/L) dx = −B A ω (2L/π) sin ωt.

Maximum:

$$ |ε|max = 2 B A ω L/π = 2 × 10 × 0.003 × 1551.6 × 0.70/π = 20.7 V $$

It occurs when |sin ωt| = 1, i.e. t = τ/4 = 1/(4 × 246.94) = 1.01 ms after the instant of maximum displacement, when the string passes through its straight position at greatest speed (and is zero at the instants of maximum displacement).

**Alternative:** flux. The area between the string and its rest line gives Φ = B ∫y dx = B A (2L/π) cos ωt and ε = −dΦ/dt: the same magnitude.

## Second harmonic
y = A₂ sin(2πx/L) cos(2ωt). ∫₀ᴸ sin(2πx/L) dx = 0 (two equal loops, opposite signs), so **ε = 0 at all times** between the ends. Each half has an emf of magnitude B ω₂ A₂ L/π (20.7 V if A₂ were also 3 mm; the question gives only the fundamental's amplitude) and the two are equal and opposite. Zero net emf holds for the ideal sinusoidal mode with the two ends as terminals; any asymmetry in a real mode shape gives a small non-zero value (standard; verify).

## Traps
Using B v_max L (32.6 V) over-estimates by π/2 because different points move at different speeds; confusing the amplitude 3 mm of the fundamental with the second harmonic; treating the string as if it had a net emf for any mode with an even number of loops (net zero by antisymmetry); using ω = 2πf with f the second harmonic for the fundamental part.

**Extra assumptions to state:** B uniform; string treated as a conductor in an open circuit; magnetic damping and the field of induced currents ignored.

### Notes: Key equations

- Wave equation on a string: ∂²y/∂t² = (T/μ) ∂²y/∂x², v = √(T/μ); assumptions: small slopes, uniform μ, constant T, no gravity or drag.
- Travelling wave: y = A sin(kx − ωt), k = 2π/λ, ω = 2πf = vk, v = fλ.
- Standing wave: y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt) = a sin(kx) cos(ωt), a = 2A; nodes x = mλ/2, antinodes x = (2m + 1)λ/4.
- Fixed–fixed string: kL = nπ, λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/2L = (n/2L)√(T/μ), n = 1, 2, 3 …; n + 1 nodes including ends, n antinodes.
- Energy of nth mode: E_n = ¼ m ω_n² a_n² = n²π²T a_n²/(4L); per loop E_n/n; time-average power across any section is zero.
- Plucked at x = pL from rest: a_n = 2h sin(nπp)/[π² n² p(1 − p)] (standard; verify). Fixed–free: f_n = (2n − 1)v/4L (standard for strings; verify).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: wave equation

- 1. Assumptions and coordinates. 2. Element between x and x + dx, tension T at both ends. 3. Horizontal force ≈ 0 (order θ²). 4. Vertical force T(sin θ₂ − sin θ₁) ≈ T (∂²y/∂x²) dx. 5. Mass μ dx. 6. Newton: μ ∂²y/∂t² = T ∂²y/∂x². 7. v = √(T/μ) and the dimension check.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: standing wave and modes

- 1. Incident A sin(kx + ωt), reflected A sin(kx − ωt) from y(0, t) = 0 (phase π). 2. Add with sin(P + Q) + sin(P − Q) = 2 sin P cos Q. 3. y = 2A sin kx cos ωt. 4. y(L, t) = 0 gives sin kL = 0, kL = nπ. 5. λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/2L. 6. Nodes and antinodes; sketch n = 1, 2, 3.

### Notes: Boundary conditions and mode patterns

- Pipe rows are contrast only (NCERT PDF pp. 14 to 15); the fixed–free string row is standard knowledge, verify.

### Notes: Harmonic versus overtone

- nth harmonic = (n − 1)th overtone for a fixed–fixed string; the fundamental is the first harmonic and has no overtone number.
- For a closed–open pipe the first overtone is the 3rd harmonic, so never convert numbering without checking the system.
- Amplitude at an antinode is a = 2A, not A; loop length is L/n, not L.
- Nodes: n + 1 including the two ends; n − 1 interior.

### Notes: Scaling laws and numbers

- f ∝ 1/L, f ∝ √T, f ∝ 1/√μ, f ∝ 1/r for one material.
- String S: L = 0.80 m, μ = 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg/m, T = 40 N: v = 100 m/s, f₁ = 62.5 Hz.
- Octave by tension: T × 4. Semitone (equal temperament, verify): f × 1.0595, T × 1.1225.
- 1 g/cm = 0.1 kg/m.

### Notes: Mode shapes

- See the diagram of module 4: N at the ends and at x = rL/n; A at (2r − 1)L/2n; adjacent loops opposite in sign.

### Core concepts

- A standing wave is two equal waves in opposite directions: the shape is fixed in space, each point does SHM with its own amplitude.
- Boundary conditions pick the allowed frequencies (normal modes).
- No net energy travels along a standing wave; energy shuttles between kinetic and potential inside each loop.

### Practice

**Q.** Define a node and an antinode on a string.

**A.** A node is a point of permanently zero displacement; an antinode is a point of maximum amplitude, midway between nodes.

**Q.** A string has μ = 5.0 × 10⁻³ kg/m and T = 20 N. Find v.

**A.** v = √(20/0.0050) = √4000 = 63.2 m/s.

**Q.** State the distance between adjacent nodes in terms of λ.

**A.** λ/2.

**Q.** How many antinodes has the 5th harmonic of a fixed–fixed string?

**A.** Five antinodes (and six nodes including the ends).

**Q.** Why does the standing wave 2A sin kx cos ωt not move?

**A.** kx and ωt are in separate factors, so the shape sin kx is fixed in space and only its height changes; a travelling wave depends on kx − ωt.

**Q.** A string is plucked exactly at a node of the 4th mode. Is the 4th mode present?

**A.** No: a mode cannot be excited at a point where it has zero displacement and the string starts from rest there; its coefficient a_n is proportional to sin(nπp) = 0.

**Q.** Why do the frequencies of a vibrating string form a harmonic series while a travelling wave has no restriction?

**A.** The two fixed-end conditions require L = nλ/2, which forces λ_n = 2L/n and f_n = nv/2L; a travelling wave on an infinite string has no boundary conditions.

**Q.** A guitar string of L = 0.65 m sounds f₁ = 110 Hz. A finger presses at 0.5 of its length. What is the new fundamental?

**A.** The vibrating length halves; f ∝ 1/L, so f₁ becomes 220 Hz (v = 2Lf = 143 m/s unchanged).

**A.** Small slopes; element dx with tension T at both ends; vertical force T ∂²y/∂x² dx; mass μ dx; μ ∂²y/∂t² = T ∂²y/∂x²; v² = T/μ.

**Q.** State f_n, the nodes and antinodes for a string fixed at both ends and the overtone number of the nth harmonic.

**A.** f_n = nv/2L; n + 1 nodes including ends, n antinodes; nth harmonic is the (n − 1)th overtone.

**Q.** Give E_n for the nth mode and the time-average power across a section.

**A.** E_n = ¼ m ω_n² a_n² = n²π²T a_n²/4L; the time-average power is zero.

**Q.** Why does a string plucked at its midpoint lack even harmonics?

**A.** The midpoint is a node of every even mode and a mode cannot be excited at its own node.
