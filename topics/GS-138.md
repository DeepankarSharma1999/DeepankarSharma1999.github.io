## GS-138: Urban hierarchy and megacities

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Geography · Human/economic/world

*Goal:* Explain Urban hierarchy and megacities and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### The lower rungs: villages, periodic markets and market towns

Rural settlements depend on land-based primary work and are smaller and more widely spaced; urban settlements process materials and provide services. The two are tied together through transport and communication. At the base of the ladder sit periodic markets, which move between places on fixed days so that traders can collect scattered demand, and rural marketing centres with wholesale mandis and retail shops but few professional services. Above them, urban marketing centres provide both ordinary and specialised goods, along with doctors, lawyers, consultants and teachers. NCERT's distinction between widespread low-order services such as grocery shops and concentrated high-order ones such as accountants and physicians gives a basis for ranking centres by the services they offer.

### The upper rungs: specialised towns, satellites and metropolises

Many Indian towns are known for a dominant function: administrative (Chandigarh, Bhopal, Gandhinagar), industrial (Jamshedpur, Bhilai, Coimbatore), transport (Kandla, Itarsi, Katni), commercial (Kolkata, Saharanpur), mining (Jharia, Digboi, Singrauli), garrison (Ambala, Mhow), educational (Roorkee, Pilani), religious (Varanasi, Tirupati) and tourist (Shimla, Ooty). The top of the hierarchy behaves differently: as cities become metropolises, functions intertwine and they turn multifunctional. Around the largest centres, older towns grow into satellites - Ghaziabad, Rohtak and Gurugram around Delhi. The Smart Cities Mission seeks compact, replicable models of better infrastructure and services that can guide other cities.

### Megacity pressures: migration, slums and waste

Urban population grows through natural increase, net in-migration and reclassification of rural areas. Because smaller towns offer little work, many poor migrants head straight for mega cities and take low-paid, insecure informal jobs. NCERT stresses that Indian cities are internally very unequal: high-income localities with full infrastructure sit beside slums and jhuggi clusters on degraded land, lacking water, toilets, light and drainage, with overcrowded lanes and health hazards. Dharavi in Mumbai, described as Asia's largest slum, is also a centre of skilled craft production. Waste is another stress: metros collect most of their solid waste, but in many other towns 30-50% is left uncollected, fouling streets and open spaces.

### Worked example

Question: 'Why do rural migrants in India often bypass small towns and move to megacities, and with what consequences?' Frame: hierarchy logic - thin demand and few jobs lower down; concentration of high-order services, industry and informal work at the top; consequences - slums on degraded land, informal low-wage work, unequal infrastructure, uncollected waste in smaller cities; remedy - strengthen intermediate towns and satellite towns, Smart Cities-type service upgrades.

### Recall

- - **q:** What are periodic markets?
  - **a:** Rural markets held on specified days that rotate among places where regular markets are absent, letting traders serve a wide area.
- - **q:** Name three satellite towns of Delhi listed by NCERT.
  - **a:** Ghaziabad, Rohtak and Gurugram.
- - **q:** What share of world population is urban now and by 2050 per NCERT?
  - **a:** About 55% now, estimated 68% by 2050.

### Common trap

Judging a centre's place in the settlement system by population alone; NCERT distinguishes centres by their functions and the order of services they offer, and notes that big cities turn multifunctional.
