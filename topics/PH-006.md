### Bridge: Building a body from rings and discs: choosing the element

Every result in this lesson is an addition of tiny pieces, and the only real skill is **choosing a piece whose contribution is easy to write down**. A *ring* of radius x and width dx has area (2πx)(dx), because the ring is a strip of length 2πx (its circumference) and thickness dx. If the surface density (mass per unit area) is σ, its mass is dm = σ 2πx dx. A *disc* is a stack of rings from x = 0 to x = R. A solid sphere is a stack of *shells*, each of radius s and thickness ds, with volume 4πs² ds and mass dm = ρ(s) 4πs² ds.

The reason rings are useful: every point of a ring sits at the **same distance** from a point on the ring's axis, so the potential of the ring is just −G dm divided by that one distance, and no angular integral is needed. In the same way every point of a shell is at one distance from the centre.

When you integrate, the **limits** are the first and last ring (or shell). For a disc they are x = 0 and x = R. Always write the limits next to the integral sign before substituting.

A useful habit: after an integral, put R → 0 (the body disappears) or the field point far away, and check that you recover a point mass. Module 4 uses this check on the disc and the ring.

### Bridge: Binomial expansion for small h/R

Several results below involve a distance such as R + h with h much smaller than R. Write R + h = R(1 + ε) with ε = h/R. For small ε the **binomial expansion** says

$$ (1 + ε)ⁿ = 1 + nε + n(n−1)ε²/2 + n(n−1)(n−2)ε³/6 + … $$

for any number n (whole, negative or fraction). Keep as many terms as the accuracy you need. The two cases you need are n = −1 and n = −2:

$$ 1/(1 + ε) = 1 − ε + ε² − …,    1/(1 + ε)² = 1 − 2ε + 3ε² − … $$

and n = ½: √(1 + ε) ≈ 1 + ε/2 − ε²/8.

Worked check with numbers: ε = 0.01. Then 1/(1.01) = 0.990099…, while 1 − ε = 0.99 and 1 − ε + ε² = 0.9901. Each extra term improves the answer. When an answer says "first correction", it means the first term beyond the leading one, here −ε.

Always state the condition (ε ≪ 1) when you use the expansion; an examiner will look for it.

### Bridge: Adding fields as vectors and 'subtracting' a hole

Gravity obeys **superposition**: the field at a point due to several masses is the vector sum of the fields each mass would produce alone. Gravitational potential is a scalar, so potentials simply add as numbers. Both statements are consequences of Newton's law being linear in the source mass (NCERT states the superposition principle for the potential energy of a system, extract page 9).

Vectors: if mass A gives field g_A = (−3, 0) N/kg and mass B gives g_B = (0, −4) N/kg at the same point, the total is (−3, −4), of magnitude 5 N/kg. Directions matter: two equal fields in opposite directions cancel.

The new idea: a body with a hole can be treated as a **complete body plus a body of negative density filling the hole**. This is legitimate because adding the filler (density +ρ) and the 'negative filler' (density −ρ) in the same region leaves density 0, a hole. The field of a negative mass points *away* from it. Write field of the holed body = field of the full body − field of the filler. Module 2 uses this to prove that the field inside a spherical hole is uniform.

## Terms used throughout

- **Gravitational field g**: force per unit mass on a small test mass; points toward the attracting matter. **Potential V**: potential energy per unit mass, with V = 0 at infinity, so V is negative everywhere near matter. They are tied by g (vector) = −dV/dr in the radial direction.
- **Density profile ρ(r)**: how density depends on the distance r from the centre. **Axis**: the line through the centre of a ring or disc, perpendicular to its plane.
- **Equipotential**: a surface on which V is constant.

### 2. Foundation: superposition and the spherical cavity

*Objective:* Prove that the field inside a spherical cavity of a uniform sphere is uniform, (4/3)πGρd, and use it outside as well.

## Superposition

A hole is a region of zero density. A uniform sphere with a spherical hole is therefore the superposition of (i) the full uniform sphere of density ρ and (ii) a smaller sphere of density **−ρ** in the hole's place (bridge B3). The field at any point P is

$$ g(holed) = g(full) + g(filler of density −ρ) = g(full) − g(filler of density +ρ) $$

## Field inside the hole: derivation

**Set-up.** Take the centre of the large sphere (radius R, density ρ) as origin O. The hole has radius a and its centre C is at the vector **d** from O (so |d| = OC, with d + a ≤ R: the cavity lies inside the sphere and may touch its surface). Let **r** be the position vector of the field point P measured from O.

Step 1: the field inside a uniform sphere, in vector form (PH-008 module 4): g_full = −(4πGρ/3) **r**. It points to O and has magnitude (4πGρ/3) r. Check: at r = R it equals GM/R², because M = (4/3)πρR³.

Step 2: the filler is a uniform sphere of density +ρ centred on C. The point P is at position **r − d** relative to C. By the same formula, g_filler = −(4πGρ/3)(**r − d**).

Step 3: subtract:

$$ g(P) = −(4πGρ/3) r + (4πGρ/3)(r − d) = −(4πGρ/3) d $$

The vector **r** cancels, so the field is the **same at every point of the cavity**. Its magnitude is (4/3)πGρ d, it is directed from C toward O (the direction of −d), and it does **not depend on the radius a** of the cavity.

**Checks.** (i) Dimensions: Gρ has units s⁻², times a length gives m s⁻². (ii) Limit d → 0: the cavity is concentric, so the field is zero at all points of the cavity, which agrees with the shell theorem (a concentric hollow is surrounded by shells only). (iii) The result holds for a hole of any radius a at fixed d.

## Using the result

Let g₀ = GM/R² = (4/3)πGρR be the surface value. Then g(cavity) = g₀ d/R: for a cavity centred halfway to the surface (d = R/2) the field in it is g₀/2, pointing toward the big centre.

**Outside the sphere.** At a point on the line OC at distance x from O the holed sphere gives

$$ g = GM/x² − G(M a³/R³)/(x − d)² $$

because the filler has mass M a³/R³ (volume ratio) and sits at distance x − d from the point. Take a = R/2, d = R/2: the filler has mass M/8. At the surface point on the near side (x = R) the filler is R/2 away: g = (GM/R²)(1 − (1/8)/(1/4)) = g₀(1 − 1/2) = **g₀/2**. (Here d + a = R, so this near point is also on the rim of the cavity; the outside formula gives g₀/2, equal to the uniform in-cavity value g₀/2, so the field is continuous there.) On the far side (x = −R, which is 3R/2 from C): g = g₀(1 − (1/8)/(9/4)) = g₀(1 − 1/18) = **17 g₀/18**.

Keep the two pictures apart. Inside the cavity the field is uniform; outside, the missing mass removes part of the pull toward the cavity side, so the near-side surface field is reduced a lot and the far-side field is only slightly reduced.

### 3. Core: spheres whose density changes with radius

*Objective:* Find g(r) and V(r) for a spherical body with density ρ(r) by the enclosed-mass and shell methods, with power-law, linear and exponential examples.

## The two tools

For a spherically symmetric body of density ρ(r) and total radius R, define the **enclosed mass**

$$ M(r) = ∫₀^r 4π s² ρ(s) ds $$

**Field (by Gauss's law or by the shell theorem).** Gauss's law for gravitation (PH-008 module 5) with a sphere of radius r gives 4πr² g = 4πG M(r), so

$$ g(r) = G M(r)/r²    (inward) $$

at every r, inside or outside. Outside, M(r) = M and g = GM/r².

**Potential (by shells).** A shell of radius s < r lies inside the point and acts like a point mass at the centre; a shell of radius s > r contains the point and gives the constant −G dm/s (PH-008 module 3). Adding:

$$ V(r) = −G M(r)/r − 4πG ∫_r^R ρ(s) s ds $$

**Consistency check.** Differentiate: d/dr[−G M(r)/r] = G M/r² − 4πG ρ r (using dM/dr = 4πr²ρ) and d/dr[−4πG ∫_r^R ρ s ds] = +4πG ρ r. The sum is G M(r)/r² = g, as required for g = dV/dr in magnitude (field points inward, V increases outward).

**Equivalent form.** Since V(R) = −GM/R and V rises by ∫ g dr, V(r) = −GM/R − ∫_r^R g(s) ds.

## Example 1: power law ρ = ρ₀ (r/R)^k, k > −2

M(r) = ∫ 4π s² ρ₀ (s/R)^k ds = 4πρ₀ r^(k+3)/((k+3)R^k). With total mass M = 4πρ₀R³/(k+3), M(r) = M (r/R)^(k+3). Then, with x = r/R,

$$ g = (GM/R²) x^(k+1),    V = −(GM/R)[1 + (1 − x^(k+2))/(k+2)]  (x ≤ 1) $$

For k = 0 this gives V = −GM(3 − x²)/(2R), the uniform sphere. For **k = −1** (density ∝ 1/r, a centrally concentrated body) g = GM/R² is **constant** inside and V = −(GM/R)(2 − x) is linear. The centre potential is V(0) = −(GM/R)(k + 3)/(k + 2): −1.5 GM/R for k = 0 and −2 GM/R for k = −1.

## Example 2: linear fall-off ρ = ρ₀(1 − r/R)

M(r) = 4πρ₀(r³/3 − r⁴/(4R)), M = πρ₀R³/3. Then g(r) = 4πGρ₀(r/3 − r²/(4R)). Set dg/dr = 0: 1/3 − r/(2R) = 0, so g is **largest inside at r = 2R/3**, where g_max = 4πGρ₀R/9. The surface value is g_s = GM/R² = πGρ₀R/3, so g_max/g_s = 4/3. The centre potential: V(0) = −GM/R − ∫₀^R g ds = −GM/R − 4πGρ₀R²/12 = −2GM/R (using GM/R = πGρ₀R²/3).

## Example 3: exponential cloud ρ = ρ₀ e^(−r/λ)

Let x = r/λ. Integrating by parts twice, ∫₀^x s² e^(−s) ds = 2 − e^(−x)(x² + 2x + 2), so

$$ M(r) = 4πρ₀λ³ [2 − e^(−x)(x² + 2x + 2)],    M(∞) = 8πρ₀λ³ $$

g = GM(r)/r². The centre potential comes straight from the shell sum: V(0) = −4πG∫₀^∞ ρ s ds = −4πGρ₀λ² (= −GM(∞)/(2λ)). The field peaks where x M′ = 2M, found numerically at x ≈ 1.45. The fraction of the mass inside r = λ is M(λ)/M(∞) = 1 − 5/(2e) = 0.080, only 8.0%.

**Python check** (done for this lesson): for ρ₀ = 2000 kg m⁻³, λ = 10⁶ m, numerical quadrature gives V(0) = −1.6774 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ (G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²), equal to −4πGρ₀λ² = −1.67736 × 10⁶ to six figures (the integral taken to 80λ); and g_max = 0.285 m s⁻² at x = 1.451.

## Reading the three results

A centrally concentrated body has a *deeper* central potential and a flatter or even constant interior field. This is why the real Earth (dense core) has g roughly constant through the mantle, then falling toward zero in the core, rather than falling linearly to zero (flag: the real profile is from seismic models, not from the extracts; verify).

### 4. Core: a ring and a disc on the axis

*Objective:* Derive the potential and field on the axis of a ring and a disc by integration with correct limits, and obtain the far-field and infinite-plane limits.

## Set-up and sign convention

Take a thin uniform **ring** of radius a and mass M, or a thin uniform **disc** of radius R, surface density σ (mass M = σπR²). Let the z axis run along the axis, origin at the centre, and take field point P at z > 0. Potential is zero at infinity; g_z is the signed z-component of the field, negative because gravity points toward the body (toward −z). Elements: bridge B1.

## Ring on the axis

Every element dm of the ring is at distance √(a² + z²) from P. The potential is the plain sum:

$$ V(z) = −G ∫ dm/√(a² + z²) = −GM/√(a² + z²) $$

The field is g_z = −dV/dz. Differentiate (chain rule): dV/dz = GM z (a² + z²)^(−3/2), so

$$ g_z = −GM z/(a² + z²)^(3/2) $$

(Check by direct addition: each element pulls along the line to P, with axial component G dm cosθ/(a² + z²), cosθ = z/√(a² + z²); the sideways components of opposite elements cancel by symmetry. Summing gives the same result.)

**Limits.** z ≫ a: V → −GM/z and g → −GM/z², a point mass. z = 0: g_z = 0, as the pulls from all sides cancel. **Maximum field:** d/dz[z(a² + z²)^(−3/2)] = (a² + z²)^(−5/2)(a² − 2z²) = 0 gives z = a/√2 and

$$ |g|_max = 2GM/(3√3 a²) ≈ 0.385 GM/a² $$

Numbers: M = 20 kg, a = 0.5 m, G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹: z = 0.354 m, |g|_max = 2.055 × 10⁻⁹ m s⁻².

**Small oscillations.** Near the centre (z ≪ a), g_z ≈ −GM z/a³: a restoring force proportional to z, so a bead on the axis oscillates in simple harmonic motion with ω² = GM/a³. For the numbers above ω = 1.033 × 10⁻⁴ rad s⁻¹ and T = 6.08 × 10⁴ s (about 16.9 hours).

## Disc on the axis

Divide the disc into rings of radius x and width dx (0 ≤ x ≤ R) with dm = σ 2πx dx. The ring distance to P is √(x² + z²). Potential, limits x = 0 to R:

$$ V(z) = −2πGσ ∫₀^R x dx/√(x² + z²) = −2πGσ [√(x² + z²)]₀^R = −2πGσ(√(R² + z²) − z) $$

Field: either differentiate, g_z = −dV/dz = −2πGσ(1 − z/√(R² + z²)), or integrate the ring field ∫₀^R G σ 2πx z dx/(x² + z²)^(3/2) = 2πGσ z [−1/√(x² + z²)]₀^R = 2πGσ(1 − z/√(R² + z²)) toward the disc. Both agree. Mathur's section 12.17 writes the same in terms of the angle α subtended by the disc radius, g = 2πGσ(1 − cos α) (extract pages 663-664).

**Far field (binomial, bridge B2), z ≫ R.** √(R² + z²) = z(1 + R²/2z² − R⁴/8z⁴ + …), so V ≈ −2πGσ(R²/2z − R⁴/8z³) = −(GM/z)(1 − R²/4z²) and g ≈ (GM/z²)(1 − 3R²/4z²). The leading term is a point mass M = σπR²; the first correction is small when z = 5R (the exact V is 1.0% below the point-mass value and the exact g is 3.0% below it).

**Infinite plane (R → ∞).** The field tends to the constant 2πGσ toward the plane, independent of distance (Mathur section 12.18). Across the plane the field reverses from +2πGσ to −2πGσ: a jump of 4πGσ in the field across a surface of density σ, the same kind of discontinuity as at a shell (PH-008 module 3).

**Numbers for a disc:** M = 100 kg, R = 2 m, z = 1 m: σ = 7.958 kg m⁻²; V = −4.125 × 10⁻⁹ J kg⁻¹ (a point mass would give −6.674 × 10⁻⁹); |g| = 1.845 × 10⁻⁹ m s⁻² (point mass 6.674 × 10⁻⁹). Near the disc the point-mass estimate is poor, as expected.

### 5. Core: concentric shells, thick hollow spheres, force on a particle

*Objective:* Combine shells by superposition, find the field and the constant potential inside a thick hollow sphere, and treat force on a test particle.

## Two concentric shells

Shell 1: mass M₁, radius a. Shell 2: mass M₂, radius b > a. Superposition of the shell results (PH-008 module 3) gives three regions, with V = 0 at infinity:

| Region | g (inward) | V |
|---|---|---|
| r < a | 0 | −G(M₁/a + M₂/b) |
| a < r < b | G M₁/r² | −G(M₁/r + M₂/b) |
| r > b | G(M₁ + M₂)/r² | −G(M₁ + M₂)/r |

The outer shell contributes only a constant to V inside it; the inner shell's field is exactly that of a point mass M₁ in the middle region. V is continuous at r = a and at r = b (check by substituting the boundary values). The field is not: across a thin shell of mass M and radius R it jumps by 4πGσ = GM/R² (PH-008 module 3), so g jumps from 0 to GM₁/a² at r = a and by GM₂/b² at r = b.

## A thick uniform hollow sphere (inner radius a, outer radius b, density ρ)

Mass M = (4π/3)ρ(b³ − a³). Use g = G M_enc/r²:

- r < a: M_enc = 0, so g = 0 and the cavity is field-free.
- a < r < b: M_enc = (4π/3)ρ(r³ − a³), so g = (4πGρ/3)(r³ − a³)/r², rising from 0 at r = a.
- r > b: g = GM/r².

The potential in the cavity is the constant V(a). Compute it with the shell sum (module 3) at r = a: M_enc = 0 and

$$ V(a) = −4πGρ ∫_a^b s ds = −2πGρ (b² − a²) = −(3GM/2)(b² − a²)/(b³ − a³) $$

**Limits.** a → 0 gives −3GM/(2b), the centre of a uniform sphere. a → b: since (b² − a²)/(b³ − a³) → 2/(3b), V → −GM/b, the thin shell. Both agree with PH-008.

## Force on a particle

A test particle of mass m at distance r from the centre of a spherically symmetric body feels the force F = m g(r), along the radius. Inside a uniform sphere, F = −(GMm/R³) r: a **linear restoring force with force constant k = GMm/R³**, which is the root of the tunnel result in module 7. By Newton's third law the sphere feels the same force in the opposite direction; the sphere's centre of mass accelerates by m/M times as much, a small correction when m ≪ M (compare PH-009 on the reduced mass).

## Worked picture: a particle at the centre of a hollow shell

Inside a shell the field of the shell is zero, but a particle there still feels any *outside* mass. A shell does not shield its interior from outside bodies (NCERT Points to Ponder 9, extract page 15). Superposition lets you add the external field to zero.

**Numbers.** M₁ = M, a = R, M₂ = 3M, b = 2R: at r = 1.5R the field is GM/(2.25R²) = 0.444 g₀ (g₀ = GM/R²), and at r = 3R it is 4GM/(9R²) = 0.444 g₀ too (an accidental equality). Inside r < R the field is zero and V = −G(M/R + 3M/2R) = −2.5 GM/R.

### 6. Core: potential energy above the Earth, the mgh limit and variations of g

*Objective:* Derive ΔU = mgh/(1 + h/R) and its first correction, and the variations of g with altitude, depth and latitude.

## Potential energy of a mass above the Earth

With V = 0 at infinity (PH-008 module 2; NCERT section 7.7), the potential energy of a mass m at distance r ≥ R from the centre of the Earth is U(r) = −GMm/r. To raise the mass from the surface (r = R) to height h (r = R + h) the work done is

$$ ΔU = −GMm/(R + h) + GMm/R = GMm h/(R(R + h)) $$

With g = GM/R² this is **ΔU = mgh/(1 + h/R)**.

## The mgh limit and its first correction

For h ≪ R use the binomial expansion (bridge B2): 1/(1 + h/R) = 1 − h/R + (h/R)² − …, so

$$ ΔU = mgh [1 − h/R + (h/R)² − …] $$

The leading term is the school formula mgh. The first correction is −mgh · h/R: **mgh overestimates** the energy because g decreases with height. The fractional error of mgh is about h/R.

**Numbers:** R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m, g = 9.81 m s⁻², m = 1 kg, h = 100 km. Exact ΔU = 9.658 × 10⁵ J; mgh = 9.810 × 10⁵ J (1.57% too large); mgh(1 − h/R) = 9.656 × 10⁵ J, within 0.02% of exact. mgh is good to 1% only up to h ≈ 64 km (when 1/(1 + h/R) = 0.99). At h = R, ΔU = mgR/2, half of mgh.

## Variation of g

**With altitude.** g(h) = GM/(R + h)² = g₀(1 + h/R)⁻² ≈ g₀(1 − 2h/R) (NCERT eq. 7.15, extract page 7). g falls by 1% at h ≈ 32.1 km (the first-order estimate gives 31.9 km).

**With depth, uniform Earth.** At depth d, only the sphere of radius R − d attracts: g(d) = g₀(1 − d/R) (NCERT eq. in section 7.6, extract pages 7-8). g falls by 1% at d = 63.7 km. Altitude changes g twice as fast as depth. **The real Earth** is denser at the core, so g does not decrease linearly; reported seismic-model values show g rising slightly below the surface (to about 10.7 m s⁻² near the core-mantle boundary; flag: standard value from memory, verify).

**With latitude λ (rotation, first order).** In the frame rotating with the Earth (angular speed ω = 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹), a body at latitude λ moves in a circle of radius R cosλ and needs a centripetal acceleration ω²R cosλ toward the axis. Resolve this along the local radial direction (component ω²R cos²λ outward) and along the tangent (component ω²R sinλ cosλ). To first order in ω²R/g₀ (about 0.0035):

$$ g_eff ≈ g₀ − ω² R cos²λ $$

The tangential part tilts the plumb line away from the true vertical by about ω²R sinλ cosλ/g₀: maximal (about 0.1°) near 45°, zero at the equator and poles.

**Numbers:** ω²R = 0.03388 m s⁻²; at the equator g falls by 0.0339 m s⁻² (0.35%); at 45° by 0.0169 m s⁻² and the deflection is 1.73 × 10⁻³ rad = 0.099°.

**Second cause: shape.** The Earth is slightly oblate (equatorial radius about 21 km larger than the polar radius, standard value, verify), so the equator is farther from the centre and g there is smaller again. Standard sea-level values are about 9.780 m s⁻² at the equator and 9.832 m s⁻² at the poles (flag: from memory; verify): the difference 0.052 is larger than the 0.034 from rotation alone, the rest coming from the shape and mass distribution.

### 7. Core: the Earth tunnel (diameter and chord) and escape speed from non-uniform bodies

*Objective:* Show that motion along any chord of a uniform Earth is SHM with period 2π√(R/g), and find escape speeds from inhomogeneous bodies.

## Tunnel through a diameter

Treat the Earth as a **uniform** sphere (radius R, no rotation, no air, tunnel smooth and narrow). At distance r < R the pull is F = −mg r/R toward the centre (PH-008 module 4; NCERT eq. 7.10). Take x along the tunnel measured from the centre, so a particle dropped at x = R obeys

$$ m ẍ = −(m g/R) x    ⟹    ω² = g/R,    T = 2π√(R/g) $$

Simple harmonic motion with amplitude R, period T, maximum speed Rω = √(gR) at the centre. Numbers: R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m, g = 9.81 m s⁻²: T = 5063 s = **84.4 min** (R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m, g = 9.8 gives 84.6 min; Mathur's Example 11 gives 5023 s = 83.72 min ≈ 84 min, and his exercise answers print 84 min, 42 min and 83.72 min, extract pages 699-709). Using only the mean density, T = √(3π/(Gρ)) = 84.4 min for ρ = 5514 kg m⁻³. The speed at the centre is 7.91 km s⁻¹.

## Tunnel along a chord

Let the chord be at perpendicular distance d from the centre O, with x measured along the chord from its midpoint C. A particle at x has position (x, d) relative to O (x along the chord, d perpendicular), so gravity is −(mg/R)(x, d). Resolve:

- along the chord: m ẍ = −(mg/R) x,
- perpendicular to it: −mg d/R, balanced by the normal force of the smooth tunnel wall.

The along-chord equation has the **same ω² = g/R**, whatever d. Hence **the period is the same for every chord**, 84.4 min; the one-way trip lasts T/2 = 42.2 min. The particle starts at rest at one end, so the amplitude is the half-chord A = √(R² − d²) and the maximum speed is Aω. For d = 3000 km: A = 5620 km, chord length 11 241 km, v_max = 6.97 km s⁻¹; the diametral tunnel (d = 0) has A = R and v_max = 7.91 km s⁻¹. This is Mathur's exercise showing the period is the same with or without passing through the centre, answer 83.72 min (extract page 709), and Upadhyaya's Example 12 (extract page 278), which resolves the force along the tunnel.

**Why 84 min again?** A satellite grazing the surface has v²/R = g, so T = 2π R/v = 2π√(R/g): the same. The tunnel motion is the projection of that circular motion on a line (Mathur exercise 39, extract page 709).

**Real Earth.** The density rises toward the core, so the interior field is stronger and the true fall time is shorter; a figure of about 38 minutes is widely quoted for a diametral trip (flag: from memory, verify).

## Escape speed from inhomogeneous bodies

For a launch from radius r with the body not otherwise disturbed, energy conservation to infinity needs ½v² + V(r) = 0, so **v_esc = √(−2V(r))**.

- **From the surface** of *any* spherically symmetric body, V(R) = −GM/R and v_esc = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR): the same whatever ρ(r) is (the outside field depends only on M). NCERT eq. 7.32 and Mathur section 12.9 (extract page 652); the Moon needs M = 7.3 × 10²² kg, R = 1.7 × 10⁶ m, giving 2.39 km s⁻¹ (Upadhyaya extract page 275 quotes 2.37).
- **From the centre** the interior matters. Uniform sphere: V(0) = −1.5 GM/R, so v = √3 v_s: for Earth 19.4 km s⁻¹ (v_s = 11.18 km s⁻¹). Linear profile ρ₀(1 − r/R) or ρ ∝ 1/r: V(0) = −2GM/R, so v = √2 v_s. In general for ρ ∝ r^k, v(0)/v_s = √((k + 3)/(k + 2)).

A launch from the surface needs only v_s, whatever the structure deeper down.

### 8. Master: tides, equipotentials, field lines and the shape of g(r) and V(r)

*Objective:* Derive the first-order tidal acceleration, read equipotentials and field lines, and compare graphs for uniform and centrally concentrated spheres.

## Tidal acceleration, first order

The Moon (mass M_m) pulls different parts of the Earth with slightly different strength. Put the Earth's centre at distance d from the Moon and take the point on the Earth nearest the Moon, at d − R. By the binomial expansion (bridge B2):

$$ GM_m/(d − R)² = (GM_m/d²)(1 − R/d)⁻² ≈ (GM_m/d²)(1 + 2R/d) $$

Relative to the Earth's centre, which accelerates at GM_m/d², the near point is pulled toward the Moon by an extra **2GM_m R/d³**. The far point, at d + R, has GM_m/(d + R)² ≈ (GM_m/d²)(1 − 2R/d), so relative to the centre it lags by the same 2GM_m R/d³, *away* from the Moon. At the points on the circle perpendicular to the Earth-Moon line the Moon's pull has a sideways component that squeezes toward the centre, of magnitude GM_m R/d³ (half as much). So the tidal field **stretches along the Earth-Moon line and squeezes across it**, and it falls off as **1/d³**, faster than the pull itself (1/d²).

**Numbers** (standard values, flag: Moon mass 7.348 × 10²² kg, d = 3.844 × 10⁸ m): 2GM_m R/d³ = 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ m s⁻², about 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ of g. For the Sun the same formula (M = 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg, d = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m) gives 5.05 × 10⁻⁷ m s⁻², 0.46 of the Moon's value: the nearby Moon wins despite its tiny mass because of the d³. This is only the first-order differential field; the ocean response is a separate topic.

## Equipotentials and field lines

An **equipotential surface** is a surface of constant V. Because g = −grad V, the field is perpendicular to the equipotentials, and the work done moving a mass along one is zero. Field lines (lines tangent to g) begin at infinity and end on mass; for a spherical body the lines are radial and the equipotentials are concentric spheres. Inside a hollow shell there are no lines (g = 0) and the whole cavity is a single equipotential, V = −GM/R. Their spacing shows the field: crowded lines mean large g.

## Graph shapes: uniform versus centrally concentrated

Both bodies have the same M and R. The "uniform" sphere has g ∝ r inside; the "concentrated" one has density ∝ 1/r (module 3, k = −1), so g is constant inside. Values (g in units of g₀ = GM/R², V in units of GM/R):

| r/R | 0 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| g uniform | 0 | 0.25 | 0.50 | 0.75 | 1 | 0.444 | 0.25 | 0.111 |
| g concentrated | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0.444 | 0.25 | 0.111 |
| V uniform | −1.500 | −1.469 | −1.375 | −1.219 | −1 | −0.667 | −0.5 | −0.333 |
| V concentrated | −2.000 | −1.750 | −1.500 | −1.250 | −1 | −0.667 | −0.5 | −0.333 |

(The concentrated body's g at r = 0 is a limit; the direction reverses at the centre, so V has a conical point there.) Reading the plots in the diagram: outside r = R both curves coincide, because only M and R matter outside; the concentrated body has the deeper well.

**Potential well.** A well's depth V(0) − V(∞) is the energy per kg to take matter from the centre to infinity: 1.5 GM/R for the uniform sphere against 2.0 GM/R for the concentrated one.

### 10. Practice: guided problems and a self-test routine

*Objective:* Choose the right method for each type of problem and rehearse the derivations from memory.

## Guided problem 1: the cavity

*A uniform sphere (R, ρ) has a spherical cavity of radius R/3 centred at R/2 from the centre. Find g in the cavity.*

Thinking: a hole means full sphere minus filler. The field inside a cavity is uniform and equals (4/3)πGρ d with d the centre-to-centre distance; the cavity radius does not enter. So g = (4/3)πGρ (R/2) = g₀/2 with g₀ = (4/3)πGρR the surface value, pointing from the cavity centre toward the centre of the big sphere. If asked for the *potential* in the cavity, superposition of potentials is needed: V = V_full − V_filler, evaluated by the interior formulas of PH-008 module 4: V(P) = −(2πGρ/3)(3R² − r²) + (2πGρ/3)(3a² − |r − d|²), with r the position from O and d the vector to the cavity centre. Check: −∇V = −(4πGρ/3) d, which is uniform and points from C toward O, as found above.

## Guided problem 2: a non-uniform sphere

*A planet has ρ = ρ₀(r/R)^k. Find M, g(r) and V(0).* Plan: (i) M(r) = ∫4πs²ρ ds = M (r/R)^(k+3); (ii) g = GM(r)/r² = (GM/R²)(r/R)^(k+1); (iii) V(0) = −GM/R − ∫₀^R g ds = −(GM/R)(k + 3)/(k + 2). Check k = 0: −1.5 GM/R, the uniform sphere.

## Guided problem 3: choose the method

For a ring or a disc you must integrate (no Gauss law, no symmetry). For a sphere use shells or Gauss. For a hole use superposition. For a mass raised a small distance use the binomial expansion. Before writing, name the method in one line; examiners award a mark for the plan.

### Notes: Definitions and conventions

- Gravitational field g: force per unit test mass, directed toward the attracting matter; SI unit N kg⁻¹ = m s⁻². In this lesson g denotes the inward magnitude unless a signed component g_r or g_z is written.
- Gravitational potential V: potential energy per unit mass with V = 0 at infinity; SI unit J kg⁻¹ = m² s⁻²; negative near matter.
- g = −grad V; for spherical symmetry |g| = dV/dr.
- Enclosed mass M(r) = ∫₀^r 4πs²ρ(s) ds; field of a spherical body at radius r is GM(r)/r².
- Superposition: fields add as vectors and potentials add as numbers; a hole is the full body plus a body of density −ρ.
- Surface density σ (kg m⁻²) for rings and discs; element of a ring dm = σ 2πx dx.

### Notes: Equations at a glance

- Cavity in a uniform sphere: g = −(4πGρ/3) d, uniform, independent of the cavity radius.
- Spherical profile: g(r) = GM(r)/r²; V(r) = −GM(r)/r − 4πG ∫_r^R ρ s ds.
- Power-law ρ = ρ₀(r/R)^k: g = (GM/R²)(r/R)^(k+1); V = −(GM/R)[1 + (1 − (r/R)^(k+2))/(k+2)]; V(0) = −(GM/R)(k+3)/(k+2).
- Ring on the axis: V = −GM/√(a² + z²); g_z = −GMz/(a² + z²)^(3/2); |g|_max = 2GM/(3√3 a²) at z = a/√2; small oscillations ω² = GM/a³.
- Disc on the axis: V = −2πGσ(√(R² + z²) − z); |g| = 2πGσ(1 − z/√(R² + z²)); infinite plane |g| = 2πGσ.
- Potential energy above the Earth: ΔU = mgh/(1 + h/R) ≈ mgh(1 − h/R); g(h) ≈ g(1 − 2h/R); g(d) = g(1 − d/R) for a uniform Earth; g_eff ≈ g − ω²R cos²λ.
- Tunnel: ω² = g/R, T = 2π√(R/g) ≈ 84 min, same for every chord; v_max = √(R² − d²)·√(g/R).
- Escape speed from radius r: v = √(−2V(r)); from the surface of any spherical body √(2GM/R).
- Tidal acceleration (first order): 2GM_mR/d³ along the line, GM_mR/d³ squeezing across it.

### Notes: Uniform versus concentrated sphere, shell and hollow sphere

- Same M and R; V = 0 at infinity; values at selected radii.

### Notes: Derivation skeletons to rewrite from memory

- Cavity: g_full = −(4πGρ/3)r; g_filler = −(4πGρ/3)(r − d); subtract; r cancels; g = −(4πGρ/3)d.
- Non-uniform sphere: M(r) by integration; g = GM(r)/r²; V from shells; verify dV/dr = g.
- Disc: ring dm = σ2πx dx; V = −2πGσ ∫₀^R x dx/√(x² + z²); far-field by binomial; infinite-plane limit.
- mgh: ΔU = GMmh/(R(R + h)) = mgh(1 − h/R + …).
- Chord: force −(mg/R)(x, d); along the chord −(mg/R)x; ω² = g/R; same period for all chords.
- Tides: GM/(d ∓ R)² ≈ (GM/d²)(1 ± 2R/d); subtract the value at the centre.

### Notes: Worked numbers to remember

- Cavity radius R/2 centred at R/2 (touching the surface, d + a = R): field in cavity g₀/2; near-side surface (cavity rim) g₀/2, continuous with the in-cavity value; far-side surface 17g₀/18.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits of validity

- The cavity result needs the cavity to lie wholly inside the sphere (d + a ≤ R; a cavity touching the surface is allowed) and both bodies uniform.
- A hollow shell has g = 0 inside only for the shell's own field; outside matter still acts (no gravitational shielding).
- The tunnel result needs a uniform, non-rotating Earth, a smooth tunnel and no drag; the real Earth is not uniform, so the real time differs.
- The first-order g(h) and ΔU formulas need h ≪ R; at h = R/2 the first correction is already visibly insufficient.
- The disc far-field series needs z > R; the closed forms are exact for all z > 0.
- The latitude formula g − ω²R cos²λ neglects oblateness; the plumb line deviates from the true radial direction.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- g versus V: g is a vector (slope of V); V is a scalar (area under g). A flat V means g = 0, not V = 0.
- Altitude h versus distance r = R + h: formulas for orbits and escape use r.
- Depth versus altitude: g falls with depth as 1 − d/R (uniform Earth) but with altitude as 1 − 2h/R.
- Escape speed from the centre of a body (√3 v_s for a uniform sphere) versus from the surface; the standard quoted 11.2 km s⁻¹ is the surface value.
- Field inside a hole of a sphere (uniform) versus field inside a shell (zero).
- Weight (effective g) versus gravitational pull: the Earth's rotation makes effective g smaller at the equator.

- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is the interior gravity of a centrally concentrated planet closer to constant than that of a uniform one?
    - **a:** Going inward, a uniform planet loses enclosed mass as r³, so g falls in proportion to r. In a concentrated planet most of the mass stays enclosed until small r, so M(r) falls only about as r² (exactly r² for ρ ∝ 1/r). M(r)/r² therefore stays nearly constant.
  - - **q:** Why does the axial field of a disc tend to 2πGσ in magnitude just above its centre (z → 0⁺), while a ring's field goes to zero at its centre?
    - **a:** At the ring centre the pulls of opposite elements cancel by symmetry. For a disc, just off the plane all the mass lies on one side, so |g| → 2πGσ. Exactly at z = 0 the disc's field is zero too (by symmetry); it jumps from −2πGσ to +2πGσ across the plane.
  - - **q:** Why does the tunnel period not depend on the chord?
    - **a:** The force component along any chord is −(mg/R)x; the ω² = g/R is the same; the perpendicular part goes to the wall.
  - - **q:** Why does the Moon cause larger tides than the Sun although its pull is smaller?
    - **a:** Tidal acceleration scales as M/d³, which is larger for the nearby Moon.
