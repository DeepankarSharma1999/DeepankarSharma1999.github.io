## GS-144: Religion, language and regional identity

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Religion, language and regional identity and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Language as the main axis of regional identity

In the 1920s the Congress reorganised its provincial units by language and promised linguistic provinces after freedom. Partition made Nehru, Patel and Rajaji cautious, fearing language loyalty could produce further divisions. Strong movements among Marathi, Kannada and especially Telugu speakers forced the issue; Potti Sriramulu's death in October 1953 after a long fast led to Andhra Pradesh and to the States Reorganisation Commission, whose report was implemented on 1 November 1956. NCERT concludes that language combined with regional and tribal identity, rather than religion, has been the strongest base of ethno-national identity in India. Yet statehood is not purely linguistic: the three States carved out in 2000 rested on tribal identity, deprivation and ecology. India currently has 28 States and 8 Union Territories.

### Religious communities and the minority idea

The 2011 Census counted around 966 million Hindus, about 80% of the population, but NCERT cautions that Hindus are not homogeneous and are divided by caste, as are other religions. Muslims, about 172 million or 14.2%, are spread across the country with sizeable pockets in several States. Christians (around 2.3%) are concentrated in the North-East and the South, with three Christian-majority States in the North-East; Sikhs (1.7%) form a majority in Punjab; Buddhists and Jains each form under one per cent. Sociologically, a minority is a group that is relatively disadvantaged and has collective solidarity. Some groups are anomalous: Parsis or Sikhs may be economically well-off yet culturally vulnerable because of small numbers relative to the majority, which is why religious and cultural minorities need protection.

### Why accommodation strengthened unity

NCERT presents comparative evidence. In 1956, the same year India redrew its map on linguistic lines, Ceylon made Sinhala its sole official language over Tamil protests, and the later civil war was partly rooted in that denial. Pakistan split in 1971 because its western wing did not respect Bengali sentiment. Against this background, being Kannadiga and Indian or Bengali and Indian proved compatible, and inter-State quarrels, though real, stayed manageable. Nationalist leaders such as Gandhi and Tagore criticised exclusive nationalism, and the Constitution protects the right of groups to conserve language, script and culture and of minorities to run educational institutions. The core lesson is that forcible imposition of one language or religion weakens a unity that rests on recognising difference.
