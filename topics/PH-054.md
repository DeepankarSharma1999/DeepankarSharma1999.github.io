### Bridge: Small angles, sin and tan, and units on the screen

Diffraction patterns of everyday slits are very narrow, so you need three habits.

**Habit 1: radians.** An angle in radians is arc length divided by radius, so it has no unit. 180 degrees = pi radians, so 1 degree = 0.017453 rad.

**Habit 2: small-angle replacement.** When theta is small, sin(theta), tan(theta) and theta (in radians) agree closely. At 1 degree: sin = 0.017452, tan = 0.017455, theta = 0.017453. At 10 degrees: sin = 0.1736, tan = 0.1763, theta = 0.1745, a difference of 1.5 per cent between sin and tan. At 30 degrees the difference is large (0.500 against 0.577). So the shortcut sin(theta) = tan(theta) = theta is safe below about 5 degrees (error under 0.4 per cent), and risky above 10 degrees.

**Habit 3: position on a screen.** A screen at distance f (or the focal plane of a lens of focal length f) places the direction theta at height x = f tan(theta), which for small angles is x = f sin(theta) = f theta. Wavelength, slit width and distance must be converted to the same unit (all in metres) before dividing. A wavelength of 6000 Angstrom is 6.0 x 10^-7 m, a slit of 0.40 mm is 4.0 x 10^-4 m.

**Trap.** Mixing millimetres and metres inside one fraction is the commonest source of an answer wrong by a factor of 1000.

### Bridge: The function sin(alpha)/alpha: zeros, limit and shape

The single slit leads to the function **sin(alpha)/alpha**, which looks strange at alpha = 0 because it is 0/0. Look at the values instead of the algebra.

| alpha | sin(alpha) | sin(alpha)/alpha |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.8415 | 0.8415 |
| 0.5 | 0.4794 | 0.9589 |
| 0.1 | 0.09983 | 0.9983 |
| 0.01 | 0.0099998 | 0.99998 |

As alpha shrinks, the ratio approaches **1**. The reason: for small alpha (radians), sin(alpha) = alpha - alpha^3/6 + ..., so sin(alpha)/alpha = 1 - alpha^2/6 + ..., which is 1 at alpha = 0. So the function is 1 at the centre, not 0/0.

The function is **zero** whenever sin(alpha) = 0 but alpha is not zero: alpha = pi, 2 pi, 3 pi, ... (about 3.14, 6.28, 9.42). Between zeros it swings through smaller and smaller humps because the numerator never exceeds 1 while the denominator keeps growing: the n-th hump is at most 1/alpha in height.

**Squaring** makes every value non-negative. The intensity of light is the square of its amplitude, so the intensity is I = I0 (sin(alpha)/alpha)^2: a tall central peak of height I0, zeros at alpha = n pi, and side humps of height about 1/alpha^2 times I0.

**Trap.** alpha = 0 is the centre of the bright peak, not a zero, even though sin(0) = 0.

### 1. Orientation: what a single slit does to light and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Name the picture, the three results of the lesson and the five verified questions, so that the later modules have a purpose.

## The picture first

Shine a narrow beam of one-colour light through a slit cut in a card, a gap about 0.1 mm wide. On a wall several metres away you do not see one thin bright line. You see a wide, bright central band, and on both sides fainter bands separated by dark gaps. The narrower the slit, the wider the pattern. This spreading of light after it passes through an opening is **diffraction**. The slit pattern is the simplest case, and it is the stepping stone to the double slit (Day 22 lesson PH-055) and the grating (Day 23 lesson PH-056).

**Fraunhofer diffraction** means a special arrangement: a plane wave front falls on the slit and the diffracted light is gathered on a screen by a lens, which is the same as putting the source and the screen very far away (Brij Lal, PDF p. 442). Module 7 compares it with Fresnel diffraction.

## Three results to carry out of this lesson

1. The intensity at angle theta is

$$ I = I0 (sin α / α)² , α = π a sin θ / λ $$

where a is the slit width, lambda the wavelength and I0 the intensity at the centre (PDF pp. 445 and 448).
2. Dark bands (minima) lie at **a sin θ = n λ** with n = 1, 2, 3 (not 0). The central bright band therefore has width **W = 2 f λ / a** on a screen at focal distance f (PDF pp. 443 and 444).
3. The side bright bands are weak. The first has about **4.7 per cent** of the central intensity (4.5 per cent by the rough half-integer rule), the next about 1.6 per cent, then 0.8 per cent.

## Notation used throughout

a is the slit width (metres), lambda the wavelength, theta the angle between the straight-on direction and the direction considered, f the focal length of the lens in front of the screen, x the height on the screen measured from the centre, n an integer. Angle theta is positive upward. The pattern is the same above and below the centre.

### 2. Foundation: many small sources, path difference and the first dark band

*Objective:* Explain, with the half-width pairing argument, why the straight-on direction is bright and a sin θ = n λ directions are dark.

## Many sources along the slit

By Huygens' principle (lesson PH-042 gives the idea), every point of a wave front that reaches the slit acts as a source of tiny secondary waves. Imagine the slit AB of width a cut into a large number of thin strips; each strip sends light in all directions. A lens focuses the light travelling in one chosen direction to one point of the screen, so the screen point at angle theta collects the light of all strips travelling in direction theta. The question is only how the strips' contributions add (Brij Lal, PDF p. 442 to 443).

## Straight on: all in step

For the direction straight ahead (theta = 0) every strip travels the same distance to the focus. They arrive **in phase** and add fully. This is the **central maximum**.

## Another direction: path difference

Now take direction theta. The light from the top edge A and the bottom edge B reaches the focus by paths that differ by

$$ path difference = a sin θ $$

(PDF p. 443, equation 18.1; the construction is a right-angled triangle ABL with AB = a and AL = a sin θ). A path difference of one wavelength is a phase difference of 2π. The strips in between have path differences between 0 and a sin θ.

## The pairing argument for the first dark band

Suppose a sin θ = λ. Split the slit into an upper half and a lower half. Take any strip in the upper half and the strip exactly a/2 below it in the lower half. Their path difference is (a/2) sin θ, half of a sin θ, that is **λ/2**. Waves a half-wavelength apart cancel. Because **every** strip in the upper half has such a partner in the lower half, the total is zero: a **minimum** (PDF p. 443).

For a sin θ = 2λ divide the slit into four quarters. The first and second quarters differ by λ/2 in path, as do the third and fourth, so again everything cancels. In general

$$ a sin θ = n λ , n = 1, 2, 3, ... $$

gives a minimum (equation 18.2), because the slit can be paired off into 2n equal parts that cancel two by two. The same argument shows that odd multiples of λ/2 leave one part uncancelled, so the directions a sin θ about (2n+1) λ/2 are near the side maxima (equation 18.3). Module 5 shows these are only approximately the maxima.

## Why n = 0 is not a minimum

n = 0 gives sin θ = 0: that is the central maximum, where all strips are in phase. The pairing argument does not apply there because the path difference is zero, not λ/2.

## Text figure of the geometry

See the diagram: slit of width a, parallel rays leaving at angle θ, the lens, the screen, and the path difference AL = a sin θ.

## Traps

- Do not put n = 0 in the minimum formula.
- The condition is on **a sin θ**, with a the slit width, not on the distance to the screen.

### 3. Core derivation 1: the amplitude A0 sin α / α by the vibration polygon

*Objective:* Derive the single-slit amplitude by adding many equal small vibrations whose phases increase steadily, and read off the intensity.

## Setting and assumptions

A slit of width a is lit by a plane monochromatic wave at normal incidence. The slit is much longer than it is wide, so only the width matters (a one-dimensional problem). Every strip of the slit sends out secondary waves of **equal amplitude** (we ignore the slow change of amplitude with direction, the obliquity factor). The screen is at the focus of a lens, so Fraunhofer conditions hold. Sign convention: θ is positive for directions above the straight-on direction; the result will be even in θ. Brij Lal (PDF pp. 444 to 445) follows the same plan.

## Step 1: the phase difference of the edges

The path difference between the two edges is a sin θ (module 2). A path difference of λ is a phase difference of 2π, so the phase difference between the edge waves is

$$ δ = (2π/λ) a sin θ = 2α , α = π a sin θ / λ $$

(equations 18.11 and 18.12). We have just defined α as **half** of the edge-to-edge phase difference.

## Step 2: the vibration polygon

Divide the slit into N thin equal strips. Each strip sends a small vibration of amplitude A0/N when all are in phase. Represent each vibration by a little arrow (a phasor) whose length is its amplitude and whose direction is its phase. Neighbouring strips differ in phase by δ/N, so each arrow is turned by δ/N from the one before. Placing the arrows head to tail gives a polygon; with N very large it becomes a **circular arc** (PDF p. 445, Fig. 18.3).

## Step 3: lengths on the arc

The arc has total length A0 (all arrows laid end to end, equal to the amplitude at the centre where there is no turning). The direction of the arrow changes by δ = 2α along the arc, so the arc subtends the angle 2α at its centre K. For an arc, length = radius x angle, so

$$ A0 = R · 2α , R = A0 / (2α) $$

The resultant amplitude A is the **chord** joining the ends of the arc. A chord of a circle of radius R subtending angle 2α at the centre has length 2 R sin α, so

$$ A = 2 R sin α = 2 (A0 / 2α) sin α = A0 sin α / α $$

(equations 18.7 to 18.9). Since intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude,

$$ I = I0 (sin α / α)² $$

with I0 = A0² (equation 18.10).

## Step 4: checks

- **Limit α → 0** (θ = 0): the arc straightens, chord = arc, A = A0 and I = I0. In the formula sin α / α → 1 (bridge B2).
- **α = π** (a sin θ = λ): the arc is a full circle, ends meet, chord 0, I = 0: the first minimum, agreeing with module 2.
- **Dimensions:** α is an angle (radians), so the formula is dimensionless inside the bracket; I and I0 have the same unit.
- **Physical reading:** the arc closing into a circle means the strips' phases have wrapped round a full turn and cancel.

## Interpretation

The pattern is symmetric about the centre because sin α / α is an even function of α. The central intensity I0 is the largest possible: no arc can have a chord longer than its length.

### 4. Core derivation 2: the same result by integration, and what it assumes

*Objective:* Obtain the amplitude as an integral over the slit, evaluate it, and list the approximations that make the result Fraunhofer.

## Setting and coordinates

Take the slit in a plane with the z axis along its width, origin O at the **centre** of the slit, so the slit runs from z = −a/2 to z = +a/2. Light travels along +x at normal incidence. Observation direction: angle θ from the x axis in the x–z plane (θ positive toward +z). Let k = 2π/λ. The extract (PDF pp. 446 to 448) puts the origin at the centre too, uses a screen distance r and finds the distance ρ from a strip to the screen point.

## Step 1: path from a strip to a distant point

A strip at height z sends light to a point P' at distance r from O, in direction θ. By the cosine rule the path ρ from the strip to P' satisfies ρ² = r² − 2 r z sin θ + z². Expanding for large r, ρ = r − z sin θ + z² cos² θ/(2r) + ... The last term shifts the phase by at most k (a/2)²/(2r) = π a²/(4 λ r). Dropping it is the **Fraunhofer approximation**; it is valid when a²/(λ r) is much less than 1 (the Fresnel-number test of module 7), not merely when r is much larger than a. A lens cancels this phase, so with a lens the pattern appears on a nearby screen. Then

$$ ρ = r − z sin θ $$

(equation 18.17): the wave fronts arriving from the slit are effectively plane.

## Step 2: add the strips

Each strip of width dz contributes a displacement K dz cos(ωt − kρ) (the extract uses sin; either works), with K the amplitude per unit width (equal for all strips). Using the complex form, the total is the real part of

$$ E = K ∫ from −a/2 to a/2 of e^{i(ωt − kr + k z sin θ)} dz = K e^{i(ωt − kr)} ∫ e^{i k z sin θ} dz $$

Write q = k sin θ. The integral is

$$ ∫ e^{i q z} dz = [e^{i q z} / (i q)] from −a/2 to a/2 = (e^{i q a/2} − e^{−i q a/2}) / (i q) = 2 sin(q a/2) / q $$

using e^{iu} − e^{−iu} = 2i sin u. Now q a/2 = (2π/λ)(a/2) sin θ = π a sin θ / λ = α. So q = 2α/a and the integral is a sin α / α. Hence

$$ E = K a (sin α / α) e^{i(ωt − kr)} $$

The amplitude is K a sin α / α, and the intensity is

$$ I = K² a² (sin α / α)² = I0 (sin α / α)² , I0 = K² a² $$

(equations 18.18 and 18.19). This agrees with module 3.

## What I0 = K² a² tells us

For uniform illumination (K fixed), the central intensity grows as **a²**, not as a. Reason: a wider slit passes proportionally more light (power ∝ a), but the pattern also becomes narrower (width ∝ 1/a), so the intensity at the centre grows as a x a. Check by power: I0 times the pattern width is proportional to a² x (1/a) = a, matching the light passed.

## Checks

- Limit θ → 0 gives α → 0 and E = K a: all strips in phase.


## Assumptions to state in an answer

1. Plane monochromatic incident wave (a single wavelength, coherent across the slit).
2. Slit long compared with its width (1-D), edges ideal.
3. Observation far away so that a²/(λ r) is much less than 1, or on the focal plane of a lens (Fraunhofer): the quadratic path term is neglected.
4. Equal amplitude from all strips (no obliquity factor), valid for small θ.
5. Scalar wave: polarisation ignored.

### 5. Core derivation 3: minima, side maxima, tan α = α and the intensity ratios

*Objective:* Find the conditions for minima and maxima, solve tan α = α, and show the side maxima are nearly 1 : 4/(9π²) : 4/(25π²) of the central one.

## Minima

I = I0 (sin α / α)² is zero when sin α = 0 and α is not zero: α = ±π, ±2π, ±3π, ... Since α = π a sin θ / λ this is

$$ a sin θ = n λ , n = 1, 2, 3, ... $$

(equation 18.21). Between the first minima on either side lies the **central maximum**.

## Maxima: set the slope to zero

Let y = (sin α / α)². Differentiate with the quotient rule:

$$ dy/dα = 2 (sin α / α) · (α cos α − sin α) / α² $$

This is zero when sin α = 0 (the minima) or when

$$ α cos α − sin α = 0 , that is , tan α = α $$

(PDF p. 449). The centre α = 0 is also a maximum. The side maxima are the non-zero roots of tan α = α. A graph of y = tan α against y = α shows the line crossing each branch of the tangent a little before the vertical asymptote at (n + 1/2) π, so each root is slightly **nearer the centre** than the middle point of the two neighbouring minima (PDF p. 449, Fig. 18.6).

Roots, found by Newton's method (start 4.5; after four steps 4.493409), recomputed:

| order n | α at the maximum | α / π | exact I/I0 | half-integer rule 4/((2n+1)²π²) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.4934 | 1.4303 | 0.04719 | 0.04503 |
| 2 | 7.7253 | 2.4590 | 0.01648 | 0.01621 |
| 3 | 10.9041 | 3.4709 | 0.00834 | 0.00827 |
| 4 | 14.0662 | 4.4774 | 0.00503 | 0.00500 |

## The rough rule and the 4.5 per cent result

If we replace each root by the midpoint α = (2n+1) π / 2, then sin² α = 1 and

$$ I_n / I0 = 1 / α² = 4 / ((2n+1)² π²) $$

For n = 1: 4/(9π²) = 0.04503 = 1/22.2. For n = 2: 4/(25π²) = 0.01621 = 1/61.7. For n = 3: 4/(49π²) = 1/121. So the intensities of the successive maxima are **nearly in the ratio**

$$ 1 : 4/(9π²) : 4/(25π²) : 4/(49π²) $$

which is the 2022 question, and the extract's values 1/22 and 1/61 (PDF p. 448). The words "nearly" and "about" matter: the exact first maximum is 4.72 per cent, which is 4.8 per cent larger than the rough 4.50 per cent.

**Worked check for the exact first maximum.** At α = 4.4934: sin α = −0.97612, so sin² α = 0.95281; α² = 20.1907; the ratio is 0.95281/20.1907 = 0.04719.

## The 2018 stem's figure: three levels

The 2018 question says "about 4.95 per cent". It is the middle of three levels of approximation. (i) Midpoint rule, α = 3π/2 and sin² α = 1: 4/(9π²) = 4.50 per cent. (ii) True position α1 = 1.4303π with sin² α still set to 1: 1/(1.43π)² = 1/20.19 = **4.95 per cent**, the figure printed in the stem (many texts write I1 = I0/(1.43π)²). (iii) Exact: at a root of tan α = α, sin² α = α²/(1 + α²), so I/I0 = 1/(1 + α²) = cos² α = 1/21.19 = **4.72 per cent**. Answer as asked with 4.95 per cent and add one line giving the exact 4.72 per cent (module 9).

## Traps

- The roots of tan α = α are not at (n + 1/2) π; they approach it from below.
- Never write the ratio as 1 : 1/9 : 1/25. The sin² factor equals 1 only at the midpoints, and the denominator is α² = ((2n+1)π/2)², giving the extra 4/π².

### 6. Core: widths, angles and the screen - how a, λ and f change the pattern

*Objective:* Compute the angular and linear widths, the position of any minimum and the slit width from data, and say how each quantity scales.

## The working formulas

From a sin θ = n λ and x = f tan θ (bridge B1):

- Position of the n-th minimum: sin θn = n λ / a, so for small angles **xn = n f λ / a** (equation 18.5 with n = 1 on PDF p. 444).
- **Half-width** of the central maximum (centre to first minimum): x1 = f λ / a.
- **Full width** of the central maximum: **W = 2 f λ / a** (equation 18.6). Angular full width = 2 λ / a.
- Every side band between neighbouring minima has width f λ / a, **half** the central one (small angles).

## How the pattern scales

| Change | Effect on the pattern |
|---|---|
| a smaller | everything spreads out; W ∝ 1/a |
| λ larger (red instead of blue) | wider, W ∝ λ |
| f larger (screen further) | linear scale grows ∝ f; angles unchanged |
| a halved | W doubles; I0 falls to one quarter (module 4) |

With white light each colour has its own pattern: the central maximum is white because all colours are bright at θ = 0, and the side bands are coloured with violet nearest the centre and red farthest, since sin θ1 = λ/a is larger for red (PDF p. 444). For a = 0.20 mm and f = 1 m, the first minimum for 400 nm is at 2.0 mm and for 700 nm at 3.5 mm (recomputed).

## Worked data problems (recomputed)

1. **Extract Example 18.1:** a = 0.2 mm, first minimum 5 mm from the centre, screen 2 m away. λ = a x / D = (0.2 x 10⁻³)(5 x 10⁻³)/2 = 5.0 x 10⁻⁷ m = 5000 Å.
2. **Slit width from minima:** minima 4.4 mm apart (first minima on each side) at 0.7 m for 5890 Å. Half separation 2.2 mm = f λ/a, so a = 0.7 x 5.89 x 10⁻⁷ / 2.2 x 10⁻³ = 1.87 x 10⁻⁴ m = 0.187 mm.
3. **From an angle:** a = 8.6 μm for 6000 Å if the first minimum is at 4 degrees, because sin 4° = 0.06976 and a = λ/sin θ = 6.0 x 10⁻⁷/0.06976. Here the small-angle rule is fine (4 degrees).

## Limiting cases

- **a much larger than λ:** sin θ1 = λ/a is tiny; the pattern shrinks to a thin central line along the geometrical shadow: ray optics is recovered.
- **a = 2 λ:** sin θ1 = 1/2, θ1 = 30 degrees; the small-angle formulas fail, use sin θ = n λ/a with x = f tan θ.
- **a = λ:** sin θ1 = 1, the first minimum is at 90 degrees: the central maximum fills the whole forward half-space.
- **a < λ:** no solution of a sin θ = n λ exists (sin θ cannot exceed 1), so no dark band appears; the light spreads over the half-space with a smooth fall-off. (These last two cases are stated within the scalar model; for a of order λ the obliquity factor and polarisation change the details.)

## Half-intensity width (standard; verify)

I falls to half of I0 where sin² α / α² = 1/2, α = 1.3916 (solved numerically). The full width at half intensity in sin θ is 2 (1.3916/π) λ / a = 0.886 λ/a, which is smaller than the minimum-to-minimum width 2 λ/a.

## Dimensional check

λ/a is dimensionless, so θ1 is an angle; f λ/a has the unit of length. The product f λ is in m², and dividing by a (m) leaves m.

### 7. Master: Fraunhofer versus Fresnel diffraction, and why a lens appears

*Objective:* State the difference between the two classes of diffraction, apply the Fresnel-number test, and explain the standard laboratory arrangement.

## Two classes of diffraction

Recap link: Day 18 lesson PH-058, section "Master: the Fresnel number, Fresnel versus Fraunhofer diffraction, and the limits of the zone model", has the same comparison; this module applies it to the slit.

**Fresnel diffraction:** the source, the screen or both are at a **finite distance** from the opening, so the wave fronts reaching the opening or the screen are curved. No lens is needed; the pattern changes shape as the screen moves, and the calculation is harder (Fresnel zones, Day 18 lesson PH-058 and Day 19 lesson PH-059).

**Fraunhofer diffraction:** the incident wave front is **plane** and the diffracted light is collected by a lens, which is equivalent to a source and a screen at infinity (Brij Lal, PDF p. 442). The path difference between rays from different parts of the opening is then a simple linear function of position, a sin θ for a slit, and the mathematics is the single integral of module 4.

| | Fresnel | Fraunhofer |
|---|---|---|
| Source and screen distance | finite | infinite (or via lenses) |
| Incident wave front | spherical or cylindrical | plane |
| Lenses | not needed | collimator and focusing lens |
| Path difference across the opening | depends on position in a nonlinear way | linear in position (a sin θ) |
| Pattern | changes as the screen moves | same shape, only scaled |
| Mathematics | Fresnel integrals | Fourier-type integral |
| Examples | straight edge, disc, zone plate | single slit, double slit, grating, Airy disc |

## The standard arrangement (PDF p. 442, Fig. 18.1)

A narrow source slit S sits at the focus of a collimating lens L1, which makes the beam plane. The beam passes the slit AB, then a second lens L2; the screen is placed in the focal plane of L2. Both lenses are achromatic. Plane waves going in direction θ are focused to one point at height x = f tan θ. The lens does not change the pattern; it only brings the far field to a nearby screen.

## A test: the Fresnel number (standard; verify)

For an opening of half-width b = a/2 at distance L, the dimensionless **Fresnel number** is N_F = b² / (λ L). If N_F is much smaller than 1, the far-field (Fraunhofer) pattern is seen even without a lens, provided the slit is lit by a plane (collimated) wave, e.g. a laser or a distant source; N_F of order 1 or more means Fresnel diffraction. Examples (recomputed): a = 0.40 mm, λ = 600 nm, L = 2 m gives N_F = (0.20 x 10⁻³)² / (6.0 x 10⁻⁷ x 2) = 0.033 (about 1/30), so for plane-wave illumination the far-field formula is a good approximation in the 2026 Q1(c) set-up even though no lens is mentioned. For a = 1 mm, λ = 600 nm, L = 1 m: N_F = 0.42, of order 1, so Fresnel effects matter; use a lens. The statement is a convention: some books use the full width a, which changes N_F by a factor of 4; the test is only an order-of-magnitude one.

## What changes when the distance increases

In the Fresnel region, the pattern evolves from a shadow with fringes along the edges (near the slit) to the Fraunhofer sinc-squared form far away. The sinc-squared form is the limit, not a different phenomenon.

### 8. Master: what the pattern teaches - Fourier view, oblique incidence, links and limits

*Objective:* See the slit pattern as the Fourier transform of the aperture, handle oblique incidence, and know what the model leaves out.

## The slit pattern as a Fourier transform (standard; verify)

In module 4 the amplitude was ∫ e^{i q z} dz over the open part of the slit, with q = k sin θ. If the aperture is described by a function g(z) equal to 1 inside and 0 outside, the far-field amplitude is the Fourier transform of g(z) with respect to q. The 'top-hat' function gives the sinc function sin α / α. Two consequences: a narrow aperture (small a) gives a wide spectrum of directions (large angular width) and vice versa; the product (slit width) x (angular half-width) equals λ at the first minimum, a wave version of the uncertainty relation. Other aperture shapes (two slits, a grating, a circle) give other transforms; that is why the same machinery serves Day 22 PH-055 and Day 23 PH-056.

## Oblique incidence (standard; verify)

If the plane wave arrives at angle i to the normal, there is already a path difference a sin i across the slit before the aperture. The edge-to-edge path difference in direction θ becomes a (sin θ − sin i), with both angles measured on the same side of the normal. Minima: a (sin θ − sin i) = n λ. The central maximum is at θ = i (the geometrical direction), and the whole pattern is the old one centred on that direction. Check: a = 0.10 mm, λ = 500 nm, i = 10 degrees. The first minima are at sin θ = sin 10° ± λ/a = 0.17365 ± 0.00500, giving 10.29 degrees and 9.71 degrees, nearly symmetric about i. For large i the pattern is no longer symmetric.

## Links to neighbouring lessons

- **Circular aperture (Day 17 lesson PH-057):** the first dark ring is at 1.22 λ/D, not λ/a. The factor 1.22 comes from the Bessel function. The slit gives the one-dimensional model.
- **Fresnel zones (Day 18 lesson PH-058, Day 19 lesson PH-059):** these describe the near-field region where Fraunhofer fails.
- **Double slit (Day 22 lesson PH-055):** the pattern is the product of the slit envelope derived here and the two-source interference; this lesson supplies the envelope.
- **Grating (Day 23 lesson PH-056):** N slits give narrow principal maxima under the same envelope.
- **Resolution (Rayleigh criterion, PDF pp. 484 to 485, eq. 19.6):** for a rectangular aperture of width a two equal patterns are just resolved when their centres differ by dθ = λ/a, the first minimum of one falling on the maximum of the other. At the midpoint each pattern gives (sin(π/2)/(π/2))² = 4/π² of its peak, so the sum is 8/π² = 0.81 of a peak, a dip of about 19 per cent, matching the extract's "about 20 per cent" (PDF p. 484). For a circular aperture the limit is 1.22 λ/D with a dip of 26.5 per cent (midpoint 0.735 of a peak; standard, verify; Day 17 lesson PH-057, "Core: Rayleigh criterion, telescope and eye"). The prism limit in the extract uses the same rectangular aperture (PDF p. 490, eq. 19.16; pointer only).

## Limits of the model

- Treats the light as a scalar wave and ignores polarisation and the obliquity factor; reliable for small θ.
- Assumes an ideal opaque screen with a sharp edge of negligible thickness.
- Assumes the source is monochromatic and coherent across the slit; a finite source size or bandwidth smooths the minima, which then do not reach exactly zero.
- Treats the slit as infinitely long; a finite height h adds a second pattern along the length (sin β/β of the same form, with h in place of a), showing the diffraction in the other direction.

## Diffraction against interference

Interference adds a **few** distinct sources (two slits). Diffraction adds a **continuous** distribution of sources across one opening. Both are superposition; the same mathematics, an integral instead of a sum.

### Notes: Definitions

- Diffraction is the spreading of waves that pass through an opening or past an edge, beyond what ray optics predicts.
- Fraunhofer diffraction uses a plane incident wave front and collects the diffracted light with a lens (or at infinity); Fresnel diffraction has curved wave fronts at finite distances.
- α = π a sin θ / λ is half the phase difference between the two edges of the slit.
- The central maximum is the bright band between the first minima on either side; secondary (side) maxima are the weak bands beyond.
- I0 is the intensity at the centre of the pattern (θ = 0); it equals K² a² for uniform illumination.

### Notes: Equations to know

- I = I0 (sin α / α)², α = π a sin θ / λ.
- Minima: a sin θ = n λ, n = 1, 2, 3, ... (never 0).
- Maxima: tan α = α; roots α = 1.4303π, 2.4590π, 3.4709π, ... (rough rule (2n+1)π/2). At a root I/I0 = 1/(1 + α²) = cos² α.

- Central width W = 2 f λ / a; angular full width 2 λ / a (small angles).
- Position of the n-th minimum on the screen: x_n = n f λ / a (small angles).

- Resolution of a slit (Rayleigh): dθ = λ/a; midpoint intensity 8/π² = 0.81 of a peak (about 19 per cent dip); circular aperture 1.22 λ/D, midpoint 0.735 (standard; verify).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: polygon method

- Set-up: slit width a, normal incidence, equal strips, Fraunhofer, θ positive upward.
- Edge-to-edge path difference a sin θ gives phase difference 2α = 2π a sin θ / λ.
- N strips give a circular arc of length A0 and angle 2α, so radius R = A0 / 2α.
- Resultant amplitude is the chord: A = 2 R sin α = A0 sin α / α.
- Intensity I = I0 (sin α / α)²; limits α → 0 (I0) and α = π (0).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: integral method

- E = K ∫ from −a/2 to a/2 of exp(i k z sin θ) dz after dropping the quadratic path term z² cos² θ/(2r) (Fraunhofer step; valid for a²/(λ r) much less than 1).
- Integral = 2 sin(k a sin θ / 2) / (k sin θ) = a sin α / α.
- I = K² a² (sin α / α)²; I0 = K² a².

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Interference of two slits against diffraction by one slit: few sources against a continuous distribution; the double-slit pattern contains the single-slit envelope (Day 22 lesson PH-055).
- Central width W = 2 f λ / a against the width of a side band f λ / a: the central one is twice as wide.
- Half-integer rule against tan α = α: the first is an approximation, the second exact.
- Slit (λ/a) against circular aperture (1.22 λ/D).
- sin θ against tan θ: use x = f tan θ; replace by θ only for small angles.

  - - **id:** PH-054-D1
    - **task:** Derive the single-slit Fraunhofer intensity by the vibration polygon (target about 12 minutes for 10 marks; practice target, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Set-up: slit of width a, plane monochromatic wave at normal incidence, observation direction θ above the straight-on direction, equal amplitude from each strip, far field or lens focal plane.
      - Path difference between the edges: a sin θ; phase difference δ = 2π a sin θ / λ = 2α with α = π a sin θ / λ.
      - Divide the slit into N equal strips with amplitude A0/N each, phase increasing by δ/N from strip to strip; as N becomes large the phasor polygon becomes a circular arc of length A0 subtending 2α at its centre.
      - Radius R from arc = radius x angle: A0 = R x 2α, so R = A0/(2α).
      - Resultant amplitude is the chord: A = 2 R sin α = A0 sin α / α.
      - Intensity I = A² = I0 (sin α / α)², with I0 = A0².
    - **checks:**
      - Limit α → 0: sin α / α → 1, I = I0.
      - α = π: the arc is a full circle, chord 0, I = 0 (first minimum a sin θ = λ).
      - α is dimensionless; I and I0 have the same unit.
      - Symmetric in θ because sin α / α is even.
    - **traps:**
      - Not defining α.
      - Writing the arc angle as α instead of 2α.
  - - **id:** PH-054-D2
    - **task:** Derive the single-slit intensity by integration over the slit (target about 12 minutes for 10 marks; practice target, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Coordinates: z along the width, origin at the slit centre, strips from −a/2 to a/2; light along +x; direction θ in the x–z plane; k = 2π/λ.
      - Path from strip z to the far point: ρ² = r² − 2 r z sin θ + z², so ρ = r − z sin θ + z² cos² θ/(2r) + ...; the last term is dropped when π a²/(4 λ r) is much less than 1 (or a lens is used): ρ = r − z sin θ (Fraunhofer approximation).
      - Each strip dz contributes K dz exp[i(ωt − kρ)], so E = K exp[i(ωt − kr)] ∫ exp(i k z sin θ) dz from −a/2 to a/2.
      - With q = k sin θ the integral is [exp(i q z)/(i q)] between the limits = (exp(i q a/2) − exp(−i q a/2))/(i q) = 2 sin(q a/2)/q.
      - q a/2 = π a sin θ / λ = α, and 2/q = a/α, so the integral equals a sin α / α.
      - The amplitude is K a sin α / α and the intensity I = K² a² (sin α / α)² = I0 (sin α / α)² with I0 = K² a².
    - **checks:**
      - θ → 0: integral → a, all strips in phase.
      - Dimensions: K a is an amplitude; α dimensionless.
      - Result agrees with D1 (the two methods are independent).
    - **traps:**
      - Forgetting to drop the quadratic term z² cos² θ/(2r) (the Fraunhofer step), or justifying it by r much larger than a instead of the phase condition π a²/(4 λ r) much less than 1.
      - Evaluating the exponential limits with the wrong sign.
  - - **id:** PH-054-D3
    - **task:** Find the secondary maxima and show the intensities are nearly 1 : 4/(9π²) : 4/(25π²) : 4/(49π²) (target about 18 minutes for 15 marks; practice target, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Take y = (sin α / α)², positive for all α; the minima are at sin α = 0, α = nπ (n ≠ 0).
      - dy/dα = 2 (sin α / α) (α cos α − sin α) / α².
      - Setting dy/dα = 0 and sin α ≠ 0 gives α cos α = sin α, that is tan α = α.
      - Solve numerically (Newton from α = 4.5): α1 = 4.4934 = 1.4303π, α2 = 7.7253, α3 = 10.9041.
      - At α1: sin α = −0.97612, sin² = 0.95281, α² = 20.191, so I1/I0 = 0.04719.
      - Approximation: the tangent function blows up at (2n+1)π/2, so the roots lie just below; replace α by (2n+1)π/2 where sin² α = 1 to get I_n/I0 = 4/((2n+1)² π²).
      - Values: 4/(9π²) = 0.04503, 4/(25π²) = 0.01621, 4/(49π²) = 0.00827; compare exact 0.04719, 0.01648, 0.00834.

    - **checks:**
      - The ratio of exact to rough is 1.048, 1.017, 1.008, converging to 1.
      - Each root lies just below (2n+1)π/2 because tan α rises to infinity there while the line y = α stays finite, so the curves cross before the asymptote.

    - **traps:**
      - Writing the ratio as 1/9 and 1/25.

### Memory skeleton

- Fraunhofer set-up: plane wave on the slit (collimator L1), diffracted light collected in the focal plane of L2
- Edge-to-edge path difference a sin θ; phase difference 2α, α = πa sin θ/λ
- Polygon: arc of length A₀ subtending 2α; chord A = A₀ sin α/α; I = I₀(sin α/α)²
- Integral: drop the quadratic path term (Fraunhofer step); ∫e^(ikz sin θ)dz = a sin α/α; I₀ = K²a²
- Minima a sin θ = nλ (n ≠ 0); maxima tan α = α at 1.4303π, 2.4590π, …, just inside the midpoints
- Side maxima: exact 0.0472, 0.0165, 0.0083 of I₀; rough rule 4/((2n+1)²π²)
- Widths: W = 2fλ/a, side bands fλ/a; a halved: W doubles, I₀ quarters; two wavelengths' minima coincide when n₁λ₁ = n₂λ₂

### Last-week summary

- Fraunhofer: plane wave in, lens focal plane (or infinity) out. α = πa sin θ/λ is half the edge-to-edge phase; I = I₀(sin α/α)², I₀ = K²a² ∝ a².
- Polygon: arc A₀, angle 2α, R = A₀/2α, chord A₀ sin α/α. Integral: drop z² cos²θ/(2r), allowed when πa²/(4λr) ≪ 1 or with a lens.
- Minima a sin θ = nλ, n = 1, 2, 3, …; maxima tan α = α: 1.4303π, 2.4590π, 3.4709π, each just inside (n + ½)π.
- First side maximum: exact 1/(1 + α₁²) = 4.72%; 2018 stem 1/(1.43π)² = 4.95%; midpoint rule 4/(9π²) = 4.50%. 2022: nearly 1 : 4/9π² : 4/25π² : 4/49π².
- W = 2fλ/a (side bands fλ/a); a halved: W × 2, I₀ × ¼. 2026: 600 and 450 nm minima first coincide at n₁ = 3, n₂ = 4 (6.0 mm for a = 0.3 mm, f = 1 m).
