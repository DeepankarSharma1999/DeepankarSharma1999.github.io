### Bridge: One House or two: unicameral versus bicameral

A **legislature** is a body that makes laws. A **unicameral** legislature has **one House**; a **bicameral** legislature has **two Houses**. The word comes from the Latin *camera*, a chamber. At the Centre India is bicameral: the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha. NCERT explains why: large, diverse countries prefer two Houses to represent all sections and regions, and a second House makes it possible to have **every decision reconsidered**, which checks haste. In the States the Constitution gives a **choice**: most States have one House and six have two. In a one-House State the Assembly alone is the law-making House (with the Governor). In a two-House State there is a second chamber, the Legislative Council, which is **weaker** than the Assembly (module 8). So 'bicameral' tells you the number of Houses, not that the two are equal.

### Bridge: Assembly versus Council: the vocabulary

The **Legislative Assembly** is the **Vidhan Sabha**; its members are **MLAs** (Members of the Legislative Assembly) and its presiding officer is the **Speaker**. The **Legislative Council** is the **Vidhan Parishad**; its members are **MLCs** (Members of the Legislative Council) and its presiding officer is the **Chairman**. 'Direct election' means voters choose the member themselves; 'indirect election' means voters choose electors, who choose the member. Assembly members are **directly elected**; Council members are **indirectly elected** or **nominated**. A House that is 'dissolved' ends its life; a 'continuing' House is never dissolved as a whole. The Assembly is dissolved after five years; the Council continues, with one-third retiring every second year. If you remember **Assembly = direct, dissolvable, powerful** and **Council = indirect, continuing, weaker**, most Prelims options become easy to sort.

### 1. Orientation: what a State legislature is, and which States have two Houses

*Objective:* Name the parts of a State legislature, state which Articles govern it, and say which States are unicameral or bicameral (with an as-of date).

## What a State legislature is

A **legislature** is the body that makes laws, debates what the government is doing and approves its money. At the Centre it is called **Parliament**. In each State it is called the **State legislature**. It is governed by **Part VI of the Constitution, Articles 168 to 212**, with Article 213 (the Governor's ordinance power) placed just after them. Laxmikanth notes that these Articles resemble the provisions for Parliament, with some differences (p. 718). So this lesson keeps asking one question: *how is the State version the same as Parliament, and where does it differ?*

## The parts of the legislature

The State legislature is not only the elected House. It consists of the **Governor** plus the House or Houses:

- In a **unicameral** State (one House): the Governor and the **Legislative Assembly** (Vidhan Sabha).
- In a **bicameral** State (two Houses): the Governor, the **Legislative Council** (Vidhan Parishad) and the Legislative Assembly.

The Assembly is the *lower, popular or first* House. The Council is the *upper House, second chamber or House of elders* (p. 719). The Governor is an integral part of the legislature, which is why no Bill becomes an Act without his assent (module 7) and why he summons and prorogues the Houses and dissolves the Assembly (GS-189, module 3).

## Which States have two Houses?

Laxmikanth (position of 2019) says only **six** States are bicameral: **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra and Karnataka**; the other twenty-two States then had one House. NCERT's Class 11 text (2026-27 reprint) lists the same six. The Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Council was abolished by the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019. Madhya Pradesh is a curious case: the 7th Amendment (1956) provided for a Council there, but the President must notify it, and no notification has been made, so the State has one House.

**As of date: verify.** These counts are from a 2019 book and a recent reprint. Before you write a number in an answer, check a current source for any State that has created or abolished a Council (module 8 explains where Councils are created and abolished).

### 2. The Legislative Assembly: strength, constituencies, reservation and term

*Objective:* State the limits on the strength of an Assembly, how constituencies and reservation work, the five-year term and when it can be extended.

## Strength

The Assembly consists of representatives **directly elected** by the people on **universal adult franchise** (every adult citizen votes). Its **maximum strength is 500 and minimum 60**, depending on the State's population (p. 721). Exceptions in Laxmikanth: the minimum is **30** for **Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim and Goa**, **40** for **Mizoram** and **46** for **Nagaland**. Some members in Sikkim and Nagaland are also elected indirectly.

Notice that these are *floors* set by the Constitution, not the actual strengths. The actual numbers are in Table 33.2 of Laxmikanth (2019): Uttar Pradesh 403 (the largest), Maharashtra 288, West Bengal 294, Karnataka 224, Telangana 119, Goa 40, Sikkim 32 (the smallest State). In the Union Territories, Delhi has 70 members and Puducherry 30 (their Assemblies rest on other Articles; module 9). Treat the Nagaland floor of 46 as printed in the textbook and verify it in Article 170 before relying on it.

## The nominated member (now ended)

The Governor could nominate **one Anglo-Indian** member if that community was not adequately represented. Laxmikanth prints the arrangement as lasting until 2020 under the 95th Amendment (2009). **Verify**: the 104th Amendment (2019) is reported to have ended this nomination from 2020; check it before you state it as law.

## Territorial constituencies and delimitation

For direct election each State is divided into **territorial constituencies**, drawn so that the ratio of population to seats is the **same throughout the State**: roughly equal population per seat, so that one vote has about equal weight. 'Population' means the last published census. After each census the total number of seats and the division into constituencies are to be readjusted, by an authority and in a manner Parliament decides. Parliament passed Delimitation Commission Acts in **1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002**.

The **42nd Amendment (1976) froze** the seats and divisions at the **1971** census level till 2000; the **84th Amendment (2001)** extended the freeze (Laxmikanth says up to 2026) to encourage population control; the **87th Amendment (2003)** allowed redrawing on the **2001** census *without changing the total number of seats*. **As of date: verify** the exact end-point of the freeze in the Constitution text.

## Reservation for SCs and STs

Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes **in proportion to their share of the State's population**. For example, in Bihar's 243 seats the 2019 table shows 38 for SCs and 2 for STs, while in Arunachal Pradesh 59 of 60 seats are for STs. A candidate from these groups may also contest an unreserved seat. The reservation, like the Anglo-Indian nomination, was first for ten years and extended each time; Laxmikanth prints 2020 (95th Amendment). **Verify** the present end-date (reported as 2030 under the 104th Amendment).

## Term of the Assembly

The Assembly is **not a continuing House**. Its normal term is **five years from its first meeting after the general elections**, and the end of five years is automatic dissolution. The **Governor may dissolve it earlier** to pave the way for fresh elections (GS-189). During a **national emergency** Parliament may extend the term by law, **one year at a time**, but not beyond **six months after the emergency ceases**.

### 3. The Legislative Council: strength, the five-way election and the six-year rolling term

*Objective:* State the strength limits of a Council, the five sources of its members, the voting system and why it is a continuing House.

## Strength: one-third and the floor of 40

Council members are **indirectly elected** (voters choose the electors, who choose the members), unlike Assembly members. The **maximum strength is one-third of the total strength of the Assembly and the minimum is 40** (p. 722). The link to the Assembly's size is deliberate: it keeps the directly elected House predominant. The Constitution fixes the limits, but the **actual strength is fixed by Parliament by law** (see the 2019 table: Andhra Pradesh 58, Bihar 75, Karnataka 75, Maharashtra 78, Telangana 40, Uttar Pradesh 100).

A neat arithmetic trap: Telangana's Assembly has 119 seats and one-third of 119 is 39.67, which is *below* 40, yet its Council has 40. The explanation is the floor: 'in no case less than 40'. Another check: Karnataka's printed 75 is slightly above one-third of 224 (74.67); the table and the law, not a calculator, are the authority (the ceiling is measured on the Assembly's total strength; hint: 75 = 225/3, so Karnataka's figure fits if the nominated Anglo-Indian member was counted as the 225th member: verify).

## How the members are chosen

Of the total members of a Council:

1. **One-third** are elected by members of **local bodies** (municipalities, district boards and so on) in the State.
2. **One-twelfth** are elected by **graduates** of three years' standing residing in the State.
3. **One-twelfth** are elected by **teachers** of three years' standing, teaching at secondary level or above.
4. **One-third** are elected by the **members of the Legislative Assembly** from persons who are not members of the Assembly.
5. The **remainder** (one-sixth) are **nominated by the Governor** from persons with special knowledge or practical experience of literature, science, art, the cooperative movement and social service.

Add the fractions in twelfths: 4 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 2 = 12. So **five-sixths are indirectly elected and one-sixth nominated**. The election uses **proportional representation by the single transferable vote**. Courts cannot question the propriety of a Governor's nomination. The scheme is 'tentative and not final': Parliament may modify it, but has never done so (p. 723).

## A continuing House

Like the Rajya Sabha, the Council is a **continuing chamber**: it is a permanent body and **not subject to dissolution**. **One-third of its members retire every second year**, so each member serves **six years**, and vacancies are filled by fresh elections and Governor's nominations at the start of every third year. Retiring members may be re-elected or re-nominated any number of times. Contrast the Assembly, which is dissolved (module 2).

## Age and a note on Jammu and Kashmir

The minimum age is **30** for the Council and **25** for the Assembly (module 4). The erstwhile State of Jammu and Kashmir had its own constitution, a six-year Assembly term and a Council of 36 members (below the 40 floor), as Laxmikanth's notes explain; its Council was abolished in 2019.

## A model to hold in your head

Think of the Council as a mixed basket: local-body members, graduates, teachers, MLAs and nominees. Because the basket is mixed and part-nominated, critics say it lacks a single mandate (module 8).

### 4. Membership: qualifications, disqualifications, oath and vacation of seats

*Objective:* State the qualifications and disqualifications for membership, who decides disqualification, and the cases in which a seat falls vacant.

## Qualifications

The Constitution requires that a person be chosen as a member only if he or she is: (a) a **citizen of India**; (b) willing to make and subscribe an **oath or affirmation** before a person authorised by the **Election Commission** (to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India); (c) at least **25 years** old for the Assembly and **30 years** for the Council; and (d) in possession of other qualifications Parliament prescribes. The **Representation of the People Act, 1951** adds that a Council candidate must be an **elector** for an Assembly constituency in the State (a Governor's nominee must be a **resident** of the State), an Assembly candidate must be an elector for an Assembly constituency in the State, and a person must belong to a Scheduled Caste or Tribe to contest a reserved seat.

## Disqualifications

A person is disqualified from being chosen as, or from being, a member if he or she: holds an **office of profit** under the Union or State government (a minister's office and offices exempted by the State legislature are excepted); is of **unsound mind** and so declared by a court; is an **undischarged insolvent**; is **not a citizen** or has voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship or acknowledged allegiance to a foreign State; or is disqualified under a law of Parliament.

The 1951 Act adds, among other grounds: conviction for an offence with **imprisonment of two or more years** (but **preventive detention is not** a disqualification); election offences or corrupt practices; failing to lodge election expenses; interest in government contracts; dismissal for corruption or disloyalty; conviction for promoting enmity or for bribery; and punishment for preaching or practising untouchability, dowry or sati. **Verify** that, after *Lily Thomas v Union of India* (2013), disqualification on conviction operates from conviction without the earlier 'three months to appeal' protection.

**Who decides?** For these ordinary disqualifications, the **Governor's decision is final**, but he must **obtain the opinion of the Election Commission** and act on it. For **defection** (Tenth Schedule), the decision is by the **Speaker** (Assembly) or **Chairman** (Council), *not* the Governor, and since *Kihoto Hollohan v Zachilhu* (1992) it is **subject to judicial review** (p. 727). Anti-defection is only mentioned here; it has its own lesson.

## The oath

Every member takes an **oath or affirmation before the Governor** or a person he appoints, swearing allegiance to the Constitution, to uphold sovereignty and integrity, and to **faithfully discharge the duty of office**. Without it a member cannot vote or take part in proceedings and does not enjoy privileges. A person who sits or votes before taking the oath, or while knowing he is disqualified, is liable to a **penalty of Rs 500 for each day**. Do not confuse this oath with the candidate's affirmation made before an Election Commission authorised person.

## Vacation of seats

A member vacates his seat on: **double membership** (a person cannot belong to both Houses; if elected to both, one seat falls vacant under a State law); **disqualification**; **resignation** in writing to the Speaker or Chairman (it takes effect when **accepted**, and the presiding officer need not accept a resignation that is not voluntary or genuine); **absence** from all meetings for **sixty days** without permission (the House *can declare* the seat vacant); election being declared void by a court; **expulsion** by the House; election as **President or Vice-President**; or appointment as a **Governor**. Salaries and allowances are fixed by the State legislature.

## Dual membership

A person cannot be a member of both Parliament and a State legislature at the same time. If elected to both, he must resign from the State legislature within a fixed period (14 days under the Prohibition of Simultaneous Membership Rules, 1950) or his seat in Parliament falls vacant (standard knowledge; verify the rules).

### 5. Presiding officers: Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman and Deputy Chairman

*Objective:* State how the four presiding officers are elected, how they leave office, what the Speaker can do that the Chairman cannot, and how impartiality is protected.

A **presiding officer** is the member who runs a House's sittings. For the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha equivalents see GS-183 (Speaker) and GS-175 (Vice-President as Chairman); this module gives the State position in outline.

## Election and tenure

The **Speaker** and **Deputy Speaker** of the Assembly, and the **Chairman** and **Deputy Chairman** of the Council, are each **elected by their own House from among its members**. The Deputy is elected after the principal officer. Usually they continue for the life of the House, but each vacates office earlier if he (1) **ceases to be a member**; (2) **resigns** in writing (the Speaker to the Deputy Speaker, the Deputy to the Speaker, the Chairman to the Deputy Chairman and the Deputy Chairman to the Chairman); or (3) is **removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members** of the House, moved only after **14 days' notice**. The Table of Articles lists Articles 178-187; Article 181 (and 185) bars the officer from presiding while a resolution for his own removal is under consideration (verify the wording).

**What happens on dissolution?** For the Lok Sabha, Laxmikanth says the Speaker vacates office *immediately before the first meeting of the newly elected House* (p. 509), which is why a Speaker Pro Tem is needed. The State position is parallel: the Assembly Speaker does not vacate merely because the Assembly is dissolved. **Verify** Article 179 (proviso).

## Powers of the Speaker

The Speaker (p. 729): **maintains order and decorum**, with final power; is the **final interpreter** of the Constitution, the rules of procedure and the legislative precedents *within the Assembly*; **adjourns or suspends** the House when there is no quorum; **does not vote** in the first instance but has a **casting vote** in a tie; may allow a **secret sitting** at the request of the Leader of the House; **decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill** (final); **decides defection disqualification** under the Tenth Schedule; and **appoints and supervises committee chairmen**, himself chairing the Business Advisory Committee, the Rules Committee and the General Purpose Committee.

The **Deputy Speaker** performs the Speaker's duties when the office is vacant or he is absent, with full powers. The Speaker nominates a **panel of chairmen**; any member of the panel can preside in the absence of both. If **both** offices fall vacant, the **Governor** can appoint a member to preside (and similarly for the Council).

## The Chairman of the Council

The Chairman's powers are similar, **except the Money Bill power**: only the Speaker decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill. Salaries and allowances of all four officers are fixed by the State legislature and are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State**, so they are not subject to the annual vote.

## Impartiality

The offices are designed to be independent: security of tenure (removal needs an effective majority and notice), charged salaries and a bar on presiding at one's own removal debate. Yet the Speaker also decides defection cases and Money Bill status, which is why the offices are often drawn into controversy (module 9 and the 2023 Mains PYQ in module 10).

For the State Speaker's wider role compare GS-183 module 8 (State legislatures, history, and Speaker against Chairman).

### 6. Sessions, quorum, voting, language and privileges

*Objective:* Explain summoning, adjournment, prorogation and dissolution; the lapsing of Bills; quorum and voting; and the privileges of State legislatures.

## Summoning, adjournment, prorogation, dissolution

The **Governor summons** each House from time to time. The gap between two sessions can be **at most six months**, so the legislature must meet at least **twice a year**. A **session** consists of many **sittings**.

- **Adjournment** pauses a sitting for hours, days or weeks. **Adjournment sine die** ends a sitting for an indefinite period. Both are the **presiding officer's** power.
- **Prorogation** ends a **session**. When the business is complete the presiding officer adjourns the House sine die, and within a few days the **Governor** issues the prorogation notification (he may also prorogue a House in session).
- **Dissolution** ends the very **life** of a House. Only the **Assembly** can be dissolved; the Council cannot.

## What happens to pending Bills at dissolution

1. A Bill **pending in the Assembly lapses**, whether it began there or came from the Council.
2. A Bill **passed by the Assembly but pending in the Council lapses**.
3. A Bill **pending in the Council but not passed by the Assembly does not lapse**.
4. A Bill passed by the Assembly (unicameral) or both Houses (bicameral) but **awaiting the assent** of the Governor or President **does not lapse**.
5. A Bill passed by the House(s) but **returned by the President** for reconsideration **does not lapse**.

Compare the Parliament rules in GS-179.

## Quorum and voting

**Quorum** is the minimum number present before business can be done: **ten members or one-tenth of the total membership (including the presiding officer), whichever is greater**. In a House of 60, one-tenth is 6, so the quorum is 10; in a House of 200 it is 20. If there is no quorum, the presiding officer must adjourn or suspend.

Matters are decided by a **majority of members present and voting, excluding the presiding officer**, who has only a **casting vote** in a tie. A few matters need a special majority (for example removing the Speaker or Chairman).

## Language, ministers and the Governor's address

Business is transacted in the **official language(s) of the State, Hindi or English**; the presiding officer can permit a member to use the mother tongue. Every **minister and the Advocate General** may speak and take part in the proceedings of either House or its committees (without a vote), so a minister can address a House of which he is not a member. The **Governor** addresses the legislature at the first session after each general election and the first session of each year (Article 176), and may send messages on Bills (Article 175).

## Rules and privileges

Each House makes its own **rules of procedure** (Article 208). **Privileges** are special rights and immunities of the Houses, committees and members (Article 194; GS-181 explains the idea). Collective ones include publishing proceedings, excluding strangers, punishing breach or contempt, and barring arrests and legal process in the precincts without the presiding officer's leave. Individual ones: freedom from **civil** arrest during the session and **40 days before and after**; **freedom of speech** (no court proceedings for anything said or any vote given); exemption from **jury service**. **Courts cannot inquire** into the proceedings of the legislature (Article 212), and no discussion may take place on the conduct of a Supreme Court or High Court judge (Article 211). The privileges **do not extend to the Governor**, though he is part of the legislature; they do extend to ministers and the Advocate General.

### 7. Law-making in the State: ordinary Bills, Money Bills and the Governor's assent

*Objective:* Trace an ordinary Bill and a Money Bill through a bicameral State legislature, state the Council's maximum delay and why there is no joint sitting.

GS-179 covers Parliament's procedure and GS-180 covers Money Bills at the Centre. Here we keep only what is **different** in a State.

## Ordinary Bills: the path

An ordinary Bill can begin in **either House** (in a bicameral State), brought by a **minister or a private member**. In the first House it has **three readings** (first, second, third). It is passed by the State legislature only when **both Houses agree**, with or without amendments. In a **unicameral** State, the Assembly's passage sends it straight to the Governor.

In the **Council** it again has three readings. The Council has four options: **pass it as received; pass it with amendments; reject it; or take no action**. If it passes it unchanged, or the Assembly accepts its amendments, the Bill goes to the Governor.

## The Assembly's override: three months plus one

If the Assembly **rejects the Council's amendments**, or the Council **rejects the Bill**, or the Council **takes no action for three months**, the Assembly may **pass the Bill again** and send it back. Then, if the Council rejects it again, or proposes amendments the Assembly does not accept, or **does not pass it within one month**, the Bill is **deemed passed by both Houses in the form the Assembly passed it the second time**. So the Council can **detain a Bill for at most four months** (three plus one). The three months and the one month run from the date the Bill is **laid before the Council** (Article 197; verify), not from the date the Assembly passed it. The Council is therefore not even a revising House of equal power; the critics' phrase is a *dilatory chamber*.

**There is no joint sitting** in a State. In Parliament a deadlock goes to a joint sitting (Article 108; GS-179), but here the Assembly's will simply prevails. **One catch:** this override applies only to Bills that **began in the Assembly**. A Bill that **began in the Council and was rejected by the Assembly is dead**.

## Money Bills: the 14-day rule

A Money Bill **cannot be introduced in the Council**. It begins in the Assembly, only on the **Governor's recommendation**, and only by a **minister**. After the Assembly passes it, the Council may only **recommend**: it cannot reject or amend. It must **return the Bill within 14 days**. The Assembly may accept or reject any or all recommendations. If it accepts any, the Bill is deemed passed in the modified form; if none, or if the Council is silent for 14 days, it is deemed passed as the Assembly first passed it. So the Council can detain a Money Bill for at most **14 days**. The **Speaker's** decision that a Bill is a Money Bill is final (see GS-180 for the later judicial comment on the Speaker's certificate; verify).

## Assent

After passage, the Bill goes to the **Governor**, who may **assent, withhold assent, return it** (not a Money Bill) or **reserve it for the President**. If he returns an ordinary Bill and the House(s) pass it again, he **must assent**: a suspensive veto. For a Money Bill he cannot return it. **Reservation is compulsory** if the Bill endangers the High Court's position. If the **President** is asked to assent to a reserved Bill and returns it, the House(s) must reconsider it within **six months**; it is not stated in the Constitution that the President must then assent. GS-189 (module 4) works through Articles 200 and 201, including the recent assent disputes (verify).

## Financial Bills

Article 207 sets special rules for **financial Bills** (those with money provisions but not wholly Money Bills): an Article 207(1) Bill needs the Governor's recommendation and cannot be introduced in the Council; after passage in the Assembly the ordinary-Bill procedure applies (verify Art. 207; GS-180 gives the Union parallel).

### 8. The Council against the Rajya Sabha, and creating or abolishing a Council (Article 169)

*Objective:* Compare the Council with the Rajya Sabha and the Assembly, explain why it is weaker, and describe how a Council is created or abolished.

## Where the Council equals the Assembly

Laxmikanth lists the areas of broad equality: introduction and passage of **ordinary Bills** (though the Assembly prevails in disagreement); **approval of ordinances**; **selection of ministers** including the Chief Minister (they may sit in either House, but are responsible only to the Assembly); consideration of **reports of constitutional bodies** (State Finance Commission, State Public Service Commission, CAG); and **enlargement of the State PSC's jurisdiction**.

## Where it is unequal

The Council **cannot** receive a Money Bill; **cannot amend or reject** one; the Speaker alone decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill; it can delay an ordinary Bill by **four months at most**; it **only discusses the budget** and does not vote on demands for grants; it **cannot remove the Council of Ministers** by no-confidence (they are collectively responsible only to the Assembly); a Council-origin Bill rejected by the Assembly **dies**; it does **not take part in electing the President** or the State's Rajya Sabha members; it has **no effective say in ratifying a Constitution amendment** (the Assembly's will prevails); and its **very existence depends on the Assembly**.

## Why the Council is weaker than the Rajya Sabha

The Rajya Sabha has equal power with the Lok Sabha in all matters except finance and control of the government, and it is a **joint-sitting** partner. The Council is subordinate in every respect. Laxmikanth gives three reasons: (1) the Rajya Sabha represents the **States** and protects the federal balance, so it must be a real revising body, whereas the federal question does not arise for a Council; (2) the Council is **heterogeneously constituted**, with five kinds of members including nominees, while the Rajya Sabha represents States and has only 12 nominated out of 250; (3) democracy requires the **popular House to prevail**, a pattern borrowed from Britain, where the House of Lords can delay an ordinary Bill for at most a year and a Money Bill for a month. Critics call the Council a 'costly ornamental luxury' and a refuge for defeated politicians; defenders say it **checks hasty legislation** and brings in **experts who cannot face elections** (the Governor's one-sixth).

## Creating or abolishing a Council (Article 169)

The Constitution lets a State have a second chamber or not 'according to its own willingness and financial strength'. The procedure has two steps:

1. The **Legislative Assembly passes a resolution** by a **special majority**: a majority of the **total membership** of the Assembly **and** at least two-thirds of the members **present and voting**.
2. **Parliament then passes a law** to create or abolish the Council.

This law is **not an amendment of the Constitution for Article 368**; it passes by **simple majority like an ordinary Act**. Note that Parliament *may* act: Article 169 says 'Parliament may by law', so the resolution is a precondition and does not bind Parliament (the point rests on the Article's wording; the extract shows no case in which Parliament ignored a resolution, so verify before citing an example).

## The record

Councils were **abolished** in **West Bengal and Punjab (1969), Andhra Pradesh (1985)** and **Tamil Nadu (1986)**. Andhra Pradesh's was **created again** by the Andhra Pradesh Legislative Council Act, 2005 (revived in 2007). Parliament passed the Tamil Nadu Legislative Council Act, 2010 to create one, but before it came into force the Tamil Nadu Assembly resolved in 2011 against it. J&K's Council was abolished by the 2019 Act. **Verify the present position**: the reported 2020 Andhra Pradesh resolution to abolish its Council, and Assembly resolutions asking for a Council (reported for Assam, Rajasthan, Odisha and West Bengal), are not in the extracts and the outcomes must be checked from a current source before you cite them for the 2021 Mains-type question 'review the working and current status'.

### 9. Wider powers, ordinances and the contemporary issues: Union Territory legislatures, women and the working of the House

*Objective:* Group the powers of a State legislature, summarise the ordinance power, and handle the 2025 J&K question and issues of working and representation with correct flags.

## Powers of the State legislature in five groups

Laxmikanth treats these through the procedure above; grouping them is a useful exam habit (the groupings are standard knowledge: verify against Articles 245-246 and Part VI).

1. **Law-making** on the **State List** and (with Parliament) the **Concurrent List**; Parliament can legislate on State List matters only in limited circumstances. The **Seventh Schedule** lists the subjects: it is the subject of GS-191 (to come on Day 6) and is only mentioned here.
2. **Financial**: the annual budget (Articles 202-206), voting on demands for grants (Assembly alone), Money Bills (module 7).
3. **Control of the executive**: the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the **Assembly**; the legislature questions, debates and can remove the government by a no-confidence motion.
4. **Electoral and constituent**: Elected members of the Assembly elect the President and the State's Rajya Sabha members and ratify certain Constitution amendments (module 8).
5. **Privileges and internal regulation** (module 6).

## Ordinances (Article 213)

When the legislature is **not in session** the Governor may issue an **ordinance** with the force of an Act, on **State and Concurrent List matters**, if he is satisfied that **immediate action** is necessary. It must be **laid before the legislature** and **ceases six weeks after reassembly** unless earlier disapproved. The details, the Governor's duty to act on advice, the President's instructions in three cases and the cases *D. C. Wadhwa* (1987) and *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017) are in **GS-189, module 5**; do not re-learn them here. The Council has **equal power with the Assembly in approving ordinances**.

## Anti-defection: mention only

The **Tenth Schedule** (anti-defection) makes a member who defects disqualified; the **Speaker or Chairman** decides, subject to judicial review (*Kihoto*, 1992). NCERT adds that the law 'has not been able to curb defections' but has increased the power of party leaders and presiding officers. It has its own topic.

## Union Territory legislatures: the 2025 J&K question

Only some Union Territories have legislatures: **Delhi** (70) and **Puducherry** (30) in Laxmikanth's table, and, after the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, **Jammu and Kashmir**. Laxmikanth notes that the Act fixes 107 seats for J&K, of which 24 (for Pakistan-occupied Kashmir) are vacant and not counted, and allows the Lieutenant Governor to **nominate two women** if they are not adequately represented. **Verify everything else**: later changes to the number of seats and nominated members, the Assembly's power to legislate on State List and Concurrent List matters **except public order and police**, and the role of the Lieutenant Governor and Union government. For the 2025 Mains question on 'nature' and 'powers', the safe structure is: it is a **Union Territory legislature** (not a State's), unicameral, with limited powers under the Act, subordinate to Parliament and with a prominent role for the Lieutenant Governor (verify in the Act).

NCERT notes a decline in **sittings** and debate time in Parliament and State Assemblies and problems of **absence of quorum** and **boycott**.   - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** number
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** What are the minimum and maximum strength of a Legislative Assembly, and the minimum age for membership?
  - **a:** 60 to 500 members (lower floors for a few States); minimum age 25.
- - **id:** GS-190-C2
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** number
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** What are the maximum and minimum strength of a Legislative Council?
  - **a:** At most one-third of the Assembly's strength; at least 40.
- - **id:** GS-190-C3
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** list
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** How is a Legislative Council filled, source by source?
  - **a:** 1/3 local bodies, 1/12 graduates, 1/12 teachers, 1/3 MLAs, 1/6 nominated by the Governor.
  - **mnemonic:**
    - **kind:** image
    - **text:** A Council clock has 12 hours: 4 go to local bodies, 1 to graduates, 1 to teachers, 4 to MLAs and the last 2 to the Governor's guests.
- - **id:** GS-190-C4
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:**
    - prelims
    - mains
  - **q:** How is a Legislative Council created or abolished?
  - **a:** By a Parliament law on the Assembly's special-majority resolution (Article 169); such a law is not an Article 368 amendment.
  - **mnemonic:**
    - **kind:** major
    - **text:** Parliament rolls up or pulls down the shutter of a Council TeaSHoP (169), but only after the State Assembly votes for it.
    - **codes:**
      - - **number:** 169
        - **word:** TeaSHoP
- - **id:** GS-190-C5
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** number
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** For how long can a Council hold up an ordinary Bill, and a Money Bill?
  - **a:** Ordinary Bill: at most four months (three plus one). Money Bill: 14 days.
- - **id:** GS-190-C6
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** How is a deadlock between the Assembly and the Council on an ordinary Bill resolved?
  - **a:** No joint sitting; the Assembly passes the Bill again and overrides the Council.
- - **id:** GS-190-C7
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** What happens to a Bill that originated in the Council and is rejected by the Assembly?
  - **a:** It dies.
- - **id:** GS-190-C8
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** Who decides whether a State Bill is a Money Bill, and where can it be introduced?
  - **a:** The Speaker, finally; only in the Assembly, on the Governor's recommendation.
- - **id:** GS-190-C9
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** list
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** On dissolution of the Assembly, which Bills do not lapse?
  - **a:** Bills pending in the Council not passed by the Assembly; passed Bills awaiting assent; Bills returned by the President for reconsideration.
- - **id:** GS-190-C10
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** What is the quorum of a State House?
  - **a:** Ten members or one-tenth of the House, whichever is greater.
- - **id:** GS-190-C11
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:** - prelims
  - **q:** How is an Assembly Speaker removed?
  - **a:** By a majority of all the then members, after 14 days' notice.
- - **id:** GS-190-C12
  - **tier:** 1
  - **type:** qa
  - **exam:**
    - prelims
    - mains
  - **q:** What did Kihoto Hollohan v Zachilhu (1992) hold on defection decisions?
  - **a:** The Speaker's or Chairman's decision on defection disqualification is subject to judicial review.
