## GS-088: Buddhist, Jain and Hindu iconography

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Buddhist, Jain and Hindu iconography and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### From symbol to image in Buddhism

Early sculptors at Sanchi presented the Buddha's presence by symbols — empty seat, tree, stupa, wheel — so a tree is not just a tree but an event. Popular motifs entered too: the shalabhanjika, a woman whose touch makes trees blossom; serpents; animals such as elephants signifying strength and wisdom. From the first century CE, Mahayana's saviour ideas and Bodhisattvas encouraged worship of images. Mathura gave the Buddha a yaksha-like, broad human form, Gandhara added Hellenistic features, and later Sarnath images wore transparent robes over both shoulders. Bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara, shown at Kurkihar in graceful tribhanga, and, with Vajrayana, goddesses such as Tara joined the repertoire.

### Hindu iconography and the Puranas

Vaishnavism and Shaivism stressed bhakti to a chosen deity. Ten avatars of Vishnu were recognised, a way of absorbing popular local gods into a broader tradition; Shiva was usually represented by the linga but sometimes in human form. Head-dresses, ornaments, seating and ayudhas carried meaning, and the Puranas, compiled in simple Sanskrit verse to be read aloud to all, supplied the stories. Regional types emerged: Kashmir and Himachal bronzes show four-headed Vaikuntha Vishnu with Vasudeva, Narasimha and Varaha faces. Chola bronzes of Tamil Nadu developed Nataraja, Kalyanasundara (Shiva taking Parvati's hand) and Ardhanarishvara, which unites Shiva and Parvati in one body.

### Jain images and the bronze medium

Lost-wax casting, known since the Indus Valley's Dancing Girl, carried icons of all three faiths from the second to the sixteenth century. Kushana-period bronzes from Chausa, Bihar, show tirthankaras with powerful physiques, Adinath singled out by long locks. The Akota hoard near Vadodara shows western Indian casting between the sixth and ninth centuries, with tirthankaras on thrones singly, in threes or in groups of twenty-four, inlaid with silver and copper, and shasanadevis such as Chakreshvari and Ambika. Portable Gupta and Vakataka Buddha bronzes spread the classical style as monks carried them. Vijayanagara bronzes at Tirupati even turned to portraiture, showing Krishnadevaraya and his queens in namaskara mudra.

### Worked example

Q: 'Indian religious iconography grew from a shared visual vocabulary.' Examine. Frame: Buddhist symbols (seat, tree, wheel, stupa) and shift to human form at Mathura/Gandhara; popular motifs (yaksha, shalabhanjika, serpent, Gajalakshmi) used across faiths; Hindu ayudhas, avatars and Puranic narratives; Jain marks (Adinath's locks, shasanadevis); bronze as a medium carrying all three. Conclude that shared forms coexisted with distinct doctrine.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which symbol represented the Buddha's first sermon?
  - **a:** The wheel (dharmachakra).
- - **q:** How is Adinath distinguished from other tirthankaras?
  - **a:** By long hair-locks falling to his shoulders; others have short curly hair.
- - **q:** What does Nataraja's raised left leg (bhujangatrasita) signify?
  - **a:** Tirobhava — kicking away the veil of maya from the devotee's mind.

### Common trap

Reading a sculpture literally or by one tradition only. The woman with elephants pouring water can be Maya (the Buddha's mother) or Gajalakshmi, and devotees may have seen both; James Fergusson misread Sanchi as a tree-and-serpent cult because he studied images without the texts.
