## GS-142: Salient characteristics and diversity

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Salient characteristics and diversity and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### What diversity means and why it is powerful

Sociology uses diversity for the presence of many distinct communities, each identified by markers such as mother tongue, faith, sect, race or caste. These belongings are ascriptive: a person is born into them without choosing or earning membership, yet they feel total and are hard to shed because others keep labelling us by them. Because they are universal and unconditional, they generate deep emotional loyalty and can mobilise large numbers of people. Expanding circles of kinship, caste, language, region and religion give people a sense of who they are, which is why a perceived threat to the community can provoke strong and even violent reactions. Diversity becomes difficult when cultural difference coincides with economic and social inequality, or when groups must divide scarce resources such as river water, jobs or government money.

### Measuring India's plurality

Census 2011 placed India's population near 1.21 billion. The population uses around 1,632 languages and dialects, and 22 languages enjoy legal status through the Eighth Schedule. In religion, Hindus form roughly four-fifths of the population but are internally divided by region, belief, caste and language; Muslims account for 14.2%, Christians 2.3% and Sikhs 1.7%, while Jains (0.4%) and Buddhists (0.7%) are smaller still. NCERT points out that small percentages still translate into very large absolute numbers in a country of this size. The textbook's opening chapter adds that sociology locates each individual on a social map of region, language, class, religion, caste or tribe, and that young people make up roughly forty per cent of the population, which shapes how identities are formed and contested.

### Nation-state choices: suppress or accommodate

Many states fear that recognising community identities will fragment them, so they pursue assimilation, centralise power where the dominant group is a majority, impose one official language or seize minority lands as national resources. NCERT argues that suppressing diversity is costly because it alienates minorities and can intensify the very identities it targets. India ruled out assimilation from the start and did not banish religion or language from public life; instead communities are explicitly recognised and minority religions receive strong constitutional protection. Drawing on the UNDP Human Development Report 2004, the chapter calls India a 'state-nation' in which multiple, complementary identities coexist with loyalty to the country, while warning that pressures to impose a single identity can erode this achievement.
