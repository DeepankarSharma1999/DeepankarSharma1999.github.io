## GS-213: Political parties and pressure groups

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Institutions, law and politics

*Goal:* Explain Political parties and pressure groups and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Functions of parties

NCERT lists what parties do: contest elections by choosing candidates; offer policies and programmes for voters to choose from; shape laws, since legislators largely follow party direction; form and run governments; act as opposition when they lose; shape public opinion by raising issues and launching movements; and give citizens access to government machinery and welfare schemes. It argues that without parties every candidate would be independent, no one could promise major policy change, and responsibility for running the country would be unclear. Popular distrust of parties coexists with their necessity, a tension worth noting in answers.

### Registration, recognition and the ECI

The Election Commission registers parties, treats them equally, and extends special facilities to recognised parties, chiefly a unique symbol reserved for their official candidates. NCERT gives simplified vote-and-seat thresholds for State and national recognition, notes six recognised national parties under the Commission's 2023 notification, and directs readers to the ECI for the detailed rules. The Class XI chapter lists recognition of parties and allotment of symbols among the Commission's core functions. India has a multiparty system in which coalitions and alliances are common, a pattern NCERT links to the country's social and regional diversity.

### Pressure and interest groups

Groups that feel parties are ignoring their interests can either form a new party or organise to lobby government. NCERT's sociology text gives examples: industrialists in FICCI and ASSOCHAM, workers in INTUC and CITU, farmers in Shetkari Sangathan, and separate unions of agricultural labourers. Such interest groups mainly work by lobbying legislators and by delegations, street protests, public meetings and media campaigns. The Class X text notes that many pressure groups are extensions of parties among social sections, so the line is blurred in India. A critique to include in answers: groups differ greatly in access and leverage, so some argue the idea underplays the dominance of class, caste or gender, though pressure groups and movements remain vital to democracy.

### Challenges and reform

Parties face four criticisms: power concentrated in a few leaders with no membership registers or regular internal elections; dynastic succession; reliance on wealthy candidates, donors and sometimes criminals; and shrinking ideological differences. Reforms so far include the anti-defection law, mandatory candidate affidavits on property and criminal cases ordered by the Supreme Court, and ECI orders on organisational elections and tax returns. Proposals include laws regulating internal party affairs, reserving about one-third of tickets for women, and state funding of elections. NCERT warns against over-regulation and stresses that citizens, pressure groups, movements and media can push parties to reform through petitions, publicity and agitation.

### Worked example

Statement: 'Under NCERT's summary of the criteria, a party winning 6% of votes and one seat in an Assembly election becomes a recognised State party.' Answer: incorrect — the summary requires at least two seats along with 6% of votes. Mains frame for pressure groups: definition (influence without seeking office) → examples (FICCI, CITU, Shetkari Sangathan) → methods (lobbying, protest, media) → link to parties → critique of unequal access.

### Recall

- - **q:** Recognition criteria for a State party per NCERT?
  - **a:** At least 6% of votes in the Assembly election and at least two seats.
- - **q:** Four challenges to parties per NCERT?
  - **a:** Internal democracy, dynastic succession, money and muscle power, lack of meaningful choice.
- - **q:** Name two business and two labour organisations NCERT cites as interest groups.
  - **a:** FICCI and ASSOCHAM; INTUC and CITU.
- - **q:** How can pressure groups push parties to reform?
  - **a:** Through petitions, publicity and agitations.

### Common trap

Registration and recognition are different: every party must register with the Election Commission, but only those meeting vote-and-seat thresholds are 'recognised' and receive reserved symbols. Likewise, pressure groups influence power without contesting for it.
