## GS-045: Civil Disobedience and Salt March

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Satyagraha, civil disobedience, non-cooperation, hartal and picketing

- **Satyagraha**: Gandhi's method of firm, non-violent insistence on truth and justice. A person who practises it is a *satyagrahi*.
- **Non-cooperation**: refusing to help the government or to use its institutions (courts, schools, offices, foreign goods) which breaks no law by itself. The Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920 to 1922 (GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written; primer in module 10) used this method. NCERT Class X notes that in 1921-22 people were asked to refuse cooperation.
- **Civil disobedience**: *deliberately breaking particular laws* that are seen as unjust, openly and without violence, and accepting the punishment. NCERT's difference line for the 1930 movement is that people were now asked not only to refuse cooperation but also to break colonial laws.
- **Hartal**: a general closing of shops and work as a form of protest.
- **Picketing**: standing outside a shop (for example one selling foreign cloth or liquor) to persuade customers not to enter.

Two more words you will meet: **no-tax campaign** (refusing to pay a tax, such as land revenue or the chaukidari tax, a levy for village watchmen) and **forest laws**, rules that closed forest areas to grazing and collection of wood. Breaking forest laws was a civil-disobedience act in forest regions.

**The test is whether a law is broken, not whether you act or refrain.** Leaving a government school breaks no law (non-cooperation). Making salt, grazing cattle in a reserved forest or refusing a legally due tax breaks a law (civil disobedience). Non-Cooperation in 1921-22 also planned a no-tax civil-disobedience phase (the Bardoli plan; GS-043 is to come on Day 14, not yet written; verify).

### Bridge: Dominion status, Purna Swaraj and the timeline 1927 to 1934

**Dominion status** meant a self-governing position within the British Empire, like that of countries such as Canada or Australia, with the Crown still the head. **Purna Swaraj** means complete self-rule, that is, complete independence, with the British connection severed (the Independence Pledge's own words: India must 'sever the British connection'). The Nehru Report of 1928 asked for dominion status; the Lahore Congress of 1929 chose Purna Swaraj. An **ultimatum** is a final demand with a deadline. The **Congress Working Committee (CWC)** is the Congress's executive body, which authorised Gandhi to lead the movement.

### 1. Orientation: the story in one page

*Objective:* State in five sentences how the Civil Disobedience Movement began and where each neighbouring topic is taught.

## The story in five sentences

1. The Simon Commission (all-British, 1927 to 1928) angered all parties, and the Nehru Report's dominion-status demand did not satisfy the young; the Calcutta Congress of December 1928 gave the government a one-year deadline.
2. When the Irwin declaration of October 1929 offered no timetable, the **Lahore Congress (December 1929)** declared **Purna Swaraj** and authorised civil disobedience.
3. Gandhi then wrote his **Eleven Demands**, chose **salt** as the symbol, and walked from Sabarmati to **Dandi** (12 March to 6 April 1930) with 78 companions, ending by breaking the salt law.
4. The movement spread to coast and interior (no-tax campaigns, forest-law breaches, boycott, picketing) with women, students, peasants, merchants and tribals; the government answered with arrests, ordinances and lathi-charges, including Dharasana.
5. The first phase ended with the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact of March 1931**, which is taught in **GS-046**.

### 2. The run-up: Simon, Calcutta 1928 and the Irwin declaration

*Objective:* Explain why the Congress moved from a dominion-status demand to a deadline and then to a civil-disobedience programme in 1928 to 1929.

## Economic background

NCERT Class X: agricultural prices fell from 1926 and collapsed after 1930, so peasants could not sell harvests or pay revenue; the countryside was in turmoil, which is why no-tax campaigns found a ready audience.

## Simon Commission (primer; full lesson GS-044, to come on Day 15, not yet written)

The Government of India Act 1919 provided for a review after ten years. The all-white Simon Commission was appointed on 8 November 1927 (Spectrum); Indians resented the exclusion and the idea that foreigners would judge their fitness for self-government. The Madras Congress (December 1927, M.A. Ansari presiding) decided to boycott it 'at every stage and in every form'. It landed at Bombay on 3 February 1928 to a hartal and 'Simon Go Back' slogans; Lala Lajpat Rai, beaten in October 1928, died on 17 November 1928. Youth, led by Nehru and Subhas Bose, came forward.

## Nehru Report (primer; full lesson GS-044, to come on Day 15, not yet written)

Lord Birkenhead challenged Indians to produce an agreed constitution; the committee under Motilal Nehru reported in August 1928, asking for dominion status, joint electorates with reserved seats for minorities, linguistic provinces and nineteen fundamental rights. Younger leaders (Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Bose) saw dominion status as a step backward.

## Calcutta Congress, December 1928: the deadline

The Congress approved the Nehru Report but the younger leaders demanded Purna Swaraj. Gandhi and Motilal Nehru wished to keep the dominion demand and suggested a two-year grace period, reduced to one year under youth pressure. The Congress decided that if the government did not accept a constitution on dominion lines by the end of 1929, it would demand complete independence and launch civil disobedience.

## 1929 and the Irwin declaration

Gandhi toured incessantly, organised constructive village work and redressed grievances on Bardoli lines (1928); a Foreign Cloth Boycott Committee was formed (Spectrum). The Meerut Conspiracy Case (March), Bhagat Singh and B.K. Dutt's Assembly bomb (April) and a minority Labour government (May) kept the temperature high.

On 31 October 1929 Irwin said the natural issue of India's constitutional progress was dominion status and promised a Round Table Conference, with no time scale. The **Delhi Manifesto** (2 November) asked that the conference frame a dominion constitution, that the Congress have a majority of representation and that political prisoners be amnestied. At the December 1929 meeting Irwin said the conference was not to draft a dominion scheme; the demands were rejected and the stage for confrontation was set (Spectrum).

**Reading rule.** The movement was not a sudden idea of Gandhi. It followed a *public deadline* that the government let pass.


### 3. Lahore 1929: Purna Swaraj and the Independence Pledge

*Objective:* State who presided at Lahore, what it decided, what happened on 31 December 1929 and 26 January 1930, and why Gandhi still needed a concrete issue.

## The president

**Jawaharlal Nehru** was chosen president of the Lahore session (December 1929), mainly through Gandhi's backing: Spectrum notes that 15 of 18 Provincial Congress Committees had opposed him. The reasons: the occasion (acceptance of complete independence) and the wish to acknowledge the youth upsurge in the anti-Simon campaign; NCERT Class XII reads it as the 'passing of the baton' to the younger generation. In his address Nehru described the aim as 'an open conspiracy to free this country from foreign rule', said he was a socialist and republican, and said that a liberation movement must be a mass movement and 'essentially peaceful'.

## The decisions

- The **Round Table Conference was to be boycotted**.
- **Complete independence** was declared the aim of the Congress.
- The Congress Working Committee was authorised to launch a **civil disobedience** programme including non-payment of taxes, and members of legislatures were asked to **resign their seats**.
- **26 January 1930** was fixed as the first Independence (Swarajya) Day.

## The flag and the pledge

At midnight on 31 December 1929, on the banks of the Ravi, Nehru hoisted the newly adopted tricolour amid slogans of Inquilab Zindabad. On 26 January 1930 meetings across villages and towns read an independence pledge, said to have been drafted by Gandhi (Spectrum). NCERT Class XII quotes Gandhi's instructions: the declaration should be made by whole villages at the same hour, with the flag hoisted and the day spent in constructive work such as spinning, service of 'untouchables', Hindu-Muslim unity or prohibition work.

The pledge in plain words: freedom is an inalienable right; British rule has ruined India economically (high revenue, destroyed village industries, manipulated customs, currency and exchange), politically, culturally and spiritually; India must therefore sever the British connection and attain Purna Swaraj, withdraw voluntary association from the government and prepare for civil disobedience, including non-payment of taxes.

## The practical problem

NCERT Class X records that the Independence Day celebrations attracted very little attention. Gandhi therefore had to find a way to relate the abstract idea of freedom to concrete problems of daily life. This sets up the next module.

**Two cautions.** (1) Do not confuse 26 January 1930 (Independence Day pledge) with 26 January 1950 (the date the Constitution came into force): they are different events, though the earlier date explains the later choice (standard link; verify). (2) Spectrum describes meetings in villages and towns while NCERT Class X stresses weak response: say the celebrations were widespread but did not by themselves create a mass campaign.

### 4. The Eleven Demands and why salt

*Objective:* List the Eleven Demands in their three groups, state the date discrepancy in the sources, and explain at least three reasons for choosing salt.

## The Eleven Demands

NCERT Class X: Gandhi's letter to Irwin of 31 January 1930 stated eleven demands, some of general interest and some specific to classes from industrialists to peasants, so all classes could identify with the campaign. Spectrum groups them in three.

| Group | Demands |
|---|---|
| General interest (1 to 6) | 1. Reduce expenditure on army and civil services by 50 per cent. 2. Introduce total prohibition. 3. Reform the Criminal Investigation Department (CID). 4. Change the Arms Act to allow popular control of firearms licences. 5. Release political prisoners. 6. Accept the Postal Reservation Bill. |
| Specific demands of the business class (7 to 9) | 7. Reduce the rupee-sterling exchange ratio to 1s 4d. 8. Introduce textile protection. 9. Reserve coastal shipping for Indians. |
| Specific demands of peasants (10 and 11) | 10. Reduce land revenue by 50 per cent. 11. Abolish the salt tax and the government's salt monopoly. |

A check on the count: 6 + 3 + 2 = 11. Notice that **independence is not among the eleven**: they were concrete reforms offered as a way to avoid a campaign.

## The date discrepancy

Spectrum: an ultimatum of 31 January 1930. NCERT Class X: the letter went on 31 January and civil disobedience would follow if the demands were not met by 11 March. Spectrum adds that on 2 March Gandhi informed the Viceroy of his plan. Safe wording: **the letter of demands is dated 31 January 1930; the march began on 12 March after no positive response (verify the exact deadline)**.

## Why salt?

1. **Universal need.** Spectrum quotes Gandhi: apart from water, no other article like salt lets the government reach the starving millions, the sick and the helpless; he called the tax 'the most inhuman poll tax'. NCERT Class X: rich and poor alike consumed salt, and the tax and monopoly were 'the most oppressive face' of British rule.
2. **A daily grievance linked to swaraj** (Spectrum): concrete and universal for the rural poor.
3. **No divisive implications**, unlike a no-rent campaign, which would divide landlords and tenants (Spectrum).
4. **Self-help and symbolism.** Like khadi, salt-making gave the poor a small income and let urban people identify symbolically with mass suffering.
5. **A clearly unjust law.** NCERT Class XII: people could not make salt even for domestic use and bought it dear. Gandhi called the monopoly a 'fourfold curse' (quoted by NCERT): it deprives people of a village industry, destroys property that nature produces, adds to national expenditure and taxes a starving people by more than 1,000 per cent (his claim, not an audited statistic).

**Interpretive point.** The tactical genius, as NCERT Class XII puts it, was to make a widely disliked law the target. Do not say salt was chosen 'only' for revenue reasons.


### 5. The Salt March: route, dates, participants, Dandi

*Objective:* Narrate the march with its dates, distance and participants, reconcile differences in the sources, and describe how the march was reported and received.

## The facts

| Item | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Start | 12 March 1930, from Gandhi's ashram at Sabarmati, near Ahmedabad | Spectrum, NCERT |
| Companions | A band of 78 members of the Sabarmati Ashram (NCERT: 78 trusted volunteers); thousands joined along the way | Spectrum, NCERT |
| Distance | Over 240 miles | Spectrum, NCERT Class X |
| Duration | 24 days at about 10 miles a day (NCERT Class X); 'three weeks later' (NCERT Class XII) | NCERT |
| Goal | Dandi, a coastal hamlet in Gujarat | All |
| Law broken | 6 April 1930, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt (Spectrum); NCERT Class X says salt was manufactured by boiling sea water | Spectrum, NCERT |

## Doing the arithmetic

From 12 March to 5 April is 24 days; at 240 miles that is 10 miles a day, matching NCERT Class X. Counting to 6 April gives 25 days: the usual 24 walking days end with arrival on the evening of 5 April and the ceremonial breaking of the law next morning. NCERT Class XII's 'three weeks' is a loose phrase for the same walk.

## The route

The march went from the Ashram through the villages of Gujarat to the coast. The village-by-village list (commonly Aslali, Nadiad, Anand, Jambusar, Bharuch, Surat, Navsari) is standard knowledge not in the extracts; verify it and learn only 'Sabarmati to Dandi in Gujarat'. NCERT Class XII notes that police reports of Gandhi's speeches trace the progress; in them he urged village officials to resign.

## Before and during

- On 2 March Gandhi told the Viceroy of his plan (Spectrum); NCERT Class XII says Irwin failed to grasp its significance.
- His directions (Spectrum): break the salt law wherever possible; picket foreign liquor and cloth shops; refuse taxes if there is strength; lawyers give up practice; boycott courts; officials resign; all subject to truth and non-violence; obey local leaders after his arrest. In Gujarat 300 village officials resigned.
- At Wasna he told upper-caste villagers that swaraj required service of untouchables and Hindu-Muslim-Parsi-Sikh unity (NCERT Class XII, police reports).
- On 5 April at Dandi he said, 'Tomorrow we shall break the salt tax law', and that when a whole nation is roused no leader is necessary (NCERT Class XII).

## Reception

NCERT Class XII: the European and American press covered the march widely (Time moved from scorn to calling the British 'desperately anxious'). It credits the march with three achievements: it brought Gandhi to world attention; it was the first nationalist activity in which women took part in large numbers (uneasy beside Spectrum's account of women in the Swadeshi movement from 1905, so treat 'first' with care); and it forced the British to realise the Raj would not last forever.

After Dandi, Gandhi asked people to make salt from sea water at home (Spectrum). His arrest in May 1930, before the Dharasana raid, is in module 8.


### 6. The spread of the movement: region by region

*Objective:* Recall the main regional episodes with leaders and methods, and understand why different regions used different forms of protest.

Once the law was broken at Dandi, defiance of the salt law began all over the country. Nehru's arrest in April 1930 for defying it produced demonstrations in Madras, Calcutta and Karachi. After Gandhi's arrest the CWC sanctioned non-payment of revenue in ryotwari areas, a no-chaukidara-tax campaign in zamindari areas and violation of forest laws in the Central Provinces (Spectrum). Use the list as a map checklist (Spectrum unless marked; the full fifteen-row regional table is in the master notes).

- **Tamil Nadu**: C. Rajagopalachari's April 1930 march from Tiruchirappalli to Vedaranniyam on the Tanjore coast.
- **Malabar**: K. Kelappan's salt march; P. Krishna Pillai defended the flag against a lathi-charge on Calicut beach (November 1930).
- **Bengal**: the largest number of arrests and highest violence; strong rural movements around salt and chaukidari tax (Midnapur); Surya Sen's Chittagong armoury raid.
- **North-West Frontier**: Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgars (Red Shirts); arrests on 23 April 1930 led to mass demonstrations in Peshawar and martial law, and a section of the Garhwal Rifles refused to fire (92 per cent Muslim province).
- **Sholapur**: textile workers struck from 7 May; a virtual parallel government until martial law after 16 May.
- **Gujarat**: a no-tax movement in Kheda, Bardoli and Bharuch; the Dharasana raid of 21 May 1930 is in module 8.
- **Bihar and United Provinces**: Bihar's salt gestures gave way to a strong no-chaukidari-tax agitation; in the UP the no-revenue campaign became virtually a no-rent campaign.
- **Forest regions** (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Central Provinces): breach of grazing and timber rules and public sale of forest produce.

## Why different forms?

The form followed the local grievance: salt where there was a coast, forest laws where forests were closed to villagers, chaukidari tax or no-rent where the revenue system pinched, picketing in towns.

Other forms of mobilisation named by Spectrum: prabhat pheris (dawn processions), vanar senas (monkey brigades of children), manjari senas, secret patrikas (newsletters) and magic lantern shows.

## Reading the table

- **Class interests shaped swaraj.** For rich peasants it meant lower revenue; for business, freedom from colonial restrictions; for the poor, rent relief; for women, service to the nation. NCERT Class X: not all groups were moved by the abstract concept of swaraj.
- **A balanced coalition.** The Congress avoided no-rent campaigns and workers' demands to keep landlords, rich peasants and industrialists on board. Spectrum frames it differently: the massive participation of peasants and business groups compensated for the decline of other features compared with Non-Cooperation.

### 8. Government response, Dharasana and the hand-off

*Objective:* Explain the government's dilemma and repression, describe Dharasana, and state where the pact and the later phase are taught.

## The government's dilemma

Spectrum calls the government's attitude in 1930 ambivalent and perplexed: force drew cries of 'repression', inaction cries of 'victory', and even Gandhi's arrest came after vacillation. Once repression began, ordinances banning civil liberties were freely used, the press was gagged and provincial governments could ban civil disobedience organisations. The Congress Working Committee was declared unlawful in June 1930 (Spectrum); the Congress as a whole was banned only in January 1932 (standard dating; verify). There were lathi-charges and firing on unarmed crowds, and thousands of satyagrahis besides Gandhi and other leaders were jailed. NCERT Class X: a frightened government began brutal repression, with women and children beaten and about 100,000 arrested; NCERT Class XII: nearly 60,000 were arrested in the wake of the march. The figures cover different spans, so state the source if a number is asked.

## Dharasana, 21 May 1930

Gandhi had announced a raid on the Dharasana Salt Works on the west coast but was arrested first (Spectrum: 4 May; some books give 5 May; verify). On 21 May, Sarojini Naidu, Imam Sahib and Manilal (Gandhi's son) led the unfinished raid. The unarmed and peaceful crowd was met with a brutal lathi-charge that left 2 dead and 320 injured in Spectrum's account (other accounts differ; verify). Spectrum quotes Naidu's instruction not to resist or even raise a hand to ward off blows, and an American journalist, Webb Miller (Spectrum prints 'Web'; a United Press correspondent, verify), who reported that the volunteers marched steadily without any sign of fear. Similar raids followed at Wadala (Bombay), Sanikatta (Karnataka), in Andhra and in Midnapore, Balasore, Puri and Cuttack.

**Why Dharasana matters.** It showed non-violence under attack, won international press coverage and turned salt raids into mass actions led by women and local volunteers once Gandhi was jailed. The argument does not depend on the casualty figures, which stay flagged.

## The political track in 1930

Talks and the first Round Table Conference (November 1930, without the Congress) are taught in GS-046 (to come on Day 10, not yet written); the Simon report (Spectrum: May 1930; usually dated June 1930, verify) did not mention dominion status, which upset even moderate opinion.

## Hand-off, stated plainly

The first phase ended with the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** of March 1931. Its terms, the Karachi session, the Round Table Conferences, the Communal Award and the Poona Pact are for GS-046 (to come on Day 10, not yet written) and are not taught here. After the second conference failed, the movement resumed in January 1932 and Gandhi withdrew it in April 1934 (Spectrum; NCERT Class X says it lost momentum by 1934); the second phase is only a pointer.

For the Mains, remember one balanced line: the government used force, but also negotiated; the combination is part of the movement's significance (module 9).

### 9. Significance, limitations and historiography

*Objective:* Assess the movement with a balanced list of achievements and limits and write a 'historians differ' paragraph using the interpretations in the extracts.

## How the movement differed from Non-Cooperation (Spectrum's eight points)

1. The stated objective was complete independence, not remedying two specific wrongs and a vague swaraj.
2. Methods involved violation of law from the beginning, not only non-cooperation.
3. A decline in forms of protest of the intelligentsia (lawyers giving up practice, students leaving government schools).
4. Muslim participation was nowhere near that of Non-Cooperation.
5. No major labour upsurge coincided with the movement.
6. Massive participation of peasants and business groups compensated.
7. The number imprisoned was about three times more.
8. The Congress was organisationally stronger.

(The Non-Cooperation side is in GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written. The cross-movement comparison is extended in GS-053, to come on Day 10, not yet written; keep this wording identical when GS-053 is written.)

## Significance

- **Mass reach.** Women, students, peasants, merchants, tribal communities and sections of workers across regions.
- **Economic and fiscal effect.** Imports of foreign cloth and other goods fell; the government lost liquor, excise and land-revenue income; elections to the Legislative Assembly were largely boycotted (Spectrum).
- **Political recognition.** NCERT Class XII: the British had to realise the Raj would not last forever and must devolve power; the government negotiated and treated the Congress leader as an equal (the pact, GS-046).
- **Women's entry into the public sphere.** Spectrum calls it the most liberating experience for Indian women and the mark of their entry into the public sphere.
- **Programmatic clarity.** Purna Swaraj became the declared aim; the Karachi resolutions of 1931 later spelled out what swaraj meant for the masses (pointer: GS-046, GS-053).

## Limitations

- **Uneven social base.** Limited participation of Muslims and Dalits; Congress reluctance to support no-rent campaigns and workers' demands (NCERT Class X, Spectrum).
- **Class interests.** Rich peasants and merchants withdrew when their demands (revenue cuts, tariff protection) were not met and fear of militancy grew.
- **Violence and local variation.** Violent clashes occurred in several places (Tamil Nadu, Peshawar, Sholapur), contrary to the intended non-violence.
- **No immediate constitutional gain.** The conferences did not deliver independence (GS-046); repression resumed.

## Historiography: writing 'historians differ'

- **Gandhi's tactical wisdom**: NCERT Class XII praises the choice of the salt monopoly; Spectrum stresses that salt linked swaraj with a concrete grievance without divisive implications.
- **Contemporary mockery and praise**: Spectrum cites Brailsford's remark that the Dandi March was the 'kindergarten stage of revolution' and Ellen Wilkinson's remark that Gandhi was 'the best policeman the British had in India'. Treat them as contemporary opinions, not as historians' conclusions.
- **Class lens and representation**: NCERT Class X shows classes joining for different aims, so no single 'national' motive; with Muslim and Dalit distance (NCERT Class X, Spectrum) it challenges the Congress claim to represent all of India.

Cite only the interpretations the extracts support; do not invent scholarly titles.

## 2026 Mains Q3 (10 marks): the masses mobilised by Gandhian movements

## Traps

Beyond the examiner traps in the exam notes: the 1922 sedition trial against the 1930 defiance, and the 1930 Working Committee ban against the 1932 ban on the Congress.


### Notes: Core definitions

- Civil disobedience: open, non-violent breaking of particular laws seen as unjust, accepting the punishment.
- Purna Swaraj: complete independence, adopted as the Congress goal at Lahore in December 1929.
- Dominion status: self-government within the British Empire under the Crown; the Nehru Report and the Irwin declaration used this goal.
- Salt law: the colonial law giving the government a monopoly on the manufacture and sale of salt and levying a tax on it; broken ceremonially at Dandi on 6 April 1930.
- Eleven Demands: Gandhi's list of demands (letter of 31 January 1930, NCERT) in three groups: general, business, peasant.
- Chaukidari tax: a levy for village watchmen; no-chaukidari-tax campaigns were part of civil disobedience in Bihar, Bengal and zamindari areas.
- Sibirams: military-style camps in Andhra that served as headquarters for salt satyagraha.
- Khudai Khidmatgars (Red Shirts): volunteer brigade of Abdul Ghaffar Khan in the North-West Frontier.

### Notes: Chronology 1927 to 1934

- 8 November 1927: Simon Commission appointed. December 1927: Madras Congress (Ansari) decides to boycott it.
- 3 February 1928: commission lands at Bombay. August 1928: Nehru Report finalised.
- October 1928: Lajpat Rai beaten; died 17 November 1928.
- December 1928: Calcutta Congress sets one-year deadline for dominion status.
- 31 October 1929: Irwin declaration. 2 November 1929: Delhi Manifesto.
- December 1929: Lahore Congress (Nehru president), Purna Swaraj, civil disobedience authorised. 31 December 1929: flag hoisted on the Ravi.

- 26 January 1930 (pledge) against 26 January 1950 (Constitution).
- Dandi (6 April 1930, the law broken) against Dharasana (21 May 1930, the raid).
- Salt March (start 12 March) against Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 March 1931, GS-046).

### Core concepts

- The Lahore Congress turned the movement's goal into Purna Swaraj and authorised civil disobedience.
- Gandhi converted an abstract goal into a concrete symbol: salt.
- The Salt March began on 12 March 1930 and ended with breaking the law at Dandi on 6 April.
- Civil disobedience varied by region: salt, forest laws, no-tax and no-chaukidari tax, picketing.
- Different social groups joined for different motives; Muslims, Dalits and labour joined less.
- The Gandhi-Irwin Pact and the Round Table Conferences are taught in GS-046.

### Practice

**Q.** What did the Lahore Congress decide about the Round Table Conference?

**A.** It decided to boycott it.

**Q.** Give the start date, end date and distance of the Salt March.

**A.** 12 March to 6 April 1930; over 240 miles.

**Q.** State the difference between non-cooperation and civil disobedience.

**A.** Non-cooperation withdraws help without breaking laws; civil disobedience deliberately breaks particular laws.

**Q.** Why did Gandhi choose salt rather than a no-rent campaign?

**A.** Salt was a universal need affecting rich and poor, linked swaraj to daily grievance, had no divisive implications between classes, and the monopoly was widely disliked.

**Q.** Why did regional forms of civil disobedience differ?

**A.** Local grievances differed: coastal areas used salt, forest regions broke forest laws, revenue-pressed areas used no-tax and no-chaukidari-tax campaigns, towns used picketing.

**Q.** Why were Muslims and Dalits less involved than other groups?

**A.** Muslim leaders' appeals and government encouragement of communal dissension, alienation after Khilafat, and Dalit leaders seeking separate electorates and reserved seats.

**Q.** A village in Bihar far from the sea wants to join the movement. Which two methods from the lesson could it use?

**A.** A no-chaukidari-tax agitation and picketing of foreign cloth and liquor shops; salt-making at a pond as at Nakhas Pond was symbolic.

**Q.** A pamphlet says the Eleven Demands called for independence. Correct it.

**A.** The eleven demands were concrete reforms in three groups; independence came through the Lahore decision and the later movement.
