## GS-027: Plassey, Buxar and East India Company rise

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words you need for Bengal's story

These words carry this whole topic, so learn them before the story.

A **subahdar** (also called a **nawab** in Bengal) was the governor of a Mughal province, a **subah**. By the 1750s the Bengal nawab was in practice a hereditary ruler who still used the emperor's name. A **farman** is a royal order, here the written trade privileges the emperor gave the Company. A **dastak** is a trade permit or pass; goods carried under a Company dastak were not charged duty on the way. A **puppet** ruler is one who keeps the title but is moved by someone else's strings.

Two words matter most. **Diwani** is the right to collect land revenue; the person who holds it is the **diwan**. **Nizamat** is the other half of government: military defence, police and the administration of justice, which the nawab (the **nazim**) held. In a normal Mughal province these two offices were separate. The dual government of 1765-72 is the story of one foreign company holding the first and controlling the second.

Lastly, a **Resident** was a Company agent posted at an Indian ruler's court. NCERT says that after Buxar the Company began placing Residents in Indian states to protect its interests.

### 1. Orientation: from trading firm to ruler, 1757 to 1772

*Objective:* State in one breath how the Company became master of Bengal and name the five events that mark the change.

## The story in one page

Between 1757 and 1765 the English East India Company changed from a trading firm with a fortified base at Calcutta into the real ruler of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, the richest province of the Mughal empire. Spectrum says power was gradually transferred from the nawabs of Bengal to the British in this short period. Five events carry the story: **Plassey (23 June 1757)**, where Nawab Sirajuddaulah was defeated after a secret conspiracy; the replacement of Mir Jafar by **Mir Qasim (1760)**; the war with Mir Qasim (1763); **Buxar (22 October 1764)**, where the Company beat the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal emperor as well; and the grant of the **Diwani (August 1765)**. From 1765 to 1772 the Company collected the revenue while its nominee carried out police and justice: the **dual government**.

## How this fits the earlier lessons

GS-025 module 8 gave this story as a short outline and GS-024 module 6 introduced the Bengal nawabs; this lesson adds the politics inside Bengal, the conspiracy, the treaty terms, dual government and the debates. The southern wars of GS-026 ran alongside (Wandiwash, 1760, falls between Plassey and Buxar); module 9 shows how Bengal money linked the two.

### 2. Foundation: Bengal and the Company before Plassey

*Objective:* Explain why Bengal mattered to the Company and how the trade privileges (farmans and dastaks) created a lasting quarrel with the nawabs.

## Recap (GS-025 modules 5 and 8)

GS-025 gave the foothold and the Bengal facts; reread those modules rather than relearning them here. In brief: Bengal was the richest Mughal province and supplied about 60 per cent of British imports from Asia. The Company's base grew from the 1651 permission and the 1691 order, each for Rs 3,000 a year in lieu of duties, through the 1698 purchase of three villages and Fort William (1700) to the farmans of 1717, which Spectrum calls its Magna Carta and which let it issue **dastaks**. What is new here is the arithmetic of the bargain and the quarrel it created.

## The unequal bargain

Spectrum says the Company paid Rs 3,000 (about 350 pounds) a year while its exports from Bengal were worth more than 50,000 pounds a year. Dividing 50,000 by 350 gives about 143, so the exports were more than 140 times the payment. This explains why every governor resented the arrangement: it was a huge loss to the provincial treasury.

## Where the quarrel came from

The farman covered the Company's trade, not the private trade of its servants. NCERT says officials who traded privately were expected to pay duty and refused. Spectrum adds that servants sold dastaks to Indian merchants for a commission and used coercion to buy goods cheaply. Simple illustration (invented): if ordinary merchants pay a toll at each checkpost and a dastak holder pays none, the buyer of a dastak saves the toll, the nawab loses it and the honest merchant is undercut.

Therefore the quarrel had three roots: lost revenue, unfair competition, and a Company that kept trying to fortify and expand. The next module shows how these roots led to war.

### 3. Core: the nawabs of Bengal and the causes of the Plassey conflict

*Objective:* Name the rulers of Bengal from Murshid Quli Khan to Sirajuddaulah and sort the causes of the Plassey conflict into economic, political and strategic groups.

## The line of rulers

Spectrum (chapter 5) says Murshid Quli Khan became Dewan of Bengal in 1700 and ruled till his death in 1727. He was succeeded by his son-in-law Shuja-ud-din (ruled till 1739). Then Sarfaraz Khan ruled for a year and was killed by **Alivardi Khan**, the deputy governor of Bihar, who ruled until April 1756. (GS-024 module 6 gave the same line.) Alivardi fought off the Marathas and allowed the English to dig a ditch around Fort William during the raids. NCERT adds that Murshid Quli Khan, Alivardi Khan and Sirajuddaulah were all strong rulers who refused concessions, demanded large tribute, denied the Company the right to mint coins and stopped its fortification.

## A young nawab with enemies

Alivardi was succeeded by his grandson **Sirajuddaulah**, in his twentieth year. Spectrum lists his troubles: a rival cousin, Shaukat Jang, Nawab of Purnea; a hostile aunt, Ghasiti Begum; a commander, Mir Jafar, who had been dismissed; and a court group including Jagat Seth, Omichand, Rai Ballabh and Rai Durlabh who were opposed to him. He defeated and killed Shaukat Jang, took Ghasiti Begum's treasure, dismissed Mir Jafar and appointed Mir Madan in his place, and made Mohan Lal the chief administrator. Spectrum calls him impulsive and inexperienced; his acts proved counter-productive.

## The Company's side: causes of the conflict

Sort the causes into three groups.

1. **Economic.** The Company's officials made rampant misuse of trade privileges (the dastak abuse of module 2), which hurt the nawab's finances.
2. **Political.** The English fortified Calcutta without the nawab's permission. The Company gave asylum to Krishna Das, son of Raj Ballabh (the Rai Ballabh named above; Spectrum spells him both ways; verify), who had fled with great treasure against the nawab's will. NCERT says it also tried, without success, to help one of Siraj's rivals to the throne, since the Company wanted a puppet who would grant concessions. Siraj asked it to stop meddling, stop fortification and pay the revenues.
3. **Strategic.** The Company suspected that Siraj would cut its privileges in collusion with the French.

## The clash

After negotiations failed, the nawab marched on the English factory at Kasimbazar (NCERT says with 30,000 soldiers), disarmed the English and blockaded their ships, then took the fort at Calcutta. Standard accounts date Calcutta's fall to June 1756 (verify).

## The Black Hole

Spectrum records the 'Black Hole Tragedy': Siraj is believed to have imprisoned 146 English persons in a tiny room, where 123 died of suffocation. It adds that historians either do not believe the story or say the number of victims was much smaller. Teach it as a **disputed incident** and never quote 123 as settled fact.

## How to weigh the causes

Spectrum calls friction over trade privileges the chief cause of the conflict; the fortification, the asylum and the French suspicion were the sparks. Start an answer with revenue and privilege, then add politics.

### 4. Core: the conspiracy and the battle of Plassey, 23 June 1757

*Objective:* Narrate how Clive arranged Siraj's defeat through a conspiracy and explain why Spectrum says the result was decided before the battle.

## Clive arrives

When news of Calcutta's fall reached Madras, Company officials sent a force under **Robert Clive**, reinforced by naval fleets (NCERT). Standard accounts say Clive retook Calcutta in early 1757 and then seized the French settlement of Chandernagore (not in the extracts; verify), which fits Spectrum's remark that the French were ousted from Bengal.

## The conspiracy

Spectrum says Clive forged a secret alliance with the traitors of the nawab: **Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, Jagat Seth** (an influential banker) and **Omichand**. Under the deal Mir Jafar was to be made nawab and would reward the Company for its services. These people were already part of the court group that opposed Siraj (module 3). A popular retelling says Omichand was tricked with a false treaty; this detail is outside the extracts and should be treated as a tradition to verify, not an examination fact.

## The battle

On **23 June 1757** Clive led the Company's army against Sirajuddaulah at **Plassey**, from Palashi, the place named after the palash tree (NCERT's footnote). Spectrum says the nawab's force of 50,000 was defeated by a handful of Clive's troops, and that this was due to the conspiracy of the nawab's officials. NCERT agrees: one of the main reasons for the defeat was that the forces led by Mir Jafar never fought the battle, because Clive had promised to make him nawab. The extracts give no figure for the Company's side; do not quote one.

## After the defeat

Siraj was captured and murdered by the order of Mir Jafar's son **Miran** (Spectrum). Mir Jafar became nawab. NCERT notes that Plassey became famous as the first major victory the Company won in India. Since Arcot (1751) came earlier (GS-026), write 'first major victory in Bengal'.

## Why Spectrum says the victory was decided beforehand

Spectrum's wording is that the English victory was 'decided before the battle was even fought'. This does not mean there was no fighting; it means that the decisive move was the secret deal with the commander and the bankers, which removed part of the nawab's army and his financial base. If you describe Plassey in an answer, give the conspiracy first and the battle second.

## Cautions

- Plassey is a place; the battle date is 23 June 1757.
- NCERT's number (30,000 men) is the nawab's march to Kasimbazar, not Plassey; Spectrum's 50,000 is for Plassey.
- Do not say Mir Jafar led the Company's army. He commanded forces on the nawab's side.

### 5. Core: after Plassey, Mir Jafar and the transfer of Bengal's wealth

*Objective:* State the immediate gains of the Company after Plassey and explain how payments, gifts and trade turned Bengal into a source of Company strength.

## The gains listed in Spectrum

As a result of Plassey, Mir Jafar became nawab and gave large sums of money plus the **zamindari of the 24 parganas** to the English. Spectrum calls it the starting point of British rule: it established military supremacy in Bengal, ousted the French, gave territory to maintain a properly equipped force and raised the Company's prestige. The sovereignty of the English over Calcutta was recognised and a **Resident** was posted at the nawab's court. After Plassey the English virtually monopolised the trade and commerce of Bengal.

Notice the phrase 'no apparent change in the form of government'. The nawab remained; the supreme control passed to Clive, on whose support the new nawab depended. This is the **puppet nawab** idea: power without the burden of administration.

## Gifts, payments and fortunes

NCERT says the nawabs after Plassey were forced to give land and vast sums as personal gifts to Company officials. Clive, who sailed to Madras in 1743 at 18 (NCERT; arrival in 1744, verify), left India in 1767 with a fortune put at 401,102 pounds. NCERT also records a letter of Clive to William Pitt (7 January 1759) saying that an income of two million pounds a year with three provinces was worth the attention of the state. NCERT, describing the restored Mir Jafar, says the nawab had to pay Rs 500,000 every month, but the Company wanted more to finance wars and trade. Officials who returned wealthy were mocked in Britain as **nabobs** (a spelling of nawab).

## From gold to Bengal revenue

NCERT explains that the Company used to buy most goods in India with gold and silver imported from Britain, because Britain had no goods to sell in India. After Plassey the outflow of gold from Britain slowed, and after the Diwani it stopped entirely. This links Plassey to what later writers call the **drain of wealth**, taught in GS-030 (to come on Day 15, not yet written). For now hold the facts: gifts, monthly payments, trade monopoly, then revenue.

## Why Mir Jafar was replaced

Spectrum says Mir Jafar was increasingly irritated by Clive's interference. He is said to have conspired with the Dutch at Chinsura, but the Dutch were defeated by the English at **Bedara in November 1759** (Spectrum chapter 3 calls the same event the Battle of Hooghly). The Company was also annoyed by his failure to pay. After his son Miran died, a contest began between Mir Jafar's son-in-law **Mir Qasim** and Miran's son. The Company's new governor, Vansittart, backed Mir Qasim.

## The lesson

NCERT says the Company's prime objective was trade through puppet nawabs. The next two modules show why that failed: the puppets resisted and the Company kept asking for more.

### 6. Core: Mir Qasim, the treaty of 1760 and the war of 1763

*Objective:* Explain what the 1760 treaty gave the Company, why Mir Qasim became its enemy, and how the dastak dispute led to war.

## The treaty of 1760

Vansittart agreed to support Mir Qasim's claim after a treaty with the Company in 1760. Spectrum lists its features.

- Mir Qasim ceded the districts of **Burdwan, Midnapur and Chittagong**.
- The Company got half the share in the chunam (lime) trade of Sylhet.
- He agreed to pay off the outstanding dues and to give five lakh rupees to finance the Company's war in south India. This is the Bengal-to-Carnatic money link: the Company's Third Carnatic War in the south (GS-026 module 4) was being partly paid for out of Bengal.
- His enemies were the Company's enemies and his friends the Company's friends.
- Tenants of one territory were not to settle on the other's land.

Mir Jafar resigned under pressure; Spectrum prints a pension of Rs 1,500 per annum, which looks like a slip (verify).

## An able nawab

Spectrum calls Mir Qasim the ablest of Alivardi's successors. He moved his capital from Murshidabad to **Munger** in Bihar to be at a safe distance from the Company at Calcutta, reorganised the bureaucracy with men of his own choice and remodelled the army to raise its skill. The Company had thought he would be an ideal puppet and was disappointed.

## The two quarrels

**Revenue accounts.** Ram Narayan, the deputy governor of Bihar, ignored repeated requests to submit the revenue accounts. Mir Qasim could not tolerate this open defiance, but Ram Narayan was supported by the English officials of Patna.

**Dastaks.** Servants of the Company claimed the Company's duty-free privilege for their private trade, sold dastaks to Indian merchants for a commission, and used coercion to buy cheap. Spectrum says the misuse cost the nawab revenue and made local merchants face unequal competition. Mir Qasim decided to abolish duties altogether. This removed the artificial advantage of the dastak (if nobody pays duty, nobody gains by dodging it). The English protested and insisted on preferential treatment against other traders. A trade dispute became a quarrel over whether the Company stood above the nawab's law.

## War, 1763

The quarrel over transit duty led to war in 1763. Spectrum names English victories at **Katwah, Murshidabad, Giria, Sooty and Munger**. Mir Jafar was made nawab again in 1763 (Spectrum). Standard accounts add that Company prisoners were killed at Patna in 1763 (the 'Patna massacre'; not in the extracts; verify). Mir Qasim fled to Awadh and formed a confederacy with the Nawab of Awadh, Shuja-ud-Daula, and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II to recover Bengal.

## After Mir Jafar's death

Mir Jafar died in 1765 (NCERT). His minor son Najim-ud-daula was made nawab, but real power lay with the naib-subahdar, whom the English could appoint or dismiss (Spectrum).

## Source caution

NCERT compresses events: it says Mir Qasim was defeated at Buxar and that Mir Jafar was reinstalled. Spectrum places the reinstatement in 1763, before Buxar. Teach the Spectrum order.

### 7. Core: Buxar (22 October 1764) and the Treaty of Allahabad (1765)

*Objective:* Narrate Buxar, state its significance, and list the terms of the Allahabad treaties and why Clive chose them.

## Buxar

The combined armies of **Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula (Awadh) and the emperor Shah Alam II** were defeated by the English forces under **Major Hector Munro** at Buxar on **22 October 1764**, in a closely contested battle, says Spectrum. Its importance lay in the fact that not only a nawab of Bengal but the Mughal emperor of India was beaten. The victory made the English a great power in northern India and contenders for supremacy over the whole country. Spectrum quotes G.B. Malleson that Buxar ranks among the most decisive battles: it saved Bengal, advanced the British frontier to Allahabad and bound the rulers of Awadh to the conqueror.

**Slip to remember.** Spectrum's summary box says Buxar was Clive's victory. Its own text, and standard accounts, give the command to Hector Munro. Clive was governor of Bengal in 1757-60 and again in 1765-67 (Spectrum), and so was not governing in 1764. NCERT's 'appointed Governor of Bengal in 1764' is not a conflict: he was appointed in London in 1764 and took charge in Calcutta in May 1765 (verify), which fits Spectrum's 1765-67.

## The two treaties

In August 1765 Robert Clive concluded two treaties at Allahabad.

| Party | Terms (Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| Nawab of Awadh | Surrender Allahabad and Kara to the emperor; pay Rs 50 lakh war indemnity; restore Balwant Singh, zamindar of Banaras, to full possession of his estate |
| Emperor Shah Alam II | Reside at Allahabad (ceded by Awadh) under Company protection; issue a farman giving the **diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** to the Company for Rs 26 lakh a year; a provision of Rs 53 lakh for the nizamat functions of those provinces |

Standard accounts describe the Rs 53 lakh as paid by the Company to the Nawab of Bengal for the expenses of the nizamat (verify). Spectrum's wording reads as if it went to the Company, which is the likely slip. Treat the flow as uncertain.

## Why Clive acted this way

Clive did not annex Awadh because it would have obliged the Company to defend a long land frontier against Afghan and Maratha invasions. Instead the treaty made the Nawab a firm friend and turned Awadh into a **buffer state**. His arrangement with the emperor was practical too: the emperor became a useful 'rubber stamp', and the farman legalised the Company's political gains in Bengal.

## Mir Qasim's end

Spectrum says Mir Qasim spent the rest of his life as a homeless wanderer and died in June 1777.

## Another cause of confusion

Spectrum says that after Buxar Mir Jafar 'agreed to hand over' Midnapore, Burdwan and Chittagong and allowed duty-free trade except a 2 per cent duty on salt. The districts were already ceded in 1760 (module 6), so the sentence compresses several arrangements. Learn the districts under 1760 and the salt duty as a feature of the restoration of 1763 (verify).

### 8. Core: the Diwani and dual government, 1765 to 1772

*Objective:* Explain how dual government worked, why it suited the Company, what it did to Bengal, and how Clive and Hastings fit in.

## The mechanism

After Buxar the Company became the real master of Bengal. Spectrum says Clive introduced the **dual system**, the rule of two, the Company and the nawab. Both **diwani** (collecting revenue) and **nizamat** (police and justice) came under the Company's control. The Company exercised diwani rights as the diwan, having received them from the emperor. It exercised the nizamat rights through its right to nominate the deputy subahdar, a power obtained from the nawab. For diwani work it appointed two deputy diwans: **Mohammad Reza Khan for Bengal** and **Raja Sitab Roy for Bihar**. Reza Khan also acted as deputy nazim.

| Function | Legal holder | Who really controlled it |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (diwani) | Company, by the emperor's farman | Company, through deputy diwans |
| Police and justice (nizamat) | Nawab | Company, through its nominee as deputy subahdar |
| Army and money | Nawab responsible for order | Company controlled army and revenue |

## Why it suited the Company

Spectrum says the system left the appearance of authority to a puppet while keeping sovereign power with the Company. The nawab had to maintain peace and order but depended on the Company for funds and forces, since it held both army and revenue. In short, the Company had **power without responsibility**.

NCERT explains the financial gain. Revenues from India now financed Company expenses: they bought cotton and silk textiles, kept troops and paid for building the fort and offices at Calcutta. The earlier outflow of gold from Britain stopped (module 5). Clive's own statement, in NCERT, was that the Company must itself become nawabs once puppet nawabs proved unhelpful.

## Effects on Bengal

Spectrum says the dual system led to an administrative breakdown and proved disastrous for the people: neither the Company nor the nawab cared for administration or public welfare. The extracts give no details; standard books link a famine of 1769-70 to this period, with death estimates differing widely (verify before quoting any number). Reason it out: where no one is answerable, neglect follows.

## Hastings and the end

Spectrum says Warren Hastings did away with the dual system in 1772. NCERT adds that Hastings (Governor-General from 1773 to 1785, the first Governor-General of Bengal (Fort William) under the Regulating Act of 1773; verify) introduced administrative reforms, notably in justice. From 1772 each district had a criminal court (faujdari adalat) and a civil court (diwani adalat), with collectors supervising; the Regulating Act of 1773 set up a Supreme Court at Calcutta. The land revenue systems that followed are in GS-029 (to come on Day 14, not yet written).

## A note on Clive

Clive was Governor of Bengal in 1757-60 and 1765-67 (Spectrum). NCERT says he was questioned by Parliament in 1772, was acquitted and died in 1774 (Spectrum: allegedly by suicide).

### 9. Master: why the Company won in Bengal, and the historians' debate

*Objective:* Build a Bengal-specific answer to 'why did the Company win' and state the main debates about Plassey and about design versus accident.

## Seven reasons from the Bengal evidence

GS-025 module 9 and GS-026 module 9 give general lists of causes. Do not repeat them; test them on Bengal.

1. **Conspiracy.** At Plassey the secret deal with Mir Jafar and the bankers (module 4) replaced a conventional battle.
2. **A base and a force from outside.** Calcutta was held, and Madras sent troops and ships (NCERT). The nawab had no equal link to outside power.
3. **The nawab's isolation.** Siraj was young and made enemies (module 3).
4. **Bengal's money.** After Plassey Spectrum says vast resources of Bengal were at the Company's disposal, and Mir Qasim's treaty promised money for the southern war; after 1765 revenue financed the army (NCERT). The Bengal revenue is the missing link between Plassey and Wandiwash in the south.
5. **The sepoy army.** NCERT says Awadh and Banaras began recruiting peasants as trained professional soldiers, and the Company adopted the same method for its sepoy army (from sipahi, soldier). This partly fills the gap noted in GS-026 module 9 about how the Company's army was organised, though the extracts say nothing about the Company's drill or pay in Bengal.
6. **Coalitions that did not hold.** At Buxar the three Indian parties fought a closely contested battle yet lost; Spectrum says Indian rulers were often fighting one another as much as the British.
7. **Puppet rule.** Nawabs kept the form of rule, sparing the Company the cost of direct administration until it could afford it.

## Debate 1: accident or design?

Seeley said conquest was made in a fit of absent-mindedness; the opposing view says it was planned. Spectrum finds both overstated; Judith Brown says interests varied in each case. For Bengal, NCERT says the Company was reluctant to take territory and wanted trade through friendly nawabs; its own failure with puppet nawabs led to 'we must indeed become nawabs ourselves'. A safe answer: reactive at first, intentional later.

## Debate 2: what was Plassey?

Spectrum's view is that Plassey was a conspiracy victory that placed Bengal's resources at the Company's disposal and 'laid the foundation' of empire. Another line is that Plassey was less a battle than a political change engineered with the help of Bengal's own financiers and officials. This modern view is not in the extracts (verify). Spectrum also notes that 1740, 1757 and 1761 have each been chosen as the start of the 'British period', but every marker is arbitrary because the change took about eighty years.

## Debate 3: Plassey or Buxar?

| Plassey | Buxar |
|---|---|
| Won by plot; only one nawab beaten | Won by fighting; nawab, Awadh and emperor beaten |
| Placed Bengal's resources at the Company's disposal | Led directly to the Diwani |
| 'Territorial foundation' (Spectrum) | 'Real foundation' (Spectrum) |

Both are right in different senses: Plassey gave political control; Buxar proved military superiority and led to the legal title.

## 2018 Prelims Q68: staple exports from Bengal

The question asks which commodities the Company exported from Bengal in the mid-18th century, with options such as raw cotton, oil-seeds and opium; sugar, salt, zinc and lead; copper, silver, gold, spices and tea; and cotton, silk, saltpetre and opium. Reason from the extracts. Spectrum lists Bengal's exports as saltpetre, rice, indigo, pepper, sugar, silk, cotton textiles and handicrafts (GS-025 module 8), and says the Dutch carried textiles and silk, saltpetre from Bihar, and opium and rice from the Ganga valley. Spectrum's list of Indian imports (chapter 4) has copper, iron and lead from Europe, tea from China and gold from Tibet. Opium has direct support: Spectrum's chapter 4 list of India's exports (PDF p107, repeated on p111) names cotton textiles, raw silk and silk fabrics, indigo, saltpetre, opium, rice and sugar. Flag only that this is a list for India and the extracts do not tie opium specifically to the Company's Bengal trade (verify); see the GS-025 pyqNote, which says the same. The first option names raw cotton and oil-seeds along with opium. Bengal's cotton left mainly as manufactured cloth, and raw-cotton and oil-seed exports grew in later periods (standard; verify), so the deciding contrast is raw materials against Bengal's manufactured goods plus silk and saltpetre; option (a) fails on that ground even though it contains opium. The options naming copper, gold, tea or lead describe imports, which leaves the fourth option, with silk, cotton, saltpetre and opium, as the best fit (analysis, not an official key).

## 2020 Prelims Q21: aurang, banian, mirasidar

None of these words is in the extracts. Standard knowledge, to verify: a **banian** was an Indian agent or broker of the Company; an **aurang** was a warehouse or manufacturing place, not a treasury; a **mirasidar** held hereditary rights in land in the Tamil country and was a revenue payer. If these are right, pair 1 is wrong and pairs 2 and 3 correct. Pair 2 is the easiest; options that omit it can be eliminated.

## 2022 Mains Q2: a different angle from GS-026

GS-026 module 10 analysed the military causes against Haidar and Tipu. Here use Bengal. The question says the Company's armies, mostly Indian soldiers, won 'consistently' against larger, better equipped armies. ### Notes: Definitions

- Farman: a royal order; Aurangzeb's and Farrukhsiyar's farmans gave the Company duty-free trade in Bengal in return for Rs 3,000 a year.
- Dastak: a trade permit or pass exempting specified goods from duty; its misuse by Company servants cost the nawabs revenue.
- Diwani: the right to collect land revenue; nizamat: military defence, police and the administration of justice.
- Dual government: Company holds diwani and, through its nominee as deputy subahdar, nizamat, 1765-72.
- Nabob: a returned rich Company servant, from the word nawab; used in Britain with ridicule.
- Puppet nawab: a ruler who keeps the title but is controlled by the Company.

### Notes: Causes of the Plassey conflict

- Dastak misuse and private trade led to heavy loss of revenue for the nawab.
- Fortifying Calcutta without permission led Siraj to see the Company as a rival power.
- Asylum to Krishna Das, with his treasure, led to open hostility.
- Company's effort to back a rival and its suspicion of the French led to Siraj's march on Kasimbazar and Calcutta.
- Siraj's weakness and the court group's opposition led to the conspiracy of 1757 and Plassey.

### Notes: How dual government worked

- Step 1: the emperor grants the diwani; the Company becomes diwan.
- Step 2: the Company nominates the deputy subahdar, so police and justice come under its control.
- Step 3: the Company uses deputy diwans (Reza Khan for Bengal, Sitab Roy for Bihar) to collect revenue.
- Step 4: the nawab is responsible for peace and order but has neither army nor revenue.
- Result: power without responsibility for the Company; administrative breakdown for Bengal; Hastings ends it in 1772.

### Core concepts

- Plassey was won by conspiracy; Buxar by battle; the Diwani turned both into legal and financial power.
- Dastak misuse was the economic root of the quarrel with the nawabs.
- Dual government gave the Company revenue and control without responsibility.
- The Company first wanted puppet nawabs and became rulers when puppets failed.
