## GS-099: Weathering, erosion and mass wasting

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Rock and slope words: joint, bedding plane, gradient, regolith

Four words come up in every page of this lesson.

**Joint.** A crack in a rock along which there has been little or no movement. Granite, for example, is usually cut by many joints. **Bedding plane.** In a layered (sedimentary) rock, the flat surface between one layer and the next. Joints and bedding planes are paths: water, roots and ice enter the rock along them, so a rock with many cracks weathers faster than a massive block with few (Leong p57-58).

**Gradient or slope.** How steeply the ground drops. A slope is often given as an angle in degrees: 0 degrees is flat ground and 90 degrees is a vertical cliff. Leong says rock falls happen on bare slopes steeper than 40 degrees, and a heap of fallen fragments settles at about 35 degrees (p63). These are the book's figures; the idea to keep is that the steeper the slope, the stronger gravity's pull along the slope.

**Regolith.** The loose, broken-up cover of weathered material that sits on solid rock. Soil is the top part of it with living matter mixed in. Regolith is what mass wasting moves.

A quick picture to carry into module 1 (a hillside, side view):

`fresh rock (joints)  ->  regolith (loose)  ->  soil (top)`

Weathering makes the regolith; gravity and moving water then take it downhill.

### Bridge: Reading a chemical equation, and a rate that multiplies with heat

Chemical weathering is written as equations, so learn to read one.

An equation such as `CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 -> Ca(HCO3)2` reads: one unit of calcium carbonate (limestone) plus water plus carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate. The arrow means 'becomes'. The numbers below the letters count atoms: CO2 has one carbon and two oxygens. A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides. Check this one: calcium 1 and 1; carbon 1+1 = 2 and 2; hydrogen 2 and 2; oxygen 3+1+2 = 6 and 6. It balances. Calcium carbonate does not dissolve well in pure water, but calcium bicarbonate does, which is why limestone can be carried away in solution (Leong p58).

A second idea is a rate that multiplies. Leong quotes van 't Hoff's rule: the rate of a chemical reaction rises 2 to 3 times for every 10 degrees C of extra temperature (p57-58). 'Times' compounds. If the temperature is 20 degrees C higher, there are two steps of 10 degrees, so the rate is multiplied by 2 x 2 = 4 at the low end and 3 x 3 = 9 at the high end. At 30 degrees C higher: 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 to 3 x 3 x 3 = 27. This is an illustration of the rule, not a measurement of any climate. Leong separately says the chemical weathering rate in the tropics is about four times that of temperate regions; do not mix the two statements.

Keep this in mind: heat speeds chemical weathering, and water must be present.

### 1. Orientation: how the land is worn down, in four words

*Objective:* Say in one minute what weathering, mass wasting, erosion and deposition each mean, and which of them involves movement.

## Two sets of forces

The surface of the earth is uneven because two sets of forces keep working against each other (NCERT kegy205 p2). **Endogenic** forces come from inside the earth and mostly build land up (mountain building, volcanoes: Day 19 lesson GS-097 and Day 20 lesson GS-098). **Exogenic** forces come from outside, from the sun's energy acting through the atmosphere, plus gravity, and they mostly wear land down. NCERT's two key words:

- **Degradation**: wearing down of high ground.
- **Aggradation**: filling up of basins and low ground.

The wearing down of relief differences through erosion is called **gradation**. Because the inner forces keep lifting land, the outer forces never finish levelling it, so hills, plains and valleys stay (p2).

## The four exogenic processes

NCERT p3 names four: weathering, mass wasting, erosion and deposition. Learn them with one question each: does the material move, and what moves it?

| Process | What happens | Does material travel? | What does the work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weathering | Rock breaks up or rots where it stands | Almost not (in-situ, on-site) | Elements of weather and climate |
| Mass wasting (mass movement) | Loose debris slides, flows or falls downslope | Yes, down the slope | Gravity alone |
| Erosion | Debris is picked up and carried away | Yes, far | Agents: running water, groundwater, glaciers, wind, waves and currents |
| Deposition | Carried debris is dropped | It stops | Loss of speed and energy of the carrying agent |

**Denudation** is the umbrella word: weathering, mass wasting, erosion and transportation together. 'Denude' means to strip off or uncover (NCERT p4).

## Process and agent are different words

A **process** is a force applied to earth material (for example, weathering stress). An **agent** is a mobile medium such as running water, moving ice, wind, waves and currents that removes, transports and deposits earth material (p3). Gravity is special: it is not an agent that carries debris but the force that switches on every downslope movement, so without gradients and gravity there would be no erosion, transportation or deposition (p3).

## A one-line story of a hillside

`weathering loosens  ->  gravity (mass wasting) and agents (erosion) move  ->  deposition fills a low place`

The order is a guide, not a law: NCERT says weathering aids mass wasting and erosion but is not a prerequisite for either (modules 6 and 7).

### 2. Foundation: what controls how fast and how rock weathers

*Objective:* Name the rock factors and the climate factors that control weathering, and state how climate changes the type and depth of weathering.

## Weathering defined

NCERT (p5): weathering is the mechanical disintegration and chemical decomposition of rocks through the actions of elements of weather and climate. Leong (p57) puts it as physical disintegration and chemical alteration. There are three kinds: **chemical**, **physical (mechanical)** and **biological**. They rarely work alone; often one dominates. Leong's example: tree roots physically split a rock, which lets rainwater in to alter its minerals chemically (p57).

The loose material left in place is the **regolith**; its thickness is the depth of the **weathering mantle**.

## Two groups of controls

Leong sorts the controls into two groups (p57).

**Endogenetic factors (inside the rock):**

- **Lithology**, which is the mineral composition. Minerals differ in how easily they decompose. Quartz is the most resistant rock-forming mineral and has about 100 per cent silica; as the silica content of a mineral falls, it becomes more susceptible to chemical change (Leong p57). Leong's Table 4.1 order of rising weatherability: quartz, muscovite, orthoclase, biotite, sodium plagioclase, calcium plagioclase (verify).
- **Structure**: the frequency of faults, joints and microfissures in crystalline rocks, and bedding planes in sedimentary rocks. These give water access. Massive igneous and metamorphic rocks with few fractures resist weathering (p57).

NCERT adds that a particular rock may resist one process and give way to another; and the same rock may behave differently under different climates (p5). NCERT's idea of structure also includes hardness and permeability (p4).

**Exogenetic factors (outside the rock):** climate (precipitation and temperature) and vegetation (Leong p57).

## Climate is the master control

NCERT p5: weathering processes differ from climate to climate, and so does the depth of the weathering mantle. Leong p57-58 gives the pattern:

- Chemical weathering needs water, so it is strongest in humid climates and negligible in arid regions where water is lacking.
- Heat speeds reactions: Leong gives van 't Hoff's rule of a 2 to 3 times rise per 10 degrees C (bridge GS-099-B2) and says the chemical weathering rate in the tropics is about four times that of temperate regions.
- Within the tropics, forests weather faster than savanna, because of higher rainfall and more plant litter and organic acids.
- Cold, waterlogged places can still have fast chemical change from organic acids.
- Temperature range matters most for some physical weathering, particularly in arid and temperate regions.

## Resistance is relative

`same rock + humid tropics  -> deep, chemically rotted mantle`
`same rock + cold or arid   -> shattered rock, thin mantle`

The effects of exogenic processes are small and slow, but repeated stress causes fatigue and in the long run rocks are severely affected (NCERT p5).

Link back: the resistance of rocks and the porosity-permeability pair were introduced in Day 18 lesson GS-096 (section 'Master: porosity, resistance, India's rocks and mineral belts').

### 3. Core: chemical weathering - solution, carbonation, hydration, hydrolysis, oxidation, reduction, chelation

*Objective:* Name each chemical weathering process, write the main equation or reaction in words, and say what it leaves behind.

## What chemical weathering is

Chemical weathering changes the minerals of a rock into other substances. NCERT (p5) lists the group as **solution, carbonation, hydration, oxidation and reduction**, acting through oxygen, surface and soil water and acids; Leong (pp58-59) writes about solution and carbonation, hydration and hydrolysis, oxidation and chelation. Water, air (oxygen and carbon dioxide) and heat must be present to speed the reactions. Decay of plants and animals adds carbon dioxide underground (NCERT p5).

## The processes one by one

1. **Solution.** Water dissolves soluble minerals and carries them off. It is the most potent process in limestone regions (Leong p58).
2. **Carbonation.** Rain water with dissolved carbon dioxide forms a weak **carbonic acid**. This attacks limestone and turns calcium carbonate into calcium bicarbonate, which is removed in solution: `CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 -> Ca(HCO3)2` (balanced: bridge GS-099-B2).
3. **Hydration.** Some minerals take up water, which sets up physical stress and eventually breaks the rock apart. Leong's examples are iron oxide becoming a hydrated iron hydroxide (limonite) and anhydrite becoming gypsum. Book equations need care: see Master Notes.
4. **Hydrolysis.** A reaction between mineral and water, with hydrogen the active part. Hydrogen ions from water push out metal ions such as sodium and calcium from silicate minerals, and the rock turns into hydroxides and **clay minerals** (notably kaolinite). In granite the plagioclase feldspar decomposes first, then orthoclase; the rock first becomes decomposed granite, called **gruss**, then silty sand. Fuller feldspar breakdown frees potassium hydroxide, which carbonic acid turns into soluble potassium carbonate that is carried away (Leong pp58-59).
5. **Oxidation.** Oxygen from air or water reacts with minerals. Reduced iron (ferrous) turns to oxidised (ferric) iron: `4FeO + O2 -> 2Fe2O3`. Oxidised iron is stable and settles out, which loosens the rock; it explains the build-up of oxidised iron and the formation of **laterite** in the tropics (Leong p59).
6. **Reduction.** NCERT names it but the extract gives no detail. The usual account is the opposite of oxidation, in oxygen-poor, waterlogged ground (standard knowledge; verify).
7. **Chelation.** Organic acids from decaying plant litter dissolve iron and other elements; plants take up the products, which keeps the reaction going. Mosses and lichens on bare rock take food from it and make acids, so they are agents of both chemical and physical weathering (Leong p59).

## What is left behind

Leong (p59) groups the products into **residual material** (a mix of rock and mineral bits; complete decay gives sand, silt and clay) and **colloids** (clay minerals and hydrated oxides of aluminium and iron). The hydrates form in quantity only under seasonal tropical climates, and iron-rich altered rock builds laterite. The final product depends on the rock: acid rocks with high silica, such as granite, give coarse products; basic rocks such as basalt, with lower silica, give fine clay and soluble substances (Table 4.2; full table in the Master Notes).

### 4. Core: physical weathering - unloading, thermal stress, frost, salt and wetting-drying

*Objective:* Name the main physical weathering processes, the force behind each and the climate where each works best.

## What physical weathering is

Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals. NCERT p5-6 says it depends on applied forces of three kinds: (i) **gravitational** forces such as overburden pressure, load and shearing stress; (ii) **expansion** forces due to temperature changes, crystal growth or animal activity; (iii) **water pressures** controlled by wetting and drying cycles. Most physical weathering is caused by thermal expansion and pressure release.

## The main processes

1. **Unloading and exfoliation.** Rocks formed deep in the crust are squeezed by the weight above. When erosion exposes them, the pressure is released, the rock expands a little and cracks into thin sheets roughly parallel to the surface, like onion peels. This is **sheeting**, and it makes rounded **exfoliation domes** (Leong p60). Pressure release is the cause of the domes; NCERT (p6) names thermal expansion as the cause of **tors**.
2. **Thermal expansion and contraction.** Rock heats by day and cools by night, or by season; different minerals expand by different amounts, so the rock cracks and flakes. This matters in arid and temperate regions where the temperature range is large (Leong p58, NCERT p6).
3. **Frost action (freeze-thaw, frost wedging).** Water gets into cracks, freezes and expands, and prises the rock apart; it thaws and the cycle repeats. This is the most important physical process in cold climates. It breaks rock into angular fragments, and a pile of frost-shattered fragments is **talus** or **scree** (Leong p61).
4. **Salt crystal growth (salt wedging).** In dry regions evaporation leaves salt crystals in cracks; as they grow they widen the cracks and wedge out sand grains. It works on coarse-grained rocks such as granite and sandstone and can widen cracks into caves on sandstone cliffs (Leong p61).
5. **Wetting and drying.** Repeated wetting and drying makes outer layers swell and shrink and peel off (NCERT p5 'water pressures'; Leong Fig. 4.3 and Table 4.3 on swelling and shrinking).
6. **Plants, animals and people.** Roots wedge into cracks; burrowers break surfaces; people with bulldozers, dynamite, mining, road building and farming break and expose rock (Leong pp61-62).

## How the rock breaks (Leong p61)

- **Granular disintegration**: grain by grain, giving sand; common in coarse-grained rocks.
- **Block separation**: into near-regular blocks along joints; common in jointed rocks such as sandstone and granite.
- **Shattering**: into angular fragments along cracks; typical of massive rocks and of frost action.
- **Exfoliation**: peeling in thin curved layers.

## Exfoliation: process or result?

NCERT (p6) says plainly that exfoliation is a **result, not a process**: it follows from unloading, thermal contraction and expansion, and salt weathering, and it leaves smooth rounded surfaces. Leong calls the whole weathering by unloading exfoliation. For Prelims and Mains use NCERT's wording, and write 'exfoliation domes form by unloading'.

### 5. Core: biological weathering, the weathering profile and why weathering matters

*Objective:* Describe biological weathering, the layers of a weathering profile and four reasons weathering is significant.

## Biological weathering

NCERT (p6) defines **biological weathering** as the contribution to or removal of minerals and ions from the weathering environment, plus physical change due to growth or movement of organisms. It has a physical side and a chemical side.

- **Physical side.** Burrowing and wedging by earthworms, termites, rodents and similar animals expose new surfaces to chemical attack and let air and moisture in. Plant roots exert a tremendous pressure and break rock apart mechanically. Humans disturb vegetation, plough and cultivate, which mixes air, water and minerals and makes new contacts.
- **Chemical side.** Decaying plant and animal matter yields humic, carbonic and other acids that speed decay and the solubility of some elements. Leong adds that mosses and lichens on bare rock take food from it and release organic acids (p59).

## The weathering profile

The weathered material that is not carried off in solution piles up as **regolith**. Leong's Fig. 4.6 shows a profile on low, gently sloping ground, listed from the top down:

1. soil (weathered regolith with organic matter);
2. laterite (may be absent), and structureless regolith where decomposition is complete;
3. regolith that still keeps the original rock structure;
4. regolith with rounded **corestones**;
5. regolith with angular corestones;
6. unweathered fresh rock with joints.

Corestones are blocks that have not yet rotted, left behind where joints are wide apart; they are common in well-jointed granites and granodiorite and less common in gneiss, which has foliation and banding instead of clear joints (pp60-61). Sedimentary rocks seldom show clear zones. The depth of the profile can be judged from colour (mottled and pallid zones). In areas of steep slope, mass movement removes the regolith and some zones disappear (p60).

`soil -> laterite/regolith -> corestone zone -> fresh rock   (top to bottom)`

## Why weathering matters (NCERT pp6, 9)

1. **It prepares material.** Weathering breaks rock into fragments and prepares the way for regolith, soils, erosion and mass movements. Erosion cannot be significant if the rock is not weathered; weathering therefore aids mass wasting, erosion and the lowering of relief.
2. **Soil.** Soil formation depends first on the weathering mantle; the weathered material is the basic input (NCERT p9; soils are a later lesson).
3. **Vegetation and biodiversity.** Forests depend on the depth of weathering mantles, so biomes and biodiversity are linked to it.
4. **Ore enrichment.** During weathering, some materials are removed by physical or chemical leaching through groundwater, so the remaining valuable materials become more concentrated. Without this the concentration of iron, manganese, aluminium, copper and similar ores might be too small to be economic. This concentration is called enrichment.

### 6. Core: erosion, transportation and deposition - agents and the rules they follow

*Objective:* Define erosion and deposition, list the five agents, say which are controlled by climate, and state the rule for what is dropped first.

## Erosion

NCERT (p8): **erosion** involves the acquisition and transportation of rock debris. Once rock has broken into smaller fragments by weathering or any other process, erosional agents remove it and carry it to other places, each in its own way. **Abrasion**, the scraping action of the rock debris carried by the agent, helps greatly. By erosion the relief is degraded, that is, the landscape is worn down. Weathering aids erosion but is **not a pre-condition** for it. Weathering, mass wasting and erosion are all called **degradational** processes, and erosion is largely responsible for the continuous changes of the earth's surface. NCERT's chapter figure says erosion and transportation are controlled by **kinetic energy** (the energy of motion).

## The five agents and what controls them

| Agent | Controlled by climate? | Note (NCERT p8) |
|---|---|---|
| Wind | Yes | State of matter: gas |
| Running water | Yes | State of matter: liquid |
| Glaciers (moving ice) | Yes | State of matter: solid |
| Waves (currents not stated) | No | Depends on the location along the contact of land and water, that is, the coast |
| Groundwater | No | Depends on the rock: if the rock is permeable and soluble and water is available, karst topography develops |

Landforms made by these agents come in the next chapters of NCERT and in Day 22 lesson GS-100 and Day 23 lesson GS-101.

## Gravity, the hidden driver

NCERT p3: gravity activates all downslope movement and indirectly drives waves, tide-induced currents and winds. Gradients are created by tectonic forces (NCERT p4). Without gravity and gradients there could be no erosion, transportation or deposition.

## Deposition

NCERT p8: **deposition is a consequence of erosion.** When an agent reaches a gentler slope it loses velocity and therefore energy, and the material it carries starts to settle. In NCERT's phrase, deposition is not actually the work of any agent. **Coarser material is deposited first and finer later**, and depressions get filled up (aggradation). The same agents that erode also deposit.

`fast steep flow: picks up and carries  ->  slope eases, speed falls: coarse drops first, then finer`

## A diagram to draw

Leong's exercise (p66) asks you to complete a figure: denudation branches into weathering, erosion and mass movement. Draw this tree and write the five agents under erosion.

## Putting weathering, mass wasting and erosion side by side

| Question | Weathering | Mass wasting | Erosion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does material move? | Almost none (in-situ) | Yes, downslope | Yes, far |
| What moves it? | Nothing; weather elements act in place | Gravity only | A moving agent |
| Needs weathering first? | Not applicable | No, but aided | No, but aided |
| Is an agent involved? | Not applicable | No | Yes |

### 7. Core: mass wasting - what it is and what starts it

*Objective:* Define mass wasting, explain why it is not erosion, and list the favouring conditions and the nine activating causes.

## Definition

Mass wasting, also called **mass movement**, is the movement of weathered material under the force of gravity. NCERT (p7) says that these movements transfer the mass of rock debris down the slope under the direct influence of gravity. Leong (p63) adds that the movement may be slow and gradual or sudden, depending on the gradient of the slope, the weight of the debris and whether rain supplies lubricating moisture. Rapid movements are often catastrophic.

## Why it is not erosion

NCERT p7 is firm on this and the point is examined.

- Air, water or ice do not carry the debris. On the contrary, the debris may carry air, water or ice with it.
- No geomorphic agent such as running water, glacier, wind, waves or currents takes part. Mass movements are aided by gravity alone, so they do not come under erosion, even though material shifts from one place to another.
- Weathering is **not a pre-requisite** for mass movement, since gravity acts on bedrock as well as on weathered debris. Weathering only aids it: mass movements are very active on weathered slopes rather than unweathered ones.

## The resistance idea

A slope holds as long as the material's resistance is bigger than the pull on it. NCERT p7: material yields only when the force is greater than the **shearing resistance** of the material. Think of a tug of war on every slope.

`pull down the slope (weight)   versus   resistance (friction, cohesion, roots, support from below)`

Gravity along the slope grows as the slope gets steeper (standard mechanics; not in the extracts). Anything that raises the pull or lowers the resistance can tip the balance.

## Conditions that favour mass movement (NCERT p7)

Weak unconsolidated material; thinly bedded rocks; faults; steeply dipping beds; vertical cliffs or steep slopes; abundant precipitation and torrential rain; scarcity of vegetation.

## Nine activating causes (NCERT p7)

1. Removal of support from below, naturally or artificially.
2. Increase in the gradient and height of slopes.
3. Overloading by natural addition or artificial fill.
4. Overloading from heavy rain, saturation and lubrication of slope material.
5. Removal of material or load from the original slope surface.
6. Earthquakes, explosions or machinery.
7. Excessive natural seepage.
8. Heavy drawdown of water from lakes, reservoirs and rivers, which leads to slow outflow of water from under the slopes or river banks.
9. Indiscriminate removal of natural vegetation.

A memory hook, one word per cause 1-9: **Support, Slope, Load-on, Rain-load, Load-off, Shake, Seep, Drawdown, Vegetation**. Water: causes 4, 7 and 8. Human action can feature in 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8 and 9; only 4 and 7 are purely natural.


## Classification

NCERT p7 says the three forms of movement are **heave** (soils heaved up by frost growth and other causes), **flow** and **slide**, ranging from slow to rapid and shallow to deep, including creep, flow, slide and fall. Module 8 uses Leong's four types (falls, slides, flows, creep), with NCERT's landslide vocabulary placed inside them.

### 8. Core: falls, slides, flows, creep and heave - the types of mass movement

*Objective:* Classify a described slope failure as a fall, slide, flow, creep or heave from its speed, water content and setting.

## Reading the four types

Leong (pp63-65) classifies by the type of material and the movement (speed and water content). (Leong states the water need only for flows.)

| Type | Speed (Leong) | Water in the debris | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fall | Extremely rapid; metres per day or per second | Not the main factor | Steep bare rock slopes over 40 degrees |
| Slide | Rapid; centimetres per day | Rain lubricates | Along a bedding plane, joint or fault, or an arc |
| Flow | Rapid to extremely rapid; metres per day or per second | A lot (Leong) | Saturated debris, soil or mud |
| Creep | Extremely slow; centimetres per year | Wet-dry cycles matter | Moderately steep soil-covered slopes, clay-rich |

## Falls

A free movement in which material falls and bounces rather than rolls or slides. Fragments break on impact and form a talus or scree slope of about 35 degrees (Leong's figure). NCERT's **rock fall** is the free fall of rock blocks from any steep slope, from the superficial layers of the rock face; **debris fall** is nearly free fall of earth debris from a vertical or overhanging face (p8).

## Slides

A slide is the rapid sliding of large masses with little flowing at first. Types by material: rock slides, debris slides, mud slides. The sliding plane may be a bedding joint or fault surface. NCERT's **rockslide** is the sliding of individual rock masses down bedding, joint or fault surfaces, planar failure along steeply dipping discontinuities; it is very fast and destructive on steep slopes and affects material to a substantial depth, unlike a rock fall that takes only the surface layers (p8). **Debris slide** is rapid rolling or sliding of earth debris without backward rotation. **Slump** is slipping of one or several units of debris with **backward rotation** relative to the slope (NCERT). Leong calls deep slides over arc-shaped planes **rotational slips**: coherent blocks move in a rotational slip, whereas in a slump coherence is lost and there is some flow.

Leong's causes of slides include river and slope undercutting, gullying and heavy rain.

## Flows

Flow needs a lot of water in the debris (Leong pp64-65). **Earthflow**: water-saturated soil, frequent in humid regions with steep slopes. **Mudflow**: a stream of fluid mud or clay, in mountains, deserts or on erupting volcanoes. **Debris avalanche**: a cataclysmic rockslide that disintegrates and starts to flow. **Solifluction** (soil flow): in subarctic regions where the ground is frozen (permafrost) a few centimetres down; in summer the top few centimetres thaw, become saturated and flow over the smooth frozen layer.

## Creep

Soil creep is extremely slow movement on soil-covered slopes. Leong lists its causes: earthquake shaking, frost needles, heating and cooling, trampling and burrowing, wetting and drying, root growth, weathering changes and heavy rain. You cannot watch it, but you can see it: tilted trees, fences and posts, bulging or broken walls and soil heaped behind walls (p65). **Heave** (NCERT's word) is the lifting of soils by frost growth and other causes; frost heave is one driver of creep (standard knowledge).

NCERT calls landslides relatively dry and names flow as a form but describes only landslide types in this reprint; Leong's flows are the wet ones. The books classify differently; they do not contradict.

`speed ladder, slowest to fastest:  creep (cm/year) < slide (cm/day) < flow and fall (m/day or m/second)`

Leong's cm/day for slides is a typical book figure; NCERT says rockslides on steep slopes are very fast, so do not use the ladder to reject 'landslides are rapid'.

### 9. Master: landslides in India - zones, causes, effects and mitigation

*Objective:* Describe the four landslide vulnerability zones of India, explain why the Himalaya and the Western Ghats both suffer, and list consequences and measures.

## Why the Himalaya

NCERT kegy205 (p8) gives reasons for frequent debris avalanches and landslides in the Himalaya: the range is **tectonically active**; it is mostly made of **sedimentary rocks and unconsolidated and semi-consolidated deposits**; and the slopes are very steep. Links: Day 19 lesson GS-097 and Day 20 lesson GS-098 (earthquakes shake slopes loose).

## Why the Western Ghats and Nilgiris too

These hills (bordering Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Kerala, and along the west coast) are relatively tectonically stable and mostly hard rock, yet slides and debris avalanches occur, less often. NCERT's reasons: many slopes are steeper, with almost vertical cliffs and escarpments; **mechanical weathering** from temperature changes and ranges is pronounced; and heavy rain falls over short periods. So rock falls are frequent, along with landslides and debris avalanches (p8). Moral for answers: tectonics, weak rock and steep slopes explain the Himalaya; steep cliffs, mechanical weathering and short intense rain explain the Ghats.

## The four zones (NCERT kegy106, PDF pp15-16)

India is divided into zones using geology, geomorphic agents, slope, land use, vegetation cover and human activity.

| Zone | Where (as NCERT lists) |
|---|---|
| Very high | Young unstable Himalaya; Andaman and Nicobar; high-rainfall steep Western Ghats and Nilgiris; north-eastern regions; areas of frequent ground-shaking by earthquakes; areas of intense human activity such as road and dam building |
| High | Similar conditions with a different combination, intensity and frequency of factors: all Himalayan states and the north-eastern states, except the plains of Assam |
| Moderate to low | Trans-Himalayan Ladakh and Spiti (low rainfall); the stable, low-rain Aravali; rain-shadow areas of the Western and Eastern Ghats and the Deccan plateau, which have occasional slides. Mining and subsidence slides are common in Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Goa and Kerala |
| Other areas | Rajasthan, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal (except Darjiling), Assam (except Karbi Anglong) and coastal regions of the southern states: NCERT calls them safe as far as landslides are concerned |

Edge cases show why you should not memorise the table as a clean rule. Kerala and Karnataka appear in the mining list and also include the Ghats; the Aravali (mostly in Rajasthan) appears in the moderate zone while Rajasthan is listed as 'other'; Assam is split. Read 'safe' as 'relatively safe' (check against the current official hazard maps; verify).

## Consequences and mitigation (NCERT kegy106 p16)

**Consequences**: the area of direct influence is small and local, but roadblocks, destroyed railway lines and rock falls that block channels have far-reaching effects. River diversion can cause flood and loss of life, and spatial interaction becomes difficult, risky and costly, which hurts development. Leong adds three effects: the geological cycle, landscape evolution and the economy (pp65-66).

**Mitigation**: area-specific measures; restrictions on construction and developments such as roads and dams; farming limited to valleys and moderate slopes; control on large settlements in high-vulnerability zones; large-scale afforestation; bunds to reduce water flow; terrace farming in the north-eastern hill states where jhumming (shifting cultivation) persists.

Landslides depend on highly localised factors, so monitoring is difficult and costly (NCERT p15).

### 10. Exam: the two official questions, answer frames and traps

*Objective:* Analyse the 2024 and 2025 Prelims items, apply a method for statement-and-reason questions, and plan Mains answers.

## The statement pattern in both years

Both items give three statements and four options built the same way: (a) both II and III are correct and both explain I; (b) both are correct but only one explains I; (c) only one of II and III is correct and that explains I; (d) neither is correct. **Method**: first judge each of II and III on its own as true or false; then ask whether each true one explains statement I. This is analysis, not an official key.

## 2024: rainfall, carbon dioxide and oxygen

Statement I: rainfall is one of the reasons for weathering of rocks. True: chemical weathering needs water and is strongest in humid climates (Leong p58). Statement II: rain water contains carbon dioxide in solution. True, and it forms carbonic acid, the basis of carbonation of limestone (Leong p58, NCERT p5). Statement III: rain water contains atmospheric oxygen. True as standard knowledge (air gases dissolve in water; verify), and oxygen in air or water drives oxidation (Leong p59). Both II and III give a mechanism by which rain weathers rock, so the analysis points to option (a).

## 2025: chalk, clay, porosity and permeability

Statement I: chalk is very permeable and clay is impermeable or least permeable. Statement II: chalk is porous and so absorbs water. Statement III: clay is not at all porous. These facts are not in the weathering extracts; they are standard knowledge taught in Day 18 lesson GS-096 (verify). **Porosity** is the share of empty space; **permeability** is how easily water moves through. Chalk has many connected pores, so water enters and passes: II is true and explains I. Clay holds water in extremely small, poorly linked pores: it is porous but nearly impermeable, so III ('not at all porous') is wrong. Only one of II and III is correct and it explains I, so the analysis points to option (c). The stem's 'very permeable' for chalk is the textbook contrast; a fuller hydrogeology account notes that fissures matter (verify).

## Traps

- Confusing 'impermeable' with 'non-porous'.
- 'Not at all', 'only', 'all' in a statement: test the absolute word.
- Treating mass wasting as erosion: NCERT says it is not.
- Calling exfoliation a process: NCERT calls it a result.
- Mixing NCERT's five chemical processes with Leong's seven.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Weathering: mechanical disintegration and chemical decomposition of rocks by elements of weather and climate; in-situ, with very little or no motion (NCERT kegy205 p5).
- Mass wasting (mass movement): transfer of rock debris down a slope under the direct influence of gravity, without a moving agent (NCERT p7).
- Erosion: acquisition and transportation of rock debris by agents (running water, groundwater, glaciers, wind, waves and currents) (NCERT p8).
- Deposition: settling of carried debris when the agent loses velocity and energy; coarser material first (NCERT p8).
- Denudation: umbrella term for weathering, mass wasting, erosion and transportation (NCERT p4).
- Degradation: wearing down of relief; aggradation: filling up of basins; gradation: the levelling of relief differences by erosion (NCERT p2).
- Regolith: the weathered, loose cover on bedrock; its depth is the weathering mantle (NCERT p5, Leong p60).
- Exogenic process: driven from outside the earth by atmosphere, sun and gravity; endogenic: driven from inside (NCERT p3).
- Geomorphic agent: a mobile medium that removes, transports and deposits earth material; geomorphic process: a force applied to earth material (NCERT p3).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Weathering versus erosion: breakup in place versus removal by an agent.
- Mass wasting versus erosion: gravity alone versus an agent; NCERT does not count mass wasting as erosion.
- Denudation versus gradation: the whole set of processes versus the levelling effect of erosion.
- Hydration versus hydrolysis: taking up water into the mineral versus a reaction with water in which hydrogen replaces metal ions.
- Solution versus carbonation: plain dissolving versus dissolving by carbonic acid.
- Exfoliation (a result) versus unloading (a process): domes form by unloading.
- Rock fall versus rockslide: surface layers falling freely versus deep sliding along a plane (NCERT p8).
- Slump versus rotational slip: Leong says slump loses coherence and has some flow; NCERT stresses backward rotation.
- Porosity versus permeability: empty space versus ease of flow (Day 18 lesson GS-096).
- Debris slide versus debris fall: rolling or sliding versus nearly free fall from a vertical or overhanging face.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- Chemical weathering is negligible in arid regions but physical weathering by temperature range and salt works there (Leong p58).
- Cold, waterlogged places can still show chemical change through organic acids (Leong p58).
- Weathering is not a pre-requisite of mass movement or erosion (NCERT p7, p8).
- A landslide is 'relatively dry' in NCERT while Leong's flows are wet: classification, not contradiction.
- Rock fall can occur on hard, tectonically stable hills (Ghats) when slopes are steep and rain is heavy.
- Leong's slide speed is typical, not a law: NCERT p8 and kegy106 p15 describe rockslides and landslides as rapid. Use the speed ladder for Leong-based items only.
- Frost action is the most important physical process in cold climates but cannot work where there is no freezing.

### Core concepts

- Weathering is breakup in place; mass wasting is gravity-only downslope movement; erosion needs a moving agent.
- Weathering is chemical, physical or biological, and climate is the master control.
- Chemical weathering needs water and is fastest when warm; physical weathering needs stress such as frost, salt, unloading or heat cycles.
- Deposition follows loss of energy; coarse first.
- Mass movement starts when the pull exceeds the resistance of the material; water, steepness, shaking and clearing of vegetation tip the balance.
- Landslides in India concentrate in the Himalaya, north-east, Western Ghats and Nilgiris and Andaman and Nicobar.

**Q.** Define weathering in one sentence using the word 'in-situ'.

**A.** Weathering is the mechanical breakup and chemical decay of rock by weather and climate at the place where the rock lies, with little or no movement of material.

**Q.** Name the three kinds of weathering.

**A.** Chemical, physical (mechanical) and biological.

**Q.** What is a geomorphic agent?

**A.** A mobile medium such as running water, ice, wind, waves or currents that removes, transports and deposits earth material.

**Q.** What does gravity do in mass wasting?

**A.** It moves debris downslope directly, with no other agent carrying it.

**Q.** Why is chemical weathering weak in a hot desert though temperature is high?

**A.** Chemical weathering needs water; in arid regions water is scarce, so the heat cannot speed the reactions much. Physical weathering by temperature range and salt dominates.

**Q.** Explain why acid rocks like granite give coarser products than basalt.

**A.** High-silica minerals such as quartz resist decay and remain as sand and grit, whereas lower-silica basic rocks decompose into fine clay and soluble substances (Leong Table 4.2).

**Q.** Explain why deposition is said to be not the work of any agent.

**A.** Deposition happens when an agent loses velocity and energy; the agent stops carrying rather than does something new.

**Q.** Why can weathering be called the preparer of erosion but not its precondition?

**A.** It makes loose fragments that agents can remove easily, but agents can also erode unweathered rock, so erosion can start without it.

**Q.** A hill road in a rainy region is widened and the forest on the slope is cleared. Explain, using NCERT's causes, why landslides become likelier.

**A.** Cutting removes support from below, clearing removes vegetation that holds the soil, and heavy rain adds weight and lubricates the slope; each is among the nine activating causes.

**Q.** A limestone cave region and a granite hill in the same rainy climate weather differently. Name the main process on each.

**A.** Limestone: solution and carbonation, because carbonic acid dissolves calcium carbonate. Granite: hydrolysis and oxidation, giving clay, gruss and iron oxide, plus physical effects.
