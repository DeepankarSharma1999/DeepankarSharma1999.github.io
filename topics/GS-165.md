## GS-165: Directive Principles and welfare state

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: What 'the State', 'justiciable' and 'directive' mean

In daily speech 'state' means Kerala or Punjab. In the Constitution, 'the State' with a capital S is a technical term. Article 12 (in Part III, the Fundamental Rights part) defines it to include the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and Legislature of every state, and all local and other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India (local authorities include municipalities and panchayats). Article 36 says that Part IV uses the word in exactly the same sense. So when a Directive says 'the State shall...', it is speaking to all of these organs at once: the Union, the states, the local bodies and public authorities.

'Justiciable' means 'a court can decide and enforce it'. If a provision is justiciable you can file a case and the court can order the government to act or to stop. Fundamental Rights are justiciable: Article 32 even lets you go straight to the Supreme Court. 'Non-justiciable' means no court can compel the government to implement it. The NCERT puts it simply: if a government does not implement a Directive, we cannot go to court and ask the court to make it do so.

'Directive' means an instruction about the direction of policy, a 'steer in this direction' rather than a command backed by a penalty. Putting the three words together: a Directive Principle of State Policy is a non-justiciable instruction to all organs of the State about the direction their laws and policies should take.

### Bridge: Rights, duties and directives; police state versus welfare state

The Constitution has three lists that beginners mix up. Part III, Fundamental Rights (Articles 12-35), are entitlements of persons that the State must not violate; they are mostly negative ('the State shall not...') and courts enforce them. Part IV, Directive Principles (Articles 36-51), are instructions to the State about what it should do; they are positive ('the State shall endeavour to...') and courts cannot enforce them. Part IV-A, Fundamental Duties (Article 51A), was added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 and lists duties of citizens; the NCERT notes that the Constitution says nothing about enforcing them and that the enjoyment of rights is not conditional on performing duties.

Now the two models of a State. A 'police state' is concerned mainly with law and order and defence against external attack. Laxmikanth links it to the nineteenth-century idea of laissez-faire, the view that government should leave economic life alone. A 'welfare state' takes active responsibility for the well-being of its people: livelihood, education, health, working conditions, social security for the old, the sick and the unemployed. Dr Ambedkar described the goal of Part IV as 'economic democracy' in addition to the 'political democracy' created by elections and rights. Keep this contrast in mind: the Directive Principles are the part of the Constitution that turns India from a police state model into a welfare state model, on paper.

### 1. What Part IV is and why the framers wrote it

*Objective:* Place Part IV in the Constitution and explain its origins and purpose in your own words.

## Where it sits

The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are in Part IV of the Constitution, Articles 36 to 51. Strictly, Article 36 only defines 'State' and Article 37 explains the nature of the Part; the actual Directives run from Article 38 to Article 51. When an exam question says 'provisions contained in Part IV', it means this whole block.

## Where the idea came from

The framers borrowed the idea from the Irish Constitution of 1937, which had itself copied it from the Spanish Constitution. Dr B.R. Ambedkar called the Directives a 'novel feature' of our Constitution. Granville Austin, the American historian of the Constitution, described the Directive Principles together with the Fundamental Rights as the 'Conscience of the Constitution'. Laxmikanth adds that the two together contain the philosophy of the Constitution.

The Directives also resemble the 'Instrument of Instructions' that the British Government issued to the Governor-General and Governors under the Government of India Act, 1935. Ambedkar's own comparison: the Directives are the same kind of instrument, except that they instruct the legislature and the executive rather than colonial governors.

## Why write instructions that cannot be enforced?

The NCERT gives the human reason. The makers knew independent India faced enormous challenges, above all bringing equality and well-being to all citizens. They wanted to give future governments a policy direction, but did not want to tie their hands with legally binding commitments. So they wrote guidelines, kept them out of the courts, and trusted two forces: the moral weight of the Constitution and the people, who would hold governments responsible at elections.

Sir B.N. Rau, the Constitutional Advisor to the Constituent Assembly, recommended dividing the rights of an individual into two categories, justiciable and non-justiciable. The Drafting Committee accepted this. Justiciable rights became Part III; non-justiciable ones became Part IV. (Some textbooks also connect this two-category idea to the Sapru Committee report of 1945; the sources read for this lesson do not say so, so treat that as a point to verify.)

Laxmikanth lists three practical reasons for non-justiciability: the country lacked the financial resources to implement them at once; vast diversity and backwardness would obstruct implementation; and a newly born State with many preoccupations needed freedom to decide the order, time, place and mode of fulfilling them. The framers 'refrained from giving teeth' to the Directives and trusted awakened public opinion more than court procedure.

## How to think about any Directive

Ask two questions every time: who is being addressed (always 'the State' in the Article 12 sense) and what is the sanction (moral and political, never legal).

### 2. Articles 36 and 37: the rules that govern all the Directives

*Objective:* Explain what Article 37 does and does not say, and how courts use the Directives without enforcing them.

## Article 36

Article 36 says that in Part IV 'the State' has the same meaning as in Part III. You already met this in bridge module B1: Union and state legislatures and executives, local authorities and other public authorities are all bound to keep the Directives in mind.

## Article 37: three statements in one Article

Article 37 does three things, and exam questions test each separately:

1. The provisions of Part IV shall not be enforceable by any court.
2. Nevertheless, the principles are fundamental in the governance of the country.
3. It shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.

Notice the structure: a limit (no court enforcement), a status (fundamental in governance), and a duty (apply them when making laws). A 2020 Prelims question offered the statements 'they shall be enforceable by courts' and 'they shall not be enforceable by any court' side by side with 'the principles are to influence the making of laws by the State'. The Article 37 structure is exactly what you need to sort such statements.

## What 'fundamental in the governance of the country' means

It means the Directives are a floor for policy, not optional decoration. Laxmikanth explains that they impose a moral obligation on State authorities, while the real force behind them is political: public opinion. Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar said that no ministry responsible to the people can afford to ignore Part IV light-heartedly. Ambedkar said in the Assembly that a government resting on popular vote can hardly ignore the Directives, and if it does it will have to answer to the electorate.

## What courts actually do with the Directives

Courts cannot strike down a law merely because it violates a Directive. But the Directives are not irrelevant in court. The Supreme Court has repeatedly used them as a guide when testing whether a law is 'reasonable' under Article 14 (equality) or Article 19 (the six freedoms): a law that gives effect to a Directive may be treated as reasonable and so saved from being declared unconstitutional. M.C. Setalvad called the Directives 'beacon-lights' to the courts in judicial review.

## What the Directives contain

The NCERT says Part IV lists three kinds of thing: goals and objectives society should adopt (welfare, social and economic justice, raising living standards, international peace); certain non-justiciable rights individuals should enjoy (adequate livelihood, equal pay for equal work, right to work, early childhood care); and policies the government should follow (uniform civil code, prohibition of alcohol, cottage industries, village panchayats).

## Two beginner confusions to clear now

First, 'non-justiciable' does not mean 'unimportant' or 'may be ignored'. Second, the Directives are not 'limitations' on the legislature or the executive; Fundamental Rights are the limitations (they say what the State must not do). Directives are positive instructions about what the State should do. A 2017 Prelims question turned on exactly this distinction.

### 3. The socialistic Directives: Articles 38 to 43A and 47

*Objective:* Recall each socialistic Directive by Article number and explain what it asks the State to do.

## The classification itself

The Constitution does not classify the Directives. Scholars group them by content into three families: socialistic, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual. The grouping is a memory aid and an analytical lens, not law. Laxmikanth's grouping is used here.

## Socialistic principles

These set the path towards a welfare state by aiming at social and economic justice.

- **Article 38**: the State shall promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order permeated by justice - social, economic and political. Clause (2), added by the 44th Amendment (1978), directs the State to minimise inequalities in income and to eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities. This is the 'welfare state' Article; a 2020 Prelims question asked which Part declares the ideal of a welfare state.
- **Article 39**: the State shall direct its policy towards securing (a) adequate means of livelihood for all citizens; (b) distribution of the ownership and control of the community's material resources to serve the common good; (c) that the economic system does not result in concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment; (d) equal pay for equal work for men and women; (e) that the health and strength of workers, men and women, are not abused and that children of tender age are not forced by economic necessity into work unsuited to their age or strength; (f) opportunities for the healthy development of children (modified by the 42nd Amendment, 1976). Clauses (b) and (c) matter enormously in module 6; a 2021 Prelims question asked what 'concentration of wealth' violates.
- **Article 39A** (added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976): equal justice and free legal aid to the poor.
- **Article 41**: within the limits of its economic capacity, the State shall secure the right to work, to education and to public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement.
- **Article 42**: just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
- **Article 43**: a living wage, a decent standard of life and social and cultural opportunities for all workers; also promotion of cottage industries (the cottage-industry part is counted as Gandhian).
- **Article 43A** (added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976): participation of workers in the management of industries.
- **Article 47**: raise the level of nutrition and the standard of living and improve public health (the prohibition clause in the same Article is counted as Gandhian).

## A vocabulary note on wages

Laxmikanth's footnote distinguishes three terms. A 'minimum wage' covers bare needs - food, shelter, clothing. A 'living wage' adds education, health, insurance and similar needs. A 'fair wage' lies between the two. Article 43 asks for a living wage, the most generous of the three.

## Why 'socialistic'?

Each of these Articles asks the State to intervene in the economy for the weaker party: the worker, the child, the woman, the unemployed, the old. That is the socialist instinct, and it is why Sir Ivor Jennings later complained that Part IV reads like Fabian socialism (module 8).

### 4. Gandhian and liberal-intellectual Directives: Articles 40 to 51

*Objective:* Recall the Gandhian and liberal-intellectual Directives by Article number and explain the ideas behind each group.

## Gandhian principles

These carry Gandhi's programme of reconstruction from the national movement into the Constitution.

- **Article 40**: organise village panchayats and give them the powers needed to function as units of self-government.
- **Article 43**: promote cottage industries on an individual or cooperative basis in rural areas.
- **Article 43B** (added by the 97th Amendment, 2011): promote the voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies.
- **Article 46**: promote the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other weaker sections, and protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation.
- **Article 47**: prohibit the consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health (the nutrition and public-health part of Article 47 was listed as socialistic in module 3).
- **Article 48**: prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle and improve their breeds.

## Liberal-intellectual principles

These reflect liberalism: individual equality before a common law, science, culture, environment and peace.

- **Article 44**: secure a uniform civil code for all citizens throughout India.
- **Article 45**: provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete six years (changed by the 86th Amendment, 2002; module 5).
- **Article 48**: organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines.
- **Article 48A** (added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976): protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife.
- **Article 49**: protect monuments, places and objects of artistic or historic interest declared of national importance.
- **Article 50**: separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State. (Tested in Prelims 2020 and 2025.)
- **Article 51**: promote international peace and security, maintain just and honourable relations between nations, foster respect for international law and treaty obligations, and encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration.

## Articles that sit in two groups

Articles 43, 47 and 48 each contain two different directions, so they appear under two heads. Article 43: living wage (socialistic) and cottage industries (Gandhian). Article 47: nutrition and health (socialistic) and prohibition (Gandhian). Article 48: cattle (Gandhian) and scientific agriculture (liberal-intellectual). If an exam question asks you to classify one of these, read which clause it quotes.

### 5. Directives added or changed by Amendments; Directives outside Part IV

*Objective:* Map each later Directive to its Amendment (42nd: three new Articles plus reworded 39(f)) and identify the three Directives that live outside Part IV.

## The original list versus later additions

An exam favourite is 'which of the following was added by the 42nd Amendment?' You can only answer it if you know which Directives were there in 1950 and which came later.

### 42nd Amendment Act, 1976: three new Articles plus one reworded clause

Counting note: the 42nd Amendment inserted three new Articles (39A, 43A, 48A) and reworded Article 39(f); Laxmikanth's DPSP chapter counts four. Both counts describe the same changes, so read a question's phrase ('new Articles' versus 'Directives added or modified') before choosing a number.

1. **Article 39(f)**: opportunities and facilities for the healthy development of children (the clause was modified to this form).
2. **Article 39A**: equal justice and free legal aid.
3. **Article 43A**: participation of workers in the management of industries.
4. **Article 48A**: protection of the environment, forests and wildlife.

A memory hook (using the four-change count): the 42nd touched 'four Cs' - Children (reworded 39(f)), Counsel (legal aid, 39A), Co-management by workers (43A), Conservation (48A). The 2017 Prelims question on this point offered 'equal pay for equal work' (original Article 39(d)), 'right to work, education and public assistance' (original Article 41) and 'living wage' (original Article 43) alongside 'participation of workers in management' (Article 43A). Only the last belongs to 1976.

### 44th Amendment Act, 1978

Added Article 38(2): the State shall strive to minimise inequalities in income and eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities. The same Amendment abolished the Fundamental Right to property (Article 31) and made it a legal right under Article 300A, which matters in module 6.

### 86th Amendment Act, 2002

Changed the subject-matter of Article 45. Originally Article 45 asked the State to provide free and compulsory education for all children up to fourteen years within ten years. The 86th Amendment made elementary education a Fundamental Right under new Article 21A, and rewrote Article 45 to cover early childhood care and education until the age of six. So one Directive was promoted into Part III and a new, earlier-age Directive took its place. The NCERT counts the right to education among the efforts to give effect to the Directives.

### 97th Amendment Act, 2011

Added Article 43B on cooperative societies: voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management.

## Directives outside Part IV

Three Articles elsewhere in the Constitution read like Directives and are also non-justiciable:

- **Article 335** (Part XVI): the claims of SCs and STs shall be taken into consideration, consistently with efficiency of administration, in appointments to services and posts of the Union or a state.
- **Article 350A** (Part XVII): every state and local authority shall endeavour to provide instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage for children of linguistic minority groups.
- **Article 351** (Part XVII): it is the duty of the Union to promote the spread and development of Hindi so that it may serve as a medium of expression for all elements of India's composite culture.

Laxmikanth notes the judiciary gives these equal attention because all parts of the Constitution must be read together. A question that says 'all Directives are in Part IV' is therefore wrong.

### 6. Rights versus Directives: Champakam Dorairajan to Minerva Mills

*Objective:* Narrate the conflict between Part III and Part IV as a sequence of judgments and Amendments and state the present position.

## Why a conflict at all

Fundamental Rights are enforceable; Directives are not; yet Article 37 makes the Directives a duty of the State. When a government legislates to implement a Directive and the law cuts into a Fundamental Right, which wins? The NCERT gives the concrete trigger: laws abolishing the zamindari system were attacked for violating the then Fundamental Right to property. The government argued that rights could be abridged to implement Directives; the courts initially held that rights were too sacred to be limited even for that purpose.

## The sequence

1. **Champakam Dorairajan (1951)**. The Supreme Court held that if the two conflict, Fundamental Rights prevail; Directives must run subsidiary to them. The separate question whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights by a constitutional amendment was answered in the affirmative in Shankari Prasad (1951) (and again in Sajjan Singh, 1965); Laxmikanth compresses both points into the Champakam step, so attribute them separately in a Mains answer (the Shankari Prasad attribution is standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify). Parliament used the amending power with the 1st (1951), 4th (1955) and 17th (1964) Amendments to implement some Directives (chiefly land reform).

2. **Golaknath (1967)**. The Court reversed direction: Parliament cannot take away or abridge Fundamental Rights, which are 'sacrosanct'; they cannot be amended even to implement Directives.

3. **Parliament's reply (1971)**. The 24th Amendment declared that Parliament can abridge or take away any Fundamental Right by constitutional amendment. The 25th Amendment inserted **Article 31C** with two provisions: (a) no law implementing the Directives in Article 39(b) or (c) shall be void for contravening Articles 14, 19 or 31; (b) no law containing a declaration that it gives effect to such policy shall be questioned in any court on the ground that it does not.

4. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**. The Court struck down the second provision of Article 31C because judicial review is a basic feature of the Constitution and cannot be removed. The first provision was upheld. This is also the case that gave us the 'basic structure' doctrine: Parliament may amend any part, but may not damage the Constitution's basic features. The NCERT adds that the Court in 1973 held the right to property was not part of the basic structure.

5. **42nd Amendment (1976)**. Extended the first provision of Article 31C to laws implementing any Directive, not only Articles 39(b) and (c). In effect Part IV was given legal primacy over Articles 14, 19 and 31.

6. **Minerva Mills (1980)**. The Court struck down that extension. It held that the Constitution rests on a balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; they are 'two wheels of a chariot, one no less than the other', and this harmony is itself part of the basic structure. The Directives' goals must be achieved without abrogating the means provided by the Rights.

7. **44th Amendment (1978)**, in between, removed the right to property from Part III and made it a legal right under Article 300A.

## Present position

Fundamental Rights generally enjoy supremacy over the Directives. Two qualifications: laws giving effect to Articles 39(b) and (c) are protected against challenge under Articles 14 and 19 (the surviving part of Article 31C); and Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights to implement Directives so long as the basic structure is not damaged or destroyed.

**A recent development (dated; verify against the judgment).** In November 2024 a nine-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court decided Property Owners Association v State of Maharashtra. It is reported to have held that Article 31C survives in its form before the 42nd Amendment (that is, protecting laws that give effect to Article 39(b) and (c), as in the Kesavananda and Minerva Mills line), and, by majority, that not every privately owned resource is automatically a 'material resource of the community' under Article 39(b), departing from the broader reading associated with Justice Krishna Iyer in earlier decisions (the Ranganatha Reddy and Sanjeev Coke line). Whether a particular private resource qualifies depends on its nature, scarcity and impact on the community. This was decided after the sources read for this lesson; confirm the holding and the strength of the majority before quoting it. For Mains, it refines but does not overturn the 'present position' above.

## Table: Fundamental Rights versus Directive Principles

| Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|
| Negative: prohibit the State from doing things | Positive: require the State to do things |
| Justiciable | Non-justiciable |
| Aim at political democracy | Aim at social and economic democracy |
| Legal sanction | Moral and political sanction |
| Welfare of the individual | Welfare of the community |
| Generally need no legislation and are enforced as they stand (exceptions: some rights need a law to bite, for example Article 17 on untouchability, where Parliament is empowered to make the penal law) | Need legislation to be implemented |
| Courts must strike down a violating law | Courts cannot strike down a law for violating them, but may uphold a law made to implement them |

### 7. Implementation: how Directives became laws, programmes and amendments

*Objective:* Link concrete laws, schemes and amendments to the Directive each implements.

## Reading this module

For Mains you need examples; for Prelims you need the Directive-to-measure link. Read each item as 'Article X was implemented by Y'.

## Planning and land

- The Planning Commission (1950) and the Five Year Plans aimed at socio-economic justice and reducing inequalities (Articles 38, 39). NITI Aayog replaced the Commission in 2015.
- Almost every state passed land reform laws: abolition of intermediaries such as zamindars, jagirdars and inamdars; tenancy reforms (security of tenure, fair rent); ceilings on holdings; distribution of surplus land to the landless; cooperative farming (Article 39(b) and (c)). The NCERT lists zamindari abolition as the first big effort.

## Labour, women and children

- Minimum Wages Act 1948, Payment of Wages Act 1936, Payment of Bonus Act 1965, Contract Labour Act 1970, Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976, Trade Unions Act 1926, Factories Act 1948, Mines Act 1952, Industrial Disputes Act 1947, Workmen's Compensation Act 1923 (Articles 42, 43). The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 1986 was renamed the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act in 2016 (Article 39(e), (f)).
- Maternity Benefit Act 1961 and Equal Remuneration Act 1976 (Articles 42, 39(d)).
- **As of late 2025 (verify against official notifications).** The four Labour Codes (on wages, industrial relations, social security, and occupational safety, health and working conditions), which consolidate many of the older labour Acts listed above, were reported to have been brought into force in November 2025. The older Acts remain the historical examples of Articles 42 and 43 being implemented; check which are now subsumed before citing one as current law.

## Resources for the common good

- Nationalisation of life insurance (1956), fourteen commercial banks (1969) and general insurance (1971); abolition of privy purses (1971) (Article 39(b), (c)).

## Justice, panchayats and cottage industry

- Legal Services Authorities Act 1987: nationwide free legal aid and lok adalats, whose awards are binding and final (Article 39A).
- The three-tier panchayati raj system and the 73rd Amendment Act 1992 giving it constitutional status (Article 40).
- Khadi and Village Industries Commission and Board, Handloom, Handicrafts, Coir and Silk Boards (Article 43).
- The 97th Amendment itself and cooperative legislation (Article 43B).

## Work, welfare and education

- Community Development Programme 1952, Integrated Rural Development Programme 1978, Jawahar Rozgar Yojana 1989, Swarnajayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana 1999, Sampoorna Grameen Rozgar Yojana 2001 and the National Rural Employment Guarantee Programme 2006 (Article 41; the NCERT calls the employment guarantee a 'partial right to work'). As of December 2025 the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (the 2005 Act behind the 2006 programme) was reported to have been replaced by a new statute; verify the current legal position and name before citing it as a live scheme. The Article 41 link and the 'partial right to work' idea remain valid as history and analysis.
- Right to education (Article 21A via the 86th Amendment) and the mid-day meal scheme (NCERT) (Articles 45, 47).
- Primary health centres and disease-eradication programmes (Article 47); old-age pensions in some states (Article 41).

## Weaker sections

- Reservations in education, services and legislatures; Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 (renamed 1976); SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989; National Commissions for SCs and STs (65th Amendment 1990, bifurcated by the 89th, 2003); National Commission for Backward Classes given constitutional status by the 102nd Amendment 2018; 10 per cent EWS reservation through the 103rd Amendment 2019 (Article 46).

## Environment, culture, judiciary, peace

- Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, Water and Air Acts and Pollution Control Boards, National Forest Policy 1988 (Article 48A).
- Ancient and Historical Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1951 (Article 49).
- Criminal Procedure Code 1973 separated judicial powers from executive officers such as collectors and placed them under judicial magistrates controlled by the High Court (Article 50). As of 1 July 2024 the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 has been replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS); the historical link between the 1973 Code and Article 50 stands, but cite the BNSS for current law and verify that the corresponding separation provisions carry over.
- Cow-slaughter laws in some states (Article 48); non-alignment and Panchsheel (Article 51).

## Why implementation is still incomplete

Laxmikanth names inadequate finances, unfavourable socio-economic conditions, population growth and strained Centre-state relations.

### 8. Criticism, utility and the meaning of the welfare state

*Objective:* Weigh the criticisms against the utility of the Directives and define the welfare state with constitutional anchors.

## The criticisms

1. **No legal force.** K.T. Shah called the Directives 'pious superfluities' and compared them to a cheque on a bank payable only when the bank's resources permit. Nasiruddin said they were no better than new-year resolutions broken on the second of January. T.T. Krishnamachari called them a 'veritable dustbin of sentiments'. K.C. Wheare saw a 'manifesto of aims and aspirations' and 'moral homily'; Sir Ivor Jennings called them 'pious aspirations'.
2. **Illogically arranged.** N. Srinivasan complained that the Directives are neither properly classified nor logically arranged, mixing vital economic questions with minor issues, and reason-based provisions with ones based on sentiment and prejudice. Jennings agreed that they lack a consistent philosophy.
3. **Conservative.** Jennings said they reflect nineteenth-century English political philosophy: 'the ghosts of Sidney and Beatrice Webb stalk through the pages', Fabian socialism without the socialism, likely to be outmoded by the twenty-first century.
4. **Constitutional conflict.** K. Santhanam argued they can create conflict between the Centre and states (the Centre could direct states to implement them and dismiss non-compliant governments), between the President and the Prime Minister (the President could refuse a Bill as violating Part IV), and between a Governor and a Chief Minister.

## The defence

L.M. Singhvi called the Directives 'the life-giving provisions of the Constitution'. M.C. Chagla said that if fully carried out India would be 'a heaven on earth', a welfare state and not only a political democracy. Ambedkar valued them for declaring that the goal of Indian polity is economic democracy, not merely political democracy. Granville Austin saw them as fostering the social revolution. B.N. Rau called them moral precepts with at least an educative value.

M.C. Setalvad, former Attorney General, gave four uses: an Instrument of Instructions to all authorities; beacon-lights to courts in judicial review; the dominating background to all State action; and an amplification of the Preamble's promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.

Laxmikanth adds six further roles: they give stability and continuity to policy across changes of ruling party; they supplement the Fundamental Rights with social and economic rights; their implementation creates conditions for enjoying Fundamental Rights ('political democracy without economic democracy has no meaning'); they let the Opposition hold the government to a constitutional yardstick; they are a test of government performance; and they are a common manifesto for every party.

## So what is a 'welfare state'?

Put the pieces together. A welfare state is one that accepts responsibility for the material and social well-being of its people rather than only for order and defence. In India this ideal is declared in Part IV: Article 38 (a just social order, reduced inequality), Article 39 (livelihood, fair distribution, equal pay, protection of workers and children), Article 41 (work, education, public assistance), Article 42 (humane work, maternity relief), Article 43 (living wage), Article 46 (weaker sections) and Article 47 (nutrition, health). The Directives operationalise the welfare state by instructing the legislature and executive to pass the laws and run the programmes you met in module 7, while the Preamble states the values and Part III protects the individual. Minerva Mills gives the balancing rule: welfare goals are pursued through, not over, Fundamental Rights.

A fair verdict for Mains: the Directives are unenforceable but not powerless; their power is political, and their record is substantial yet incomplete.

### 9. How UPSC tests Part IV: patterns, traps and a model approach

*Objective:* Recognise the recurring question patterns on Part IV and apply an elimination method and a Mains structure.

## Prelims patterns in the verified questions

1. **Amendment mapping** (2017): 'which was added by the 42nd Amendment?' Method: recall the four Cs from module 5 (three new Articles plus reworded 39(f)); everything else in the options is original 1950 text.
2. **Nature of the Directives** (2017): 'do the Directives constitute limitations upon legislative and executive function?' Method: the Directives are positive instructions, not limitations; the sources describe them as recommendations and a duty to apply in making laws. The analysis in module 2 therefore points away from treating them as limitations on either function. Fundamental Rights are the limitations.
3. **Enforceability wording** (2020): statements saying 'enforceable by courts', 'not enforceable by any court' and 'to influence the making of laws'. Method: rebuild Article 37 and the contradiction between the first two statements resolves itself.
4. **Which Part declares what** (2020, 2021): 'ideal of welfare state' and 'concentration of wealth'. Method: welfare state is the content of Part IV (Article 38); concentration of wealth is Article 39(c). Watch the distractor 'Concept of Welfare', which is not a Part of the Constitution.
5. **Provision-to-Part matching** (2020, 2025): separation of judiciary from executive is Article 50 in Part IV; 'valuing and preserving the rich heritage of our composite culture' is a Fundamental Duty (Article 51A(f)); prohibition of employment of children below fourteen in factories is a Fundamental Right (Article 24). The 2020 question on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights is not covered by the sources read here; verify from a source that discusses the UDHR before forming a view.

## Elimination heuristics

- Words like 'shall be enforceable', 'courts can compel', 'binding on courts' attached to Part IV are red flags.
- 'Limitation' belongs to Part III; 'instruction' belongs to Part IV.
- If a Directive mentions legal aid, environment, workers' participation or children's development, think 1976.
- Cooperatives means 2011; early childhood care below six means 2002.

### Notes: Definitions

- Directive Principles of State Policy: non-justiciable constitutional instructions to the State (Article 12 sense) about the direction of its laws and policies, contained in Part IV, Articles 36-51.
- Justiciable: enforceable by a court; non-justiciable: a court cannot compel compliance.
- Welfare state: a State that takes responsibility for the well-being of its people (livelihood, education, health, social security), as opposed to a police state concerned mainly with law and order and defence.
- Economic democracy (Ambedkar): the goal of Part IV, as distinct from the political democracy created by elections and Fundamental Rights.
- Living wage: covers bare needs plus education, health and insurance; minimum wage covers bare needs only; fair wage lies between.

### Notes: Origins and nature

- Borrowed from the Irish Constitution of 1937, which copied the idea from the Spanish Constitution.
- Ambedkar: 'novel features'; Granville Austin: with Fundamental Rights, the 'Conscience of the Constitution'.
- Resemble the Instrument of Instructions under the Government of India Act, 1935.
- B.N. Rau proposed the division of rights into justiciable (Part III) and non-justiciable (Part IV); accepted by the Drafting Committee.
- Reasons for non-justiciability: insufficient finances; diversity and backwardness; the new State needed freedom over order, time, place and mode.
- Article 37: not enforceable by any court; fundamental in the governance of the country; duty of the State to apply them in making laws.

### Notes: Articles 36-51 at a glance

- 36 definition of State; 37 application of the principles.
- 38 social order for welfare, minimise inequalities (clause 2 by 44th Amendment).
- 39 livelihood, distribution of resources (b), no concentration of wealth (c), equal pay (d), workers and children (e), healthy development of children (f, modified by 42nd).
- 39A equal justice and free legal aid (42nd); 40 village panchayats; 41 work, education, public assistance; 42 humane work and maternity relief.
- 43 living wage and cottage industries; 43A workers' participation (42nd); 43B cooperatives (97th); 44 uniform civil code.
- 45 early childhood care and education below six (86th); 46 SCs, STs, weaker sections; 47 nutrition, health, prohibition.
- 48 agriculture, animal husbandry, cattle; 48A environment, forests, wildlife (42nd); 49 monuments; 50 separation of judiciary; 51 international peace.
- Outside Part IV: 335 (SC/ST claims to services), 350A (mother-tongue instruction), 351 (Hindi).

### Notes: Classification

- Socialistic, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual are scholarly labels, not constitutional text; Articles 43, 47 and 48 appear in two groups.

### Notes: Amendments that touched Part IV

- 42nd (1976): three new Articles (39A, 43A, 48A) plus reworded Article 39(f) (Laxmikanth's DPSP chapter counts four); also extended Article 31C to all Directives (struck down 1980) and inserted Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A).
- 44th (1978): added Article 38(2) on minimising inequalities; abolished the Fundamental Right to property (now Article 300A).
- 86th (2002): new Article 21A (right to education, a Fundamental Right); Article 45 rewritten for early childhood care and education below six.
- 97th (2011): added Article 43B on cooperative societies.
- 25th (1971): inserted Article 31C; 24th (1971): Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

### Notes: Rights versus Directives: chronology

- 1951 Champakam Dorairajan: Fundamental Rights prevail; Directives subsidiary. 1951 Shankari Prasad: Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights (Laxmikanth compresses the two; Shankari Prasad is standard knowledge, verify).
- 1951, 1955, 1964: 1st, 4th and 17th Amendments implement Directives (land reform).
- 1967 Golaknath: Parliament cannot abridge Fundamental Rights.
- 1971: 24th Amendment (power to amend Rights) and 25th Amendment (Article 31C).
- 1973 Kesavananda Bharati: basic structure doctrine; first part of 31C valid, second part (ouster of judicial review) invalid.
- 1976: 42nd Amendment extends 31C to all Directives.
- 1978: 44th Amendment removes right to property from Part III.
- 1980 Minerva Mills: extension struck down; balance between Parts III and IV is basic structure.

### Notes: Key judgments

- State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Directives must conform to and run subsidiary to Fundamental Rights. (The amendability of Fundamental Rights was upheld separately in Shankari Prasad, 1951; Laxmikanth compresses the two.)
- Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967): Fundamental Rights are sacrosanct; Parliament cannot amend them.
- Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): basic structure; judicial review cannot be excluded; Article 31C partly upheld.
- Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directives is a basic feature; 'two wheels of a chariot'.

### Notes: Implementation examples by Directive

- Article 39(b),(c): land reforms, bank nationalisation 1969, insurance nationalisation 1956 and 1971, abolition of privy purses 1971.
- Article 39A: Legal Services Authorities Act 1987, lok adalats.
- Article 40: three-tier panchayati raj; 73rd Amendment 1992.
- Article 41: employment programmes including the National Rural Employment Guarantee Programme 2006 (as of December 2025 reported replaced by a new statute; verify); old-age pensions.
- Articles 42-43: Minimum Wages Act 1948, Factories Act 1948, Maternity Benefit Act 1961, Equal Remuneration Act 1976 (as of late 2025 the four Labour Codes were reported in force and consolidating many older labour Acts; verify which are subsumed).
- Article 45/21A: right to education, mid-day meal scheme.
- Article 46: reservations, Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955, SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989, 102nd and 103rd Amendments.
- Article 48A: Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, Forest (Conservation) Act 1980.
- Article 50: Criminal Procedure Code 1973 (replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023 from 1 July 2024). Article 49: Ancient Monuments Act 1951. Article 51: non-alignment, Panchsheel.

### Notes: Criticism and utility

- Critics: K.T. Shah ('pious superfluities', cheque on a bank), Nasiruddin (new-year resolutions), T.T. Krishnamachari ('dustbin of sentiments'), K.C. Wheare ('manifesto of aims and aspirations'), Ivor Jennings ('pious aspirations', Fabian socialism without socialism), N. Srinivasan (illogical arrangement), K. Santhanam (constitutional conflict).
- Defenders: L.M. Singhvi (life-giving provisions), M.C. Chagla (heaven on earth), Ambedkar (economic democracy), Granville Austin (social revolution), B.N. Rau (educative value), M.C. Setalvad (Instrument of Instructions, beacon-lights to courts, background to State action, amplify the Preamble).
- Reasons for incomplete implementation: inadequate finances, unfavourable socio-economic conditions, population explosion, strained Centre-state relations.

### Notes: Concepts commonly confused

- Non-justiciable is not the same as ignorable: Article 37 makes application a duty, and courts use Directives to judge reasonableness.
- Directives are instructions, not limitations; Fundamental Rights are the limitations on the State.
- Article 39(d) equal pay (original) versus Article 43A workers' participation (1976).
- Article 45 before 2002 (free and compulsory education to fourteen) versus after (early childhood care below six); Article 21A is the Fundamental Right.
- Composite culture (Fundamental Duty 51A(f)) versus Hindi promotion (Article 351 Directive outside Part IV).
- Article 24 (child labour, a Fundamental Right) versus Article 39(e),(f) (children's health and development, Directives).

### Core concepts

- Directives are non-justiciable but 'fundamental in the governance of the country' (Article 37).
- They embody the welfare state and economic democracy, complementing the political democracy of Part III.
- Three scholarly groups: socialistic, Gandhian, liberal-intellectual.
- The Rights-Directives conflict ended in a balance: Fundamental Rights generally supreme, Article 39(b),(c) laws protected, basic structure untouchable.
- Courts cannot enforce Directives but use them to uphold laws as reasonable.

**Q.** What does 'non-justiciable' mean in one sentence?

**A.** A court cannot enforce it or compel the government to implement it.

**Q.** Which Article defines 'State' for Part IV, and how?

**A.** Article 36, by adopting the Part III (Article 12) meaning: Union and state governments and legislatures, local and other public authorities.

**Q.** Name the three scholarly groups of Directives.

**A.** Socialistic, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual.

**Q.** What is the difference between a police state and a welfare state?

**A.** A police state looks after law, order and defence; a welfare state also takes responsibility for livelihood, health, education and social security.

**Q.** Why can a court uphold a law by reference to a Directive but not strike one down for violating a Directive?

**A.** Because Article 37 bars enforcement, a Directive cannot be the ground for invalidity; but when testing reasonableness under Articles 14 or 19, a law implementing a Directive may be treated as reasonable and saved.

**Q.** How does Minerva Mills reconcile Parts III and IV?

**A.** It holds that harmony and balance between them is part of the basic structure: Directive goals must be achieved without abrogating the means provided by Fundamental Rights; absolute primacy to either disturbs the Constitution.

**Q.** Why is Article 39(b),(c) special within Part IV?

**A.** Laws implementing these two clauses are protected by the surviving first provision of Article 31C against challenge under Articles 14 and 19, a protection no other Directive enjoys after Minerva Mills.
