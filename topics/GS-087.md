## GS-087: Modern Indian visual arts

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Modern Indian visual arts and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Colonial patronage and academic realism

From around the eighteenth century, British officers commissioned Indian painters to record customs, people, flora and fauna. Painting on paper and relying on close observation rather than rule books, these artists produced the hybrid Company School, popular in albums in India and Britain until photography displaced it. Colonial art schools in Lahore, Calcutta, Bombay and Madras taught academic realism and crafts suited to European taste. The most successful academic painter, however, worked outside them: Raja Ravi Varma of Travancore mastered oil realism to show Ramayana and Mahabharata scenes, reproduced by lithography as oleographs that entered homes. By the late nineteenth century nationalists criticised this style as too Western for Indian myths.

### Bengal School and Shantiniketan

Early in the twentieth century the Bengal School, led by Abanindranath Tagore with E.B. Havell, sought art that was Indian in both subject and style, turning to Mughal and Pahari miniatures rather than Company or academic models, and Abanindranath created the journal Indian Society of Oriental Art to make that heritage better known. After the 1905 partition, Ananda Coomaraswamy wrote on Swadeshi in art and Okakura's pan-Asianism brought two Japanese artists who taught the wash technique at Shantiniketan. The 1922 Bauhaus exhibition in Calcutta exposed Indians to abstraction, seen in Gaganendranath's Cubist interiors. At Kala Bhavana, Nandalal Bose's Haripura posters showed farmers and drummers as nation-builders, using a bold, patua-like folk manner. Jamini Roy rejected academic training for flat, folk-derived forms meant to reach ordinary people.

### Towards modernism and political art

Kala Bhavana students Benode Behari Mukherjee and Ramkinkar Baij turned to their surroundings; Mukherjee's mural Medieval Saints narrated Tulsidas and Kabir, and Baij's cement Santhal Family monumentalised a tribal family going to work. Rabindranath turned his manuscript doodles into faces and landscapes. Amrita Sher-Gil, trained in Paris, fused miniature and mural traditions with European modernism in the 1930s. The Bengal famine pushed the 1943 Calcutta Group towards art of its own time and moved Chittoprasad and Somnath Hore to printmaking; Chittoprasad's Hungry Bengal sketches annoyed the British. The Bombay Progressives of 1946 split into Husain's Indian-themed figuration and Raza's abstraction drawing on bindu, mandala and yantra.

### Worked example

Q: 'Modern Indian art was shaped by the conflict between colonialism and nationalism.' Discuss. Frame: colonial institutions and Company painting; Ravi Varma's popular academic realism; Swadeshi turn of Abanindranath and Havell; Shantiniketan's folk and environmental idiom (Nandalal, Baij, Mukherjee) and Gandhi's Haripura commission; Jamini Roy's folk renaissance; post-1943 political and modernist groups (Calcutta Group, Progressives). Conclude with NCERT's internationalism vs indigeneity balance.

### Recall

- - **q:** For which Congress session did Nandalal Bose paint posters?
  - **a:** The Haripura session, at Gandhi's invitation (NCERT gives 1937 in the text and 1938 for the panels).
- - **q:** Which British principal partnered Abanindranath Tagore?
  - **a:** E.B. Havell of the Calcutta School of Art.
- - **q:** Who led the Progressive Artists' Group?
  - **a:** Francis Newton Souza, with M.F. Husain, S.H. Raza, K.H. Ara, S.A. Bakre and H.A. Gade.

### Common trap

Treating the 'Bengal School' as a regional style confined to Bengal or as anti-British in personnel. NCERT notes the label is not fully accurate because the movement influenced artists across India, and that the British principal E.B. Havell was Abanindranath's key ally.
