## GS-070: Fascism and Nazism

Mains GS1 · World History · 18th–20th century

*Goal:* Explain Fascism and Nazism and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why Weimar democracy failed

Germany's 1918 defeat brought a democratic republic, framed at Weimar, with universal adult suffrage. It was burdened from birth. The Versailles Treaty stripped Germany of colonies, territory, iron and coal, imposed a War Guilt Clause and heavy reparations, and opponents mocked the republic's supporters as 'November criminals'. Communists and Socialists became bitter enemies after the Spartacist rising. In 1923, when Germany stopped paying and France seized the Ruhr, reckless printing produced hyperinflation; American loans under the Dawes Plan brought only brief recovery. The 1929 crash then cut industrial output to 40 per cent of its 1929 level and left 6 million unemployed. Proportional representation and Article 48 decrees made governments short-lived.

### Hitler's rise and the Nazi state

Hitler, an Austrian-born war veteran, took over the German Workers' Party, renamed it the National Socialist German Workers' Party and failed in a 1923 bid to seize Bavaria. The Depression made Nazism a mass movement: its vote rose from 2.6 per cent in 1928 to 37 per cent in 1932. Rallies, the Swastika and propaganda cast Hitler as a saviour. Appointed Chancellor in January 1933, he used the Reichstag fire to suspend liberties, sent Communists to concentration camps and passed the Enabling Act to rule by decree. Other parties and trade unions were banned, and the Gestapo, SS and security police gained powers to arrest and deport without legal process. Youth groups and propaganda films spread the ideology.

### Racial ideology, war and comparisons

Nazi thought rejected equality: a racial ladder placed 'Aryans' at the top and Jews at the bottom, borrowing a distorted reading of Darwin and Spencer, while Lebensraum justified conquest in the east. Jews were persecuted, ghettoised and finally murdered in gas chambers; Gypsies, the disabled, Poles and Russians were also targeted. Hitler left the League in 1933, retook the Rhineland, absorbed Austria and Czechoslovakia, invaded Poland in 1939 and joined Italy and Japan in the 1940 Tripartite Pact. Invading the USSR proved fatal after Stalingrad. NCERT's world-history text notes Germany's long tradition of ethnic rather than civic nationalism, and Japan's parallel state-centred nationalism also repressed dissent at home.

### Worked example

Q: 'The rise of Nazism was the product of the crisis of Weimar democracy.' Examine. Frame: (1) Versailles humiliation and the 'November criminals' myth; (2) economic shocks - 1923 hyperinflation, 1929 Depression, 6 million unemployed; (3) institutional weakness - proportional representation, Article 48; (4) Nazi strategy - propaganda, rallies, Hitler as messiah, vote rising from 2.6% to 37%; (5) destruction of democracy - Fire Decree, Enabling Act, Gestapo/SS; (6) ideology - racial hierarchy and Lebensraum leading to genocide and war. Conclude that crisis created the opening but Nazi choices made the catastrophe.

### Recall

- - **q:** What did the Enabling Act of March 1933 do?
  - **a:** It established dictatorship by letting Hitler sideline Parliament and rule by decree; all parties and unions except Nazi ones were banned.
- - **q:** Which two features of the Weimar constitution made it unstable?
  - **a:** Proportional representation (coalition rule) and Article 48 (emergency rule by decree).
- - **q:** What was Lebensraum?
  - **a:** Hitler's geopolitical idea of acquiring 'living space' by expanding eastwards for German settlement and resources.

### Common trap

Saying Hitler seized power in a coup. His 1923 Bavarian putsch failed; he became Chancellor constitutionally in January 1933 after the Nazis became the largest party, and only then dismantled democracy through the Fire Decree and the Enabling Act.
