### Bridge: Words you need: revolutionary, conspiracy case, dacoity, ordinance

- **Revolutionary (in these sources)**: a person who believed British rule could end only by force or by a secret armed organisation.
- **Dacoity**: armed robbery by a gang; the movement called its fund-raising dacoities swadeshi.
- **Conspiracy case**: a criminal trial named for a plot (Alipore, Delhi, Lahore). The charge was often waging war against the King, which carried death.
- **Approver**: an accused who turns Crown witness for a lighter fate.
- **Transportation**: sentence of exile and imprisonment, usually to the Andamans, here simply 'for life'.
- **Ordinance**: a rule issued by the executive without the legislature; ordinances were used to jail without trial.
- **Propaganda by deed**: using a dramatic act, rather than speeches, to educate and mobilise.

Dates in this lesson are CE (the usual calendar).

### 1. Orientation: two waves of revolutionary effort

*Objective:* State in five sentences what the revolutionary movement was, its two phases and where it sits beside the Moderate and Gandhian strands.

## What the topic means

The **revolutionary movement** was the effort, by small secret groups, to end British rule by force or to prepare for force. It stood beside two other strategies: the petitions and council work of the Moderates, and the mass non-violent campaigns later led by Gandhi. British officials called its members terrorists; its followers called themselves revolutionaries. Spectrum separates *method* (individual violence) from *aim* (a national government) and quotes Subhas Chandra Bose to the effect that the aim was revolution, not terrorism. As a student of history you can use both words, but you must say which you mean.

## The story in five sentences

1. Spectrum says the **first phase (1907 to 1917)** grew out of the Swadeshi and Boycott movement (taught in GS-038), when young nationalists found open protest blocked and the Extremist leaders gave them no organisation.
2. Groups arose in **Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab**, with a Delhi and United Provinces thread, and Indians **abroad** (London, Paris, Berlin, North America) supplied shelter, printing presses and arms contacts.
3. The First World War seemed a golden chance: the **Ghadr plot** and the **German Plot** of 1915 failed through treachery and were crushed by the **Defence of India Act**.
4. The **second phase** began after the withdrawal of Non-Cooperation in 1922: the **Hindustan Republican Association**, **Kakori (1925)**, the **HSRA** with **Bhagat Singh** and **Chandrashekhar Azad**, and the **Chittagong raid (1930)** of **Surya Sen**.
5. The second phase turned towards socialism and mass politics in its thinking, but it was ended by repression and by the deaths of its leaders in 1931 to 1934.

### 2. Why it began and what the revolutionaries believed

*Objective:* Explain three causes of the first phase and the programme, ideology and weak points of revolutionary violence.

## Three causes (Spectrum)

- **Younger nationalists would not retreat.** When the open Swadeshi phase declined, activists who had given time and risk to it could not simply go home.
- **The leaders offered no outlet.** Spectrum says even the Extremists called for sacrifice but built no effective organisation and found no new forms of political work to channel youthful energy.
- **Repression closed the peaceful road.** Press and meeting restrictions convinced many that only physical force could expel the British.

## The programme

The revolutionaries weighed two large options and set both aside as impractical at that stage: a violent mass revolution across the country, and subverting the loyalty of the army. They chose instead to copy **Russian nihilists** and **Irish nationalists**. Their methods were:

- assassination of unpopular officials, and of traitors and informers inside their own circles;
- **swadeshi dacoities** (armed robberies) to raise funds;
- during the First World War, **military conspiracies** hoping for help from Britain's enemies.

**Dacoity** means armed robbery by a gang. **Approver** means an accused person who turns Crown witness. Both words appear in the case histories of modules 3 and 4.

## The idea behind the violence

The aim was to strike terror in rulers, awaken the people and remove the fear of authority; idealistic youth would then drive the British out. Notice what this theory is: a belief that the heroic act of a few can do the work of a mass movement. Later (module 8) Bhagat Singh's group would reason differently.

## Weak points the sources record

- Spectrum faults the Extremist leaders for not explaining the difference between a revolution of the masses and individual violence, so the latter took root.
- No mass involvement was planned.
- In Bengal the social base was narrow and upper caste, and an overemphasis on Hindu religious ideas (the Spectrum verdict in module 3) kept Muslims aloof.

## Moral argument in the air

Spectrum's boxed quotations show the range of opinion. Tilak's June 1897 article in *Kesari* urged readers to look beyond the Penal Code to the Gita when judging the acts of great men (a paraphrase of the box). Madanlal Dhingra's last statement offered his life for the motherland. Gandhi, according to the box, asked whether the critics would not read what the young were writing with their blood. Each shows respect for sacrifice; none proves the writer approved of the method, so be careful before using these in an answer.

## Mass revolution versus individual terror

| | Mass revolution | Individual terror |
|---|---|---|
| Who acts | Millions | A secret circle |
| Aim | Remove the system | Shock rulers and inspire youth |
| Risk | Large scale | Easily broken by informers |

### 3. Bengal in the first phase: Anushilan, Yugantar and the Alipore case

*Objective:* Narrate the main Bengal groups and events from 1902 to 1915 in order and give Spectrum's verdict.

## Groups and papers

Calcutta's student world had secret societies by the 1870s but they were inactive. The first real groups date from **1902**: one in **Midnapore** under Jnanendranath Basu, and the **Anushilan Samiti** in Calcutta (Spectrum names Promotha Mitter as founder, with Jatindranath Banerjee, Barindra Kumar Ghosh and others). Until 1907-08 it offered only physical and moral training. In **April 1906** an inner circle (Barindra Ghosh, Bhupendranath Dutta) started the weekly **Yugantar**; after police violence at the Barisal Conference (April 1906) it declared that force must be stopped by force. *Sandhya* was another paper of this kind.

## Events in order

- **1907**: an abortive attempt on Fuller, former head of the new Eastern Bengal and Assam province; in **December 1907** an attempt to derail the train of Lieutenant-Governor Fraser.
- **1908, Muzaffarpur**: Prafulla Chaki and Khudiram Bose threw a bomb at a carriage thought to carry the judge Kingsford. He was not in it; two British women were killed. Chaki shot himself; Khudiram was tried and hanged.
- **The Alipore (Manicktolla or Muraripukur) conspiracy case**: the Anushilan group was arrested, including the brothers Aurobindo and Barindra Ghosh, charged with waging war against the King. Chittaranjan Das defended Aurobindo, who was acquitted. Barindra Ghosh (leader) and Ullaskar Dutt (bomb maker) were sentenced to death, later commuted to life. The approver Narendra Gosain was shot dead in jail by two co-accused.
- **1908, Barrah dacoity** by Dacca Anushilan under Pulin Das for funds; shootings of a public prosecutor (February 1909) and a deputy superintendent of police (February 1910).
- **December 1912, Delhi**: Rashbehari Bose and Sachin Sanyal bombed Viceroy Hardinge's procession in Chandni Chowk on his entry into the new capital; he was injured, not killed. The Delhi Conspiracy trial followed; Bose evaded arrest (Spectrum; see masterNotes for the executed names).

## The German Plot, 1915

During the war **Jatindranath Mukherjee (Bagha Jatin)** led the Jugantar party in a plan to import German arms. He asked Rashbehari Bose to take Upper India, aiming for an all-India rising with a guerrilla force, the seizure of Fort William and a mutiny. Funds came from the **taxicab and boat dacoities**. A traitor leaked the plot; Jatin, waiting at **Balasore** for arms, was traced and died in a gunfight in **September 1915**. Do not confuse him with **Jatin Das**, the hunger striker of module 8; Spectrum's chapter summary slips and writes Jatin Das here.

## Spectrum's verdict

Revolutionary activity was the most substantial legacy of Swadeshi Bengal and influenced educated youth for a generation. But the Hindu religious emphasis kept Muslims aloof, the heroism was sometimes quixotic, no mass involvement was planned, the base was narrow and upper caste, and the movement could not withstand State repression.

### 4. Maharashtra, Punjab and the Delhi thread

*Objective:* Compare the Maharashtra and Punjab groups, their leaders, events and fate, and place Rashbehari Bose between them.

## Maharashtra

- **1879**: Vasudev Balwant Phadke organised the **Ramosi Peasant Force** to expel the British by an armed revolt disrupting communications, raising money by dacoity. It was suppressed early.
- **1890s**: Tilak spread militant nationalism through the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals and his papers *Kesari* and *Maharatta* (Kal was another journal of the region). Two disciples, the **Chapekar brothers**, killed Poona's Plague Commissioner **Rand** and Lt. Ayerst in **1897**.
- **1899**: the Savarkar brothers formed **Mitra Mela**, which merged into **Abhinav Bharat** in **1904** (named after Mazzini's Young Italy). Nasik, Poona and Bombay became centres of bomb making.
- **1909**: Anant Lakshman Kanhere of Abhinav Bharat killed **A.M.T. Jackson**, Collector of Nasik. The investigation uncovered a wider conspiracy, 38 arrests, and Savarkar was held to be its brain and sentenced to transportation for life with forfeiture of property.

## Punjab

Spectrum ties Punjab's extremism to frequent famines, higher land revenue and irrigation tax, the practice of *begar* (forced unpaid labour) by landlords, and events in Bengal. **Lala Lajpat Rai** published *Punjabee* (motto: self-help at any cost); **Ajit Singh**, Bhagat Singh's uncle, ran the **Anjuman-i-Mohisban-i-Watan** at Lahore with the journal *Bharat Mata*, after urging peasants of the Chenab colonies and Bari Doab not to pay revenue and water rates. Others were Aga Haidar, Syed Haider Raza, Bhai Parmanand and the Urdu poet Lalchand 'Falak'. In **May 1907** the government banned political meetings and deported Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh, and extremism faded quickly; Ajit Singh and a few associates (Sufi Ambaprasad, Hardayal among them) became full revolutionaries and some moved abroad.

## The Delhi and United Provinces thread

**Rashbehari Bose** and **Sachin Sanyal** built a secret society reaching Punjab, Delhi and the United Provinces, and bombed Hardinge in 1912. In 1913 Bose met Jatin to discuss an all-India armed rising of the 1857 type and extended the Bengal plan to Punjab and the upper provinces. Spectrum also calls him a leading figure of the **Ghadr** revolution. When the 1915 plans failed he escaped to **Japan**, and much later played a part in founding the Indian National Army. This link between regions is why he appears in a Prelims question on the Ghadr Party (module 10).

## Compare the two regions

- Maharashtra: cultural and religious festivals as a base, secret societies, bomb making and assassinations of officials.
- Punjab: agrarian grievance (revenue, canal taxes, begar), open agitation first, repression, then exile and the overseas Ghadr.

## Check your map

Locate Poona, Nasik and Bombay on the Maharashtra side; Lahore, Ferozepur and Rawalpindi for Punjab (these garrison towns return in module 5).

### 5. Abroad: India House, Paris, Berlin and the Ghadr Party

*Objective:* Explain why revolutionaries went abroad and describe London, Paris, Berlin and North America in order, including Komagata Maru and 21 February 1915.

## Why go abroad

Spectrum gives three reasons: shelter, the chance to publish revolutionary literature beyond reach of the Press Acts, and the search for arms.

## London, Paris and Berlin

- **1905, London**: Shyamji Krishnavarma founded the **Indian Home Rule Society**, called **India House**: a hostel for students, a scholarship scheme to bring radical youth and a journal, *The Indian Sociologist*. Savarkar and Hardayal were members.
- **1909**: Madanlal Dhingra, from this circle, killed the India Office official **Curzon-Wyllie**. London became too dangerous, especially after Savarkar was extradited in **1910** and transported in the Nasik case.
- **Paris and Geneva**: **Madame Bhikaji Cama**, a Parsi revolutionary with contacts among French socialists, brought out *Bande Mataram*, with Ajit Singh also working there.
- **Berlin**: after 1909, when Anglo-German relations worsened, Virendranath Chattopadhyaya chose Berlin.

## The Ghadr Party (North America)

The **Ghadr** (also spelled Ghadar) grew round a weekly paper of that name, headquartered at **San Francisco**, with branches along the US coast and in the Far East. Its members were mostly ex-soldiers and peasants from Punjab who had migrated to the USA and Canada for work. Earlier workers included Taraknath Das, Sohan Singh Bhakna and Lala Hardayal (who arrived in 1911); they set up a Swadesh Sevak Home at Vancouver and a United India House at Seattle before the party was founded in **1913**. Its programme: assassinate officials, publish anti-imperialist literature, work among Indian troops abroad, procure arms and bring a simultaneous revolt in all British colonies. Leaders named by Spectrum include Hardayal, Ramchandra, Bhagwan Singh, Kartar Singh Saraba, Barkatullah and Bhai Parmanand. Spectrum judges its achievement ideological: a militant nationalism with a completely secular approach, but it lacked sustained leadership and underestimated the preparation needed.

## Komagata Maru and 1915

The **Komagata Maru** carried about 370 passengers, mainly Sikh and Punjabi Muslim would-be immigrants, from Singapore to Vancouver. Canadian authorities turned it back after two months; it reached Calcutta in **September 1914**. The passengers refused to board a Punjab-bound train and in a clash with police at **Budge Budge** 22 died. With the war, the Ghadr leaders urged fighters to go home; Kartar Singh Saraba and Raghubar Dayal Gupta went; Bose and Sanyal were asked to lead; the political dacoities of January-February 1915 in Punjab often targeted moneylenders and debt records. The armed revolt was fixed for **21 February 1915** in the garrisons of **Ferozepur, Lahore and Rawalpindi**, and was foiled at the last moment by treachery.

## Berlin Committee and the missions

The **Berlin Committee for Indian Independence** (Spectrum: 1915; often dated to 1914 and renamed in 1915, verify; Chattopadhyaya, Bhupendranath Dutta, Hardayal and others) worked with the German foreign office under the so-called Zimmermann Plan, aiming to send volunteers and arms and even an invasion. Missions went to Baghdad, Persia, Turkey and Kabul. The Kabul mission (Raja Mahendra Pratap, Barkatullah, Obaidullah Sindhi) formed a **provisional Indian government** with Crown Prince Amanullah. The notable mutiny of the time was at **Singapore on 15 February 1915** (5th Light Infantry and 36th Sikhs), crushed with 37 executed and 41 transported.

### 6. Suppression, decline and verdict on the first phase

*Objective:* State how the government crushed the first phase, why it faded after 1917 and what it achieved.

## Repression

Spectrum calls the wartime measures a formidable battery, the most intensive since 1857. The **Defence of India Act** passed in March 1915 mainly to smash the Ghadr movement allowed detention without trial, special courts with very severe sentences and numerous courts-martial of soldiers. Rebellious regiments were disbanded and leaders arrested and deported; 45 Ghadr men were hanged. Bengal revolutionaries and the Ghadrites were not alone: the pan-Islamists, Ali brothers, Maulana Azad and Hasrat Mohani, were interned for years.

## Decline

Spectrum gives three reasons for a pause after the war: the release of prisoners held under the Defence of India Rules cooled passions; Montagu's August 1917 statement and talk of reforms created an atmosphere of conciliation; and Gandhi's non-violent non-cooperation promised new hope. Notice that the third reason is a competing method, not defeat alone.

## Why the first phase failed

- It was secret, small and easily infiltrated; both major 1915 plots collapsed through informers or traitors.
- It had no organised mass base and no sustained leadership.
- Its supply of arms depended on foreign powers that had their own aims.
- It could not match the state's coercive power.

## What it achieved

- It kept the idea of complete independence alive when Moderates still sought reforms.
- It supplied martyrs and stories that inspired later youth.
- It linked India with an international network of Indians, and Ghadr preached secular militant nationalism.

## Source note

The Spectrum figures (45 hanged, 37 executed and 41 transported at Singapore) are as printed and are not cross-checked here; treat them as sourced numbers if asked, with the attribution.

### 7. The second phase begins: new influences, HRA and Kakori

*Objective:* Explain why revolutionary activity revived after 1922, the new influences, the HRA's aims and the Kakori case, and list the strands of the 1920s.

## The revival

Many revolutionaries had been released under a general amnesty in early 1920 and, persuaded by Gandhi and C.R. Das, either joined Non-Cooperation or suspended activity. Its sudden withdrawal left them disillusioned with the leadership's strategy and its stress on non-violence. They were not drawn to the council work of the **Swarajists** (who wished to enter the legislative councils) or to the constructive work of the **No-Changers**. Nearly all major leaders of the second phase had taken part in Non-Cooperation, including Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee, Surya Sen, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Chandrashekhar Azad and Jatin Das.

## New influences

- The post-war upsurge of trade unionism, which revolutionaries wished to harness.
- The Russian Revolution of 1917 and the survival of the Soviet state.
- New communist groups emphasising Marxism and the proletariat.
- Journals extolling sacrifice (Atmasakti, Sarathi, Bijoli) and books such as *Bandi Jiwan* by Sachin Sanyal and *Pather Dabi* by Sharatchandra Chatterjee, whose ban increased its popularity.

Spectrum names two strands: one in Punjab, the United Provinces and Bihar (the HRA, later HSRA) and one in Bengal (Yugantar, Anushilan, later the Chittagong group). Note that this is the first major *turn towards socialism* in the revolutionary line.

## HRA and Kakori

The **Hindustan Republican Association/Army (HRA)** was founded in **October 1924 at Kanpur** by Ramprasad Bismil, Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee and Sachin Sanyal. Its goal was an armed revolution to overthrow colonial rule and set up a Federal Republic of the United States of India resting on adult franchise. Its **1925 Manifesto** declared opposition to every system that allowed exploitation of man by man, and its organ *Revolutionary* proposed nationalising railways and heavy industry.

In **August 1925** the HRA held up the **8 Down train at Kakori**, near Lucknow, and took its official railway cash. The crackdown led to many arrests: 17 jailed, 4 transported for life, and 4 hanged (**Bismil, Ashfaqullah, Roshan Singh, Rajendra Lahiri**). Spectrum calls Kakori a setback. Bismil, in his last days, urged youth to give up pistols, support Hindu-Muslim unity, and work openly under the Congress, affirming faith in communism.

## The wider 1920s (for the PYQ)

The revolutionaries were one strand among several in the 1920s. Spectrum lists Swarajists and No-Changers, spread of Marxist and socialist ideas (Nehru and Bose on the Congress left; CPI formed in Tashkent in 1920 and formalised at Kanpur in 1925; the Meerut case of 1929), student leagues, peasant agitations, trade unions (AITUC 1920) and caste movements (Justice Party, Self-Respect movement of 1925 and others). The list sits in masterNotes; module 10 uses it for the 2020 Mains question.

## Name caution

Textbooks vary: Spectrum writes both HRA and HSRA with Association or Army; NCERT Class X writes Hindustan Socialist Republican *Army*. Expect either in options; focus on what changed (the added word *Socialist*, 1928).

### 8. HSRA, Saunders, the Assembly bomb and Bhagat Singh's ideas

*Objective:* Narrate the HSRA's actions from 1928 to 1931 in order, explain its changed ideology and describe the trials and deaths.

## Reorganisation, September 1928

After Kakori, young revolutionaries met at the ruins of **Ferozshah Kotla, Delhi (September 1928)** under **Chandrashekhar Azad** and renamed the body **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA)**. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev Thapar, Bhagwati Charan Vohra, Bejoy Kumar Sinha, Shiv Verma and Jaidev Kapur took part. It chose collective leadership and socialism as official goal.

## Events in order

- **October 1928**: Lala Lajpat Rai, 'Sher-i-Punjab', was fatally injured by lathi blows at an anti-Simon Commission procession.
- **December 1928, Lahore**: Bhagat Singh and Shivram Rajguru shot dead John P. Saunders, mistaking him for Superintendent James Scott who had ordered the lathi charge. Azad shot a constable who pursued them. A statement justifying the killing called it a duty to efface an insult to the nation.
- **8 April 1929**: Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw bombs into the **Central Legislative Assembly** against the Public Safety Bill and Trade Disputes Bill. The bombs were made harmless, aimed at making 'the deaf hear'; the aim was to be arrested and use the court for propaganda.
- **Trials**: Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were tried in the **Lahore conspiracy case**. In jail they fasted for political prisoners' treatment; **Jatin Das** became the first martyr on the 63rd day of his fast. Congress leaders organised their defence and Bhagat Singh became a household name.
- **December 1929**: a bid to blow up Viceroy Irwin's train near Delhi.
- **27 February 1931**: Azad, betrayed, was besieged at Alfred Park, Allahabad, and shot himself rather than be captured.
- **23 March 1931**: Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged (the Gandhi-Irwin Pact, signed on 5 March, did not save them: GS-046).

## The ideological turn

Spectrum says the real breakthrough was ideological and that rethinking began in the mid-1920s. By the late 1920s the group was moving from individual action to mass politics. **Bhagwati Charan Vohra** wrote *The Philosophy of the Bomb*. Bhagat Singh set up the **Punjab Naujawan Bharat Sabha (1926)** as an open wing for work among youth, peasants and workers, and the **Lahore Students' Union**. He said real revolutionary armies were in villages and factories. He redefined revolution: not the cult of bomb and pistol but a change of an order based on injustice, aiming beyond national liberation at ending exploitation of man by man. He accepted Marxism and a class approach, and was consciously secular (the Sabha's rules barred members from communal bodies). NCERT Class X cites his trial statement and says he did not wish to glorify the cult of the bomb and pistol.

## Why then use violence?

Spectrum's explanation: a new ideology takes long to spread, while the need was a quick change of thinking; the young intellectuals faced the problem of mobilising and recruiting, so they chose **propaganda by deed**: individual heroic action and the courtroom as a platform.

## Source cautions

Spectrum summaries use 'Jatin Das' for the Bengal German Plot leader; the HSRA Jatin Das is a different man (module 3). Spectrum prints the Alfred Park date (27 February 1931) and the executions (23 March 1931) exactly; it gives the Saunders killing only as December 1928.

### 9. Bengal's second phase, repression and the verdict

*Objective:* Describe the Chittagong raid and the new features of Bengal's revolutionaries, then evaluate the movement's contributions and limits.

## Bengal in the 1920s

Many Bengal groups worked under Congress cover and supported C.R. Das's Swarajist work. After Das's death (1925) the Bengal Congress split between J.M. Sengupta (backed by Anushilan) and Subhas Bose (backed by Yugantar). In 1924 Gopinath Saha attempted to kill Calcutta Police Commissioner Charles Tegart, and a man named Day was killed instead; Saha was hanged, and Subhas Bose was among many arrested under a new ordinance.

## Chittagong armoury raid, April 1930

**Surya Sen**, a teacher in a national school who had joined Non-Cooperation, was jailed 1926-28 and was secretary of the Chittagong District Congress Committee. With Anant Singh, Ganesh Ghosh and Lokenath Baul he planned an armed rebellion to show the empire could be challenged. The plan was to seize two armouries, cut telephone and telegraph lines and break the rail link. In **April 1930**, 65 activists under the **Indian Republican Army, Chittagong Branch** raided; Sen hoisted the flag, took a salute and proclaimed a provisional revolutionary government, then they dispersed to the villages. Sen was arrested in **February 1933** and hanged in **January 1934**, but the raid inspired recruits.

## New features (Spectrum)

- Large-scale participation of young women (shelter, messages, arms); names and dates are taught in GS-052 module 3.
- Emphasis on **group action against the organs of the State**, not individual killing.
- Less Hindu religious ritual (no oath-taking), so Muslims such as Satar and Mir Ahmed joined.
- Drawbacks: conservative elements remained, no broader socio-economic goals, and Swarajist-linked leaders did not support Muslim peasants against zamindars.

## Official reaction

Panic, then repression under about 20 repressive acts: villages in Chittagong burned and collective fines imposed. Spectrum says that in 1933 Nehru was sentenced to two years for sedition for condemning imperialism and praising revolutionary heroism (standard accounts date the arrest and sentence to February 1934; verify).

## The verdict: contribution and limits

**Contribution.** (1) Kept alive the demand for complete independence. (2) Gave the national movement symbols and martyrs. (3) In the second phase, brought socialism, secularism and mass politics into nationalist thinking. (4) Drew Muslims and women into revolutionary activity (Chittagong) and addressed workers and peasants in its programme (HRA Manifesto, Naujawan Bharat Sabha). 

**Limits.** (1) No mass base in the first phase. (2) Easily infiltrated and suppressed. (3) Dependence on foreign powers in the war plots. (4) Hindu religiosity in Bengal's first phase. (5) The second phase's ideas were not fully practised before the leaders died. (6) Gandhian mass action drew far larger numbers (compare GS-045).

**Sort the three features.** Of women's participation, group action against State organs and the fall of ritual, say which two widened the social base and which changed the method.

**Debate.** Spectrum says the Extremist leadership failed to counter individual terror. Whether revolutionary violence helped or hindered the mass movement is an open interpretive question that the extracts do not settle, so present it as a debate in an answer, not as fact.

### 10. Examination application: the two verified PYQs and answer plans

## 2022 Prelims Q53: who was associated with the Ghadr Party

The question lists Barindra Kumar Ghosh, Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee and Rash Behari Bose. Build the answer by *placing each name* in the lesson, not by guessing.

## 2020 Mains Q13 (15 marks): ideological strands of the 1920s

Directive: **Discuss**. The question has two parts: *various ideological strands* and *expanded its social base*. Plan:

1. Introduce: the 1920s as a decade when mass entry and many currents arrived together.
2. Strands (module 7 and masterNotes): Gandhian constructive work and the Swarajist council route; socialist and communist ideas on the Congress left (Nehru, Bose) and the CPI; revolutionary terrorism with a socialist turn; caste and non-Brahmin movements.
3. Social base: peasants (Bardoli 1928), workers (AITUC, strikes), youth and students, depressed castes and, in Bengal, women.
4. Link: the strands pulled and the Congress held them under a common anti-imperial platform.
5. Limits and conclusion.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Revolutionary movement: secret organisations that sought to end British rule by force or to prepare for armed rebellion.
- First phase: 1907 to 1917 (Spectrum), an offshoot of Swadeshi and Boycott; second phase: after Non-Cooperation, c.1922 to 1934.
- Ghadr: a revolutionary party organised in 1913 around a weekly paper of the same name, based at San Francisco.
- HRA/HSRA: Hindustan Republican Association (October 1924, Kanpur) renamed Hindustan Socialist Republican Association in September 1928 (Delhi).
- Propaganda by deed: individual dramatic action and trial speeches used to spread the message.
- Anushilan Samiti: Calcutta group founded in 1902; the name suggests training or cultivation of body and mind (translation is a standard gloss; verify).

### Notes: Source differences and cautions

- HSRA: Spectrum says Association; NCERT Class X says Army; Spectrum also writes Association/Army for the HRA.
- Jatin Mukherjee (Bagha Jatin, 1915) is not Jatin Das (hunger striker, 1929); Spectrum's first-phase summary prints 'Jatin Das' in error.
- Spectrum gives Saunders as December 1928 only; every day-level date in this lesson (8 April 1929, 27 February 1931, 23 March 1931) is printed by Spectrum.
- Spectrum prints 'Sir Fuller', 'Chandheri' and 'Zimmerman'; standard spellings are Bampfylde Fuller (verify), Chaudhuri and Zimmermann.
- NCERT Class X says Bhagat Singh was 23 when executed (consistent with the standard account).
- Nehru's sedition sentence: Spectrum p402 prints 1933; standard accounts date the arrest and sentence to February 1934 (verify).
- Berlin Committee: Spectrum gives 1915; many standard accounts date its formation to September 1914 and its renaming as the Indian Independence Committee to 1915 (verify).

### Notes: Frequently confused

- Ghadr Party (North America, 1913) against Berlin Committee (Europe; Spectrum 1915, often dated to 1914 and renamed 1915; verify).
- Anushilan (Bengal, 1902) against Abhinav Bharat (Maharashtra, 1904).
- Bagha Jatin against Jatin Das; Rash Behari Bose against Subhas Chandra Bose.
- Saunders (killed in error) against Scott (the intended target); Kingsford (not in the carriage) against the two women killed.
- Ajit Singh (Punjab, uncle of Bhagat Singh) against Bhagat Singh himself.

### Memory skeleton

- Two phases: 1907-1917 (Swadeshi offshoot) and c.1922-1934 (after Non-Cooperation)
- Bengal: Anushilan 1902, Yugantar 1906, Muzaffarpur and Alipore 1908
- Maharashtra and Punjab: Chapekars 1897, Abhinav Bharat, Nasik 1909; Ajit Singh
- Abroad: India House 1905, Ghadr 1913, Komagata Maru 1914, 21 February 1915 plan, Berlin
- HRA 1924, Kakori 1925, HSRA 1928, Saunders, Assembly bomb 1929, executions 1931
- Chittagong 1930 and Bengal's women revolutionaries
- Verdict: no mass base, repression; socialist turn and influence

### Last-week summary

- First phase: Anushilan 1902, Yugantar April 1906, Muzaffarpur bomb and Alipore case 1908 (Aurobindo acquitted), Dhingra kills Curzon-Wyllie 1909, India House London 1905 (Krishnavarma).
- Ghadr 1913, San Francisco weekly; Komagata Maru September 1914 (Budge Budge); 21 February 1915 plan betrayed, 45 hanged; Defence of India Act March 1915; Rash Behari Bose.
- Second phase: HRA October 1924 Kanpur; Kakori August 1925 (4 hanged); HSRA September 1928 Ferozshah Kotla; Saunders December 1928; Assembly bomb 8 April 1929.
- Chittagong April 1930 (Surya Sen, hanged January 1934); Azad dies 27 February 1931; Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Rajguru hanged 23 March 1931.
- Phase 1 individual terror, foreign links; phase 2 group action, socialism, more secular; both lacked a mass base.
