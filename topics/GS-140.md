## GS-140: Geography of disaster-prone regions

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Geography · Human/economic/world

*Goal:* Explain Geography of disaster-prone regions and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Hazard, disaster and vulnerability

NCERT separates natural hazards - circumstances such as steep unstable Himalayan slopes or desert extremes that could harm people - from disasters, which are sudden events causing large-scale loss of life and property beyond people's control. Not all disasters are natural: Bhopal and Chernobyl were human-made, and deforestation or unscientific construction indirectly intensify landslides and floods. The key shift is vulnerability. Earlier, people avoided intensive use of hazard-prone land, but technology now lets settlement spread onto floodplains and seafronts, so cities such as Mumbai and Chennai face cyclones and tsunamis. Hence prevention is limited for natural hazards, and mitigation and preparedness become the main strategy.

### Mapping India's hazard belts

Earthquakes cluster along the Himalayan arc where locked plates release stored stress; besides the very high zone, remaining Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, northern Punjab, eastern Haryana, Delhi, western UP and northern Bihar are high risk. Tsunami waves are low over deep ocean but rise sharply in shallow water, which is why coasts suffer; the 26 December 2004 event killed over 300,000 people. Cyclones lose force inland, so coasts of Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Gujarat face surges. Floods hit Assam, West Bengal, Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh hardest, while Tamil Nadu may flood in November-January from the retreating monsoon. Severe drought spans eastern Rajasthan, much of MP, eastern Maharashtra and interior peninsular plateaus.

### Cascades and mitigation

Hazards trigger one another. Earthquakes open fissures, cause liquefaction, set off landslides that dam rivers and divert channels, and on the sea floor generate tsunamis. Landslides are controlled by local geology, slope, land use and rainfall, so they cut roads and railways - NCERT recalls disruptions on routes to Srinagar and the Konkan Railway. Droughts cascade into scarcity of food, fodder and water, cattle deaths, migration and water-borne disease. Mitigation follows the hazard: seismic monitoring, vulnerability maps and earthquake-resistant design; embankments, afforestation and clearing encroachments on floodplains; rainwater harvesting and drought-resistant crops; terrace farming and controlled construction on slopes; and cyclone shelters, embankments and warnings on coasts.

### Worked example

Question: 'Identify the landslide vulnerability zones of India and suggest area-specific mitigation.' Frame: very high - young Himalaya, Andaman-Nicobar, steep wet Western Ghats and Nilgiris, north-east, quake-shaken and heavily constructed areas; high - Himalayan and north-eastern states except Assam plains; moderate-low - Ladakh and Spiti, Aravalis, rain-shadow Ghats, Deccan, mining belts; mitigation - curb roads and dams in high zones, restrict settlements, afforestation, bunds, terracing instead of jhum.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which areas does NCERT list in the extreme drought category?
  - **a:** Most of Rajasthan west of the Aravalis (Marusthali) and the Kachchh region of Gujarat.
- - **q:** Why do tropical cyclones not form within 5 degrees of the equator?
  - **a:** The Coriolis force there is too weak to prevent the low-pressure centre from filling.
- - **q:** How much land did the National Flood Commission identify as flood-prone?
  - **a:** 40 million hectares.

### Common trap

Assuming peninsular India is immune to earthquakes; NCERT cites severe events in Gujarat (1819, 1956, 2001) and Maharashtra (1967, 1993) and a possible fault along the Bhima near Latur and Osmanabad.
