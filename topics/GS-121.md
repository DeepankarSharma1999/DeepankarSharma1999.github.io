### Bridge: Words you need first: parent rock, weathering, humus, leaching

Soil is built from five ingredients: broken rock, dead plant and animal matter, living things, air and water. The broken rock that a soil starts from is called its **parent material**. **Weathering** is the breaking down of rock in place by weather, water and living things (Day 21 lesson GS-099). **Humus** is dark, well-rotted organic matter that sticks to mineral particles; it is what makes topsoil dark and fertile. **Organisms** means plants, animals and microscopic **micro-organisms** such as bacteria and fungi. **Leaching** is the washing of dissolved minerals downward out of the upper soil by water that moves through it. A **horizon** is a layer of soil, parallel to the surface, that differs from the layers above and below in colour, texture or composition. A **profile** is the vertical cross-section from the surface down to the unaltered rock, showing the horizons one under another. **Nutrient** means a chemical element that a plant takes from the soil to grow, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium and sulphur.

### Bridge: Reading sizes, percentages and the pH scale

Soil particles are measured by **diameter** in millimetres (mm): 1 mm is one-thousandth of a metre, and 0.002 mm is two micrometres, too small to see without a microscope. A statement such as 'clay content above 40 per cent' means that out of every 100 parts of the soil's mineral particles by weight, more than 40 parts are clay; the parts of sand, silt and clay always add to 100. The **pH scale** measures acidity. Leong prints the scale as 1 to 14 (the standard scale is 0 to 14), with 7 neutral: below 7 the soil is acidic, above 7 it is alkaline (alkaline means the opposite of acidic, rich in hydroxyl ions). Each step on the real pH scale is a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion strength, so pH 5 is ten times more acidic than pH 6 (standard; the extract does not say this). The words **acidic soil** and **alkaline soil** are exam vocabulary: heavy rain tends to make soil acidic, and dry climates tend to make it alkaline or salty.

### 1. Orientation: soil on one page

*Objective:* See soil as a thin layer shaped by five factors and four regimes, and know what this lesson can and cannot source.

## Soil in one sentence

Soil is the thin outer layer of the earth made of mineral and organic materials, air and water, formed from the underlying rocks and from plant and animal material by physical, chemical and biological processes (Leong p. 209).

## The one idea to carry

A soil is the **result** of five **factors** (climate, parent material, organisms, relief and time) acting through a few **processes** (weathering, humus formation, leaching and eluviation) over a long time. Learn the five factors and four regimes and you can predict most soil questions; the names of soils are labels for the results.

## What soil does (Leong p. 209)

1. It controls how land stores and yields water to rivers, streams and lakes.
2. It supplies building materials such as sand, clay and gravel.
3. It gives mechanical support to all living things and structures, from houses to runways.

### 2. Foundation: what soil is made of

*Objective:* List soil's four constituents and describe the mineral, organic, air and water parts, including particle sizes, clay types and humus.

## Soil versus rock (Leong pp. 209-210)

Soils come from rocks but differ in three ways:

1. Soils are made mostly of **secondary minerals** (clays and oxides formed from the weathering products of the primary rock minerals).
2. Soils hold **active organic matter**: humus, roots and micro-organisms.
3. Soils are distributed in a **regular pattern** over the earth that follows climate, rock, vegetation and relief.

## The four constituents (Leong p. 210)

Soil has **solid materials** (mineral and organic) and **pore spaces**: large pores (**macropores**) normally hold air, small pores (**micropores**) normally hold water. So the four constituents are mineral matter, organic matter, air and water. Leong's figure of a well-drained silty loam shows roughly half the volume as solid and half as pore space, with the organic part small (read from the figure; verify).

## Mineral matter: sizes and clay

Particle classes by diameter (Leong Fig. 15.3): **gravel** above 2.0 mm, **sand** 2.0-0.02 mm, **silt** 0.02-0.002 mm, **clay** below 0.002 mm. Leong uses the International (Atterberg) sand-silt boundary of 0.02 mm; the USDA system uses 0.05 mm. Follow Leong here. Sand and silt are mostly inert quartz; **clay is chemically active** and holds nutrients and moisture. Two clay minerals matter: **smectite** has a weak structure, holds much more moisture and nutrients, **swells when wet and cracks when dry**; **kaolinite** has a stronger structure, holds less, and does not swell and shrink so much. Rock fragments act as a mineral reserve and make tillage harder when abundant.

## Organic matter (Leong pp. 210-211)

Organic matter includes the **litter** of fallen leaves and twigs, the **humus** formed from its decay, and the living **micro-organisms** that cause the decay. Humus forms best with good drainage, air, warmth and water. **Mull humus** is well-decomposed and mixed with mineral particles, typical of agricultural land; **mor humus** is partly decomposed and poorly mixed, common in cool temperate soils where cold or poor drainage slows decay. **Peat** is raw humus (decayed vegetation) formed where it is cold or poorly drained, usually highly acidic except over limestone or chalk.

## Soil air and soil water (Leong p. 211)

Soil air is always saturated with moisture, has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than the atmosphere and varies more. Soil water has three forms:

| Form | Where it sits | Use to plants |
|---|---|---|
| Gravitational (free-draining) | Macropores after rain, draining to the water table | None; it washes nutrients away |
| Capillary | Micropores | Readily available |
| Hygroscopic | Film on clay and humus particles | Available through chemical exchange |

**Field capacity** is the ideal state: micropores full of water, macropores empty. If both fill, the soil is **waterlogged**.

Slips in Leong's wording are listed in the master notes.

### 3. Core: the soil profile and soil properties

*Objective:* Describe the O, A, B and C horizons, eluviation and illuviation, and the physical and chemical properties that decide a soil's behaviour.

## The profile and its horizons (Leong p. 212)

A **profile** is a vertical cross-section of the soil. Its layers are **horizons**, named by letters:

- **O (or Ao):** the organic layer, present where raw humus accumulates on the surface.
- **A:** the surface mineral layer, in contact with air and life, so the zone of maximum chemical and biological activity; dark because of humus. It loses fine humus, clay and silt downward by **eluviation**, so it is called the **eluvial** horizon and is coarser and looser than the layer below.
- **B (subsoil):** receives the fine material, an **illuvial** horizon (the process is **illuviation**); finer, more compact and lighter in colour because it holds less humus.
- **C:** the parent material, the regolith or weathered rock from which the soil forms.

Keep the pair straight: **E**luviation = **E**xit from the layer; **I**lluviation = **I**n to the layer. The usual direction is downward; in arid regions Leong notes that rising capillary water can carry material up instead (p. 215), so test the direction before assuming it.

## Physical properties (Leong pp. 212-213)

- **Colour.** Iron oxides give red, brown and yellow; aluminium and calcium give whitish tones; humus gives black, brown or grey. The final controller is **drainage**: well-drained soils are bright red, orange or brown, poorer drainage turns them yellow and grey, and waterlogged soils look greenish or bluish.
- **Texture.** The relative proportions of sand, silt and clay. It is the most permanent and basic physical property, hardly changed by people, and it affects structure, drainage, moisture and nutrients. The classes (sand, loamy sand, sandy loam, loam, clay-loam, sandy-clay, clay) are in the master notes.
- **Structure.** The arrangement of particles into aggregates called **peds**. It decides **porosity**, hence drainage and aeration, and how well the soil resists erosion.
- **Drainage.** The ease with which water moves through. **Excessively drained** soils (very porous sandy soils or steep slopes) lose water too fast; **freely drained** soils keep enough; **poorly drained** soils have water in macropores as well.

## Humus and chemical properties (Leong p. 214)

Humus stores and supplies nitrogen, phosphorus and sulphur; like clay it carries **negative charges** and so holds positively charged bases (calcium, magnesium, potassium); it holds moisture; it binds particles into structure; and it makes humic acids. Clay and humus also hold hydrogen ions, which are positively charged: the more hydrogen ions they hold, the more **acidic** the soil. A pH of 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic and above 7 alkaline (Leong prints 1 to 14; the standard scale is 0 to 14).

## Why the exam cares

A statement such as 'organic matter reduces the water-holding capacity of soil' can be tested at once: humus attracts and holds moisture, so more organic matter does not drastically reduce water holding. A statement such as 'soil plays no role in the sulphur cycle' can be tested too: humus stores and supplies sulphur.

### 4. Core: the five factors of soil formation and the zonal idea

*Objective:* Explain how climate, parent material, organisms, relief and time shape soil and distinguish zonal, intrazonal and azonal soils.

## 1. Climate (Leong pp. 214-215)

Climate acts through precipitation, temperature and evaporation and is regarded by many as the most important factor, partly because it also shapes the others. It works in three ways: it **weathers** rock into parent materials; it **transports** weathered material by ice, wind and running water to form many parent materials; and it drives the formation of the soil body itself through **weathering**, **humification** (making humus), **eluviation** (movement of fine material from one layer to another) and **leaching** (washing away of dissolved minerals by acidic water). Broadly, the major soil types of the world follow the major climatic zones. Climate also drives accelerated **erosion**.

## 2. Parent material (Leong p. 215)

It is the raw material from which soil forms, and it can pass on texture, stoniness, colour and nutrient content. Two broad classes:

- **Sedentary** (still lying on the rock it came from): **residual** (not moved at all, for example on flat hilltops) and **colluvial** (moved down a slope by gravity).
- **Transported** (moved by water, ice, wind or waves): **alluvial** (rivers), **lacustrine** (lakes), **marine**, **glacial till** (ice) and **aeolian** (wind).

## 3. Organisms (Leong pp. 215-216)

Vegetation holds the soil together against erosion, shields it from the drying effect of strong sun and, most importantly, supplies litter for humus. Micro-organisms form humus and release nutrients, **fix atmospheric nitrogen**, mix particles and open burrows that improve drainage and aeration. Larger animals matter little.

## 4. Relief (Leong pp. 216-217)

Relief mainly **modifies** the other factors and counts most locally. Slope angle, shape and aspect affect depth, texture, stones, drainage, moisture and colour. A **catena** is the sequence of soil types down a hillslope: upper convex slopes are shallow, coarse, stony, dry and light; footslopes are deeper, finer, moister and darker. In temperate latitudes south-facing slopes are warmer and drier with better humus formation. With **altitude**, soils become shallower, colder and poorer in humus and nutrients.

## 5. Time (Leong p. 217)

Soils take hundreds to thousands of years to develop. At first the soil copies its parent material; with age climate and vegetation dominate. Some stay youthful because the rock is very hard, the slope steep and unstable, or the environment too dry or cold for chemical change.

## Zonal, intrazonal and azonal soils

| Class | Meaning | Examples (Leong) |
|---|---|---|
| **Zonal** | Well-developed profile that reflects regional climate and vegetation | Podzol, chernozem, tropical red earth |
| **Intrazonal** | Well-developed, but shaped mainly by local drainage, rock or salt | Hydromorphic (poorly drained), calcimorphic (on limestone), halomorphic (saline) |
| **Azonal** | Weakly developed, immature | Soils of arid lands, mountains, coastal sandy beaches |

## One rule, tested

'Climate decides the soil type' is a good rule for large areas but fails locally: on a hillslope, relief and drainage can override it (a catena); on a limestone ridge, parent material can override it (calcimorphic soil); in a young floodplain, time overrides it (weakly developed, azonal).

### 5. Core: the four soil-forming regimes - podzolization, lateritization, calcification, salinization

*Objective:* For each regime, state the climate, the key condition, the chain of events and the soil that results.

## Two opposing forces (Leong p. 217)

Clay minerals, humus, oxides and nutrient elements **bind** particles into aggregates (peds). **Eluviation** and **leaching** do the opposite and **move** material from one layer to another. Horizons form because these two forces compete, and the balance sets the maturity of the soil. Binding wins where weathering and humus formation are strong but leaching is weak; leaching wins where rain is heavy and cold or poor drainage produces raw humus and humic acids. Four regimes follow (Leong pp. 217-219).

## 1. Podzolization (humid, cool temperate)

Chain: **cold, wet, poorly drained** -> raw humus piles up on the surface -> abundant **organic acids** -> intense leaching just below -> bases and clay are removed and the oxides of iron and aluminium dissolve and move out -> the layer is left rich in silica, **bleached ash-grey** (the name podzol comes from this ash colour) -> the removed material is deposited in the **subsoil**, which is clayey, compact and bright orange, with a thin hard **iron pan** under the grey layer. The process is helped by nutrient-poor litter (conifer needles, moorland grass) and by acidic (granite) or very porous (sand) parent material. Few micro-organisms live in such acid soil, so little mixing occurs and the horizon boundaries are **sharp**.

## 2. Lateritization (hot, humid tropics)

Chain: **heat and rain all year** -> very active micro-organisms -> litter rots fast, so **no raw humus** and no extreme acidity -> hydrolysis forms clay minerals and releases many bases, so the soil is first **alkaline** -> silica dissolves and is removed while the oxides of iron and aluminium stay -> an **excess of oxides over silica**, whatever the parent rock -> oxides gather into nodules and a **laterite horizon** at the level of the fluctuating water table -> once exposed to strong sun it hardens into a **laterite crust**. If aluminium oxides dominate, **bauxite** forms. Contrast with podzolization: both leach intensely, but the tropics lack raw humus and acid, so silica rather than iron is lost.

## 3. Calcification (sub-humid grasslands)

Chain: **light rain** in cool temperate continental interiors (and parts of sub-humid tropical savannas) -> leaching is weak, humus formation strong -> only **sodium and potassium** are washed out -> **calcium and magnesium** move a short way down and become carbonates and sulphates -> a humus-rich, nutrient-rich soil with a lime layer below, sometimes cemented into **calcrete**. The typical soils are the **chernozems**.

## 4. Salinization (arid)

Chain: **evaporation exceeds precipitation** -> almost no leaching, so even sodium and potassium stay -> water moves **upward** and evaporates at the surface -> salts **crystallise on the surface**. It is an advanced form of calcification.

## The regimes in one table

| Regime | Climate | Key condition | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Podzolization | Humid cool temperate | Raw humus and organic acids | Podzol: bleached, acidic, low fertility |
| Lateritization | Hot humid tropics | Fast decay, strong leaching of silica | Laterite: oxides, reddish, crust |
| Calcification | Sub-humid, cool grasslands | Weak leaching, lime layer | Chernozem: dark, rich |
| Salinization | Arid | Evaporation above rainfall | Saline soil, salt crust |

No sharp lines separate the regimes; each fades into the next and gives **intergrades** (Leong p. 219).

### 6. Core: world soils - from the poles to the equator

*Objective:* Name the main zonal, intrazonal and azonal soils in order from the cold poleward side to the tropics and give one trait of each.

The world map of soils (Leong Fig. 15.12) follows the climate and vegetation belts. Read the belts from the poles to the tropics (Leong pp. 221-222), and attach the process from module 5.

## Zonal soils, poleward to tropical

| Belt | Soil | Key traits |
|---|---|---|
| Tundra and sub-arctic | **Organic soils**, **peaty podzols** | Very thick peat on poorly drained or very cold ground |
| Coniferous forest and moor grassland, humid cool temperate | **Podzols** | Highly leached, bleached, acidic; few micro-organisms, sharp horizon boundaries; very low fertility, used for forestry and grazing |
| Equatorward and coastal side of the podzols | **Podzolic soils** | Thinner raw humus, less acidic; podzol features not fully developed |
| Warmer deciduous woodland | **Brown earths** | No sharp horizons, uniformly brown, mull humus; fairly good arable soils, improved by liming and fertiliser |
| Semi-arid continental interior grasslands, cool temperate | **Chernozems** | Thick humus-rich surface layer, abundant nutrients; best for grain, especially wheat |
| Tropical savannas | **Black earths** (similar to chernozems) | Parts of the tropical savannas |
| Humid tropical forest | **Tropical red (or yellow) earths**, also called **ferrallitic** soils | Uniformly red, or yellow in the wettest places; iron oxides spread through the profile; kaolinite clay |
| Drier savanna side | **Ferruginous tropical soils, laterite soils, latosols** | Iron oxides gather in nodules and may harden to a laterite horizon |

**Tropical soils** are deeply weathered and intensely leached, so they hold few weatherable minerals and little plant nutrient; kaolinite holds little moisture or nutrient, so fertility depends on **humus** (Leong p. 221). That is why a tropical rain forest, in spite of its lush growth, does not stand on a nutrient-rich soil.

## Intrazonal soils (local factors win)

- **Hydromorphic (wet) soils** on floodplains, river valleys, deltas, coastal and lake plains, and in depressions in arid lands.
- **Terra rosa** (red clayey soils with abundant iron oxides, developed on limestone in the Mediterranean regions) and, in general, **calcimorphic** (lime-rich) soils on limestone, chalk or marl.
- **Saline (halomorphic) soils** in arid lands and coastal areas: in deserts salts are carried up by capillary water and crystallise as the water evaporates; on coasts the salt comes from marine water.

## Azonal soils

Weakly developed soils of the **arid lands, mountain regions and coastal sandy beaches**.

## How to use the map

Do not memorise the map; use the clue-to-soil route: (1) temperature and rain -> regime; (2) vegetation -> soil name; (3) local override: poor drainage -> hydromorphic, limestone -> terra rosa, aridity or coast -> saline. A forest question is also a soil question: coniferous forest means podzol, deciduous woodland means brown earth, grassland interior means chernozem, tropical forest means red or yellow ferrallitic earth. Test the rule on edge cases: moor grassland also gives podzols; tropical savanna gives black earths or ferruginous soils, not ferrallitic soils; limestone under Mediterranean climate gives terra rosa whatever the vegetation.

Leong's **black earths** and **tropical red earths** resemble India's black and red soils but have their own names and parent materials (module 7): use Leong's names for world answers, Indian names for Indian answers.

### 7. Core: India's soils - what the extracts say and the standard classes (verify)

*Objective:* Place India's soil classes on the map using parent material and climate, and separate NCERT-sourced facts from standard knowledge marked verify.

## What the staged NCERT extracts say

- **Northern plains** (kegy102 pp. 5-6): formed by alluvium of the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra. From north to south: the **Bhabar** (a narrow belt where streams drop boulders and may disappear), the **Tarai** (streams re-emerge, marshy), then the alluvial plains of **Bhangar** (older alluvium) and **Khadar** (newer alluvium). The plain has a fertile alluvial soil cover that supports wheat, rice, sugarcane and jute and so a large population.
- **Peninsular plateau** (kegy102 p. 6): the western and north-western part has an **emphatic presence of black soil**. The north-west has a complex relief of **ravines** (Chambal, Bhind, Morena); kegy103 p. 6 calls the Chambal famous for badland topography.
- **Meghalaya plateau** (kegy102 p. 7): maximum south-west monsoon rainfall, a highly eroded surface; Cherrapunji has a bare rocky surface with no permanent vegetation.
- **Indian desert** (kegy102 pp. 7-8): rainfall below 150 mm a year; the surface is carved by weathering and wind; streams end in lakes and playas whose **brackish water** is the main source of salt.

Everything below this line is **standard knowledge, marked verify**.

## The standard Indian classes

| Class | How it forms (link to Leong) | Typical region (verify) | Key trait |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alluvial** | Transported **alluvial** parent material from rivers | Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra plains, coastal plains and deltas | Fine, fertile; Khadar new, Bhangar old (NCERT) |
| **Black** (regur, black cotton) | Weathering of **basalt lava** (Deccan Trap) | Western and north-western Peninsular plateau (NCERT) | Clay-rich; holds moisture, swells when wet and cracks when dry; cotton |
| **Red and yellow** | Weathering of ancient crystalline rock (granite, gneiss); iron oxides give red colour in well-drained ground | Eastern and southern Peninsula | Light, porous, low in humus |
| **Laterite** | **Lateritization**: heavy seasonal rain, strong leaching, oxides left behind | Western Ghats and parts of the Eastern Ghats, Chotanagpur, Meghalaya | Hardens on exposure; poor in nutrients; plantation crops |
| **Arid (desert)** | Low rain, wind and physical weathering; salts present | Rajasthan desert | Sandy, low humus; productive with irrigation |
| **Saline and alkaline** | **Salinization** by evaporation; marine influence on coasts; poor drainage under irrigation | Arid and semi-arid plains, coasts | White salt crust, poor crops |
| **Peaty and marshy** | Poor drainage and heavy humus accumulation (peat) | Waterlogged coastal and delta areas; heavy-rainfall humid areas (north Bihar, coastal Odisha, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala) | Heavy, black, rich in organic matter; acidic in Kerala, alkaline in places |
| **Forest and mountain** | Altitude and slope: shallower, colder, poorer in humus at height | Himalayan and other mountain slopes | Varies by altitude |

## Why the table is a chain, not a list

1. **Alluvial:** river -> sediment from the Himalayan and Peninsular rivers -> flat plain -> fertile fine soil.
2. **Black:** lava -> weathering -> clay-rich with swelling clay (smectite type in Leong's terms) -> moisture retention in a hot semi-arid region (the Day 17 lesson GS-123 connects this soil to cotton, sorghum, soyabean and pulses).
3. **Red:** old crystalline rock + warm climate + iron -> red, because well-drained soils with iron oxides are red (module 3).
4. **Laterite:** hot, wet season + leaching -> silica lost, oxides stay (module 5).
5. **Saline:** dry heat + poor drainage -> salts rise and crystallise (module 5).

- Classifications differ between agencies; no area percentage is given here. Crops, nutrient profiles and local names are in the master notes (verify).
- Students often merge **Khadar/Bhangar** (alluvial sub-types) with the **Bhabar/Tarai** belts (zones). They are different things.

### 8. Master: soil in trouble - deterioration, desiccation, erosion and conservation

*Objective:* Explain the three dangers to soil once vegetation is cleared, the forms of water erosion, and the conservation measures, with Indian examples from the NCERT extracts.

This module is a deferred deep dive. It is also the resource-use side of the syllabus (Mains GS I-11): soil is a natural resource that can be lost.

## The three dangers (Leong p. 222)

Soil is at its best under natural vegetation. When people clear it, three dangers follow:

1. **Deterioration:** a fall in fertility as humus and nutrients decline and the structure breaks down, usually from repeated cropping without replenishment. Effects: lower yields and quality, more weeds, less resistance of crops to pests and disease, more erosion. Prevention: long **fallow**, **manuring**, balanced chemical fertiliser.
2. **Desiccation:** progressive drying out and hardening from long exposure to strong sun. Signs: ironstone gravel, lateritic crusts and **claypans** (hardened clay layers) in the profile; the hard surface sheds water, so runoff and erosion rise. Prevention: keep ground cover, plant cover crops, control grazing and bush burning.
3. **Accelerated erosion:** rapid removal of soil by wind or water because of human action such as deforestation, cultivation, overgrazing, burning and quarrying.

## Water erosion in three forms (Leong p. 223)

| Form | What happens | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Splash** | Raindrops break soil aggregates and throw particles out | Bigger drops do more work |
| **Sheet** | Runoff removes thin layers over broad, gentle slopes | Most widespread and often unnoticed; removes the finest humus and clay and their nutrients, so most damaging to fertility |
| **Gully** | Deep channels cut into strong relief or loose sandy soil | More devastating where it occurs, but less widespread than sheet erosion |

Gullies expose subsoil to the sun, which then hardens into claypans and laterite crusts. Sediment from gullies fills rivers, lakes and reservoirs, causing floods and, in extreme cases, danger to dams.

## Conservation (Leong p. 223)

Afforestation (protects against raindrops and runoff, acts as a windbreak); cover crops; **crop rotation**; no cultivation on steep unstable slopes; **contour ploughing, terracing and strip cropping** on strong relief; control of grazing and burning. NCERT's forest policy aims include checking soil erosion, extension of desert land and floods and droughts (kegy105 p. 5), and social forestry and agroforestry restore tree cover on barren land (kegy105 pp. 5-6).

## Indian pictures in the NCERT extracts

- **Chambal, Bhind and Morena ravines** (kegy102 p. 6; kegy103 p. 6): the standard gully-erosion landscape.
- **Meghalaya and Cherrapunji** (kegy102 p. 7): heavy rain on a plateau leaves a bare rocky surface.
- **Kosi** (kegy103 p. 4) and **Brahmaputra** (p. 7): heavy sediment from the upper catchments, then floods and channel shifting in the plains.
- **Desert** (kegy102 pp. 7-8): wind and physical weathering carve the surface.

## Irrigation and salinisation (standard; verify)

Under salinization, evaporation pulls salt-bearing water upward and salts crystallise on the surface (module 5). Irrigation without drainage can do the same: water evaporates, the dissolved salts stay, and the water table may rise (Day 17 lesson GS-123 lists salinisation and waterlogging among irrigation problems; Day 19 lesson GS-125 covers irrigation projects).

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Soil: the thin outer layer of the earth made of mineral and organic materials, air and water, formed from rocks and plant and animal material by physical, chemical and biological processes (Leong p. 209).
- Parent material: the raw material from which a soil forms; sedentary (residual, colluvial) or transported (alluvial, lacustrine, marine, glacial till, aeolian).
- Horizon: a soil layer differing from those above and below; profile: the vertical cross-section; O, A, B and C are the main horizons.
- Eluviation: movement of material out of a layer; illuviation: deposition of that material in a lower layer; leaching: washing away of dissolved minerals.
- Humification: formation of humus by decomposition of litter; mull humus (mixed, well decomposed) versus mor humus (partly decomposed, not mixed); peat (raw humus in cold or poorly drained ground).
- Texture: relative proportions of sand, silt and clay; structure: arrangement into peds; porosity: pore space; field capacity: micropores full, macropores empty.
- Zonal soil: well developed and reflecting regional climate; intrazonal: shaped by local drainage, parent rock or salt; azonal: weakly developed.
- Catena: the sequence of soil types down a hillslope.
- Salinization: salts crystallising at the surface where evaporation exceeds precipitation; calcification: lime accumulation under weak leaching; lateritization: oxide enrichment in the hot humid tropics; podzolization: bleaching by organic acids in the cool humid zone.
- Deterioration, desiccation and accelerated erosion: the three dangers when vegetation is cleared (Leong p. 222).

### Notes: Mineral particle sizes and the four constituents (Leong pp. 210-211; recomputed ordering)

- Sizes by diameter, coarse to fine: gravel, sand, silt, clay. The USDA system puts the sand-silt boundary at 0.05 mm, the International (Atterberg) system, which Leong follows, at 0.02 mm: a system difference, not a slip.

### Notes: Horizons compared (Leong p. 212)

### Notes: Factors of soil formation (Leong pp. 214-217)

- Five factors; climate is called the most important by many, but relief and parent material dominate locally.

### Notes: The four regimes as cause-effect chains (Leong pp. 217-219)

- Podzolization: cold + wet + poor drainage -> raw humus -> organic acids -> intense leaching -> bleached grey layer, orange subsoil, iron pan -> low fertility.
- Lateritization: hot + wet + active micro-organisms -> fast decay, no raw humus -> clay and bases released, alkaline start -> silica lost, iron and aluminium oxides stay -> nodules, laterite crust, bauxite where aluminium dominates.
- Calcification: light rain on continental grasslands -> weak leaching -> sodium and potassium washed out, calcium and magnesium retained as carbonates -> humus-rich chernozem, calcrete.
- Salinization: evaporation above rainfall -> no leaching, upward water movement -> salts crystallise at the surface -> saline soil.

### Notes: Soil degradation and conservation (Leong pp. 222-223)

- Overgrazing, burning, bare ground in strong sun -> desiccation, claypans and crusts -> prevent by cover, cover crops, control of grazing and burning.
- Deforestation, cultivation, overgrazing -> accelerated erosion by wind or water (splash, sheet, gully) -> prevent by trees, cover crops, rotation, contour ploughing, terracing, strip cropping.
- Sediment from erosion -> silting of rivers, lakes and reservoirs -> floods and risk to dams.

### Last-week summary

- Soil = mineral matter, organic matter, air, water; differs from rock by secondary minerals, active organic matter and a regular pattern. Factors: climate, parent material, organisms, relief, time.
- A eluvial (loses fines), B illuvial (gains them), C parent material. Humus supplies N, P, S, holds bases and moisture, binds particles, forms humic acids. Smectite swells and cracks; kaolinite hardly.
- Podzol: cool, wet, raw humus, acids, bleached grey layer, orange subsoil, iron pan. Laterite: hot, wet, silica lost, iron and aluminium oxides stay, crust; bauxite.
- Calcification: weak leaching, lime layer, chernozem. Salinization: evaporation above rain, surface salt. Zonal = regional climate; intrazonal = local drainage, rock, salt; azonal = weakly developed.
- Dangers: deterioration, desiccation, accelerated erosion; sheet erosion most widespread, gully most devastating locally. NCERT: black soil emphatic on the western and north-western Peninsular plateau.
