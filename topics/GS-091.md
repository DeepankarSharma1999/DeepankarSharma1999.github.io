## GS-091: Regional language literary traditions

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Regional language literary traditions and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### How languages became literary

NCERT links India's linguistic plurality to five language families. Sanskrit was standardised by Panini, but people spoke Prakrits; Pali carried Buddhist texts and Apabhramshas became the base of the modern regional languages. Tamil is the oldest Dravidian literary language; Kannada has the ninth-century Kavirajamarga and tenth-century Jaina poets Pampa, Ponna and Ranna, Pampa's Ramayana shaping later poets. In Kerala, the Chera rulers inscribed in Malayalam while drawing on Sanskrit: temple theatre borrowed epic stories, and a fourteenth-century grammar and poetics text was composed in Manipravalam, mixing Sanskrit and the regional language. Each case shows a regional language growing beside, not against, Sanskrit.

### Bengal as a case study

Early inscriptions suggest Bengal's people did not originally speak Sanskritic languages. Trade with Magadha from the fourth–third centuries BCE, Gupta settlement of Brahmanas, and Pala and Sultanate rule shaped Bengali; Xuan Zang found Sanskrit-related languages across Bengal in the seventh century. Under the Mughals, Persian was the administrative language while Bengali grew as the regional one, unified around the western spoken form. Its literature had a Sanskrit-linked written stream — epic translations, Mangalakavyas on local deities, Chaitanya biographies — and an oral stream of Nath songs, Dharma Thakur stories, folk tales and ballads that is hard to date because it was rarely written down.

### Bhakti, confluence and modern print

Medieval regional literatures were energised by bhakti: Alvar and Nayanar hymns in Tamil, Basavanna's vachanas with Akka Mahadevi and Allama Prabhu in Kannada, Jnanadeva, Namadeva and Tukaram in Marathi, Chaitanya in Bengali, Surdas in Hindi, Mirabai in Rajasthani and Narsinha Mehta in Gujarati. Lal Ded and Habba Khatoon wrote mystical Kashmiri verse. Urdu emerged as a confluence of Persian and Hindustani, bringing qasida, marsiya and masnavi forms. In the colonial era printing, journalism and Macaulay's 1835 Minute changed the field; English writing began with Derozio and Michael Madhusudan Dutt, and Rabindranath Tagore, a Bengali writer, became India's Nobel laureate in literature.

### Worked example

Q: 'Regional literary traditions were shaped by interaction rather than isolation.' Illustrate. Frame: Prakrit–Apabhramsha base; Malayalam and Manipravalam (Sanskrit + local); Kannada Jaina poets; Bengali's two streams and its mixed vocabulary; bhakti across Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Hindi, Gujarati, Kashmiri; Urdu as confluence; print and English in the colonial period.

### Recall

- - **q:** What does Manipravalam mean?
  - **a:** 'Diamonds and corals' — a style mixing Sanskrit and the regional language (Malayalam).
- - **q:** Which is the earliest extant Kannada work per NCERT?
  - **a:** Kavirajamarga, ninth century.
- - **q:** What are Mangalakavyas?
  - **a:** Auspicious Bengali poems about local deities.

### Common trap

Assuming a region's language is ancient and fixed. NCERT shows Bengali emerged through contact with Magadha, Gupta-era Brahmana settlement and later mixing with tribal, Persian and European words, and that regional boundaries themselves keep changing.
