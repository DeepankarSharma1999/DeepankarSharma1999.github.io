### Bridge: Rural and urban governance: the vocabulary

**Government** in India works at three constitutional levels: the **Union**, the **State** and the **local** level. **Local government** means elected bodies that look after local affairs close to the people. The subject of 'local government' is in the **State List**, so each State makes its own laws on it; the 73rd and 74th Amendments then added common constitutional rules.

The **rural** system is called **Panchayati Raj**. A **panchayat** (traditionally 'an assembly of five') is a rural local body. It works at three levels: the **village** level (**Gram Panchayat**), the **intermediate** level (a body for a block, taluka or mandal, called Panchayat Samiti, Mandal Parishad, Taluka or Block Panchayat in different States) and the **district** level (**Zila Parishad** or Zilla Panchayat). A **block** or **taluka** is a sub-division of a district.

The **urban** system consists of municipalities (**Nagarpalikas**): city corporations, town municipalities and nagar panchayats. The **74th Amendment** created it (cross-reference GS-196). NCERT tells you how the Census separates the two: an area is urban if it has a minimum population of 5,000, at least 75 per cent of male workers in non-farm jobs and a density of at least 400 persons per square kilometre. Everything else is treated as rural.

One more word, **Schedule**: a list at the end of the Constitution. The **Eleventh Schedule** lists the 29 subjects for panchayats; the Twelfth lists the subjects for municipalities.

### Bridge: Gram Sabha versus Gram Panchayat

These two names sound alike and examiners exploit that.

The **Gram Sabha** is the **village assembly**. According to the 73rd Amendment (Article 243A), it consists of persons whose names are in the **electoral roll** of the village that falls within the area of a panchayat at the village level. So every adult voter in the village is a member. Its powers and functions are whatever the **State legislature** decides. Laxmikanth calls it the foundation of the system; the Singhvi Committee called it the embodiment of direct democracy.

The **Gram Panchayat** is the **elected council** at the village level. Its members are chosen **directly** by the people. It carries out day-to-day work such as water, roads and sanitation, and it makes plans for the village. Think of the Gram Sabha as the **general meeting of the whole village** and the Gram Panchayat as the **executive committee** that the meeting elects, answerable to the meeting.

In **Scheduled Areas** under PESA (module 7) the Gram Sabha has stronger powers: it approves development plans before the panchayat takes them up and issues a certificate that funds were properly used.

Example: a Gram Panchayat proposes a new road. In a village with a strong Gram Sabha, the voters meet, discuss and approve; in a weak one, the panchayat acts alone and the villagers learn about it later. This is why Mains answers say 'Gram Sabha is the heart of grassroots democracy'.

### 1. Orientation: why local government, and why it came late

*Objective:* Explain in three sentences why a democracy needs elected local bodies and why the Constitution gave them weak protection at first.

**Panchayati Raj** is the system of **rural local self-government**. By Laxmikanth's account it is set up in every State by State Acts to build democracy at the grass-roots and is entrusted with rural development. It was given constitutional protection by the **73rd Amendment Act, 1992**. If the words 'local government' or 'Schedule' are new, read bridge modules B1 and B2 first.

**Why local government?** NCERT's argument is simple. A democracy is not complete if elected governments exist only at the Centre and in the States. Local government is government **closest to ordinary citizens**: people can approach it quickly and cheaply, it uses **local knowledge**, and it ensures both **participation** and **accountability**. Tasks that can be done locally should be left to local people and their representatives. NCERT's stories make the point: a woman Sarpanch in Madhya Pradesh renovated water tanks and a school building, and a Tamil Nadu panchayat went to the High Court to protect its village land and won.

**Why did it come late?** The original Constitution kept 'local government' in the **State List**, and mentioned village panchayats only in **Article 40**, a **Directive Principle** (a non-justiciable policy guide: courts do not enforce it). NCERT gives two reasons for the weak treatment. First, the turmoil of **Partition** produced a strong unitary inclination, and Nehru feared that extreme localism would threaten national unity. Second, a powerful voice in the Constituent Assembly led by **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** felt that village society, divided by faction and caste, would defeat the purpose of rural self-government. Others, inspired by **Mahatma Gandhi's** vision of self-sustaining village republics, wanted panchayats as the base of democracy. The compromise was Article 40.

**What went wrong before 1992?** NCERT records that many States did not set up elected local bodies; some bodies lacked powers and money; some were dissolved and handed to officials; elections were postponed again and again. The 73rd Amendment tried to repair exactly these four problems: no guarantee of existence, no regular elections, no powers and no money.

### 2. Evolution I: Community Development, Balwant Rai Mehta and the first generation

*Objective:* Describe the Community Development Programme, the Balwant Rai Mehta recommendations, the first States and the pattern differences.

**Before the Committee.** In **1952** the Government launched the **Community Development Programme** (CDP), followed by the **National Extension Service** in **1953**, to promote development with people's participation.

**The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee.** In **January 1957** the Government appointed a committee, chaired by **Balwant Rai G. Mehta**, to examine how the CDP and the National Extension Service were working. It submitted its report in **November 1957** and recommended **'democratic decentralisation'**, which became known as Panchayati Raj. Its recommendations, as the source lists them:
1. A **three-tier** system: **Gram Panchayat** (village), **Panchayat Samiti** (block) and **Zila Parishad** (district), linked by indirect elections.
2. The village panchayat to be **directly elected**; the samiti and the parishad **indirectly elected**.
3. All planning and development work to be entrusted to these bodies.
4. The **samiti as the executive body**; the **zila parishad** advisory, coordinating and supervisory.
5. The **District Collector** to be the chairman of the zila parishad.
6. A **genuine transfer** of power and responsibility, with **adequate resources**, and further devolution in the future.

The **National Development Council** accepted these in **January 1958** but did not insist on a rigid pattern: States were to evolve patterns suited to local conditions, provided the basic principles stayed the same.

**First States.** **Rajasthan** was first. The Prime Minister inaugurated the scheme on **2 October 1959** at **Nagaur**. **Andhra Pradesh** followed in 1959. NCERT adds that Gujarat and Maharashtra adopted elected local bodies around 1960. Most States had created panchayati raj institutions by the mid-1960s.

**One idea, many patterns.** Laxmikanth records that States differed in tiers, the relative position of samiti and parishad, tenure, composition, functions and finances. For example: **Rajasthan** three tiers; **Tamil Nadu** two tiers; **West Bengal** four tiers. In the **Rajasthan-Andhra Pradesh** pattern the **panchayat samiti** was powerful because the **block** was the unit of planning; in the **Maharashtra-Gujarat** pattern the **zila parishad** was powerful because the **district** was the unit. Some States also set up **nyaya panchayats** (village judicial bodies for petty civil and criminal cases).

**Decline.** The milestone table in Laxmikanth marks **1964 to 1977** as the decline of the **first-generation** institutions. NCERT gives the reasons: many bodies lacked powers and money and depended on State and Central governments; some States did not hold elections; some bodies were dissolved and handed to officers; indirect elections were common. Hence the next phase of committees (module 3).

### 3. Evolution II: the later committees and the road to 1992

*Objective:* Match each committee with its main idea and trace the bills that led to the 73rd Amendment.

After 1960 many study teams looked at panchayats; Laxmikanth's Table 38.1 lists thirteen. The ones that matter for exams:

| **Ashok Mehta** (appointed Dec 1977; report Aug 1978; 132 recommendations) | Replace the three tiers with **two**: **zila parishad** and below it a **mandal panchayat** (a group of villages, population 15,000 to 20,000). The district as the first point of decentralisation, the zila parishad as executive body for district planning; **political parties** to take part officially; compulsory **taxation powers**; regular **social audit**; no supersession (and if unavoidable, elections within **six months**); **nyaya panchayats** separate; seats for SC/ST by population; a minister for panchayati raj in the State; **constitutional recognition**. |

| **G.V.K. Rao** (appointed 1985, report 1986) | Development had become **bureaucratised** and divorced from panchayats, summed up as **'grass without roots'**. | **L.M. Singhvi** (1986) | A new chapter in the Constitution to give panchayats **constitutional recognition**; regular, free and fair elections; **Gram Sabha** as the embodiment of direct democracy; more finance; **judicial tribunals** for election and related disputes. |

**Notice the pattern.** The same demands recur: constitutional status, fixed term, reservation, a finance commission and an election commission. The 73rd Amendment is, in effect, the sum of these demands.

**The bills.**
1. **64th Amendment Bill**: introduced in the Lok Sabha in **July 1989** by the Rajiv Gandhi government; passed by the Lok Sabha in August 1989; **defeated in the Rajya Sabha** (the table gives 15 October 1989). The Opposition said it strengthened **centralisation** in the federal system.
2. **1990 bill** (National Front government of V.P. Singh): introduced in September 1990 after a conference of Chief Ministers; it **lapsed** when the government fell.
3. **1991 bill** (P.V. Narasimha Rao government): modified to **delete the controversial aspects**, introduced in September 1991, sent to a Joint Select Committee and passed by the **Lok Sabha on 22 December 1992 and the Rajya Sabha on 23 December 1992**. It was approved by 17 State assemblies, received the President's assent on **20 April 1993** and came into force on **24 April 1993** as the **73rd Amendment Act, 1992**. The source's table also shows that the 74th (urban) Amendment took effect on 1 June 1993.

### 4. The 73rd Amendment: Part IX, three tiers, Gram Sabha, compulsory and voluntary provisions

*Objective:* State the structure of Part IX and separate compulsory from voluntary provisions.

**What the Act did.** The **73rd Amendment Act, 1992** added **Part IX, 'The Panchayats'**, with **Articles 243 to 243O**, and a new **Eleventh Schedule** of **29** subjects (Article 243G). It gave practical shape to **Article 40** and gave panchayats **constitutional status**. Because Part IX is a justiciable part of the Constitution, the States are **obliged** to adopt the new system; neither the formation of panchayats nor regular elections depends any more on the will of the State government. States had to bring their laws into line within **one year** of **24 April 1993**, and most passed conformity Acts in 1993 and 1994. In Laxmikanth's phrase, the Act turns representative democracy into **participatory democracy**.

**Articles at a glance** (from the source table): 243 definitions; **243A** Gram Sabha; **243B** constitution of panchayats; **243C** composition; **243D** reservation of seats; **243E** duration; **243F** disqualifications; **243G** powers, authority and responsibilities; **243H** taxes and funds; **243I** finance commission; **243J** audit; **243K** elections; **243L** Union Territories; **243M** areas where the Part does not apply; **243N** continuance of existing laws; **243-O** bar to interference by courts in electoral matters.

**Three tiers.** Every State has panchayats at the **village, intermediate and district** levels. The Act defines the terms: a *panchayat* is an institution of self-government for rural areas; a *village* is one specified by the Governor by notification (or a group of villages); the *intermediate level* is between village and district, specified by the Governor. **Exception:** a State with a population **not exceeding 20 lakh** may skip the intermediate tier. NCERT adds that the intermediate body is called Mandal, Block or Taluka Panchayat. States use different local names for the tiers (see the master notes).

**Gram Sabha** (Article 243A): all persons registered on the electoral roll of the village; it exercises the powers and performs the functions that the **State legislature** determines.

**Compulsory (mandatory) provisions**, which State laws must contain:
1. Gram Sabha organised in a village or group of villages.
2. Panchayats at the village, intermediate and district levels.
3. **Direct** elections to all seats at all three levels.
4. **Indirect** election of the chairperson at the intermediate and district levels.
5. Voting rights of the chairperson and members.
6. **21 years** as the minimum age to contest.
7. Reservation of seats and chairperson posts for **SC/ST**.
8. **One-third** of seats and chairperson posts for **women**.
9. A **five-year** term and fresh elections within **six months** of supersession.
10. A **State Election Commission**.
11. A **State Finance Commission** every five years.

**Voluntary (discretionary) provisions**, which States may adopt: powers for the Gram Sabha; the way the village chairperson is elected; representation of lower-tier chairpersons and of **MPs and MLAs** in upper tiers; reservation for **backward classes**; making panchayats institutions of self-government; devolution of the **29 functions** and of planning; granting **taxation powers**; assigning State taxes; grants-in-aid; and funds. The voluntary list lets States take local factors into account, but notice that **most of the money and function provisions are voluntary**. That is the root of the ground-reality problems in module 8.

### 5. Elections, reservation, term, disqualification and the State Election Commission

*Objective:* Apply the rules on election, reservation, term, age, disqualification and the State Election Commission to short scenarios.

**Election of members and chairpersons.** All members of panchayats at all three levels are elected **directly** by the people. The chairperson at the **intermediate and district** levels is elected **indirectly**, by and from the elected members; at the **village** level the chairperson is elected as the State legislature decides. Chairpersons and members all have the right to vote in meetings.

**Reservation.** For **SC and ST**: seats at every level in **proportion to their population** in the panchayat area; the State legislature also provides for chairperson posts for SC/ST. For **women**: **not less than one-third** of the seats, counting seats reserved for SC/ST women, and not less than one-third of the chairperson posts at each level. For **backward classes**: optional, by State law. Standard knowledge (verify): in *K. Krishna Murthy v Union of India* (2010) the Supreme Court held that Article 243D does not itself require OBC reservation in local bodies and that the 50 per cent ceiling and other limits must be respected; in *Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v State of Maharashtra* (2021) it laid down a 'triple test' for OBC reservation in local bodies: set up a dedicated commission to study backwardness, specify the proportion of seats on its recommendation, and ensure that total reservation (SC, ST and OBC) does not exceed 50 per cent of seats. NCERT explains the practical result: a seat can be reserved for a woman and for an SC or ST candidate at the same time, so a Sarpanch might have to be a Dalit or Adivasi woman. *The statements on the 50 per cent reservation for women introduced by many States by their own laws are standard knowledge outside the sources; verify the list of States. A 2009 Bill to raise the constitutional minimum from one-third to 50 per cent lapsed (verify).* The Act's tie of SC/ST reservation to the period in **Article 334** (printed as ending in 2020) needs checking: that period was reported extended by a later amendment (the 104th Amendment, 2019, to January 2030; verify). Note the link: **Article 243D(5)** ties the duration of SC/ST seat reservation in panchayats to the period specified in Article 334, so the extension matters here as well; verify. Arunachal Pradesh is exempt from SC reservation (no SC population), added by the **83rd Amendment Act, 2000**.

**Term (Article 243E).** A panchayat has a **five-year** term. It can be dissolved earlier, but fresh elections must be completed **before the end of the five years**, or, if dissolved, **within six months** of dissolution. If the remaining period would be **less than six months**, no election need be held. A body elected after dissolution serves **only for the remainder** of the original term.

**Worked example.** A district panchayat is dissolved after **3 years**. The remainder is 2 years, which exceeds six months. So the election must be completed within six months, and the new body serves only until the old term would have ended. Had it been dissolved after **4 years 8 months**, the remainder (4 months) is under six months, so no election is needed.

**Disqualification (Article 243F).** A person is disqualified if disqualified under the law for **State legislature elections** or under a **State law**. But a person is **not disqualified for being under 25 if he has reached 21**. All disqualification questions go to the authority the State legislature decides.

**State Election Commission (Article 243K).** Superintendence, direction and control of **electoral rolls and elections** to panchayats are vested in the State Election Commission, headed by a **State Election Commissioner appointed by the Governor**. His conditions of service and tenure are decided by the Governor, but he can be **removed only as a High Court judge is** (so the Governor cannot remove him at will; Laxmikanth's note explains this) and his conditions cannot be varied to his disadvantage. NCERT points out that this is an independent office, **not under the Election Commission of India**.

**Courts and elections (Article 243-O).** Laws about **delimitation of constituencies** and **allotment of seats** cannot be questioned in any court; an election can be challenged only by an **election petition** to the authority and in the manner the State law provides.

### 6. Powers, the Eleventh Schedule, finances, finance commissions and audit

*Objective:* Explain how panchayats get functions and money, and link the State Finance Commission to the Central Finance Commission.

**Powers and functions (Article 243G).** The State legislature **may** give panchayats the powers needed to function as **institutions of self-government**, including devolution of responsibility for (a) preparing **plans for economic development and social justice** and (b) implementing schemes for them, including those on the **29 matters of the Eleventh Schedule**. NCERT stresses that the transfer depends entirely on **State legislation**: each State decides how many of the 29 subjects to hand over. The Schedule lists agriculture, minor irrigation and watershed development, animal husbandry, fisheries, social forestry, small-scale industries, rural housing, drinking water, roads, rural electrification, poverty alleviation programmes, education, health and sanitation, women and child development, social welfare, the public distribution system and maintenance of community assets, among others (full list in the master notes).

**Money (Article 243H).** The State legislature may (a) **authorise a panchayat to levy, collect and keep taxes, duties, tolls and fees**; (b) **assign** to it taxes levied by the State; (c) provide **grants-in-aid** from the State's Consolidated Fund; and (d) provide for **funds** to credit all panchayat money. All four are **voluntary** for States.

**State Finance Commission (Article 243-I).** The **Governor** constitutes a finance commission **after every five years**; the first commission was due within one year of the commencement of the 1992 Amendment (the 73rd), and then every five years (verify). It reviews the financial position of panchayats and recommends (1) the principles for **distributing between the State and the panchayats the net proceeds** of taxes, duties, tolls and fees, and for sharing among the panchayats at all levels; the taxes that may be assigned to panchayats; and **grants-in-aid** from the State's Consolidated Fund; (2) measures to improve panchayat finances; and (3) any other matter the Governor refers. The State legislature decides its composition. The Governor places the recommendations with an **action-taken report** before the State legislature. NCERT's view: this makes the allocation of funds to local government **less of a political matter**.

**Link to the Central Finance Commission (see GS-193).** The Union Finance Commission, under Article 280, also suggests the **measures needed to augment a State's Consolidated Fund** so that States can supplement panchayat resources, **on the basis of the State Finance Commission's recommendations**. So the chain is: State Finance Commission examines panchayat needs, and the Central Finance Commission considers the State's resources to support them.

**Audit (Article 243J).** The State legislature may make provisions for **panchayat accounts** and their **audit**.

**Where the money comes from.** The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (summarised in Laxmikanth) lists five sources: (i) **grants from the Union** on Central Finance Commission recommendations; (ii) **devolution from the State** on State Finance Commission recommendations; (iii) loans and grants from the State; (iv) **programme-specific allocations** under centrally sponsored schemes and additional central assistance; (v) **own resources**, tax and non-tax. Its diagnosis: **own resources are weak**, grants are heavily **scheme-specific**, States are not keen to devolve funds, and in key Eleventh Schedule subjects the **State government** itself implements and spends. Result: panchayats have **responsibility without adequate resources**.

**Prelims facts to verify (standard knowledge, not in the extracts):** the Ministry of Panchayati Raj was set up in 2004; National Panchayati Raj Day is 24 April (the 73rd Amendment came into force on 24 April 1993); the Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) is the annual panchayat plan prepared with the Gram Sabha; Union Finance Commission grants to panchayats are partly tied and partly untied (link GS-193).

### 7. Exempted areas, Union Territories and the PESA Act 1996

*Objective:* State which areas are exempt from Part IX, how Parliament extended it by PESA, and the central features of PESA.

**Exempted States and areas (Article 243M).** Part IX does **not** apply to the States of **Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram**, and certain other areas: (a) **Scheduled Areas** and **tribal areas** in the States (the Fifth and Sixth Schedule regions); (b) the **hill areas of Manipur** for which district councils exist; and (c) the **Darjeeling district** of West Bengal, which has the Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council. But **Parliament may extend** Part IX to the Scheduled and tribal areas with exceptions and modifications. **Union Territories**: Part IX applies, and the **President** may direct exceptions and modifications.

**Why an Extension Act?** NCERT notes that many Adivasi communities traditionally manage forests and small water bodies as common resources. A plain copy of the general model could weaken those customary systems, so Parliament passed the **Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996**, known as **PESA** or the **Extension Act** (assent and commencement both on **24 December 1996**, per Laxmikanth). Laxmikanth (2019) lists ten States with **Fifth Schedule** areas: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha and Rajasthan, and says each enacted compliance legislation. *Verify the present list.*

**Objectives**, in brief: extend Part IX with modifications; give **self-rule** to the bulk of the tribal population; make the **Gram Sabha** the nucleus of village activity; evolve an administrative framework consistent with **traditional practices**; safeguard **tribal customs**; give panchayats powers suited to tribal needs; and prevent **higher-level panchayats from taking over the powers of the Gram Sabha**.

**Main features:**
- State laws must be in line with **customary law, social and religious practices and traditional resource management**.
- A village is a habitation or group of habitations; each village has a **Gram Sabha** of persons on the panchayat electoral roll.
- The Gram Sabha protects traditions, cultural identity, community resources and **customary dispute resolution**; it **approves plans and projects** before the village panchayat implements them and **identifies beneficiaries** of poverty programmes; the panchayat must get a **utilisation certificate** from it.
- **Reservation**: seats for STs in proportion to population but **not less than one-half**, and **all chairperson posts** at all levels for STs; the State may nominate STs lacking representation at upper tiers (not more than one-tenth).
- The **Gram Sabha or panchayat must be consulted** before **land acquisition** and resettlement; planning of **minor water bodies** goes to panchayats; the **recommendation of the Gram Sabha or the panchayat at the appropriate level** is needed for licences and leases for **minor minerals** and for auctions of exploitation.
- Specific powers: regulating **intoxicants**, ownership of **minor forest produce**, preventing **alienation of tribal land**, managing **village markets**, control over **money lending** to STs, control over **social-sector institutions** and local plans.
- Safeguards so that higher-level panchayats do not take powers of lower ones or of the Gram Sabha; the State is to follow the **Sixth Schedule** pattern when designing district-level arrangements.

**Do not confuse** Fifth Schedule areas (covered by PESA) with Sixth Schedule tribal areas (autonomous districts in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, as the 2019 note lists), which are exempt from Part IX and have their own councils.

### 8. Ground reality I: the three Fs and why the promise is only half kept

*Objective:* Explain functions, funds and functionaries, and give the reasons for weak performance, using only sourced points.

**The three Fs.** Mains answers use a formula: **functions, funds and functionaries**. *Functions* are the subjects panchayats are actually allowed to handle; *funds* the money; *functionaries* the staff who work under their control. The 2020 Mains question adds a newer word: **functionality**, meaning whether the body actually works as a living institution rather than merely existing on paper. Both NCERT and Laxmikanth say that, although the law is good, practice lags.

**Laxmikanth's list of reasons for ineffective performance** (drawn from a Ministry roadmap of 2011-16; the figures there are old and not repeated here):
1. **Lack of adequate devolution** of the 3Fs: many States have not devolved enough, and few implemented State Finance Commission recommendations.
2. **Excessive control by bureaucracy**: sarpanches spend time in block offices for funds and approvals, which distorts their role as elected representatives.
3. **Tied nature of funds**: scheme-specific money may be unsuitable for local needs, leading to wrong activities or under-spending.
4. **Overwhelming dependence on government funding**: people are less likely to demand social audit of money they did not raise.
5. **Reluctance to use fiscal powers**: very few panchayats levy taxes; it is hard to tax your own neighbours.
6. **Status of the Gram Sabha**: many State Acts do not spell out its powers, procedures or penalties for officials.
7. **Parallel bodies**: bodies created for faster delivery bypass panchayats, with little evidence they avoid corruption or elite capture, and they demoralise panchayats.
8. **Poor infrastructure and capacity**: many panchayats lack a full-time secretary, buildings and data; many elected members are semi-literate or untrained.

**NCERT's summary.** Local governments enjoy **limited autonomy**; many States have not transferred most of the 29 subjects, so the election of so many representatives becomes somewhat **symbolic**. Local bodies earn little and spend more; NCERT's figure that rural local bodies raised about 0.24 per cent of total revenue while accounting for about 4 per cent of expenditure is **dated**, so use it only as an illustration with a date. The conclusion: local governments remain largely **agencies implementing schemes** of the Centre and States.

**The core diagnosis in three lines.** (1) The Constitution made the structure compulsory but left **functions and funds largely voluntary** (module 4). (2) Real power over most Eleventh Schedule subjects stayed with State departments and parallel agencies. (3) Without money and staff, even a good Gram Sabha cannot deliver.

**Way forward** (for Mains): devolve all three Fs through State law and activity mapping; implement State Finance Commission recommendations; untie funds; strengthen own-source revenue; give statutory force to the Gram Sabha; integrate parallel bodies; train and staff panchayats; use technology and social audit.

### 9. Ground reality II: women, SC/ST representation and the Gram Sabha in practice

*Objective:* Discuss the social impact of reservation and the limits revealed by proxy representation and weak Gram Sabhas.

**What reservation achieved (NCERT).** The reservation of seats and of chairperson posts means that a large number of women now hold positions as Sarpanch and Adhyaksha. Qualitatively (NCERT's numbers are old; none are used here): women gained **confidence and a political education**; they brought a new perspective and greater sensitivity to discussions; reservation for SC and ST (and in most States for backward castes) **changed the social profile** of local bodies so that they became more representative of the society around them.

**Limits.** NCERT also says that in many cases women were unable to assert themselves or were **mere proxies** for male family members who sponsored their election, although such instances were becoming fewer. It adds that when dominant groups that once controlled the village do not wish to give up power, **tension and struggle** increase; NCERT's view is that this is not always bad, because making democracy meaningful for those who did not have power is bound to cause some conflict. This supports the 2019 Mains question ('limited impact on the patriarchal character of the political process'): a balanced answer must give both the **presence** and the **limits of agency**.

**How to answer on patriarchy.** A useful structure: (i) numbers and presence (qualitative, dated), (ii) examples of empowerment (the NCERT story of a Sarpanch who led water and school projects), (iii) barriers (proxy representation, male relatives, social pressure, lack of training, resistance by dominant groups), (iv) enabling measures (training, capacity building, support networks, strict action against proxies, reservation of leadership posts together with access to funds) and (v) a conclusion that structural change needs both reservation and social change.

**The Gram Sabha in practice.** Laxmikanth records that many State Acts do not spell out Gram Sabha powers or procedures. NCERT's student voice asks whether Gram Sabhas actually meet regularly, a question the sources do not answer with data; do not claim numbers.

**Decentralisation and effective governance.** Two Mains questions (2022 and 2025) ask about the effect of decentralisation. A framework: **why** decentralise (closeness, local knowledge, participation, accountability: NCERT); **what changed** (elected bodies everywhere, regular elections, reservation, a State Election Commission and a State Finance Commission); **what did not** (the three Fs, parallel bodies, tied funds, scheme-driven agenda); **conclusion** (structurally strong, functionally weak; the test of democracy is in practice, not only in the law, as NCERT concludes).

**Revamped Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan (RGSA): a flagged stub.** The Ministry of Panchayati Raj runs this Central scheme to strengthen panchayat governance and capacity: training and capacity building of elected representatives and functionaries, support for panchayat institutions and better delivery of local services. It is the scheme behind the 2026 Prelims question on panchayat capacity-building (upsc-2026-prelims-gs-i-q74). **Not in the sources read.** Its period, the funding pattern (central and State shares, and whether any share is 100 per cent central) and its coverage of States and Union Territories are marked **verify against official documents**; no figures are given here. Treat any absolute claim in an option (for example 'for all States and UTs' or '100 per cent central funding') with suspicion until you have checked the scheme document.

### 10. How UPSC tests this topic: PYQ patterns, frameworks and elimination

*Objective:* Recognise the repeated themes of the eleven PYQs and apply a Mains skeleton and Prelims elimination.

**What the eleven verified PYQs show (2017 to 2026).**
- **Prelims (3)**: the concept of local government as **democratic decentralisation** (2017); Part IX facts about the **intermediate tier, age and the finance commission** (2025); a **scheme** on panchayat capacity-building (2026).
- **Mains (7)**: effectiveness of local self-government (2017); importance of the Panchayat system and **sources of finance** apart from grants (2018); **women's reservation** and patriarchy (2019); **functions, functionaries, funds and 'functionality'** (2020); the effect of **decentralisation** (2022); local bodies, good governance and **merging rural with urban bodies** (2024); decision-making **close to the source of information** (2025).
- **Ethics case study (1, GS IV 2025)**: a district administrator finds the **mismanagement of a rural wage-employment scheme** by Gram Panchayats. It touches this topic through the Gram Sabha's role and social audit, but belongs to the ethics paper.

**Skeleton: 'Local self-government has not proved effective. Critically examine.'** (15 marks, 250 words)
1. *Intro*: Panchayati Raj, Part IX, the 73rd Amendment (24 April 1993), the aim of grassroots democracy.
2. *Achievements*: constitutional status, regular elections, State Election Commission, reservation and representation.
3. *Shortfalls*: the three Fs, tied funds, parallel bodies, weak Gram Sabha, capacity gaps, proxy representation.
4. *Why*: the voluntary nature of many provisions; State reluctance; fiscal dependence.
5. *Way forward*: devolution via activity mapping, untied funds, own revenue, Gram Sabha strengthened, training, social audit.
6. *Conclusion*: effectiveness depends on real devolution.

**Sources of finance** (2018 question): own tax (house, profession, land, vehicles, markets and fairs, licences) and non-tax revenue (fees, rents), grants through the Central and State Finance Commissions, scheme funds; the 'beyond grants' part asks for own-source revenue, user charges and community contributions, with the caution that tax effort is politically hard (source).

**Prelims elimination moves.**
- **Absolute claims** ('all States', 'only', '100% for all') are risky: the intermediate tier is optional for a State of 20 lakh or fewer.
- **Wrong authority**: the **Governor** constitutes the State Finance Commission and appoints the State Election Commissioner; the Chief Minister does not.
- **Wrong number**: minimum age **21**, term **five years**, **six months** for elections after dissolution, **one-third** for women, **29** subjects.
- **'Not correct' stems**: read whether the question asks for correct or incorrect statements before choosing.

**Worked thinking.** Statement: 'To be a member of a panchayat at the intermediate level a person must be 30.' Step 1: recall that the minimum age to contest is 21. Step 2: recall that a person of 21 cannot be disqualified for being under 25. Step 3: conclude that the statement conflicts with the rule in module 5. This is analysis, not an official answer key.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Panchayati Raj: the system of rural local self-government, constitutionalised by the 73rd Amendment Act, 1992.
- Panchayat: an institution of self-government for rural areas; village, intermediate and district levels.
- Gram Sabha: the village assembly of all persons on the electoral roll of the village within the panchayat area (Article 243A).
- Democratic decentralisation: the Balwant Rai Mehta term for transferring planning and development power to elected local bodies.
- Three Fs: functions, funds, functionaries; 'functionality' is the newer word for whether devolved bodies actually work.
- Compulsory provisions: those that State laws must contain; voluntary provisions: those States may adopt at discretion.
- Scheduled Areas: areas under the Fifth Schedule where Part IX applies only through the PESA Act, 1996.
- Supersession: dissolution of a panchayat by the State government; fresh elections must follow within six months.
- Parallel bodies: agencies created outside the panchayat system to deliver schemes quickly.

### Notes: Part IX: Articles 243 to 243-O

- 243: definitions (panchayat, village, intermediate level, district).
- 243A: Gram Sabha. 243B: constitution of panchayats. 243C: composition. 243D: reservation of seats.
- 243E: duration of panchayats. 243F: disqualifications. 243G: powers, authority and responsibilities. 243H: powers to impose taxes and funds.
- 243-I: finance commission. 243J: audit of accounts. 243K: elections (State Election Commission). 243L: Union Territories.
- 243M: Part not to apply to certain areas. 243N: continuance of existing laws and panchayats. 243-O: bar to interference by courts in electoral matters.
- Eleventh Schedule: 29 subjects (Article 243G). Article 40 (Directive Principle) is the source of the idea.

### Notes: Committees on Panchayati Raj

- Balwant Rai Mehta (1957): three tiers, democratic decentralisation; samiti executive; Collector as chairman of zila parishad.
- Ashok Mehta (1977-78): two tiers (zila parishad and mandal panchayat); political party role; taxation powers; social audit; constitutional recognition.
- Dantwala (1978) block-level planning and Hanumantha Rao (1984) district planning: Collector as coordinator in the latter.
- G.V.K. Rao (1985-86): 'grass without roots'; zila parishad pivotal; District Development Commissioner; regular elections.
- L.M. Singhvi (1986): constitutional recognition through a new chapter; Gram Sabha as direct democracy; judicial tribunals for election disputes.
- P.K. Thungon (1988): constitutional recognition; three tiers; five-year term; State Finance Commission.
- V.N. Gadgil (1988, Congress party committee): basis of the amendment bill; State Finance Commission and State Election Commission.
- After 1992 (Table 38.5): Lalit Mathur task force (2001); V. Ramachandran expert group (2005-06) and committee on DRDA restructuring (2010-12); Rajwant Sandhu manual for district planning (2008); Mani Shankar Aiyar committee on leveraging panchayats (2012-13).

### Notes: Chronology of Panchayati Raj

- 1952: Community Development Programme begins on 2 October; 1953: National Extension Service.
- January 1957: Balwant Rai Mehta Committee appointed; report in November 1957; National Development Council accepts it in January 1958.
- 2 October 1959: Panchayati Raj inaugurated at Nagaur, Rajasthan; Andhra Pradesh follows in 1959.
- 1964-77: decline of first-generation institutions.
- 1977-78: Ashok Mehta Committee; 1978: West Bengal holds party-based panchayat elections.
- 1985-86: G.V.K. Rao and L.M. Singhvi committees; 1988: Thungon and Gadgil committees.
- 1989: 64th Amendment Bill passed by Lok Sabha, defeated in Rajya Sabha; 1990 bill lapses; 1991 bill introduced.
- 22 and 23 December 1992: 73rd Amendment passed by Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha; assent 20 April 1993; in force 24 April 1993 (74th in force 1 June 1993).
- 1993-94: States pass conformity Acts.
- 24 December 1996: PESA Act receives assent and comes into force (both on the same date, per Laxmikanth).
- 2000-01: 83rd Amendment dispenses with SC reservation in Arunachal Pradesh.

### Notes: PESA Act 1996 at a glance

- Extends Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas with modifications; ten States with Fifth Schedule areas in 2019 (verify).
- Gram Sabha approves plans and projects before implementation and identifies beneficiaries; panchayat must obtain a utilisation certificate.
- ST reservation not less than one-half of seats; all chairperson posts at all levels reserved for STs; nominated STs not more than one-tenth.
- Consultation with the Gram Sabha or panchayat before land acquisition and resettlement; planning of minor water bodies; recommendation needed for minor minerals licences, leases and auctions.
- Specific powers: prohibition or regulation of intoxicants, ownership of minor forest produce, preventing land alienation, managing markets, controlling money lending, controlling social-sector institutions, controlling local plans.
- Safeguards against higher-level panchayats taking powers of lower ones or of the Gram Sabha; follow the Sixth Schedule pattern for district-level arrangements.

### Notes: Finances of Panchayati Raj

- Source 1: Union grants on Central Finance Commission recommendations (Article 280).
- Source 2: State devolution on State Finance Commission recommendations (Article 243-I).
- Source 3: loans and grants from the State.
- Source 4: programme-specific allocation under centrally sponsored schemes and additional central assistance.
- Source 5: own resources, tax and non-tax; Gram Panchayats have a tax domain (house and property tax, profession tax, land tax, vehicle taxes, entertainment tax, licence fees, water and sanitation rates, fairs and festivals), while higher tiers depend on tolls, fees and non-tax revenue.
- Chain: tied scheme funds and weak own revenue -> limited discretion and dependence on grants -> weak accountability to local citizens -> weak demand for social audit.

### Notes: Examples and key sourced stories

- NCERT story: a woman Sarpanch in Madhya Pradesh led water-tank renovation, a school building, roads and awareness work.
- NCERT story: the Vengaivasal Gram Panchayat in Tamil Nadu refused to endorse a land allotment; a High Court division bench held that the order infringed the panchayat's constitutional status.
- Rajasthan three tiers, Tamil Nadu two tiers, West Bengal four tiers (early patterns).
- Source table of State names: Gram Panchayat, Mandal Parishad, Panchayat Samiti, Panchayat Union, Zilla Panchayat and others vary by State.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Gram Sabha versus Gram Panchayat: the voters' assembly versus the elected village council.
- Part IX (panchayats, 73rd) versus Part IX-A (municipalities, 74th); Eleventh versus Twelfth Schedule (GS-196).
- State Finance Commission (Article 243-I, Governor) versus Union Finance Commission (Article 280, President).
- State Election Commission versus Election Commission of India: separate, independent offices.
- Fifth Schedule areas (PESA) versus Sixth Schedule tribal areas (exempt, own councils).
- Compulsory versus voluntary provisions; reservation for women (compulsory one-third) versus for backward classes (voluntary).

### Notes: Cause and effect: why the promise is half kept

- Voluntary functions and finance provisions -> States devolve little -> panchayats have responsibility without resources.
- Tied scheme funds and parallel bodies -> weak local discretion and duplication -> demoralised panchayats.
- Weak Gram Sabha rules -> little social audit -> corruption and elite capture risk.
- Reservation -> more women and SC/ST in office -> changed social profile, but proxy representation limits the effect.

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** What is Panchayati Raj?
    - **a:** The system of rural local self-government, given constitutional status by the 73rd Amendment Act, 1992.
  - - **q:** Name the three levels of panchayats.
    - **a:** Village (Gram Panchayat), intermediate (block, taluka or mandal) and district (Zila Parishad).
  - - **q:** What is the difference between a Gram Sabha and a Gram Panchayat?
    - **a:** The Gram Sabha is the assembly of all registered voters; the Gram Panchayat is the elected village council.
  - - **q:** How many subjects does the Eleventh Schedule contain?
    - **a:** 29.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why did the Constitution give panchayats weak protection in 1950?
    - **a:** Partition favoured a unitary tilt and Nehru feared localism; Ambedkar doubted caste-ridden villages; Article 40 was only a Directive Principle.
  - - **q:** Why are the voluntary provisions the root of weak devolution?
    - **a:** Functions, plans and taxation powers depend on State law, so States may devolve little.
  - - **q:** What does PESA change for Scheduled Areas?
    - **a:** It makes the Gram Sabha central and gives STs not less than half the seats and all chairperson posts.
  - - **q:** Why is the State Election Commission important?
    - **a:** It removes election control from the State government, giving independent, regular elections.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A State has not held panchayat elections for four years. List two constitutional provisions it violates.
    - **a:** The five-year term and the rule of fresh elections within six months of dissolution; also the compulsory State Election Commission arrangement for conducting elections.
  - - **q:** A Gram Panchayat in a Scheduled Area wants to grant a minor-mineral lease without consulting anyone. What does PESA say?
    - **a:** The recommendation of the Gram Sabha or the panchayat at the appropriate level is mandatory.
  - - **q:** Write a three-sentence reply to 'Panchayats are weak because villagers are uneducated'.
    - **a:** Weakness stems chiefly from limited devolution of functions, funds and staff, and tied funds, not from villagers' capacity. Training can help, but structural devolution is the main need. Reservation and a strong Gram Sabha show that participation grows when power is real.
