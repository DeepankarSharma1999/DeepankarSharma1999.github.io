## GS-110: World climatic regions and biomes

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Reading a climate: the words you need

A climate is the usual weather of a place over many years. **Mean monthly temperature** is the average temperature of a month; the **annual range** is the hottest month's mean minus the coldest month's mean, and the **diurnal range** is the difference between day and night temperature. **Precipitation** means all water that falls from the sky, rain and snow; it is measured in millimetres (mm) or centimetres (cm), and 10 mm equals 1 cm, so 635 mm is 63.5 cm. The **tropics** are the belt between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn (about 23.5 degrees north and south). The **Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** is the belt near the equator where winds from the two hemispheres meet and air rises, giving heavy rain. A **sub-tropical high** is a belt of high pressure near 30 degrees where air sinks, so rain is rare. **Westerlies** blow from the west in the middle latitudes; **trade winds** blow toward the equator from the east. A **cold ocean current** chills air above it, which gives fog but little rain. **Humid** means moist and **arid** means very dry. A **continental interior** is land far from the sea. These range and temperature terms are developed in Day 18 lesson GS-103, 'Core: isotherms, January and July patterns, and temperature range' and 'Core: latitudinal heat balance and the factors controlling temperature'.

### Bridge: Vegetation and soil words

**Vegetation** is the plant cover of an area as a community. **Evergreen** plants keep leaves all year; **deciduous** plants shed them in a dry or cold season. The **canopy** is the upper layer of leaves in a forest. An **epiphyte** grows on another plant for support without taking its food, and a **liana** is a woody climber. A **xerophyte** is a plant adapted to dry conditions, a **hydrophyte** lives in water, and a **halophyte** tolerates salty soil. **Litter** is dead leaves and twigs on the ground; **decomposers** are tiny organisms that break litter into **humus**, the dark organic part of soil. **Leaching** is the washing of dissolved nutrients down through soil by water. **Permafrost** is soil that stays frozen all year. An **ecosystem** is living things plus their physical environment interacting as a system, and a **biome** is the largest such unit that is easy to recognise on a world map, defined by its vegetation under a given climate.

### 1. Orientation: one world, five climate letters and the biomes they carry

*Objective:* State what a climatic region and a biome are, name the three approaches to classifying climate, and describe the belt pattern on the west and east sides of an ideal continent.

## What this topic is about

The earth does not have one climate. Near the equator it is hot and wet all year, in the great deserts it is hot and almost rainless, and near the poles it is frozen. A **climatic region** is a part of the earth where the long-run pattern of temperature, rainfall and seasons is similar. A **biome** is the largest ecosystem unit that is easy to mark on a world map: a characteristic type of vegetation growing under a particular climate, together with its own group of soils and its own community of animals (Leong, chapter 16). The idea that ties the whole lesson together is a chain: **climate shapes vegetation, climate and vegetation shape soil, and all three shape the animals and the ways people can live there.**

## Why classify

NCERT names three approaches. An **empirical** classification is built from observed data, mainly temperature and rainfall. A **genetic** classification groups climates by their causes. An **applied** classification is made for a specific purpose. The scheme examiners use most is the empirical scheme of V. Koeppen (spelt Koeppen in NCERT; also written Koppen), which relates climate to the distribution of vegetation. Module 2 teaches its letters.

## The one-page picture

Read the sketch below as a story of belts. Start at the equator and walk poleward along the **west side** of a continent: rain forest, savanna, hot desert, the Mediterranean type and the marine west coast type; then, further poleward and inland, cold forest; and finally tundra and ice. On the **east side** the sequence differs: wet tropical or monsoon climates, then humid subtropical, then cold forest. In the **deep interior** of the big continents rain is scarce, so steppe and desert appear even at middle latitudes. The east-west difference at one latitude is the first idea to hold on to; its causes (pressure belts, winds, ocean currents) belong to later topics such as GS-104 and GS-108.

## How the lesson runs

Modules 3 to 8 take the regions in the order rain forest, monsoon, savanna, dry climates, warm temperate, cold and polar. Module 9 compares them with altitude, mountains and climate change, and module 10 is the exam lens. The long region-by-region table is in the master notes; hand-drawn sketch maps of this belt pattern are worth practising.

### 2. Foundation: how Koeppen's letters work

*Objective:* Use Koeppen's capital and small letters and the NCERT temperature rules to decode a climate code and place it in group A, B, C, D or E.

## The idea

Koeppen noticed that vegetation follows climate, so he chose particular values of **mean annual and mean monthly temperature and precipitation** as boundaries and gave each climate a letter code. NCERT says he developed the scheme in 1918, that it has been modified over time, and that it is still the most widely used. **Capital letters** name the climatic groups; **small letters** name types inside a group.

## The five groups (NCERT Table 11.1)

| Group | Name | NCERT rule |
|---|---|---|
| A | Tropical humid | coldest month averages 18 degrees C or higher |
| B | Dry | potential evaporation exceeds precipitation |
| C | Warm temperate (mid-latitude) | coldest month above minus 3 degrees C but below 18 degrees C |
| D | Cold snow forest | coldest month minus 3 degrees C or below |
| E | Cold (polar) | every month below 10 degrees C |

Four groups rest on temperature (A, C, D, E) and only one, B, rests on water balance. **Potential evaporation** is the amount of water the air could evaporate if water were always available; when it exceeds rainfall, the climate is dry. NCERT calls A, C, D and E the humid groups and B the dry group.

## The small letters

- **f**: no dry season. **m**: monsoon, a short dry season. **w**: winter dry season. **s**: summer dry season.
- Inside B: **S** is steppe (semi-arid) and **W** is desert.
- **a, b, c, d** show how severe the temperature is. NCERT's table also uses **h** and **k** (BSh, BWh, BSk, BWk) for low-latitude and mid-latitude dry climates; in standard Koeppen usage h means hot and k means cold (verify, not defined in the extract).

## Decoding practice

- **Af**: tropical (A) with no dry season (f) is tropical wet.
- **Aw**: tropical with a winter dry season is tropical wet and dry, the savanna climate.
- **BWh**: dry (B), desert (W), low latitude (h).
- **Cs**: warm temperate with a dry summer (s) is the Mediterranean type.
- **Cfb**: warm temperate, no dry season, is the marine west coast type.
- **ET** is tundra and **EF** is perennial ice.

## Two cautions from NCERT's own pages

NCERT's text names the humid subtropical type with a dry winter Cwa, yet Table 11.2 lists only Cfa. The table also labels Df "humid continental" and Dw "subarctic", while the text describes Df as cold with humid winters and Dw as cold with dry winters. Learn the descriptions in the text and the codes as printed; do not try to fix NCERT's table in the examination.

### 3. Core: the equatorial climate (Af) and the tropical rain forest

*Objective:* Describe the location, temperature and rainfall pattern, vegetation layers, soils and human use of the hot wet equatorial region, and explain why its soils are poor.

## Where and what

NCERT places the tropical wet climate (Af) near the equator: the Amazon basin, western equatorial Africa and the islands of the East Indies. Leong gives the core belt as roughly 5 to 10 degrees north and south of the equator, with cooler highland variants (such as Bogota in the Andes at about 2,640 m; Leong prints 2,850 m in the text and 2,661 m in a table, **verify**) where altitude lowers the temperature.

## The climate

- **Temperature** is almost the same every month: Leong gives monthly means near 27 degrees C and NCERT puts the daily maximum near 30 and minimum near 20 degrees C. There is no winter, so the annual range is tiny (Leong: 2 to 3 degrees C at Warri, Nigeria, 1 degree C at Bogota).
- **Rain** falls in every month, often as afternoon convectional thunderstorms. Leong gives totals of about 1,524 to 2,699 mm a year and notes two rainfall peaks after the equinoxes (April and October), a pattern it calls a feature of the equatorial climate not found elsewhere; local conditions can blur it.
- **Humidity** stays above 80 per cent, which makes the heat sticky.

## Vegetation: the tropical rain forest

The heat and rain produce a dense **evergreen** forest in which growth never stops: flowering, fruiting and decay go on all year. Leong stresses four features. First, great variety: hardwoods such as mahogany and ebony, palms, **lianas** (woody climbers) and **epiphytes** (plants that grow on other plants without drawing food from them). Second, **layering**: the tallest trees reach about 60 m, with lower tree layers and a shaded, sparse ground layer. Third, many species mixed together, not pure stands, which makes logging costly. Fourth, when cleared land is abandoned, a lower, tangled secondary forest grows back. In coastal swamps the **mangroves** have stilt and breathing roots (Leong, chapter 16).

## Soil and the nutrient trap

Fallen leaves decay very quickly in the heat and wet, so little litter lies on the surface. Leong calls it a misconception that tropical soils are rich: in the untouched state, heavy leaf-fall and bacterial decay build a humus mantle that keeps the soil fairly fertile for a while, but once the cover is cleared and the humus used, torrential rain washes out the nutrients and the soil degrades fast with erosion. The practical reading (standard ecology; verify) is that most of the nutrients sit in the living forest and are recycled at speed, not stored in the soil.

## People

Population is sparse. Peasants use **bush fallow** (clear, crop for some years, leave to recover). Large plantations grow rubber, cocoa, oil palm and other cash crops. Difficulties include disease, pests, the cost of keeping clearings and roads free of growth, tall coarse grass that is poor fodder, and the tsetse fly in Africa. NCERT adds that rain forests hold about half the world's species (chapter 14).

Diagram idea: a vertical profile of rain forest layers beside a rainfall bar graph with two peaks.

### 4. Core: the tropical monsoon climate (Am) and monsoon forests

*Objective:* Explain the seasonal wind reversal behind monsoon climate, describe the three seasons and the vegetation and farming that follow, and distinguish tropical marine from tropical monsoon.

## Where and why

NCERT places the tropical monsoon climate (Am) over the Indian sub-continent, north-eastern South America and northern Australia, with heavy summer rain and a dry winter. Leong adds the wider monsoon lands of Myanmar, Thailand, Indo-China and south China. The cause is the unequal heating of land and sea. In summer the great Asian land mass heats up and a low-pressure region forms; the cooler sea sends moist winds inland as the south-west monsoon. In winter the land cools, high pressure builds, and dry winds blow out toward the sea. The monsoon, in short, is a seasonal wind reversal on a very large scale. (Mechanism details: GS-106 and GS-119.)

## Three seasons (Leong, using India)

1. **Cool dry season (October to February)**: dry out-blowing winds; a little winter rain in Punjab from cyclonic sources, and heavy rain on the south-east peninsula coast in October to November where the winds cross the Bay of Bengal.
2. **Hot dry season (March to mid-June)**: temperature climbs, an intense low forms in the north-west and duststorms precede the burst of the monsoon.
3. **Rainy season (mid-June to September)**: almost all the year's rain falls. Mumbai in Leong's table gets 505, 610, 368 and 269 mm in June to September, which is 1,752 mm; against an annual 1,829 mm that is 95.8 per cent, so "about 95 per cent in four months" is right.

## Vegetation and farming

The vegetation depends on rainfall: dense tropical forest where rain is heavy, **monsoon deciduous forest** that sheds leaves in the hot dry season (teak and sal are Leong's examples), bamboo thickets at medium rainfall and thorny scrub or savanna toward drier areas. Wet rice is the characteristic crop: Leong says it needs rain over 1,778 mm (70 inches) in lowland areas and at least 1,270 mm (50 inches) in the growing season. Highland plantations grow tea and coffee; **shifting cultivation** also persists. Late or weak monsoons cause drought and failure; heavy ones cause floods.

## Tropical marine variant

On east coasts in the trade-wind belt (eastern Brazil, north-eastern Australia, the West Indies, Philippines), rain comes all year with a summer maximum and no distinct dry season. Leong's Cairns data give 1,575 mm in the four summer months out of 2,235 mm, 70.5 per cent. Such coasts are prone to cyclones, hurricanes and typhoons.

Common trap: Leong writes that the Punjab plains "receive the South-West Monsoon earliest". The monsoon reaches the south-west coast first and the north-west last; Punjab is among the first areas to see it withdraw. Teach the standard order (verify in GS-119).

### 5. Core: the tropical wet and dry climate (Aw) and the savanna

*Objective:* Locate the savanna belt, describe its wet and dry seasons, vegetation, animals and people, and list the conditions that keep savanna from becoming forest.

## Where

NCERT places the tropical wet and dry climate (Aw) north and south of the Af belt. On the western side of a continent it borders dry climate; on the eastern side it borders humid subtropical types. Large areas lie north and south of the Amazon forest in Brazil and in Bolivia and Paraguay, and in Sudan and south of Central Africa. Leong calls it a transitional climate between the equatorial forest and the trade-wind hot deserts, best developed in the Sudan, with the llanos (Orinoco basin) and campos (Brazilian Highlands) in South America and a savanna south of the monsoon strip in northern Australia.

## Climate

NCERT: rain is much less than in Af and Am and highly variable; the wet season is shorter and the dry season longer, so drought is more severe; temperature stays high and the daily range is largest in the dry season. Leong's station figures: Kano, Nigeria, 864 mm (34 inches) mostly from May to September; Harare, Zimbabwe, about 813 mm with rain from October to March (the southern summer). Rain and the length of the wet season shrink toward the desert. The hottest weather comes just before the rains, not at the highest sun, because cloud and rain then cool the land. In West Africa the dry, dusty north-east wind is called the **Harmattan**.

## Vegetation

The typical picture is tall grass with scattered, short, deciduous trees. Leong gives grass heights of 2 to 4 m (elephant grass up to 5 m), and tree adaptations: deep roots (acacias), water-storing trunks (baobab), thorns, gum and umbrella-shaped crowns. The landscape turns green in the rains and yellow-brown in the dry season. Leong sorts savanna structurally into **woodland, savanna woodland, tree savanna, shrub savanna and grass savanna**, and in West Africa into derived, Guinea, Sudan and Sahel savanna from forest edge to desert edge.

## Why trees stay few

Rainfall is not the only brake. Leong says bush burning is the oldest human interference and that many savannas are sustained by annual burning; the trees that survive have thick bark (fire-resistant) while fire-tender forest trees are absent. Animals eat seedlings and burrowing animals damage roots and soil; the seasonal drought and the infertile lateritic soils (iron oxidised in the dry season) also limit trees. A question may therefore list fire, grazing, burrowing, seasonal rain and soil together (module 10). Termites are a common standard answer too, though the extract does not name them (verify).

## Animals and people

Savanna is "big game country": zebra, antelope, giraffe and elephant as grazers and browsers, lion and cheetah as hunters; national parks protect them. Cattle herders move with the seasons (Fulani with transhumance, Maasai); Hausa farmers grow millet, maize, groundnuts and cotton and rotate fields. Pests (locusts, birds), recurrent drought and **desertification** (module 6) are the main problems.

NCERT exercise 1(iii) offers Af, BSh, Cfb and Am, prints no key and omits Aw; standard Koeppen maps show the west coast as Am, much of the peninsular interior as Aw and the rain-shadow parts as BSh (**verify**). India's savanna-like dry deciduous areas are covered under GS-122.

### 6. Core: dry climates (B) - deserts, steppes and desertification

*Objective:* Locate the hot and mid-latitude deserts and the steppe, explain their rainfall, temperature and vegetation, and explain why desertification is not tied to desert climate.

## Where and why (NCERT)

Dry climates (B) extend over a very large area between roughly 15 and 60 degrees north and south. At **low latitudes (15 to 30 degrees)** they lie in the sub-tropical high, where sinking air and a temperature inversion prevent rain. On the **western margins of continents** next to a cold current they reach nearer the equator and sit on the coast, as along the west coast of South America. At **middle latitudes (35 to 60 degrees)** they lie in continental interiors where moist maritime winds do not reach, often walled in by mountains. Steppe (BS) is the drier edge of the humid zone and receives a little more rain than desert (BW), enough for sparse grass; rainfall variability hurts the steppe most and often causes famine. Desert rain comes in short violent showers that do not build soil moisture. Fog forms on coastal deserts beside cold currents. The record shade temperature in NCERT is 58 degrees C at Al Aziziyah, Libya, on 13 September 1922 (Leong prints 1992, a slip). This is NCERT's figure; the WMO disqualified it in 2012, and the current official record is Death Valley, USA, 56.7 degrees C in 1913 (standard knowledge, **verify**).

## Hot deserts (Leong)

They lie on the western sides of continents at 15 to 30 degrees: the Sahara (about 5,150 km east-west, at least 1,600 km wide, about 9 million sq km), Great Australian, Arabian, Iranian, Thar, Kalahari and Namib deserts, the Sonoran and Mohave in North America, and the **Atacama**, driest of all with 13 mm a year or less. Few deserts exceed 250 mm. Days are very hot and nights cold: diurnal ranges of 17 to 22 degrees C are common. Coastal deserts are cooler (Arica mean 19 degrees C) because of cold currents.

## Mid-latitude deserts and steppes

Gobi, Turkestan and Patagonia lie far from the sea; Patagonia is mainly a rain shadow of the Andes. Kashgar (Leong): 89 mm a year, July about 27 degrees C and January minus 5, annual range 32 degrees C (Leong prints 70 F for 27 C, which should be about 81 F). The temperate **grasslands** (steppe, prairie, pampas, veld, downs) are treeless; Leong puts the boundary at about 508 mm (20 inches), with tall nutritious grass above it and short wiry grass below. The Eurasian steppe has the black **chernozem** soil and the prairies are the wheat and ranching lands; southern-hemisphere grasslands have milder winters (Pretoria range 11 degrees C against Winnipeg 39).

## Life in the desert

Plants are **xerophytes**: cacti, thorny bushes, wiry grasses, few or no leaves, long roots, seeds that wait for rain; **halophytes** survive salty soil; date palms grow at oases. Soils are poor in humus and may form salty hard pans. People are nomadic herders (Bedouin), oasis farmers and miners.

## Desertification

Leong defines it as deterioration of an ecosystem until biological productivity is destroyed. It sets in on the savanna and steppe margins: overgrazing and trampling cut cover and infiltration, runoff and gullying rise, and drought speeds the process (West Africa: possible near 1,000 mm, severe below 700 mm). So it follows human pressure on dry-leaning land, not the Koeppen boundary of a desert.

### 7. Core: warm temperate (C) climates - Mediterranean, humid subtropical and marine west coast

*Objective:* Distinguish the Mediterranean, humid subtropical and marine west coast climates by location, rainfall season, temperature, vegetation and land use.

## Where C climates lie

NCERT: warm temperate (mid-latitude) climates extend from about 30 to 50 degrees latitude, mostly on the eastern and western margins of continents, with warm summers and mild winters. The four types are Cwa, Cs, Cfa and Cfb.

## Mediterranean (Cs)

It lies around the Mediterranean Sea and on west coasts between 30 and 40 degrees: central California, central Chile, south-western and south-eastern Australian coasts (NCERT), and the Cape region of South Africa (Leong). In summer the sub-tropical high dominates, so summers are hot and dry; in winter the westerlies bring rain, so winters are mild and rainy. NCERT: summer mean about 25 degrees C, winter below 10 degrees C, rainfall 35 to 90 cm; Leong takes about 635 mm as typical (Rome 838 mm). Cyclonic winter rain is the key feature, since most climates have a summer maximum. Local winds: the hot dry **Sirocco** from the Sahara and the cold **Mistral** down the Rhone valley.

Vegetation is xerophytic: cork oak and other evergreen oaks in the wetter parts, eucalyptus in Australia, pines and cedars on cooler highlands, and widespread scrub named **maquis** (France), **macchia** (Italy), **chaparral** (California), **mallee** (Australia) and **garigue** (thin limestone soils). Grass is poor, so sheep and goats, not cattle. The region is the "world's orchard": citrus, olives and grapes (Leong). A slip: Leong's Cape Town heading prints 835 mm (25 inches); 25 inches is 635 mm.

## Humid subtropical (Cfa and Cwa)

On eastern sides of continents, rain falls through the year: NCERT gives 75 to 150 cm, summer about 27 degrees C and winter 5 to 12 degrees C, with a small daily range. Regions: eastern USA, southern and eastern China, southern Japan, north-eastern Argentina, coastal South Africa, eastern Australia; Cwa (dry winter) covers the north Indian plains and the south China interior. Leong calls the northern-hemisphere type the China type (monsoonal), plus the Gulf type (south-east USA) and the Natal type (southern hemisphere). Typhoons (July to September), hurricanes and tornadoes occur. Vegetation is mixed evergreen broad-leaf and deciduous forest with conifers on highlands; these regions are densely populated and heavily farmed.

## Marine west coast (Cfb)

It lies poleward of the Mediterranean type on west coasts: north-western Europe, western North America north of California, southern Chile, south-eastern Australia and New Zealand (NCERT). Sea influence keeps temperatures moderate: summer 15 to 20 degrees C, winter 4 to 10 degrees C, small ranges, rain all year, 50 to 250 cm. Leong adds that mountains cause great local contrasts (Southern Alps west slope 5,080 mm against 635 mm on the Canterbury Plains). Vegetation is deciduous forest (oak, elm, ash, beech) growing in fairly pure stands.

### 8. Core: cold snow forest (D) and polar (E) climates - taiga, Laurentian type and tundra

*Objective:* Describe the location, climate, vegetation and resource use of D and E climates, including the fragility and economic importance of the tundra.

## Cold snow forest climates (D)

NCERT: D climates occur in the large northern-hemisphere continental areas between about 40 and 70 degrees north in Europe, Asia and North America. Df has cold snowy winters with humid conditions, a short frost-free season and a large annual range; Dw is a dry-winter variant over north-eastern Asia where a strong winter anticyclone and its summer weakening bring a monsoon-like reversal; many places are below freezing for up to seven months and NCERT gives rainfall of 12 to 15 cm. Leong notes the Siberian type is absent from the southern hemisphere (the land at those latitudes is too narrow; standard, verify).

Leong's Siberian (sub-Arctic) type has long bitter winters and short cool summers: Moscow range 30 degrees C (19 in July, minus 11 in January) and Churchill 40 (12 and minus 28); Verkhoyansk has recorded minus 68 degrees C, 94 degrees colder than Warri's coolest month (26). Rivers freeze (the lower Ob, Lena and Yenisei for over seven months). Snow insulates the soil. Rainfall is about 380 to 635 mm; NCERT's 12 to 15 cm for Dw is the extreme interior case, so the two books differ in what they average.

The vegetation is the **coniferous forest (taiga)**: pine, fir, spruce and larch. Conifers are evergreen so growth starts the moment the temperature allows, cone-shaped so snow slides off, and often grow in pure stands of moderate density up to about 30 m. Soils are acidic **podzols** (Leong). Softwood, pulp and newsprint are the products; lumbering is done in winter when logs can be dragged over snow and floated down thawing rivers.

The cool temperate eastern type (Laurentian, Leong) in north-eastern North America and north-eastern Asia has cold dry winters and warm wet summers; conifers dominate north of about 50 degrees and deciduous forest south of it. In north China winter winds deposit loess. Leong places Beijing here; NCERT would place much of that region in Cwa or D.

## Polar climates (E)

NCERT: beyond 70 degrees latitude, in two types, **tundra (ET)** and **ice cap (EF)**. Tundra takes its name from low mosses, lichens and flowering plants; the sub-soil is permanently frozen (**permafrost**), and the short growing season and waterlogging allow only low plants. Leong adds: warmest month under 10 degrees C (the tree-survival line), growing season under three months, summer thaw of only about 150 mm of soil, precipitation under about 300 mm (Upernavik 231 mm). The ice cap covers interior Greenland and Antarctica (Plateau Station, 79 degrees S); ice there may be 3,000 m thick (Leong).

## Fragile but valuable

Leong lists gold (Alaska), nickel (Petsamo), oil (Alaska's Kenai Peninsula), copper (Rankin Inlet), iron ore (Labrador, Kiruna), the Alaska oil pipeline and Siberian gas, plus reindeer herding, fur and the Arctic sea route. Fragility follows from slow growth, frozen ground and poor drainage; recovery from damage is slow and permafrost thaw is a climate-change worry (standard; verify).

### 9. Master: the biome as a system - habitat factors, altitude zones, mountain barriers, biodiversity and climate change

*Objective:* Explain how habitat factors, altitude and mountain barriers modify the world climate pattern, summarise the biodiversity pattern across biomes, and link climate change to shifting climate belts.

## Habitat factors

At world scale climate decides the vegetation; locally soil (the **edaphic** factor), relief, other living things and human activity matter more (Leong, chapter 16). Leong groups plants by temperature need: **megathermal** (warmer than about 20 degrees C in most months: tropical), **mesothermal** (10 to 20 degrees C: temperate) and **microthermal** (below 10 degrees C for several months: cold). This lines up loosely with Koeppen's A, C and D/E. By water need they are **hydrophytes** (water), **mesophytes** (middle) and **xerophytes** (dry). **Physiologic drought** means water exists in the soil but plants cannot use it because it is frozen or too salty, which is why tundra and deserts are both "dry" for plants.

## Altitude zones

Climate and vegetation change with height (the temperature fall with altitude is taught in Day 18 lesson GS-103, 'Core: latitudinal heat balance and the factors controlling temperature'), so temperate-type vegetation appears on tropical mountains. The **tree line** (the height above which trees do not grow) is higher in tropical than temperate latitudes. Leong's tropical sequence reads upward: rain forest, transitional sub-montane forest, montane forest, scrub or grassland, snowline and ice. On cool temperate mountains: lowland coniferous forest, sub-alpine forest, woodland and pasture, alpine tundra vegetation, permanent snowline and ice cap. Cool tropical uplands are grassy and free of the tsetse fly, hence good grazing (Jos plateau example).

## Mountains as barriers

A range that faces the rain-bearing wind gets heavy rain on the windward side and leaves a dry rain shadow behind. Leong: the Southern Alps of New Zealand receive 5,080 mm on the west slope against 635 mm at Canterbury; the Lake District over 2,540 mm against 610 mm in East Anglia; Patagonia is a desert mainly because the Andes shelter it; the Chinook warms the Prairies after crossing the Rockies; the Mistral is funnelled down the Rhone valley between the Alps and the Central Massif. NCERT calls the Himalayas a physical, climatic, drainage and cultural divide, and Leong notes heavy rain on Himalayan foothills (Cherrapunji). The Western Ghats act in the same way for India (Day 17 lesson GS-116).

## Biodiversity across biomes

NCERT (chapter 14): biodiversity is consistently richer in the tropics because of the greater input of solar energy and water; toward the poles one finds larger populations of fewer species. Tropical rain forests hold about 50 per cent of the earth's species; India is one of twelve mega-diversity countries listed by NCERT.

## Climate change moves the belts

NCERT stresses that climate change is natural and continuous: the last major glacial peak was about 18,000 years ago and the present inter-glacial began about 10,000 years ago. Causes are astronomical (sunspots, Milankovitch cycles), volcanic (aerosols) and anthropogenic (greenhouse gases). The Rajasthan desert area was wetter around 8,000 BC and about 3,000 to 1,700 BC, then drier. NCERT's record (global mean about 14 degrees C; about 0.6 degrees C warming over the 20th century; 1998 the warmest year then) is dated; check later records (verify). Warming can shift belts, raise sea level and flood low coasts and islands.

### 10. Exam: the eight official questions, description-to-type reasoning and answer frameworks

*Objective:* Identify the climate type or biome from a description, reason through the eight official questions without relying on memory of keys, and structure Mains answers on monsoon, desertification, mountains and the tundra.

## The eight questions in one view

- **Prelims 2021 Q60**: identify the biome from a passage about fast leaf-litter decay and climbers and epiphytes. **Prelims 2021 Q61**: conditions that keep savanna from becoming forest. **Prelims 2023 Q63**: two statements on rain forest soil nutrients and decomposition. **Prelims 2024 Q13**: identify the climate type from three numeric and seasonal clues.
- **Mains 2017 Q17** (15 marks): characteristics of the monsoon climate that feed over half of humanity. **Mains 2020 Q5** (10): desertification has no climatic boundaries. **Mains 2021 Q14** (15): alignment of major mountain ranges and their effect on local weather. **Mains 2026 Q5** (10): tundra regions are fragile but economically important.

Official answers are not asserted here; the exam notes give analysis only.

## Reading a description question

1. **Temperature first**: coldest month at or above 18 degrees C points to A; very cold winters point to D or E; mild winters with warm summers point to C.
2. **Rain pattern next**: all-year rain, summer rain, winter rain, little rain.
3. **Numbers last**: compare with NCERT's ranges (Cs 35 to 90 cm; Cfa 75 to 150 cm; Cfb 50 to 250 cm; Dw 12 to 15 cm). The 2024 clue "50 to 250 cm" matches the NCERT range for Cfb exactly; equatorial rainfall (Leong about 152 to 270 cm) starts well above 50 cm, and NCERT's equatorial daily range (30 and 20 degrees C) is about 10 degrees C, not low.
4. **Two-statement questions**: judge each statement alone first (soils of the rain forest are not rich, Leong), then ask whether the second explains the first.

## Mains frameworks

- **Monsoon (15 marks)**: define; seasonal wind reversal; three seasons; rain concentrated in the warm season; wet rice and two crops; rivers and irrigation; high population; risks (drought, flood, variability). Keep the 50 per cent population claim as the question's premise.
- **Desertification (10)**: define; show it occurs on savanna, steppe and semi-arid margins (examples: Sahel and West African savanna in Leong, India's dry margins); drivers (overgrazing, deforestation, mismanaged irrigation, drought); measures (shelter belts, afforestation, pasture improvement).
- **Mountains (15)**: brief alignment (verify in an atlas), barrier effect, rain shadow, local winds, examples from module 9.
- **Tundra (10, examine critically)**: location beyond 70 degrees; fragility (permafrost, short growing season, slow recovery); economic importance (minerals, oil, gas, reindeer, sea route); the critical balance (extraction risk, climate change); conclusion.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Climatic region: a part of the earth with a similar long-run pattern of temperature, rainfall and seasons.
- Biome: the largest easily recognised ecosystem unit; a characteristic vegetation under a particular climate with its own soils and animal community (Leong chapter 16).
- Empirical, genetic and applied classification: based on observed data, on causes, and on a specific purpose respectively (NCERT).
- Koeppen's scheme: an empirical scheme of 1918, modified since, using mean annual and monthly temperature and precipitation and letter codes (NCERT).
- Potential evaporation: the water the air could evaporate if water were always available; when it exceeds precipitation the climate is Koeppen group B.
- Physiologic drought: plants lack usable water although water is present, because it is frozen or too salty.
- Desertification: deterioration of an ecosystem until its biological productivity is destroyed (Leong), seen on savanna and steppe margins.
- Tree line: the height or latitude above which trees do not grow.

### Notes: Process chains

- Rain forest soil: heat and rain, fast decay of litter, nutrients recycled into plants, clearing removes the cover, torrential rain leaches nutrients, soil erodes and yields fall.
- Hot desert: sub-tropical high, sinking air and inversion, no cloud or rain, intense day heating, rapid night cooling and a very large daily range.
- Mediterranean seasons: sub-tropical high in summer gives a hot dry summer; westerlies shift equatorward in winter and bring cyclonic rain.
- Monsoon: summer land heats, low pressure forms, moist sea winds blow inland, heavy rain; winter land cools, high pressure forms, dry winds blow out.
- Desertification: overgrazing and cover loss, lower infiltration, more runoff and gullying, falling productivity, drought deepens the loss.
- Tundra fragility: short growing season, permafrost and waterlogging, slow growth, slow recovery after damage (standard; verify the recovery claim).

### Notes: Biome groups in Leong and the Koeppen link

- Leong groups biomes into forest and woodland (tropical rain forest, tropical deciduous forest, coniferous, temperate deciduous, mixed, Mediterranean), grassland (temperate grassland, tropical savanna) and desert (cold desert or tundra, hot desert).
- Leong's grouping counts the tundra as a cold desert; Koeppen puts it in group E.
- Megathermal, mesothermal and microthermal plants correspond only loosely to Koeppen's A, C and D/E.
- NCERT lists twelve mega-diversity countries: Mexico, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Brazil, Democratic Republic of Congo, Madagascar, China, India, Malaysia, Indonesia and Australia (NCERT prints Columbia).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Af versus Am: no dry season versus short dry season with monsoonal heavy rain.
- Aw versus Am: long dry season, tall grass and scattered trees versus heavy summer rain and monsoon forest.
- BS versus BW: steppe or semi-arid with sparse grass versus desert.
- Cs versus Cfb: dry summer and winter rain versus rain throughout the year.
- Cfa versus Cwa: no dry season versus dry winter; NCERT's table lists only Cfa.
- ET versus EF: tundra with permafrost and low plants versus perennial ice with no plants.
- Savanna (Aw) versus steppe (BS): tropical, tall grass and trees versus mid-latitude, treeless grassland.
- Desert versus desertification: a climate region versus a land-degradation process.

### Core concepts

- Climate shapes vegetation; climate, vegetation and soil form a biome with its animals.
- Koeppen's scheme uses capital letters for groups A to E and small letters for types.
- The same latitude has different climates on the west, interior and east of a continent.
- Each climatic region has a matching vegetation: rain forest, monsoon forest, savanna, xerophytic scrub, steppe grass, Mediterranean scrub, deciduous forest, taiga, tundra.
- Mountains, altitude and climate change modify the belt pattern.

**A.** A summer dry season; Cs is the Mediterranean climate.

**Q.** Define a biome in your own words.

**A.** The largest easily recognised ecosystem unit: a characteristic type of vegetation under a particular climate, with its own soils and community of animals.

**Q.** What annual range of temperature does Leong show for equatorial Warri and for highland Bogota?

**A.** About 2 degrees C at Warri (the monthly means run 28 to 26; the heading prints 3) and 1 degree C at Bogota.

**Q.** Most climates have a summer rainfall maximum. Why does the Mediterranean climate have a winter maximum?

**A.** In summer the sub-tropical high dominates and the westerlies shift poleward, so there is no rain; in winter the westerlies shift equatorward and bring cyclonic rain.

**Q.** According to NCERT, in which of steppe and desert does variability of rainfall do more harm to life, and with what result?

**A.** The steppe, where life depends on the more modest rain that does fall; the result is more frequent famine.

**Q.** Give two reasons conifers suit the taiga climate.

**A.** They are evergreen, so growth starts as soon as the temperature allows without growing new leaves, and their conical shape sheds snow.

**Q.** Why are some west-coast deserts foggy although they get almost no rain?

**A.** A cold current chills the air above it, producing mist and fog; the air is then warmed over land, so little rain falls (Leong, the Atacama example).

**Q.** A station has its warmest month at 18 degrees C, its coldest at 5 degrees C, 120 cm of rain spread through the year, and small ranges. Name the Koeppen group and the best-fitting type, and say what rules out the Mediterranean type.
