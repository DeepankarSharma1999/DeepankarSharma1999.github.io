### Bridge: Integrating powers of r and adding thin shells

The Poiseuille derivation adds up flow through thin rings. Two small tools are needed.

**Rule 1 (powers).** The integral of rⁿ is rⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1). So ∫ r dr = r²/2 and ∫ r³ dr = r⁴/4. A definite integral from 0 to a means: evaluate at a, subtract the value at 0. For example ∫₀ᵃ r³ dr = a⁴/4 − 0 = a⁴/4.

**Rule 2 (thin ring).** Imagine a ring of radius r and thickness dr. If you cut it open and straighten it, it is a thin strip of length 2πr (the circumference) and width dr, so its area is 2πr dr. Adding the areas of all rings from r = 0 to r = a must give the disc: ∫₀ᵃ 2πr dr = 2π a²/2 = πa² ✓. This check shows the rule is right.

**Why it matters.** Within one thin ring the speed is practically the same, so (speed) × (ring area) is the volume per second through that ring; the integral adds all rings.

### Bridge: Dimensional analysis by matching exponents

Every physical quantity has dimensions written with M (mass), L (length) and T (time): speed is LT⁻¹, force is MLT⁻², and viscosity is ML⁻¹T⁻¹ (module 3). Both sides of a true equation have the same dimensions.

**Method.** Suppose you guess that a quantity Q depends on three others as Q = k Aˣ Bʸ Cᶻ, with k a pure number. Write the dimensions of each symbol, then collect the total power of M, of L and of T on the right and set each equal to the power on the left. That gives three simple equations for x, y and z.

**Example.** Speed ∝ (length)ˣ (time)ʸ: LT⁻¹ = Lˣ Tʸ gives x = 1, y = −1, so speed ∝ length/time.

**Limit.** The method fixes *powers* but never the pure number k. In this lesson it gives the form of Stokes' drag and of the Reynolds number, but the 6π in Stokes' law needs a full hydrodynamic calculation.

### 2. Foundation: streamlines, steady, laminar and turbulent flow, and continuity

*Objective:* Define streamline, steady flow, laminar and turbulent flow, and apply the continuity equation A v = constant.

**A fluid** is a liquid or gas: it has no shape of its own and flows. At rest it offers almost no resistance to a shear (NCERT: about a million times smaller than a solid's), which is why PH-022's shear modulus is of no use here. Upadhyaya (extract p. 219) notes that fluids are, to good approximation, homogeneous and isotropic.

**Describing flow.** Do not follow one particle; stand at a fixed point and watch which velocity each passing particle has. The flow is **steady** if at any given point the velocity of every particle that passes stays constant in time (NCERT 9.3). Different points may have different velocities. Steadiness means constant *at a point*, not the same *everywhere*.

**Streamline.** A **streamline** is a curve whose tangent at every point points along the fluid velocity at that point (NCERT 9.3). In steady flow it is also the path a particle follows. **Two streamlines cannot cross**: at a crossing a particle would have two possible velocities, and the flow would not be steady (NCERT, Points to ponder 5). Draw streamlines through the rim of a small area and you get a *tube of flow*: no fluid crosses its side, so what enters one end leaves the other.

**Continuity.** Mass entering a tube of flow per second equals mass leaving. With cross-sections A₁, A₂, speeds v₁, v₂ and densities ρ₁, ρ₂:

$$ ρ₁ A₁ v₁ = ρ₂ A₂ v₂ $$

For an incompressible fluid (liquids to a good approximation) ρ cancels:

$$ A₁ v₁ = A₂ v₂ = constant $$

The product A v is the volume flow rate Q in m³ s⁻¹ (NCERT 9.10-9.11). **Narrow section, fast flow; wide section, slow flow.** Dimension check: [L²][LT⁻¹] = L³T⁻¹ ✓.

**Laminar versus turbulent.** In **laminar** flow the fluid moves in smooth layers (*lamina* means layer) that slide over one another like the pages of a book pushed sideways (NCERT 9.5); velocities at different places may differ in size but are parallel in direction (NCERT Fig. 9.8a). Beyond a limiting **critical speed** the steadiness is lost and the flow becomes **turbulent**: whirlpool-like eddies appear, as in white-water rapids (NCERT 9.3). A tap opened slowly gives a smooth stream that turns ragged as the speed grows.

| Feature | Laminar | Turbulent |
|---|---|---|
| Layers | smooth, parallel, no mixing across layers | irregular eddies, strong mixing |
| Steady? | yes, at low speed | no: velocity at a point fluctuates |
| Governing law in this lesson | Newton's law of viscosity, Poiseuille, Stokes | not covered by those formulae |
| Appears when | speed below critical speed | speed above critical speed |

Poiseuille's and Stokes' formulae hold *only* for laminar flow. Using them on a turbulent flow is a standard exam error. What fixes the critical speed is the Reynolds number (module 7).



### 3. Core: Newton's law of viscosity, units, dimensions and temperature

*Objective:* State F = η A v/h and τ = η dv/dy, derive the units and dimensions of η, and state the temperature trend for liquids and gases.

**The experiment (NCERT 9.5).** A film of oil of thickness h lies between two plates of area A. The lower plate is fixed; the upper moves at constant speed v. Experiment shows that the fluid touching a surface moves with that surface (the **no-slip condition**): the top layer has speed v, the bottom layer has speed 0, and in between the speed rises uniformly. Each layer is pulled forward by the layer above and backward by the layer below. Replacing oil by honey needs a larger force for the same v, so honey is more viscous.

**From strain to strain rate.** Take a small block of fluid ABCD of height h. In time Δt its top edge slides sideways by Δx = vΔt, so the shear strain is Δx/h = vΔt/h and keeps growing. The *rate* of growth is

$$ d(strain)/dt = v/h $$

NCERT defines the coefficient of viscosity as the ratio of shear stress to this strain rate:

$$ η = (F/A) / (v/h)  ⇒  F = η A v / h $$

In differential form, with u(y) the speed of the layer at perpendicular distance y, the **velocity gradient** du/dy plays the role of v/h, and the shear stress on a layer is

$$ τ = η (du/dy) $$

(magnitude; the force on a layer acts to reduce the relative speed of neighbouring layers). The gradient is a *rate of shear*, in s⁻¹.

**Units and dimensions.** Rearranging, η = τ / (du/dy). Shear stress is N m⁻² = [ML⁻¹T⁻²]; the gradient is [T⁻¹]:

$$ [η] = [ML⁻¹T⁻²] / [T⁻¹] = [ML⁻¹T⁻¹] $$

- **SI:** N s m⁻² = Pa s = kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹. NCERT calls this the poiseuille (Pl); do not confuse the unit with Poiseuille's *equation*.
- **CGS:** poise (P) = dyne s cm⁻² = g cm⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- **Conversion:** 1 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ = 1000 g / (100 cm × 1 s) = 10 g cm⁻¹ s⁻¹, so **1 Pa s = 10 poise** and 1 poise = 0.1 Pa s. Water near 20 °C: 1.0 mPa s = 0.01 poise.

| Fluid (NCERT Table 9.2) | T (°C) | η (mPa s) |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 20 / 100 | 1.0 / 0.3 |
| Blood | 37 | 2.7 |
| Machine oil | 16 / 38 | 113 / 34 |
| Glycerine | 20 | 830 |
| Air | 0 / 40 | 0.017 / 0.019 |

**Temperature.** NCERT states that the viscosity of *liquids falls* with temperature, while that of *gases rises*. Reason offered: heating makes liquid molecules more mobile, and in a gas it increases the random motion (Points to ponder 7). The table agrees: water 1.0 to 0.3 mPa s from 20 to 100 °C; air 0.017 to 0.019 from 0 to 40 °C. Standard formulas not in the extract (flagged): liquids roughly η = A exp(B/T); dilute gases roughly η ∝ √T.

**Worked example (NCERT 9.8, recomputed).** A block of area 0.10 m² slides at 0.085 m s⁻¹ on a film 0.30 mm thick, pulled by a 0.010 kg weight. F = mg = 0.010 × 9.8 = 0.098 N, stress = 0.98 N m⁻², strain rate = 0.085 / 0.30 × 10⁻³ = 283.3 s⁻¹, so η = 0.98 / 283.3 = **3.46 × 10⁻³ Pa s**.

**Newtonian fluids.** The law τ = η du/dy with constant η describes *Newtonian* fluids (standard terminology, flagged). The comparison with solids: solid τ = n × strain; fluid τ = η × strain rate.

### 4. Core: Poiseuille's equation, part 1 (assumptions and the velocity profile)

*Objective:* State the assumptions of Poiseuille flow and derive the parabolic velocity profile v(r) from a force balance on a coaxial cylinder of fluid.

**Problem.** A liquid of viscosity η flows through a long straight horizontal tube of radius a and length L. The pressure is P₁ at the entrance and P₂ at the exit. Find the speed at distance r from the axis, then the volume flowing per second. Exam practice target, not an official allotment: 15 marks, about 18 minutes (the two modules together).

**Assumptions (state them first; examiners award marks for them).**

1. The flow is **steady** and **laminar**, with streamlines parallel to the axis: every particle moves along x only, so the speed depends on r alone, v = v(r).
2. The liquid is **incompressible** and **Newtonian**: η is constant (module 3).
3. The tube is rigid, uniform and horizontal, so gravity gives no net effect along x; end effects at the entrance are ignored (the flow is "fully developed").
4. **No slip at the wall:** v(a) = 0 (module 3; NCERT: fluid in contact with a surface moves with it).
5. The pressure is uniform over any cross-section (no motion across the tube, so no radial pressure difference) and P₁ > P₂. Put ΔP = P₁ − P₂ > 0.

**Coordinates and signs.** x along the axis in the flow direction, r outward from the axis. Then v ≥ 0 and v *decreases* as r grows, so dv/dr < 0.

**Step 1: the fluid cylinder.** Isolate a coaxial cylinder of fluid of radius r and length L (see the diagram). Because the flow is steady, its acceleration is zero, so the forces on it along x sum to zero.

**Step 2: pressure force.** The fluid behind pushes forward with P₁ πr²; the fluid ahead pushes back with P₂ πr². Net forward force:

$$ F_p = (P₁ − P₂) π r² = ΔP π r² $$

**Step 3: viscous force.** The curved surface has area 2πrL. The outer fluid moves more slowly than the cylinder's surface layer and so drags it backward. By Newton's law (module 3) the magnitude is η |dv/dr| × 2πrL. Since dv/dr < 0, the signed force along +x is

$$ F_v = η (dv/dr) (2π r L)   (negative, a backward drag) $$

**Step 4: balance.**

$$ ΔP π r² + η (dv/dr) 2π r L = 0 $$

Divide by 2πrL η:

$$ dv/dr = − ΔP r / (2 η L) $$

The minus sign confirms that speed falls outward.

**Step 5: integrate.** Both sides with respect to r:

$$ v(r) = − ΔP r² / (4 η L) + C $$

**Step 6: boundary condition.** v(a) = 0 gives 0 = −ΔP a² / (4ηL) + C, so C = ΔP a² / (4ηL). Hence

$$ v(r) = ΔP (a² − r²) / (4 η L) $$

**Interpretation.** The profile is a **parabola**: maximum speed on the axis, v_max = ΔP a² / (4ηL), falling to zero at the wall (NCERT Fig. 9.12b shows the same shape). Every cylindrical surface of radius r has one constant speed.

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* ΔP a² / (ηL) = [ML⁻¹T⁻²][L²] / ([ML⁻¹T⁻¹][L]) = LT⁻¹ ✓. *Limits:* ΔP = 0 gives v = 0 (no push, no flow); η → ∞ gives v → 0; at r = a, v = 0 as imposed. *Slope at the wall:* |dv/dr| at r = a is ΔP a / (2ηL), the largest shear, which is why the wall exerts the retarding drag.

### 5. Core: Poiseuille's equation, part 2 (flow rate, resistance and the Ohm analogy)

*Objective:* Integrate over concentric shells to get Q = π ΔP a⁴ / (8 η L), then use it for scaling problems and the electrical analogy.

**Step 7: flow through one shell.** Take a thin ring-shaped shell between r and r + dr. Its cross-section area is 2πr dr (circumference × thickness). Its speed is v(r). The volume crossing it per second is

$$ dQ = v(r) · 2π r dr = [ΔP (a² − r²) / (4ηL)] · 2π r dr $$

**Step 8: add all shells** from the axis (r = 0) to the wall (r = a). Take the constants outside and multiply out:

$$ Q = [π ΔP / (2ηL)] ∫₀ᵃ (a² r − r³) dr $$

Using ∫ r dr = r²/2 and ∫ r³ dr = r⁴/4:

$$ ∫₀ᵃ (a² r − r³) dr = a² · a²/2 − a⁴/4 = a⁴/4 $$

so

$$ Q = π ΔP a⁴ / (8 η L)   (Poiseuille's equation) $$

**Dimension check.** [ML⁻¹T⁻²][L⁴] / ([ML⁻¹T⁻¹][L]) = L³T⁻¹ ✓, the dimension of volume per second.

**Mean speed.** v_mean = Q / (πa²) = ΔP a² / (8ηL) = **v_max / 2**: the parabola's average is half its peak. Limiting cases: ΔP → 0, η → ∞ or a → 0 all give Q → 0; doubling L halves Q.

**The a⁴ law.** Q ∝ a⁴ at fixed ΔP, η, L, because both the area (∝ a²) and the typical speed (∝ a²) grow with a. Halving a cuts Q to 1/16; widening by 10% raises Q by a factor 1.1⁴ = 1.4641. Equivalently, to keep Q fixed, ΔP ∝ 1/a⁴ (this is the 2026 question, module 8).

**Consistency check by force balance.** At the wall the shear stress is η |dv/dr|ₐ = ΔP a / (2L); times the wall area 2πaL it gives ΔP πa², exactly the pressure force on the whole tube cross-section. The wall carries the full push, as it must in steady flow.

**Worked example.** Water, η = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s, in a tube of a = 1.0 mm, L = 0.50 m with ΔP = 2000 Pa. Then Q = π × 2000 × (10⁻³)⁴ / (8 × 10⁻³ × 0.50) = **1.571 × 10⁻⁶ m³ s⁻¹** (94.2 mL per minute); v_max = 1.0 m s⁻¹, v_mean = 0.50 m s⁻¹, and Re = ρ v_mean D / η = 1000 × 0.50 × 2 × 10⁻³ / 10⁻³ = 1000, below about 2000 (module 7), so the laminar assumption is plausible.

**Hydraulic resistance and the Ohm analogy.** Write Poiseuille's equation as ΔP = R_h Q with

$$ R_h = 8 η L / (π a⁴) $$

| Electrical | Fluid (Poiseuille) |
|---|---|
| potential difference V | pressure difference ΔP |
| current I | volume flow rate Q |
| resistance R = ρ_e L / A | R_h = 8ηL / (πa⁴) |
| R ∝ L, ∝ 1/area | R_h ∝ L, ∝ 1/a⁴ (steeper) |

Resistances in **series** add and in **parallel** combine as 1/R = Σ 1/Rᵢ, as in circuits (pressure plays the part of voltage). The analogy is for *laminar* flow only; the a⁴ differs from the a² of a wire. For the water example R_h = 2000 / 1.571 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.273 × 10⁹ Pa s m⁻³.

**NCERT exercise 9.13 (sourced example, extract p. 21).** Glycerine flows steadily through a horizontal tube, L = 1.5 m, a = 1.0 cm, mass flow 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg s⁻¹; ρ = 1.3 × 10³ kg m⁻³, η = 0.83 Pa s. Volume flow Q = 4.0 × 10⁻³ / 1.3 × 10³ = 3.077 × 10⁻⁶ m³ s⁻¹. Then ΔP = 8ηLQ/(πa⁴) = 8 × 0.83 × 1.5 × 3.077 × 10⁻⁶ / (π × 10⁻⁸) = **975 Pa**. Laminar check: v_mean = Q/(πa²) = 9.79 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹ and Re = ρ v_mean D/η = 1.3 × 10³ × 9.79 × 10⁻³ × 0.020 / 0.83 = **0.31**, far below about 2000, so the laminar assumption holds. (NCERT data, our working.)

### 6. Core: Stokes' law and terminal velocity

*Objective:* State Stokes' law, show by dimensional analysis that F ∝ η a v, and derive the terminal velocity of a sphere with buoyancy.

**Stokes' law (NCERT 9.5.1).** A body falling through a fluid drags the neighbouring fluid layers along, creating relative motion between layers, and so feels a retarding viscous force opposite to its velocity. For a sphere of radius a moving slowly at speed v through a fluid of viscosity η:

$$ F = 6 π η a v $$

NCERT states the law and says explicitly that it does *not* derive it. The factor 6π comes from solving the full fluid equations for flow around a sphere (Stokes, 1851; standard knowledge, not derived in the extract), which is beyond this lesson.

**What dimensional analysis proves, and what it cannot.** Assume F depends on η, a and v only, F = k ηˣ aʸ vᶻ with k dimensionless. Dimensions: [MLT⁻²] = [ML⁻¹T⁻¹]ˣ [L]ʸ [LT⁻¹]ᶻ. Match each base dimension:

- M: 1 = x.
- T: −2 = −x − z, so z = 2 − x = 1.
- L: 1 = −x + y + z = −1 + y + 1, so y = 1.

Hence F = k η a v: linear in speed, radius and viscosity (matching NCERT's description that F depends on v, η and a). The number k = 6π needs hydrodynamics. *Validity:* low speeds (small Reynolds number, roughly below 1, standard; flagged), laminar flow, a sphere in a fluid of unlimited extent.

**Terminal velocity (derivation).** A sphere of radius a and density ρₛ falls (down positive) through a fluid of density ρ_f. NCERT writes ρ for the density of the sphere and σ for the density of the fluid (so its (ρ − σ) is our (ρₛ − ρ_f)). Forces:

- weight (down): (4/3) π a³ ρₛ g;
- buoyancy (up, Archimedes): (4/3) π a³ ρ_f g;
- drag (up): 6 π η a v.

The speed rises until acceleration stops, which is when the net force is zero:

$$ 6π η a v_t + (4/3) π a³ ρ_f g = (4/3) π a³ ρₛ g $$

Move the buoyancy term across:

$$ 6π η a v_t = (4/3) π a³ (ρₛ − ρ_f) g $$

Divide by 6πηa:

$$ v_t = 2 a² (ρₛ − ρ_f) g / (9 η) $$

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* [L²][ML⁻³][LT⁻²] / [ML⁻¹T⁻¹] = LT⁻¹ ✓. *Limits:* ρₛ = ρ_f gives v_t = 0 (neutral buoyancy); ρₛ < ρ_f gives a negative v_t (the sphere *rises*, as a bubble does); η → ∞ gives v_t → 0. *Scaling:* v_t ∝ a² and ∝ 1/η.

**How the speed builds up (simplified).** Ignoring the extra "added mass" of fluid dragged along (standard refinement, flagged), Newton's second law gives m dv/dt = F_net − 6πηa v. The solution is v(t) = v_t (1 − e^(−t/t_c)) with t_c = m / (6πηa) = 2a²ρₛ / (9η). The graph rises from 0 and flattens at v_t; after three time constants it is within 5% of v_t (1 − e⁻³ = 0.950).

**Worked example (original numbers).** A steel ball (ρₛ = 7800 kg m⁻³, a = 1.5 mm) in glycerine (ρ_f = 1260 kg m⁻³, η = 0.83 Pa s): v_t = 2 × (1.5 × 10⁻³)² × (7800 − 1260) × 9.8 / (9 × 0.83) = **3.86 × 10⁻² m s⁻¹**. Check: Re = ρ_f v_t (2a) / η = 0.176, well below 1, so Stokes' law is acceptable; t_c = 4.7 ms. NCERT Example 9.9 computes η = 0.99 Pa s for a copper ball (checked).

### 7. Master: Reynolds number and critical velocity

*Objective:* Obtain Re = ρ v D / η by dimensional analysis, interpret it, and use it to judge whether Poiseuille and Stokes formulae apply.

**The question.** NCERT says steady flow turns turbulent beyond a critical speed, but gives no formula for it. The critical speed must depend on the fluid and the pipe. Assume the critical speed v_c depends on the viscosity η, the density ρ and the pipe diameter D only (a standard assumption, flagged). Write v_c = k ηˣ ρʸ Dᶻ with k dimensionless:

$$ [LT⁻¹] = [ML⁻¹T⁻¹]ˣ [ML⁻³]ʸ [L]ᶻ $$

- M: 0 = x + y, so y = −x.
- T: −1 = −x, so x = 1 and y = −1.
- L: 1 = −x − 3y + z = −1 + 3 + z, so z = −1.

Therefore

$$ v_c = k η / (ρ D),   i.e.   k = ρ v_c D / η = Re_c $$

**Definition.** The **Reynolds number** is

$$ Re = ρ v D / η $$

with v the mean speed and D the pipe diameter (for a sphere, the sphere's diameter or radius, by convention). *Dimensions:* [ML⁻³][LT⁻¹][L] / [ML⁻¹T⁻¹] = M⁰L⁰T⁰ ✓: **dimensionless**, so its value is the same in any unit system.

**Meaning.** Re compares inertia with viscosity. Inertial forces scale as ρv² (per unit area) and viscous stresses as ηv/D; their ratio is ρvD/η. Large Re: inertia wins, small disturbances grow into eddies (turbulence). Small Re: viscosity damps disturbances (laminar). Kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ gives Re = vD/ν (standard).

**Thresholds (standard, not in the extracts; flagged).** For flow in a straight circular pipe, textbooks commonly quote Re below about 2000 as laminar, above about 4000 as turbulent, and a transitional range between; the exact values depend on how smooth the pipe and entrance are. Hence v_c = Re_c η / (ρD), with Re_c ≈ 2000 as a working figure.

**Worked example.** Water (η = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s, ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³) in a pipe of D = 1.0 cm: v_c = 2000 × 10⁻³ / (1000 × 0.010) = **0.20 m s⁻¹**. Halve the diameter and the critical speed doubles; use a fluid ten times more viscous and it becomes ten times larger.

**Dynamical similarity.** Flows of similar shape with the same Re behave alike, which is why scale models are tested (NCERT exercise 9.3(e); at equal Re a smaller model needs a higher speed, standard result, flagged).

**Checking assumptions in practice.** Poiseuille (module 5) assumes laminar flow: always compute Re afterwards. NCERT exercise 9.13 asks you to do this check. Stokes' law (module 6) assumes small Re based on the sphere. The glycerine example gave 0.18; a drizzle drop of radius 0.05 mm in air falling at the Stokes speed of 0.30 m s⁻¹ gives Re ≈ 2, already marginal (computed with ρ_air = 1.2 kg m⁻³, η_air = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s, round values).

**Common error.** Using the radius instead of the diameter changes Re by a factor of 2; state which length you use. For 2019 part (i), the short answer "Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, tells whether the flow is laminar or turbulent, and allows scaling between model and prototype" earns the marks of part (i).

The increase is **52.4%** (about 52%). Trap: answering "40%" by multiplying 10% by four, or 34% from 1 − 0.6561 (that is the *fall in flow* at fixed pressure). State that the answer rests on laminar Poiseuille flow and a rigid, uniform artery, as the question allows.

**2019 Q1(b), 10 marks.** (i) Reynolds number: Re = ρvD/η, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. The kinetic energy per kg, ½v², therefore does **not change**: strictly, Δ(KE) = **0**. The 6.25 × 10⁻³ J kg⁻¹ is not gained as kinetic energy; it is the work done against viscous friction and is lost as heat. NCERT (p. 9) says Bernoulli's equation neglects the energy lost to internal friction. Trap: writing only one number with no explanation, or claiming that the speed rises because the pressure falls. **2023 Q4(b), 15 marks (practice target about 18 minutes).** *Define streamline flow; using the equation of continuity for an isotropic fluid, find the different components of total energy per unit volume.*

Part 1: module 2 (steady flow; streamline definition; two streamlines cannot cross). Part 2, answered from continuity plus work-energy (NCERT 9.4): in time Δt the same volume ΔV = A₁v₁Δt = A₂v₂Δt (continuity, incompressible fluid) enters at point 1 and leaves at 2. Work done on it by the pressures: (P₁ − P₂)ΔV. Gain in kinetic energy: ½ρΔV(v₂² − v₁²). Gain in potential energy: ρgΔV(h₂ − h₁). Equate and divide by ΔV:

$$ P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂ $$

The three components of the energy per unit volume are the **pressure energy P**, the **kinetic energy ½ρv²** and the **potential energy ρgh**, and their sum stays constant along a streamline for steady, incompressible, *non-viscous* flow (NCERT 9.12-9.13). The differential form of continuity for a compressible fluid, ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρ**v**) = 0, reduces to ∇·**v** = 0 for incompressible flow (standard; flagged). The full Bernoulli lesson is *to come on Day 13, not yet written*. Dimension check: Pa = J m⁻³ ✓. Trap: forgetting that viscosity breaks the constancy; with viscosity the total falls along the flow.

### Notes: Definitions and symbols

- Streamline: a curve whose tangent at each point is along the fluid velocity there; in steady flow it is the particle path and two streamlines never cross.
- Steady flow: the velocity at each fixed point does not change in time.
- Laminar flow: smooth layers slide over each other; turbulent flow: irregular eddies above the critical speed.
- Coefficient of viscosity η = (shear stress)/(rate of shear strain); SI unit Pa s = N s m⁻² = kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹; CGS unit poise; 1 Pa s = 10 poise; dimension [ML⁻¹T⁻¹].
- Velocity gradient du/dy: the change of layer speed per unit perpendicular distance, in s⁻¹; equals the rate of shear strain.
- Kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ (standard), dimension [L²T⁻¹].
- Reynolds number Re = ρvD/η, dimensionless.
- Hydraulic resistance R_h = ΔP/Q = 8ηL/(πa⁴) (laminar tube).

### Notes: Solid versus fluid in shear

- Link back to PH-022 module 2: n relates stress to strain; η relates stress to strain rate.

### Notes: Key equations

- Continuity (incompressible): A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ = Q.
- Newton's law of viscosity: F = ηAv/h; τ = η du/dy.
- Poiseuille: v(r) = ΔP(a² − r²)/(4ηL); v_max = ΔP a²/(4ηL); v_mean = v_max/2; Q = πΔPa⁴/(8ηL).
- Stokes: F = 6πηav; terminal velocity v_t = 2a²(ρₛ − ρ_f)g/(9η).
- Reynolds: Re = ρvD/η; critical speed v_c = Re_c η/(ρD).
- Bernoulli (non-viscous, steady, incompressible): P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline (NCERT 9.13).

### Notes: Poiseuille derivation in nine lines

- Assume steady laminar incompressible Newtonian flow, horizontal tube, no slip v(a) = 0, v = v(r).
- Cylinder of radius r: pressure force ΔP πr² forward.
- Viscous force on its surface: η (dv/dr) 2πrL (negative).
- Steady, so the sum is zero: dv/dr = −ΔP r/(2ηL).
- Integrate: v = −ΔP r²/(4ηL) + C.
- Apply v(a) = 0: C = ΔP a²/(4ηL), so v = ΔP(a² − r²)/(4ηL).
- Shell: dQ = v 2πr dr.
- Q = [πΔP/(2ηL)] ∫₀ᵃ (a²r − r³)dr = [πΔP/(2ηL)](a⁴/4).
- Q = πΔPa⁴/(8ηL); check units L³T⁻¹ and the wall force balance ΔP πa².

### Notes: Temperature behaviour of viscosity

- Liquids: η falls as T rises (water 1.0 to 0.3 mPa s from 20 to 100 °C, NCERT Table 9.2).
- Gases: η rises as T rises (air 0.017 to 0.019 mPa s from 0 to 40 °C).
- NCERT: relative viscosity of blood to water stays constant between 0 and 37 °C.
- Standard forms, flagged: liquids η ≈ A exp(B/T); dilute gases η ∝ √T.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Poiseuille the equation versus poiseuille (Pl, NCERT's name for Pa s) versus poise (CGS).
- Viscosity η (a material property, Pa s) versus kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ.
- Velocity gradient versus velocity: the law uses the gradient, so a fast but uniform flow has no viscous stress.
- Radius versus diameter: Q has a⁴ (radius) while Re uses D (diameter).
- Steady flow does not mean uniform flow: steady is constant in time at a point.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits of the laws

- Poiseuille: laminar, fully developed, rigid tube, Newtonian fluid; fails near the entrance and in turbulence (entrance length not covered, flagged).
- Stokes: sphere, low Reynolds number, unbounded fluid.
- Bernoulli: neglects viscous loss (NCERT 9.4, Points to ponder 6), so it cannot give the pressure drop along a uniform tube.
- Blood is not a simple Newtonian fluid (standard; flagged), so Poiseuille is only an approximation for arteries.

    - **task:** Derive Poiseuille's equation for the steady laminar flow of a viscous liquid through a horizontal tube, stating the assumptions and the boundary condition. Practice target, not an official allotment: 15 marks, about 18 minutes.
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: steady, laminar, incompressible, Newtonian fluid; rigid horizontal tube of radius a and length L; flow along x with speed v(r) only; pressure P₁ at x = 0 and P₂ at x = L, uniform over each section, ΔP = P₁ − P₂ > 0; no slip, v(a) = 0.
      - Signs: x along the flow, r outward from the axis; v ≥ 0 and dv/dr < 0.
      - Take a coaxial fluid cylinder of radius r, length L; steady flow means zero net force along x.
      - Pressure force forward: (P₁ − P₂) πr² = ΔP πr².
      - Viscous force on the curved surface (area 2πrL), by Newton's law, is η (dv/dr) 2πrL, negative (backward).
      - Balance: ΔP πr² + η (dv/dr) 2πrL = 0, so dv/dr = −ΔP r/(2ηL).
      - Integrate: v = −ΔP r²/(4ηL) + C. Boundary condition v(a) = 0 gives C = ΔP a²/(4ηL), so v(r) = ΔP(a² − r²)/(4ηL), a parabola with v_max = ΔPa²/(4ηL) on the axis.

      - Mean speed Q/(πa²) = ΔPa²/(8ηL) = v_max/2; resistance R_h = ΔP/Q = 8ηL/(πa⁴), the analogue of Ohm's law.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions of Q: [ML⁻¹T⁻²][L⁴]/([ML⁻¹T⁻¹][L]) = L³T⁻¹.

      - Forgetting 2πr in the shell area.
