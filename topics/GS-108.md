### Bridge: Bridge: right and left turning, clockwise and anticlockwise

Currents curve because the Earth spins. NCERT states the rule: moving water turns to the **right** of its path in the Northern Hemisphere and to the **left** in the Southern Hemisphere. 'Right of its path' means as you would see it if you were travelling along with the water.

Take water flowing north in the Northern Hemisphere: its right is east, so it bends towards the east. Water flowing south in the Southern Hemisphere bends to its left, which is also towards the east when it faces south. This is why the great gyres turn **clockwise** in the north (west edge up, top eastward, east edge down, bottom westward) and **anticlockwise** in the south.

A clock face helps: clockwise runs 12 to 3 to 6 to 9, so on a map the north side of a gyre flows east, the east side flows south, the south side flows west and the west side flows north. Check any gyre you draw against this order, and remember the North Indian Ocean is the exception because the monsoon winds change its direction.

### 1. Orientation: the ocean's rivers, and the five questions of this lesson

*Objective:* Define an ocean current, separate it from waves, tides and upwelling, and list the five questions the lesson answers.

## Meaning

An **ocean current** is the continuous flow of a huge amount of water in a definite direction (NCERT kegy213, PDF p1). NCERT likens currents to river flow in the oceans: a regular volume of water in a definite path (PDF p4). Three other movements are easy to confuse with it:

- **Waves** move energy; the water itself only circles on the spot as the wave passes (PDF p1).
- **Tides** are the rise and fall of the sea level, once or twice a day (PDF pp1-2); see Day 17 lesson GS-109.
- **Upwelling** and sinking are *vertical* movements: cold water rising from below or surface water sinking (PDF p1). A current is mainly *horizontal*.

## The five questions

1. **Why** does water flow? The forces: heat, wind, gravity, Earth's rotation, density (modules 3 and 4).
2. **Where** does it flow? The circuits in the Atlantic, Pacific and Indian Oceans (modules 5 and 6).
3. **Which** are warm and which are cold, and where do they sit? The placement rule and its edge cases (module 7).
4. **What** do they do to climate, navigation and fish? (module 8)
5. **Where** does cold water rise, and what is the El Nino link? (module 9)

Module 10 turns this into answers for the four official questions.

## One picture to carry

Each ocean basin has a great loop of water called a **gyre**: clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, anticlockwise in the Southern (Leong, PDF p153). The North Indian Ocean is the exception, because its currents reverse with the monsoon (Leong, PDF pp155-156).

Winds are in Day 19 lesson GS-104, El Nino in Day 21 lesson GS-106, and salinity, tides and marine life in Day 17 lesson GS-109. ### 2. Foundation: warm and cold currents, drift, gyre and other words

*Objective:* Define surface and deep currents, warm and cold currents, drift, strength and gyre, and read directions such as westward or poleward correctly.

## Surface and deep currents

NCERT (PDF p4) sorts currents by depth. **Surface currents** hold about 10 per cent of ocean water, in the upper 400 m. **Deep water currents** hold the other 90 per cent and move because of differences in density and gravity; deep waters sink into the basins at high latitudes, where the cold makes them dense.

## Warm and cold currents

"Warm" and "cold" are **relative**: a current is warm or cold compared with the water it enters. Leong (PDF p151) says currents that flow from equatorial regions polewards have a higher surface temperature and are warm; those that flow from polar regions equatorwards are cold. NCERT agrees: cold currents bring cold water into warm-water areas and warm currents bring warm water into cold-water areas. The word describes the contrast with the surroundings, not an absolute temperature.

## Drift, strength and speed

NCERT (PDF p4) uses **drift** for the *speed* of a current, measured in **knots**. The **strength** of a current is its speed, and a fast current is strong. Currents are strongest at the surface, often over five knots, and slow at depth, below 0.5 knots; most are 5 knots or less. One knot is 1.852 km per hour, so 5 knots is about 9.3 km/h and 0.5 knots about 0.9 km/h (arithmetic, standard conversion). Leong gives the Gulf Stream about 5 km per hour, around 2.7 knots, which fits.

**Two meanings of 'drift'.** Leong also uses *drift* in names, such as North Atlantic Drift and West Wind Drift, for a broad, slow, wind-driven flow. Read the context.

## Gyre

Water moving over a large ocean basin is turned by the Earth's rotation: to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern (NCERT, PDF p4). The result is a circular flow round the basin, a **gyre**. Leong gives the rotation sense: clockwise in the north (Gulf Stream Drift, Canaries Current) and anticlockwise in the south (Brazilian Current, West Wind Drift).

## Direction words

A **westward** current flows *towards* the west. **Poleward** means towards the nearest pole and **equatorward** towards the equator. The **eastern** side of an ocean is next to the continent on its east.

The North and South **Equatorial Currents** flow westward in the tropics; the **Equatorial Counter Current** flows eastward between them (Leong, PDF p155).

### 3. Core: the forces that start the flow - heat, wind, gravity and rotation

*Objective:* Explain NCERT's four primary forces and show step by step how the winds and the Earth's rotation build a gyre.

## NCERT's two groups of forces

NCERT (PDF p4) says currents are influenced by **primary forces**, which start the water moving, and **secondary forces**, which shape the flow. The primary forces are:

1. **Heating by solar energy.** Warm water expands, so near the equator the ocean surface is about 8 cm higher than in the middle latitudes. This gives a very slight slope, and water tends to flow down it.
2. **Wind.** Wind blowing over the sea drags the surface water along by friction.
3. **Gravity.** It pulls piled-up water down, creating the differences in gradient.
4. **Coriolis force.** It turns moving water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. The large accumulations of water and the flow round them are the **gyres**.

Density is the secondary force, taken up in module 4.

## Which force is the main one?

Leong (PDF p152) judges the **planetary winds** probably the dominant influence, and offers the North Indian Ocean as the strongest evidence, since currents there reverse with the monsoon. NCERT agrees in its own words: the oceanic circulation roughly corresponds to the atmospheric circulation (PDF p4).

## Winds that push

Between the equator and the tropics the **trade winds** drive equatorial water westward (the North and South Equatorial Currents). In the middle latitudes the **westerlies** drive water eastward: Leong says they send the Gulf Stream towards Europe as the North Atlantic Drift (PDF pp151-152). See Day 19 lesson GS-104 for the wind belts.

## Building a gyre in five steps

1. Trade winds push surface water **westward** along the tropics.
2. The western side of the ocean is closed by a continent, so the water piles up there and turns **poleward** along the western edge (Leong: currents flowing westward pile up against the coasts and are deflected north and south).
3. In the middle latitudes the westerlies push the water **eastward**.
4. On the eastern side the water turns **equatorward** along the eastern edge.
5. The loop closes at the equatorial current.

Run the five steps in the north and you get a clockwise gyre; run them in the south and the sense is anticlockwise. In the North Atlantic the five named currents of the loop are the North Equatorial Current, the Florida Current, the Gulf Stream, the North Atlantic Drift and the Canaries Current (Leong, PDF p153).

## Standard knowledge (verify)

Textbooks add that the western edge of a gyre is usually narrow, deep and fast (the Gulf Stream and Kuroshio) and the eastern edge broad and slow. The extracts support only the Gulf Stream being one of the strongest currents (Leong gives a width of 56 to 160 km), so treat the general rule as standard knowledge.

### 4. Core: density, salinity, land shape and the monsoon

*Objective:* Explain how temperature and salinity differences, the shape of land masses and the monsoon winds act on currents, and distinguish surface from deep circulation.

## Temperature and density

Warm water is lighter and rises; cold water is denser and sinks (NCERT, PDF p4; Leong, PDF p152). So warm equatorial waters creep poleward along the surface while the heavy cold polar waters creep equatorward along the bottom. NCERT adds that warm-water currents travel out from the equator along the surface to replace the cold water that sinks at the poles. Leong gives a measure of this: almost two-thirds of the water the Gulf Stream brings to the Arctic is returned to the tropics each year by dense cold polar water creeping south in the depths (PDF p153).

## Salinity

Salty water is denser than fresh water (salinity is in Day 17 lesson GS-109). Leong (PDF p152) says low-salinity water flows over the surface of high-salinity water, while high-salinity water flows at the bottom towards water of low salinity. His example is the Mediterranean: less saline Atlantic water flows in at the surface, and denser bottom water flows out to the Atlantic to compensate.

## Shape of the land masses

Currents flowing west pile up against the coasts, and are deflected north and south, even divided into branches (Leong, PDF p153). Examples in Leong: the South Equatorial Current divides on reaching Madagascar and the East African coast; at the north-east 'shoulder' of Brazil (Cape Sao Roque) it splits into the Cayenne Current and the Brazilian Current. A coastline is therefore part of the cause as well as the guide.

## The monsoon

NCERT (PDF p5) says that in regions of pronounced monsoonal flow, the monsoon winds influence the current movements. Leong describes it for the North Indian Ocean (PDF pp155-156): in summer, from June to October, the dominant wind is the South-West Monsoon and the water is blown from the south-west as the **South-West Monsoon Drift**; in winter, from December, the North-East Monsoon blows the water from the north-east as the **North-East Monsoon Drift**. The currents there thus reverse completely, which is the clearest proof that wind controls the surface circulation. The monsoon itself is in Day 21 lesson GS-106.

## Deep water

The deep 90 per cent moves slowly under density and gravity and sinks at high latitudes where the water is cold and dense (NCERT, PDF p4); it is often called the global overturning or conveyor circulation (standard term, not in the extracts; verify).

## Summary table

| Cause | What it does | Example in the extracts |
|---|---|---|
| Heating | Warm water expands, slight slope | Equator about 8 cm higher (NCERT) |
| Planetary winds | Push surface water | Trades west, westerlies east |
| Rotation | Right in north, left in south | Clockwise and anticlockwise gyres |
| Temperature | Warm rises, cold sinks | Gulf Stream and returning polar water |
| Salinity | Dense salt water sinks | Mediterranean inflow and outflow |

### 5. Core: the Atlantic circuits, north and south

*Objective:* Trace the North and South Atlantic gyres from Leong's description with direction, warm or cold and coast, including the cold inflows and the Sargasso Sea.

## North Atlantic (clockwise)

All details here are from Leong (PDF pp153-154).

- **North Equatorial Current** (warm, flows west) and the Cayenne Current (from the splitting of the South Equatorial Current at north-east Brazil) head north-west into the Caribbean Sea.
- Part enters the Gulf of Mexico and leaves through the Florida Strait between Florida and Cuba as the **Florida Current**; the rest flows north, east of the Antilles, to join the **Gulf Stream** off the south-eastern United States.
- The Gulf Stream is one of the strongest currents, 56 to 160 km wide and 610 m deep, flowing at about 5 km per hour. It hugs the American coast to Cape Hatteras (35 degrees N), where the westerlies and the Earth's rotation turn it east.
- It reaches Europe as the **North Atlantic Drift**, moving about 16 km per day for over a thousand kilometres, and fans out: eastward to Britain, northward to the Arctic, southward along the Iberian coast as the cool **Canaries Current**.
- The Canaries Current flows south and merges with the North Equatorial Current: the clockwise loop is closed.
- **Sargasso Sea**: the still water in the middle of the loop, where floating seaweed collects.

The cold **Labrador Current** from the Arctic drifts south-east between west Greenland and Baffin Island and meets the warm Gulf Stream off Newfoundland (Leong). Leong says icebergs carried south by the Labrador Current melt as far south as 50 degrees N, as printed (the Grand Banks lie at about 43 to 46 degrees N and icebergs commonly drift south of 45 degrees N; verify against an atlas).

**Irminger Current.** Leong describes it as cold water drifting south-west between west Iceland and Greenland and cooling the North Atlantic Drift. Standard atlases call it a warm branch of the North Atlantic Drift off south-west Iceland, and name the cold south-west flow the East Greenland Current (verify). For Prelims treat the Irminger as warm.

## South Atlantic (anticlockwise)

The South Equatorial Current splits at Cape Sao Roque. One branch turns south as the warm **Brazilian Current**, whose deep blue water Leong contrasts with the muddy Amazon water. At about 40 degrees S the westerlies and the Earth's rotation push it east, where it merges with the cold **West Wind Drift** as the **South Atlantic Current**. On the west coast of Africa it is diverted north as the cold **Benguela Current**, the counterpart of the Canaries Current, and it flows north-west with the south-east trades to rejoin the South Equatorial Current. Between the North and South Equatorial Currents the **Equatorial Counter Current** flows east.

The cold **Falkland Current** (named in Leong's Fig. 12.10; standard knowledge, verify) flows north along Patagonia and meets the Brazilian Current near the Rio de la Plata, at about 38 to 40 degrees S. Leong says the southern westerlies 'drive the West Wind Drift equatorwards' as the Peruvian and Benguela Currents; the drift itself flows west to east, and its branches turn north along the western coasts of South America and Africa.

## The Atlantic at a glance

|---|---|---|
| Florida, Gulf Stream | North, west | Warm |
| North Atlantic Drift | North, east, high latitude | Warm |
| Canaries | North, east | Cool |
| Labrador | North, west | Cold |

| Brazilian | South, west | Warm |

| West Wind Drift | South, all round | Cold |

### 6. Core: the Pacific and Indian Ocean circuits

*Objective:* Trace the North and South Pacific gyres and the Indian Ocean circuits, including the monsoon reversal, with direction and warm or cold for each current.

## North Pacific (clockwise; Leong, PDF p155)

- The north-east trades blow the **North Equatorial Current** west; an **Equatorial Counter Current** runs eastward opposite to it. Because the Pacific is wider and has no obstructing land mass, the volume of water is much greater than in the Atlantic.
- Off the Philippines and Taiwan the current enters the East China Sea as the warm **Kuroshio** (Kuro Siwo, Japan Current).
- Its water goes poleward as the **North Pacific Drift**, which keeps the ports of the Alaskan coast ice-free in winter.
- The cold **Bering Current** (called the Alaskan Current by Leong) creeps south from the Bering Strait, is joined by the Okhotsk Current and meets the warm Japan Current as the cold **Oyashio** off Hokkaido. Cold water sinks below the warmer drift.
- Part drifts east as the cool **Californian Current** along the western United States and joins the North Equatorial Current: the loop is closed.

## South Pacific (anticlockwise)

The south-east trades drive the **South Equatorial Current**, which flows south along the Queensland coast as the warm **East Australian Current**. Under the westerlies in the Tasman Sea it turns east towards New Zealand and merges with part of the cold West Wind Drift as the **South Pacific Current**. The southern tip of Chile obstructs it, and it turns north along the western coast of South America as the cold **Humboldt or Peruvian Current**, and finally links with the South Equatorial Current. Leong adds that the cold water chills any onshore wind, so the Chilean and Peruvian coasts are practically rainless, while the water is rich in minute plants and animals that attract shoals of fish and sea birds; their droppings form **guano**, a valuable fertiliser (module 9).

## Indian Ocean

- **South Indian Ocean**: the Equatorial Current turns south past Madagascar (Leong writes Malagasy) as the warm Agulhas or Mozambique Current (naming as Leong gives it; verify), merges with the West Wind Drift flowing east, and turns equatorward as the cold **West Australian Current**, completing an anticlockwise circuit.
- **North Indian Ocean**: no fixed loop. In summer (June to October), when the south-west monsoon dominates, the water is blown from the south-west as the South-West Monsoon Drift; in winter (from December) the north-east monsoon blows it from the north-east. Leong's Fig. 12.12 also marks a Somali Current, which the text does not discuss; its summer behaviour and upwelling are standard knowledge to be verified.

Name check: Leong uses 'Alaskan Current' for the cold south-flowing water from the Bering Strait, but other books use Alaska Current for a warm current along the Alaskan coast; read the description in a question.

### 7. Core: the placement rule for warm and cold currents, and its edge cases

*Objective:* State NCERT's rule for where warm and cold currents lie, test it against named currents, and handle the exceptions.

## NCERT's rule (PDF p4)

- **Cold currents** lie usually on the **west coasts of continents in the low and middle latitudes** (true in both hemispheres) and on the **east coasts in higher latitudes in the Northern Hemisphere**.
- **Warm currents** lie usually on the **east coasts of continents in the low and middle latitudes** (both hemispheres) and, in the Northern Hemisphere, on the **west coasts in high latitudes**.

NCERT p5 states the same pattern as climate: tropical and subtropical west coasts are bordered by cool water (except close to the equator), and mid and high latitude west coasts by warm water.

## Edge cases (do not memorise blindly)

1. **Equatorial currents** run in mid-ocean, not along a coast, so the coast rule does not apply; the Equatorial Counter Current flows east against them.
2. **The North Indian Ocean** reverses with the monsoon, so no fixed coast rule applies.
3. **The Southern Hemisphere at high latitudes**: NCERT states its high-latitude clause only for the Northern Hemisphere. In the south, the cold West Wind Drift runs unbroken round the globe, because there is almost no land at those latitudes; and the cold Falkland Current runs north along the east coast of South America in mid to high southern latitudes, so the east-coast-warm clause holds only for low and middle latitudes (Falkland: standard knowledge; verify).
4. **Sargasso Sea** is a still area in the middle of the North Atlantic, with no perceptible current (Leong).

## West and east sections of an ocean

Think of one ocean with continents on both sides. The **western section** of the ocean touches the continent on its west, whose coast is the continent's *east* coast. In the tropics and subtropics that coast has the warm current, so the western section of the ocean is warmer. In the mid latitudes the westerlies carry warm water to the *eastern* section, along the west coast of the continent on that side. So, in the tropical zone, the ocean's western section is warmer, and in the temperate zone the eastern section is warmer (module 10 uses this).

### 8. Core: effects on climate, fog, navigation and fishing

*Objective:* Explain with named cases how currents change temperature, rainfall, fog, ice and fish, and use Leong's paired stations as evidence.

## Temperature

Coastlands with a warm current offshore have mild climates for their latitude; those with a cold current are cooler (Leong, PDF p156). The effect is strongest with onshore wind. Examples:

- The British Isles, which Leong says are some 10 to 16 degrees C warmer than the average for their latitudes because westerlies bring the North Atlantic Drift's warmth ashore (Leong's figure; verify). The Norwegian coast is ice-free all year even at 60 to 70 degrees N.
- The Labrador coast, in the same latitude as the British Isles, is frozen for most of the winter because of the cold Labrador Current and the icy winds from the Canadian interior; ports of eastern Canada even at 45 degrees N are icebound for almost half the year (Leong, PDF pp150, 156).

Leong's pairs give a feel for the size of the effect (printed values; the book's latitudes for Halifax are unclear, so only the contrast is used):

| Pair | Warm-current coast | Cold-current coast | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coldest-month temperature | Victoria 3.9 degrees C | Halifax minus 4.4 degrees C | 8.3 degrees C |
| July temperature at similar latitudes | Charleston 27.2 degrees C (Gulf Stream) | San Diego 19.4 degrees C (Californian) | 7.8 degrees C |
| Coldest month at 23 S | Rio de Janeiro 20 degrees C (Brazilian) | Walvis Bay 15 degrees C (Benguela) | 5 degrees C |

## Rainfall and aridity

Onshore winds over warm currents bring rain, especially against mountains (western Britain, Norway). Winds over cold currents are dry: they are cooled, lose moisture and are heated again over land, so the Californian, Chilean and Peruvian coasts are dry (Leong, PDF p156). NCERT (PDF p5) adds that tropical west coasts bordered by cool water have fog but are generally arid, while warm currents along tropical east coasts give warm rainy climates. This is one reason hot deserts sit on west coasts (Day 23 lesson GS-101 module 6).

## Fog

Fog forms where warm and cold currents meet, because warm moist air is cooled by the colder air over the cold current. Leong cites the Labrador and Newfoundland coasts (Labrador Current against Gulf Stream) and the Western Pacific around Japan (Kuro Siwo against Oya Siwo). Summer onshore winds also make fog off California and Peru (PDF p157).

## Navigation

The Labrador Current brings icebergs south until they melt, and a warm drift keeps Norwegian and Alaskan ports open in winter (Leong, PDF pp150, 155).

## Fish

NCERT (PDF p5): the mixing of warm and cold currents replenishes oxygen and favours the growth of plankton, the primary food of fish, so the best fishing grounds lie in these mixing zones. Leong (PDF pp147, 157) names the Grand Banks off Newfoundland (cod), the seas round Japan and the Mauritanian shelf, whose cool water comes from the Canary Current. The Grand Banks lie off Newfoundland where the Labrador Current meets the Gulf Stream, and Japan lies where the Kuro Siwo meets the Oya Siwo; the books give both facts, so the link is an inference worth stating with care.

### 9. Master: upwelling, fishing grounds and the El Nino link

*Objective:* Define upwelling, explain why it feeds fisheries, state the simple NCERT cause and the standard fuller cause (flagged), and link it to El Nino.

## What upwelling is

NCERT (kegy213, PDF p1) lists the **upwelling of cold water from the subsurface** and the **sinking of surface water** as vertical motion in the ocean. In kegy212 (PDF p5), on the temperature of ocean water, NCERT gives the simple cause: **winds blowing from the land towards the oceans drive warm surface water away from the coast, and cold water rises from below to take its place**. The opposite case, onshore winds, piles warm water against the coast and raises its temperature.

## From cold water to fish

Water that rises from depth carries dissolved nutrients and is cold. In sunlight the tiny plant plankton multiply; animal plankton eat them, and fish and sea birds follow (Leong, PDF pp151-157). The Leong case is the **Humboldt or Peruvian Current**: the region is rich in minute plants and animals, which attract huge shoals of fish and millions of sea birds whose droppings form guano. Chilean and Peruvian coasts are also practically rainless because the cold water chills the wind (module 8). The marine-life side is in Day 17 lesson GS-109 (productivity).

## The fuller cause (standard knowledge; verify)

NCERT and Leong give no mechanism beyond the simple one. Standard texts explain it with the Earth's rotation: along a west coast with winds blowing towards the equator, rotation turns the surface water to the left of the wind in the Southern Hemisphere and to the right in the Northern, so in both cases the surface water moves away from the coast and cold water rises. Textbooks also add upwelling at the equator and off other west-coast zones such as California, north-west Africa and south-west Africa (the Benguela region). These names are not in the extracts; treat them as standard knowledge and check them against an atlas.

## El Nino and the cold current

Day 21 lesson GS-106 (the El Nino section) records NCERT's account: in an El Nino the warm water of the central Pacific drifts towards South America and replaces the cool Peruvian current off the coast. The upwelling is weakened, so plankton and fish decline, and the dry coast can have heavy rain. For this lesson the exam link is: **El Nino = warm water replaces the cold Peruvian current; upwelling and fisheries are damaged**.

### Notes: Effects of currents as cause and effect

- Warm current + onshore wind -> mild climate, rain on windward mountains, ice-free ports (British Isles, Norway, Alaskan coast).
- Cold current + onshore wind -> cool, dry, foggy coast and nearby desert (California, Peru, Chile, Namib).
- Warm meets cold -> fog and plankton -> rich fishing ground (Newfoundland, Japan); icebergs carried south by the Labrador Current.
- Wind off the land -> warm surface water pushed away -> cold, nutrient-rich water rises -> plankton -> fish -> birds -> guano (Peru).
- El Nino -> warm water replaces the cold Peruvian current -> upwelling weakens -> fish decline (GS-106).

### Notes: Upwelling and fishing grounds

- Winds blow from land to sea, or along a west coast towards the equator (NCERT simple cause; fuller cause is standard knowledge, verify).
- Surface water moves away from the coast; cold, nutrient-rich water rises from below.
- Plant plankton multiply in sunlight, animal plankton and fish follow, then sea birds; guano forms on dry coastal cliffs and islands (Leong, Peru).
- Other routes to a rich fishery: shallow shelves (Grand Banks, North Sea, Sunda Shelf) and mixing of warm and cold currents (NCERT).
- El Nino: warm water replaces the cold Peruvian current and the chain weakens.

### Notes: Speeds and sizes quoted in the extracts

- Surface currents about 10 per cent of ocean water in the upper 400 m; deep currents about 90 per cent (NCERT p4).
- Surface speed often over 5 knots (about 9.3 km/h); below 0.5 knots (about 0.9 km/h) at depth (NCERT p4).
- Equator about 8 cm higher than the middle latitudes (NCERT p4).
- Gulf Stream: 56 to 160 km wide, 610 m deep, about 5 km/h (about 2.7 knots); North Atlantic Drift about 16 km a day, about 0.7 km/h (Leong p153).
- Almost two-thirds of the water brought to the Arctic by the Gulf Stream returns annually in cold deep water (Leong p153).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Wave (energy moves) against current (water moves on) against tide (rise and fall) against upwelling (vertical rise of cold water).
- Warm current (flowing from the tropics poleward) against cool or cold current (flowing equatorward); both are relative.
- 'Drift' as speed (NCERT) against 'drift' as a named broad current (Leong).
- Gulf Stream (strong, narrow, along the American coast) against North Atlantic Drift (broad, towards Europe).
- Labrador Current (cold, from the north) against Gulf Stream (warm); fog and fish where they meet.
- Upwelling (cold nutrient water rises) against El Nino (warm water replaces it).
- Equatorial Counter Current (flows east) against the equatorial currents (flow west).

- **coreConcepts:**

  - Heating, wind, gravity and the Earth's rotation start the flow; density, land shape and the monsoon shape it.
  - Gyres turn clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern; the North Indian Ocean reverses with the monsoon.
  - Cold currents lie mostly on tropical and middle-latitude west coasts; warm currents on east coasts there.
  - Upwelling brings cold nutrient-rich water up and makes rich fisheries; El Nino weakens it off Peru.

  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CURRENT-FISHING (verified):** NCERT: mixing of warm and cold currents replenishes oxygen and favours the growth of plankton, the primary food for fish, and the best fishing grounds of the world exist mainly in these mixing zones.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-ELNINO-INDIA (verified):** NCERT: El Nino is a complex weather system that appears once every three to seven years, with warm currents off the coast of Peru temporarily replacing the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current, and it brings drought, floods and other extremes to different parts of the world; it is used in India to forecast long-range monsoon rainfall, and in 1990-91 a wild El Nino delayed the onset of the south-west monsoon over most of the country by five to twelve days.
  Source: [NCERT Class XI, India: Physical Environment, ch. 4 Climate (kegy104)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy104.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-ENSO (verified):** NCERT: the appearance of warm water off the coast of Peru is the El Nino, which is closely associated with pressure changes over the central Pacific and Australia known as the southern oscillation; the combined phenomenon is ENSO, and in years when ENSO is strong the arid west coast of South America receives heavy rainfall, drought occurs in Australia and sometimes in India, and floods occur in China.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WEST-COAST-CURRENTS (verified):** NCERT: west coasts of the continents in tropical and subtropical latitudes (except close to the equator) are bordered by cool waters and are generally arid, with fog; warm currents flow parallel to the east coasts of the continents in tropical and subtropical latitudes, giving warm and rainy climates; west coasts in middle and higher latitudes are bordered by warm waters that give a distinct marine climate.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-OCEAN-WIND-LINK (verified):** NCERT: major ocean currents are greatly influenced by the stresses of the prevailing winds and the Coriolis force, and the oceanic circulation pattern roughly corresponds to the earth's atmospheric circulation pattern; in regions of pronounced monsoonal flow the monsoon winds influence the current movements, and oceanic circulation transports heat between latitude belts in a manner similar to the general circulation of the atmosphere.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CURRENT-FORCES (verified):** NCERT: ocean currents are like river flow in the oceans, a regular volume of water in a definite path and direction, influenced by primary forces that initiate the movement (heating by solar energy, wind, gravity and the Coriolis force) and secondary forces that influence the flow; heating makes the water expand, so near the equator the sea level is about 8 cm higher than in the middle latitudes, giving a very slight gradient down which water flows, and the Coriolis force turns water to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, the large accumulations of water and the flow around them being gyres.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CURRENT-DENSITY-DEPTH (verified):** NCERT: differences in water density affect the vertical mobility of ocean water, since water with high salinity is denser than water with low salinity and cold water is denser than warm water, and denser water tends to sink while lighter water tends to rise; by depth, surface currents (the upper 400 m) make up about 10 per cent of ocean water and deep water currents the other 90 per cent.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CURRENT-DRIFT (verified):** NCERT: the speed of a current is its drift, measured in knots; currents are strongest near the surface (they may exceed five knots) and weaken with depth, where speeds are generally below 0.5 knots, and most currents have speeds of five knots or less.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 13 Movements of Ocean Water (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy213.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-UPWELLING-WIND (verified):** NCERT: winds blowing from the land towards the oceans drive warm surface water away from the coast, resulting in the upwelling of cold water from below, whereas onshore winds pile up warm water near the coast and raise its temperature.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 12 Water (Oceans) (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy212.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GULF-LABRADOR (verified):** NCERT: ocean currents change water temperature locally; the Gulf Stream, a warm current, raises the temperature near the eastern coast of North America and the west coast of Europe, while the Labrador current, a cold current, lowers the temperature near the north-east coast of North America.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 12 Water (Oceans) (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy212.pdf)

### Last-week summary

- Current: horizontal flow in a definite direction; upwelling and sinking are vertical. Warm flows poleward, cold equatorward (relative). NCERT 'drift' = speed in knots; Leong's named Drifts = broad slow currents.
- Primary: heating (equator about 8 cm higher), wind, gravity, Coriolis (right north, left south). Secondary: density (Mediterranean in at surface, out at bottom); land shape splits currents; monsoon reverses North Indian drifts (SW June-October, NE from December).
