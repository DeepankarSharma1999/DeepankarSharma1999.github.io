## PH-065: Single-mode and graded-index fibres

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Bessel functions in one page

**What problem it solves.** On a straight string the natural wave shapes are sine and cosine. On a round drum or in a round fibre the natural shapes depend on the distance r from the centre, and the functions that play the role of cosine and sine are the **Bessel functions** J0(x), J1(x), J2(x) and so on. You do not need to compute them by hand. You need to know their picture and a few numbers.

**Picture.** J0(x) starts at 1 at x = 0 and falls, crossing zero at x = 2.405, goes negative, comes back up through zero at 5.520, then 8.654. J1(x) starts at 0, rises, falls through zero at 3.832, then 7.016. The waves die away slowly, like a cosine with shrinking height.

**The decaying partner.** Outside the core the field must fall off with distance, as an exponential does. The Bessel-type function that does this is written K0, K1, and so on. It never crosses zero; it only decays.

**Why it matters here.** A mode is allowed when the oscillating core function and the decaying cladding function can be joined smoothly at the core edge. The numbers 2.405, 3.832, 5.136 and 5.520 that appear as cut-off values of V are zeros of J0, J1, J2 and J0 (second zero). Those zeros are listed by any computer algebra system; module 4 of this lesson used scipy to check them.

### 1. Orientation: why modes exist, how many there are, and what graded-index fibre changes

*Objective:* State the five questions this lesson answers and separate them from what Day 17 lesson PH-064 already taught.

## Where this lesson sits
**Day 17 lesson PH-064** (section "Core: modes, the V-number and the fibre types") defined the **V-number**, quoted that a step-index fibre carries about V²/2 modes, and quoted the single-mode condition V < 2.405. It also derived the intermodal delay and the parabolic-profile delay. This lesson does not repeat those derivations. It explains **why** the numbers are what they are, then moves to **single-mode design** and to the **ray paths and acceptance angle of graded-index fibre**. The quantitative pulse-dispersion comparison of step-index and parabolic-index fibre belongs to **Day 18 lesson PH-208**, "Step-index versus parabolic-index fibre pulse dispersion".

## The five questions
1. **Why do only some ray directions survive?** A wave that zig-zags in a core must reproduce itself after two reflections. That condition picks out discrete angles, the modes (module 2).
2. **How many modes are there?** About V²/2 for step-index fibre. We count them by a phase-space argument and check against an exact calculation (module 3).
3. **Where does 2.405 come from?** From the first zero of the Bessel function J0 (module 4).
4. **How is a single-mode fibre designed?** Core radius, index step and wavelength are tied by one inequality (module 5).
5. **What do rays do when the index changes smoothly?** They follow sine-shaped paths, and the acceptance angle now depends on where the light enters (modules 6 and 7).

## Vocabulary reminder (full definitions in PH-064)
- **Core** and **cladding**: the glass centre of index n1 and the surrounding glass of slightly lower index n2.
- **Mode**: one allowed ray path or field pattern of the guide.
- **Numerical aperture** NA = sqrt(n1² − n2²) for a step profile; **acceptance angle** θ0 with sin θ0 = NA when launching from air.
- **Step-index**: n1 constant across the core and drops abruptly at the boundary. **Graded-index**: n falls smoothly from the axis.
- **Single-mode** and **multimode**: one guided mode versus many.

## Notation used throughout
a is the core radius and d = 2a the diameter. λ is the free-space wavelength and k = 2π/λ. The fractional index difference is written Δ. For a step profile the book defines Δ = (n1 − n2)/n1. For the graded profile of module 6 we use the slightly different but equivalent-to-first-order definition n2² = n1²(1 − 2Δ), that is Δ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²). For small Δ the two agree to a fraction of a per cent (for n1 = 1.48 and n2 = 1.46 they are 0.01351 and 0.01342).

## Source and scope
The source is Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam, *A Textbook of Optics*, Chapter 24 (PDF pages 683 to 701 and 712 to 716 for this lesson). The chapter has no verified past paper question for these two topics; the lesson says so plainly and uses clearly labelled original questions. Where a result is standard but is not printed in the extract (the Bessel-function origin of 2.405, the exact mode counts, the field-in-cladding fraction) it is marked "standard; verify".

### 2. Foundation: why only some zig-zag directions survive (derivation 1, the waveguide condition)

*Objective:* Derive the transverse-resonance condition 2 k n1 d cos φ − 2δ = 2π m for a planar guide and use it to show that the number of modes is about 2V/π.

## Setting and assumptions
The extract (PDF pp. 683 and 689 to 690) explains modes with a ray that is totally reflected twice and must then be in step with its neighbour. We follow that idea in its cleanest setting, a **planar guide**: a slab of thickness d and index n1 between two half-spaces of index n2 < n1, light travelling along z, everything uniform in y, **TE polarisation**. φ is the angle of incidence at the boundary, measured from the normal, so the angle with the axis is θ = 90° − φ. The vacuum wave number is k = 2π/λ.

## Derivation 1: the self-consistency condition
1. Inside the core the plane wave has wave number k n1. Its **transverse** component is kx = k n1 cos φ and its **longitudinal** component (the propagation constant) is β = k n1 sin φ.
2. The wave crosses the core, reflects at the top boundary, crosses back and reflects at the bottom boundary. It has then travelled a transverse distance 2d and points the same way again.
3. The phase gained in the two crossings is 2 kx d = 2 k n1 d cos φ.
4. Each total internal reflection changes the phase by −δ, where for TE waves tan(δ/2) = sqrt(n1² sin² φ − n2²)/(n1 cos φ) (the form of the extract's Eq. 24.23).
5. If the wave does not reproduce itself, successive passes interfere destructively and the field dies out. Reproduction requires a net phase that is a whole multiple of 2π:

$$ 2 k n1 d cos φ − 2 δ(φ) = 2π m,   m = 0, 1, 2, ... $$

6. Only the angles φ_m satisfying this are modes, and m is the **mode number**. Larger m means larger cos φ, a smaller φ and a steeper ray: the higher-order modes of the extract.

## How many modes?
- At the critical angle, sin φ_c = n2/n1, we have δ = 0 and cos φ_c = NA/n1. The left side of step 5 is then 2 k d NA.
- For a grazing ray, φ → 90°, cos φ → 0 and δ → π, so the left side tends to −2π.
- The left side falls continuously from 2 k d NA to −2π, so a root exists for every integer m with 2π m ≤ 2 k d NA. Because m = 0 always qualifies, **the lowest mode always exists**: it has no cut-off.
- Using V = π d NA/λ (the book's definition) we have k d NA = 2V, hence

$$ m ≤ 2V/π,   number of modes ≈ 2V/π (rounded up) $$

*Dimension check:* k d NA is dimensionless, so m is a pure number. *Limits:* V → 0 leaves one mode; large V gives many modes, growing with d/λ, as the extract states (PDF p. 683).

## Numbers (recomputed)
n1 = 1.48, n2 = 1.46, d = 10 micrometres, λ = 0.85 micrometre: NA = 0.2425, V = 8.96, 2V/π = 5.70, so 6 modes. Solving the exact even and odd planar-guide equations numerically also gives 6. Other checks against the exact equations: V = 1.0 gives 1 mode (2V/π = 0.64), V = 5.0 gives 4 (3.18) and V = 20 gives 13 (12.73).

## Limits of this model
One transverse direction and one polarisation only. A round fibre has two transverse directions and two polarisations, so its count grows as V², not V (module 3).

## Trap
The extract's text layer garbles the last lines of its derivation (Eqs. 24.26 and 24.27), so the algebra above is derived independently. A result of the form m ≤ V/π would differ from this one by a factor of 2; if your own algebra disagrees, check that d is the full thickness and that 2 k n1 d cos φ is the phase for the double crossing.

### 3. Core: how many modes? V, the phase-space count and the V²/2 and V²/4 results (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive N ≈ V²/2 for a step-index fibre and N ≈ V²/4 for a parabolic graded-index fibre by counting cells in position and ray-direction space, and test the result against exact counts.

## Setting and idea
The extract (PDF p. 691) states N = V²/2 for a step-index fibre and about V²/4 for graded-index at large V, without derivation. In the planar guide of module 2 one mode occupied a cell of size 2π in the (x, kx) plane: 2V/π is (width d) × (range of kx, 2 k NA)/2π. We repeat this for a round core.

## Derivation 2: the count
1. A guided ray makes an angle θ ≤ θ_c with the axis. Its transverse wave-vector component is k n1 sin θ ≤ k n1 sin θ_c = k NA. So the allowed transverse wave vector (kx, ky) lies in a disc of radius k NA, area π k² NA².
2. The ray can be anywhere in the core cross-section, a disc of radius a and area π a².
3. The allowed region in four-dimensional (x, y, kx, ky) space therefore has volume (π a²)(π k² NA²).
4. In the planar case one mode took a cell of size 2π in the (x, kx) plane. In two transverse directions one mode takes (2π)² = 4π².
5. Each such transverse pattern exists in **two polarisations**, so multiply by 2.

$$ N = 2 × (π a²)(π k² NA²)/(2π)² = (k a NA)²/2 = V²/2,   V = (2π a/λ) NA = π d NA/λ $$

6. For a graded-index core the disc radius depends on position, k NA(r), with NA(r) = sqrt(n²(r) − n2²) (module 7). Summing over positions,

$$ N = 2 ∫ [π k² NA²(r)]/(2π)² d²r = (k²/2π) ∫ NA²(r) d²r $$

7. For the power-law profile n²(r) = n1²[1 − 2Δ (r/a)^α], NA²(r) = NA²[1 − (r/a)^α]. The integral over the disc is π a² α/(α + 2), so

$$ N = (V²/2) × α/(α + 2) $$

8. The checks: α → ∞ is the step profile and gives V²/2; the parabolic profile α = 2 gives **V²/4**, half the step-index value, as the extract says (PDF p. 701, "about half"). A linear profile α = 1 gives V²/6.

*Dimension check:* V is dimensionless. *Assumption:* one mode per (2π) cell; valid when N is large.

## How good is V²/2?
An exact scalar calculation lists every mode and its cut-off (module 4). Counting both polarisations and all orientations, the exact numbers (recomputed with scipy) are:

| V | exact count | V²/2 |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 2 | 2.0 |
| 3.99 | 12 | 7.9 |
| 10 | 54 | 50 |
| 20 | 210 | 200 |
| 42.14 | 900 | 888 |

The formula is a large-V estimate: the error is a few per cent at V of 20 and larger, but poor at V of about 4. The extract's V = 10 gives 50 (PDF p. 691), within ten per cent of 54.

## Numbers
- Extract Problem 6: n1 = 1.54, n2 = 1.50, d = 50 micrometres, λ = 1.3 micrometres: NA = 0.3487, V = 42.14 (printed 42.15, a rounding difference), N ≈ 888.
- Extract Example 24.10: n1 = 1.52, d = 29 micrometres, Δ = 0.0007, λ = 1.3 micrometres: V = π d n1 sqrt(2Δ)/λ = 3.986. The extract prints 4.049, which does not follow from its own data; N = 7.9, so 8 modes either way.
- Extract Problem 14 prints V = 44.64 and 996 modes for a core of "radius 50 micrometres"; those values correspond to a **diameter** of 50 micrometres. With radius 50 micrometres, V = 89.3 and N ≈ 3989.

## Trap
V uses the **radius** in V = (2π a/λ) NA and the **diameter** in V = (π d/λ) NA. Mixing a and d changes V by a factor of 2 and N by a factor of 4.

### 4. Core: the single-mode condition V < 2.405 and where it comes from

*Objective:* Explain that 2.405 is the first zero of the Bessel function J0 and the cut-off of the first higher mode, show that the fundamental mode has no cut-off, and compute the cut-off wavelength of a fibre.

## What the ray picture cannot say
Module 2 showed that the lowest mode always exists in a planar guide. The ray picture gives the V²/2 estimate but cannot give a sharp number such as 2.405 for a round fibre, because the fields are Bessel functions and the sharp number is a property of those functions. The extract states the condition (PDF p. 691) and the cut-off wavelength λc = π d NA/2.405 (Eq. 24.32) without deriving it. What follows is a sketch of the standard derivation (standard; verify).

## Sketch: the weakly guiding fibre
1. For Δ ≪ 1 a scalar wave equation holds for each transverse field component ψ(r, φ) e^{i(βz − ωt)}. The fibre modes are then labelled **LP_lm**: l is the number of azimuthal field cycles, m the number of radial maxima.
2. In the core: ψ = J_l(U r/a) cos(lφ), with U² = a²(k² n1² − β²). In the cladding: ψ ∝ K_l(W r/a) cos(lφ), with W² = a²(β² − k² n2²), a function that **decays** outward.
3. Adding U² and W² gives the invariant U² + W² = a² k² (n1² − n2²) = V².
4. Matching ψ and its slope at r = a gives the eigenvalue equation

$$ U J_{l−1}(U)/J_l(U) = − W K_{l−1}(W)/K_l(W) $$

5. **Cut-off** is the moment the field stops decaying in the cladding, W → 0 and U → V. For l = 1 the equation reduces to J0(V) = 0. For l ≥ 2 it reduces to J_{l−1}(V) = 0 (first non-zero root), and for l = 0 to J1(V) = 0.

## The fundamental mode, LP01
For l = 0 the cut-off condition is J1(V) = 0, which is satisfied at V = 0. So **LP01 has no cut-off**: whatever V is, a solution of the eigenvalue equation exists. A numerical solution (scipy) gives U = 0.979 at V = 1.0, 1.528 at V = 2.0 and 1.771 at V = 3.0. This matches the planar guide, where m = 0 never disappears. LP01 is the fundamental mode of the extract, the "zero-order" ray along the axis (PDF p. 686).

## The first higher mode, LP11
LP11 has l = 1, so its cut-off is the first zero of J0:

$$ J0(V) = 0   ⇒   V = 2.4048 (first zero),   5.5201 (second),   8.6537 (third) $$

The numerical solution confirms it: for l = 1 the eigenvalue equation has no root at V = 2.4 and has a root (U = 2.474) at V = 2.5. LP11 consists of TE01, TM01 and HE21 in the full vector theory (standard). The other cut-offs come from the zeros of J1 (3.832, for LP21 and LP02), J2 (5.136, LP31) and J0 again (5.520, LP12), as in the diagram.

So **V < 2.405 means that only LP01 is guided**. The two orthogonal polarisations of LP01 are both present, so "single-mode" means a single spatial mode. The exact count below 2.405 is therefore 2; just above it the count jumps to 6.

## Cut-off wavelength
Since V = (2π a/λ) NA falls as λ rises, a fibre is single-mode for all wavelengths longer than

$$ λc = 2π a NA / 2.405 = π d NA / 2.405 $$

Numbers (recomputed): n1 = 1.452, n2 = 1.447, a = 3.9 micrometres gives NA = 0.1204 and λc = 1.227 micrometres. For the extract's Problem 16 (n1 = 1.480, n2 = 1.478, λ = 0.82 micrometre) the condition gives the largest radius a = 2.405 λ/(2π NA) = 4.08 micrometres, as printed (NA = 0.0769).

## Trap
Single-mode fibres exist at all wavelengths **above** the cut-off wavelength, not below it. A telecom fibre with λc ≈ 1.2 micrometres is single-mode at 1.31 and 1.55 micrometres but carries several modes at 0.85 micrometre. Real cut-offs shift slightly with bends (standard; verify).

### 5. Core: designing a single-mode fibre (core radius, index step, wavelength, source)

*Objective:* Use a·NA < 0.3827 λ to choose a core radius or an index step, state the practical consequences for acceptance angle and source, and say which dispersions remain.

## The design inequality
Single-mode operation needs V < 2.405, that is 2π a NA/λ < 2.405, or

$$ a × NA < 0.3827 λ $$

(the number is 2.4048/(2π) = 0.3827). The right side is a length: at λ = 0.85 micrometre it is 0.325 micrometre, at 1.31 micrometres it is 0.501 and at 1.55 micrometres it is 0.593. The product on the left is a radius times a pure number, so it is also a length. There are **three levers**, exactly those the extract names (PDF p. 691): make the core thinner, make the index step Δ smaller, or use a longer wavelength.

## Worked designs (recomputed)
- **Extract Problem 16** (n1 = 1.480, n2 = 1.478, λ = 0.82 micrometre): NA = 0.0769, so a < 4.08 micrometres, as printed.
- **A telecom-style design** (n1 = 1.450, Δ = 0.0035, λ = 1.31 micrometres): NA = 0.1213, a < 4.13 micrometres. Choosing a = 4.1 micrometres gives cut-off λc = 1.30 micrometres, V = 2.39 at 1.31 micrometres and V = 2.02 at 1.55 micrometres: single-mode at both. The index values are illustrative, not from the extract.
- **Why a multimode fibre cannot just be used at a longer wavelength:** a = 25 micrometres and NA = 0.2425 give λc = 15.8 micrometres, far longer than any of the wavelengths used in this lesson (0.85 to 1.55 micrometres). The fibre must be redesigned, as the extract says: reduce the diameter and/or Δ (PDF p. 691).

## Consequences for the acceptance angle and the source
The extract says that single-mode fibres have a very small Δ and NA and a core of 8 to 12 micrometres (PDF p. 686). For NA = 0.1213 the acceptance angle in air is only 6.97 degrees, against 14.0 degrees for NA = 0.2425. Together with the tiny core area, this means:
- an LED, which emits over a wide angle from a large area, couples almost no light;
- a **laser diode**, narrow in angle and in area, is needed, and its alignment is critical (the extract: launching light and coupling are difficult and costly, PDF p. 701).

## What dispersion is left
A single-mode fibre has no intermodal dispersion (the table of what exists in which fibre is in the PH-064 section on waveguide and total dispersion). **Material dispersion** and **waveguide dispersion** remain; both depend on the source's spectral width and on the operating wavelength. That is why a narrow laser near the zero of the material dispersion is combined with single-mode fibre, and why the extract claims the single-mode fibre carries signals up to 50 times farther than multimode fibre (PDF p. 701; treat the factor as the extract's own claim, not a general law). Material and waveguide dispersion are in **Day 17 lesson PH-064**, sections "Core: material dispersion, the D parameter and the spread formula" and "Core: waveguide dispersion, total dispersion, bandwidth and the numbers that decide a link".

## How much of the light is inside the core?
The mode-field diameter is not in the extract. A quantity that the exact LP01 solution gives is the fraction of power carried in the core (recomputed with scipy; standard; verify):

| V | fraction of LP01 power in the core |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.17 |
| 1.5 | 0.54 |
| 2.0 | 0.74 |
| 2.405 | 0.83 |
| 3.0 | 0.90 |

At the single-mode limit about 17 per cent of the power already travels in the cladding. The cladding must therefore be thick and clean (about 125 micrometres across, PDF p. 686).

## Trap
Do not choose the thinnest core to be "safe": lower V means weaker confinement and harder coupling. Design just below 2.405 at the shortest wavelength used.

### 6. Core: graded-index profile and ray paths, the ray equation and the sinusoidal path (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Write the power-law profile, derive the ray equation r'' = (1/2β²) d(n²)/dr, show that the parabolic profile gives sinusoidal paths of period Λ = (2π a/sqrt(2Δ)) cos θ_ax, and compute a period.

## The profile
The extract (PDF p. 687, Eq. 24.20) describes the graded-index core by a **power law**. In the n² form it reads

$$ n²(r) = n1² [1 − 2Δ (r/a)^α]  for r < a,   n(r) = n2 for r ≥ a $$

n1 is the index on the axis, a the core radius and α the **grading profile index**, from 1 to ∞. Setting r = a gives n2² = n1²(1 − 2Δ), so Δ = (n1² − n2²)/(2 n1²). The value α = 2 is the **parabolic** profile preferred in practice and α → ∞ is the step profile.

## Setting and assumptions
Meridional rays only (rays that cross the axis), a straight fibre, n depending on r alone. A meridional ray stays in one plane through the axis, so it is a curve r(z), its signed distance from the axis at position z; θ(r) is its angle with the axis.

## Derivation 3, step 1: the ray invariant
Slice the core into thin cylindrical layers. Every boundary is parallel to the axis, so Snell's law at each boundary keeps the z-component of "index times direction" unchanged:

$$ β = n(r) cos θ(r) = constant along the ray $$

This is the invariant used in **Day 17 lesson PH-064** (section "Core: intermodal (pulse) dispersion ... graded-index cure"); it is the ray form of the conserved wave number along z in module 2.

## Step 2: the ray equation
1. dr/dz = tan θ, so (dr/dz)² = (1 − cos²θ)/cos²θ = (n² − β²)/β².
2. Differentiate with respect to z: 2 r' r'' = (1/β²)(d(n²)/dr) r'.
3. Divide by 2 r' (the ray is not parallel to the axis):

$$ r'' = d²r/dz² = (1/2β²) d(n²)/dr $$

*Interpretation:* the ray curves towards larger n²; since n² falls with r, it curves back to the axis.

## Step 3: the parabolic profile is simple harmonic motion
For α = 2, d(n²)/dr = −4Δ n1² r/a², so

$$ r'' = −K r,   K = 2Δ n1² / (β² a²) $$

K is constant for each ray, so r(z) = r0 sin(sqrt(K) z) for a ray crossing the axis at z = 0. *Check:* (dr/dz)² = K (r0² − r²) with r0² = a²(n1² − β²)/(2Δ n1²), which is the condition n(r0) = β at the turning point.

## Step 4: the period
$$ Λ = 2π/sqrt(K) = (2π a/sqrt(2Δ)) (β/n1) = (2π a/sqrt(2Δ)) cos θ_ax $$

θ_ax is the ray's angle with the axis where it crosses it (β = n1 cos θ_ax). For small angles Λ ≈ 2π a/sqrt(2Δ), **independent of the amplitude**, which is why the extract speaks of "periodic focussing" (PDF p. 687): rays launched from one point at different angles all return to the axis after the same distance.

## Numbers (recomputed)
- a = 25 micrometres, Δ = 0.01: Λ = 1.11 mm, about 9.0 × 10⁵ oscillations per kilometre.
- n1 = 1.5, n2 = 1.43 (Δ = 0.0456 on the n² definition), a = 25 micrometres: Λ = 520 micrometres for a ray near the axis and 496 micrometres for the marginal ray (4.7 per cent shorter). A numerical ray trace (scipy) reproduced the analytical transit times to better than one part in 10⁹.
- The marginal ray has β = n2, so on the axis it makes the critical propagation angle θ_c, cos θ_c = n2/n1 (PH-064 section "Foundation: guiding, critical propagation angle, acceptance angle and numerical aperture").

## For other α
d(n²)/dr ∝ r^(α−1) is not proportional to r, so the motion is not harmonic and the period depends on the amplitude: rays do not refocus together. This is what singles out α = 2.

## Trap
Λ is the full period (one maximum to the next on the same side); successive axis crossings are Λ/2 apart. Angles θ here are from the axis, not from a layer normal.

### 7. Core: local numerical aperture in graded-index fibre and the three fibre types compared

*Objective:* Derive NA(r) = sqrt(n²(r) − n2²), explain how acceptance angle varies with launch position, link it to the mode count, and compare step-index multimode, graded-index and single-mode fibre.

## Setting
A step-index fibre has one acceptance angle for the whole end face. In a graded-index fibre the index at the entry point depends on the entry radius r. The extract states that "the acceptance angle and numerical aperture decrease with radial distance from the axis" (PDF p. 688, Eq. 24.21). We derive it.

## Derivation: local numerical aperture
1. A ray enters the flat end face at radius r from air at angle θ_i to the axis. Snell's law: sin θ_i = n(r) sin θ, where θ is the angle with the axis inside.
2. The ray is guided if it is totally reflected or turned before the cladding, that is β = n(r) cos θ ≥ n2 (module 6).
3. Square the guidance condition: n²(r) cos²θ ≥ n2², so n²(r) sin²θ ≤ n²(r) − n2².
4. Using step 1, n²(r) sin²θ = sin²θ_i. So the largest launch angle at radius r satisfies

$$ sin θ_0(r) = NA(r) = sqrt(n²(r) − n2²) $$

5. For the power-law profile n²(r) − n2² = 2Δ n1² [1 − (r/a)^α], so

$$ NA(r) = NA(0) sqrt(1 − (r/a)^α),   NA(0) = sqrt(n1² − n2²) = n1 sqrt(2Δ) $$

*Checks:* at r = 0 it equals the step-index NA with the same n1 and n2; at r = a it is zero, since the index there equals n2; α → ∞ gives the constant step-index NA.

**Numbers (recomputed)** for n1 = 1.5 and n2 = 1.43, parabolic: NA(r) = 0.4529, 0.4385, 0.3922, 0.2996, 0.1974, 0 at r/a = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1 (acceptance angles 26.9°, 26.0°, 23.1°, 17.4°, 11.4°, 0°).

## Consequences
- **Coupling.** For a source that fills the core uniformly in position and angle, the power accepted is proportional to the integral of NA²(r) over the face. Comparing with a step-index fibre of the same n1, n2 and a, the parabolic profile accepts the fraction α/(α + 2) = 1/2 of the power (standard; verify). It is the same integral as in the mode count of module 3, so the fraction is the same factor of one half in modes and in accepted power.
- **Modes.** N ≈ V²/4, the extract's "about half" (PDF p. 701).
- **Dispersion.** Fewer modes and the equalisation of ray transit times (the subject of Day 18 lesson PH-208) are why graded-index fibre has the higher bandwidth. The extract's statement that intermodal dispersion in graded-index fibre is "zero" means "greatly reduced": PH-208 shows that a residue remains.

## The three fibre types compared
| Feature | Multimode step-index | Graded-index (multimode) | Single-mode step-index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core diameter | 50 to 100 micrometres | 50 to 100 micrometres | 8 to 12 micrometres |
| Index profile | constant, abrupt drop | smooth fall, parabolic preferred | constant, abrupt drop |
| Numerical aperture | larger, order 0.3 | smaller than multimode step-index | very small |
| Modes (large V) | about V²/2 | about V²/4 | 1 spatial mode (V < 2.405) |
| Intermodal dispersion | large | much reduced | none |
| Light source | LED or laser | LED or laser | laser diode |
| Coupling and cost | easy, cheapest | harder, dearest of the multimode | hardest, costly |
| Typical use in the extract | data links | telephone links | long distance, undersea cables |
| Bandwidth | lowest | better than step-index | highest |

- **Radius or diameter.** V = (2π a/λ) NA = (π d/λ) NA. Write which you use.

### Notes: Definitions

- Mode: a field pattern (or in the ray picture a zig-zag direction) that reproduces itself after two reflections and therefore travels along the fibre unchanged in shape.
- Normalised frequency (V-number): V = (2 pi a/lambda) NA = (pi d/lambda) NA; the single dimensionless number that controls how many modes a step-index fibre supports.
- Cut-off: the value of V below which a particular mode is no longer guided. LP01 has none; LP11 has V = 2.405.
- Cut-off wavelength: lambda_c = 2 pi a NA/2.405; the fibre is single-mode for all wavelengths longer than this.
- LP_lm mode: a linearly polarised mode of a weakly guiding fibre with l azimuthal cycles and m radial maxima.
- Grading profile index alpha: the exponent in n^2(r) = n1^2[1 - 2 Delta (r/a)^alpha]; alpha = 2 parabolic, alpha to infinity step.
- Local numerical aperture: NA(r) = sqrt(n^2(r) - n2^2), the sine of the largest acceptance angle for light entering at radius r.
- Ray invariant: beta = n(r) cos(theta), constant along a meridional ray in a medium whose index depends on r only.

### Notes: Equations to know

- Planar guide resonance: 2 k n1 d cos(phi) - 2 delta(phi) = 2 pi m, with tan(delta/2) = sqrt(n1^2 sin^2 phi - n2^2)/(n1 cos phi) for TE; modes m <= 2V/pi.
- Step-index modes: N = V^2/2; graded-index power law: N = (V^2/2) alpha/(alpha + 2); parabolic N = V^2/4.
- Single-mode: V < 2.405; lambda_c = pi d NA/2.405; a NA < 0.3827 lambda.
- Power-law profile: n^2(r) = n1^2[1 - 2 Delta (r/a)^alpha], Delta = (n1^2 - n2^2)/(2 n1^2).
- Ray equation: r'' = (1/(2 beta^2)) d(n^2)/dr; parabolic: r'' = -K r, K = 2 Delta n1^2/(beta^2 a^2); period Lambda = (2 pi a/sqrt(2 Delta)) cos(theta_ax).
- Local NA: NA(r) = NA(0) sqrt(1 - (r/a)^alpha); NA(0) = n1 sqrt(2 Delta).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: number of modes

- Guided ray: transverse wave vector at most k NA, so (kx, ky) lies in a disc of area pi k^2 NA^2.
- Position: disc of area pi a^2 (step-index); one mode per (2 pi)^2 of phase-space volume; two polarisations.
- N = 2 (pi a^2)(pi k^2 NA^2)/(4 pi^2) = (k a NA)^2/2 = V^2/2.
- Graded-index: N = (k^2/2 pi) integral of NA(r)^2 d^2r; power law gives (V^2/2) alpha/(alpha + 2); parabolic V^2/4.
- Planar guide, same method: N = d (2 k NA)/(2 pi) = 2V/pi, matching the exact equation count.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: ray path in a parabolic profile

- Invariant beta = n cos(theta); (dr/dz)^2 = (n^2 - beta^2)/beta^2.
- Differentiate: r'' = (1/2 beta^2) d(n^2)/dr.
- n^2 = n1^2(1 - 2 Delta r^2/a^2) gives r'' = -K r, K = 2 Delta n1^2/(beta^2 a^2).
- r = r0 sin(sqrt(K) z); period 2 pi/sqrt(K) = (2 pi a/sqrt(2 Delta))(beta/n1).
- Numbers: a = 25 um, Delta = 0.01 gives 1.11 mm.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Radius a versus diameter d in V: factor 2 in V and 4 in N.
- Single-mode fibre versus fibre with a single polarisation: LP01 has two polarisations.
- Wavelengths above the cut-off wavelength are single-mode, not below.
- NA of a graded-index fibre is the on-axis value NA(0); the local value falls to zero at the core edge.
- Mode number N (a count of modes) versus mode index m (which one): m runs from 0.

### Core concepts

- A zig-zag wave must reproduce itself after two reflections; only discrete angles (modes) satisfy this.
- The number of step-index modes is about V^2/2, found by counting cells in position and direction space; graded-index parabolic gives V^2/4.
- V < 2.405 (first zero of J0, cut-off of LP11) makes a step-index fibre single-mode; LP01 has no cut-off.
- In a parabolic-index fibre rays follow sinusoidal paths with nearly amplitude-independent period.
- In graded-index fibre the local numerical aperture falls from the axis value to zero at the core edge.

### Memory skeleton

- Waveguide condition: the zig-zag wave must reproduce itself after two reflections, so only discrete angles (modes) survive
- V = (2πa/λ) NA = (πd/λ) NA; a is the core radius, d the diameter
- Phase-space count: N ≈ V²/2 (step), (V²/2) α/(α + 2) (power law), V²/4 (parabolic); a large-V estimate
- Single-mode: V < 2.405, λc = πd NA/2.405; smaller a, smaller NA or longer λ; laser diode source
- Graded profile n²(r) = n1²[1 − 2Δ(r/a)^α], Δ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²); invariant β = n cos θ; sine-shaped ray in the parabolic core
- Local NA(r) = √(n²(r) − n2²), falling to zero at the core edge; three fibre types compared
