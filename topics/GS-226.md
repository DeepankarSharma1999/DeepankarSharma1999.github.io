## GS-226: Balance of payments, forex and exchange rates

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Basic macro and monetary

*Goal:* Explain Balance of payments, forex and exchange rates and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Closing the balance of payments

A country can finance an excess of payments over receipts in two ways: by attracting net capital inflows, or by having the central bank sell foreign exchange from its reserves. NCERT calls the second an official reserve sale and defines the change in reserves as the overall BoP balance. Transactions undertaken for their own sake, such as trade or profit-seeking investment, are autonomous ('above the line'); reserve transactions, which exist only to fill the gap, are accommodating ('below the line'). Because no statistical system captures everything, an errors-and-omissions line completes the accounts. Reserve operations matter most under fixed exchange rates and much less under a pure float.

### How exchange rates are set

Demand for dollars comes from imports, gifts abroad and purchases of foreign assets; supply comes from exports, inward transfers and foreign purchases of Indian assets. Under a flexible regime, the rate settles where these meet, and more rupees per dollar means rupee depreciation. Short-run shifts come from interest-rate differentials, which move capital, from income growth, which raises imports, and from speculation, which can be self-fulfilling. In the long run, purchasing power parity suggests rates adjust to relative price levels. Under a fixed regime the government sets the rate and must buy or sell reserves to defend it, risking speculative attack; managed floating, or dirty floating, allows intervention to smooth swings.

### India's buffers and the rupee, 2025-26

The Economic Survey 2025-26 reports foreign exchange reserves of USD 701.4 billion on 16 January 2026, up from end-March 2025, enough for around 11 months of goods imports and about 94% of external debt. The gold component rose sharply, reflecting high gold prices and central-bank diversification. The rupee nonetheless depreciated about 5.4% against the dollar from 1 April 2025 to 15 January 2026, driven by foreign investment outflows and tariff-related uncertainty. The Survey notes that years of high CAD often coincided with sharper depreciation, but in FY08, FY18 and FY22 strong capital inflows made the rupee appreciate despite wider trade deficits — the capital account can dominate.
