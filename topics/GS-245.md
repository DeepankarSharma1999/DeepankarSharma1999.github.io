## GS-245: Infrastructure: energy, roads, rail, ports, aviation

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Infrastructure: energy, roads, rail, ports, aviation and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why infrastructure drives growth

The Economic Survey 2025-26 calls infrastructure central to India's growth strategy. Central capital outlay nearly doubled between FY22 and the FY26 budget, justified by multiplier estimates of roughly 2.5–3.5 times over the medium term. The emphasis has moved from adding capacity to integrating networks: PM GatiShakti provides GIS-based multimodal planning, and the National Logistics Policy with digital platforms such as ULIP cuts transaction costs. Digital public infrastructure, including UPI, Aadhaar and BharatNet, now counts as infrastructure too. The Survey warns that effectiveness depends on execution, since overruns are often traced to weak feasibility studies, incomplete detailed project reports, rigid procurement and slow dispute resolution.

### Roads, rail, aviation and ports

For rural roads, PMGSY-IV (launched September 2024) aims to connect 25,000 habitations through 62,500 km of roads and bridges over FY25 to FY29. Railways are expanding and modernising, with near-universal electrification, record capital spending and three economic corridor programmes for energy and minerals, port connectivity and high-density routes mapped on GatiShakti. Dedicated Freight Corridors, the Eastern one complete, shift freight off passenger lines and cut transit times; Kavach and electronic interlocking improve safety. India is now the world's third-largest domestic aviation market, helped by the UDAN regional connectivity scheme and greenfield airports, though airport density remains far below the US and China. Ports have strengthened the landlord model, under which authorities retain land and core assets while private operators run terminals, and the number and value of PPP port projects have risen sharply.

### Energy: capacity versus viability

By November 2025 installed power capacity stood at 509.74 GW, an 11.6 per cent annual rise, and the national gap between energy demand and supply fell from 4.2 per cent in FY14 to nil. Schemes such as DDUGJY, IPDS and SAUBHAGYA extended distribution and household connections, and the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme funds smart metering. The weak link is distribution: DISCOMs operate as regulated monopolies with losses accumulating to ₹6.47 lakh crore by 2024-25, rooted in tariffs below cost, late state subsidy payments and high technical and commercial losses. Late Payment Surcharge rules sharply cut overdue payments to generators, and a proposed Electricity (Amendment) Bill, 2026 seeks regulated competition and cost-reflective tariffs.
