## GS-135: Service sector industries and locations

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Geography · Human/economic/world

*Goal:* Explain Service sector industries and locations and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### From trading centres to service hierarchies

Trade, transport, communication and services form the tertiary sector. Trading centres range from rural marketing centres - quasi-urban places with mandis and basic retail - to urban marketing centres offering specialised goods, labour and housing markets and professional services. Where regular markets are absent, periodic markets rotate on fixed days so traders stay busy while serving a wide area. Retail sells directly to consumers through stores or non-store channels; wholesale works through intermediaries, often extending credit to retailers. Services also come in orders: everyday low-order services are dispersed, while high-order professional ones concentrate. Recreation follows the market and land price, which is why multiplexes seek the CBD and golf courses seek cheaper land.

### Transport, communication and tourism as location shapers

Transport adds value at every stage of production and distribution. Distance can be read as kilometres, time or cost, and isochrones join places reachable in equal time. Networks consist of nodes and links; more links mean better connectivity. Route choice depends on the location of settlements and resources, trade patterns, terrain, climate and available funds. Telecommunications and satellites have largely freed communication from transport. Tourism, by NCERT's account the largest tertiary activity in registered jobs, is pulled by climate, landscape, history and art, culture and low cost, as in the Mediterranean coast and India's west coast. Medical tourism has made Indian metropolitan hospitals destinations for overseas patients, alongside outsourcing of tasks such as reading radiology images.

### Knowledge services, outsourcing and the digital divide

Quaternary workers handle information and research - from fund managers and software developers to statisticians - and in developed economies over half of workers are in this knowledge sector. Quinary workers are senior executives, officials, scientists and consultants whose influence exceeds their numbers. Because such work is not bound to resources or local markets, firms outsource or offshore it: call centres opened in India, China, Eastern Europe, Israel, the Philippines and Costa Rica, and data processing moves to countries with English-speaking IT staff on lower wages. KPO covers R and D, e-learning, IP research, legal and banking work. Yet access to ICT is uneven, producing a digital divide between and within countries, such as metros versus peripheral rural areas.

### Worked example

Question: 'Why do periodic markets exist, and why is IT-enabled work offshored to countries such as India?' Frame: periodic markets - where regular markets are absent, rural demand is thin and scattered, so traders rotate on fixed days to stay busy and serve a wide area; offshoring - IT-skilled, English-speaking staff at lower wages, lower overheads, and quaternary work that is not tied to resources or local markets; end with the digital divide as the factor limiting the spread of such services to peripheral rural areas.

### Recall

- - **q:** How is tertiary output measured according to NCERT?
  - **a:** Indirectly, in terms of wages and salaries.
- - **q:** What distinguishes KPO from BPO?
  - **a:** KPO uses highly skilled workers for information-driven knowledge work such as R and D, IP research and legal or banking analysis.
- - **q:** What is an isochrone?
  - **a:** A line on a map joining places that take equal time to reach.

### Common trap

Assuming every service can be outsourced or located anywhere; retail, personal services and recreation still depend on nearby customers, and NCERT notes that market location decides recreational sites such as multiplexes versus golf courses.
