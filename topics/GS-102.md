### Bridge: Bridge: words for the coast

The **coast** is the broad strip of land next to the sea; the **shoreline** is the thin line where land meets water at a given moment. The **shore** is the zone between the lowest and the highest water levels, and the **beach** is the sand, shingle or pebble deposit along it. Leong describes the area between the low-water level and the high-water level as the zone where tides extend the line of erosion into a zone (PDF p127). **Offshore** means the water seaward of where waves break; **onshore** means towards the land.

A **headland** (cape) is a piece of land jutting into the sea; a **bay** is a curve of sea set into the land. A **cliff** is a very steep rock face by the sea. A **lagoon** is a shallow stretch of water cut off from the sea by a bank of sand. A **delta** is a low fan of river deposits at a river mouth. **Sea level** is the average height of the sea surface, the zero from which heights on land are measured.

Two words recur: **erosion** is wearing away and carrying off of material, and **deposition** is dropping it. A **landform** is a small to medium part of the Earth's surface with its own shape and cause; several landforms make a **landscape** (NCERT kegy206, PDF p1). A **geomorphic agent** is whatever does the work, such as running water, groundwater, wind, glaciers and waves.

### Bridge: Bridge: rock resistance and sediment sizes

Not all rock wears at the same speed. **Hard (resistant) rock**, such as granite, stands up to waves; **soft rock**, such as clay and loose sand, is worn back quickly. Where hard and soft rock alternate along a coast, the soft rock is cut back into bays and the hard rock is left as headlands (Leong, PDF p128). **Joints** are cracks in rock, and **bedding planes** are the layers in sedimentary rock; waves enter these weaknesses first.

Loose material, called **sediment**, is graded by size from large to small: **boulders**, **cobbles**, **pebbles or shingle** (small stones), **sand**, **silt** and **clay**. Strong water carries big pieces and drops them first; weak water carries only fine material. This is why waves sort a beach: coarser material stays at the top of the beach and finer pieces are carried down towards the sea by the backwash (Leong, PDF p132).

A useful habit: ask of any coastline two questions. Is the rock hard or soft? Is sediment arriving (from rivers or from erosion) or being removed? The first answers what erodes; the second answers what is built.

### 1. Orientation: the coast as a meeting of land, sea and moving water

*Objective:* State what makes a coastal landform, name the agents that shape it and see the four questions that organise this whole topic.

## Why coasts change faster than most landforms

NCERT notes that most geomorphic processes act slowly, but **coastal processes are the most dynamic and hence the most destructive**. Some coastal changes are fast: at one place there may be erosion in one season and deposition in another, and most changes are done by **waves** (kegy206, PDF p11). **Storm waves and tsunami waves** cause far-reaching changes in a much shorter time than ordinary breaking waves (PDF p11).

Leong adds that on calm days waves do little damage and may even build beaches; in storms their effect is greatest (PDF p127).

## The three moving agents

- **Waves**: energy carried by the wind over the water surface. The main agent of both erosion and deposition at the coast.
- **Tides**: the regular rise and fall of sea level. Leong says tides and currents make very little direct attack on the coast; tides extend a line of erosion into a **zone** of erosion between low water and high water (PDF p127). How tides are caused, and spring and neap tides, are taught in Day 17 lesson GS-109 module 6; here tides matter only as an agent of erosion and deposition.
- **Currents**: they carry eroded debris and deposit it as silt, sand and gravel along the coasts (Leong, PDF p127). Large ocean currents also affect climate (modules 7 and 8).

NCERT adds two controls: other than waves, coastal landforms depend on (i) the configuration of the land and the sea floor, and (ii) whether the coast is **advancing (emerging) seaward or retreating (submerging) landward** (PDF p11).

## The four questions this lesson answers

1. **How do waves, tides and currents do work?** (Modules 2, 7 and 8.)
2. **What do they carve?** Erosional landforms: cliffs, platforms, caves, arches, stacks (module 3).
3. **What do they build?** Depositional landforms: beaches, bars, spits, lagoons, dunes (module 4), and how the type of coast changes the mix (module 5).
4. **What do living and floating things add?** Coral reefs and islands (module 6), and the human story: coastal erosion and its management as a general sediment-budget mechanism (module 9), with India's data in Day 17 lesson GS-130.

### 2. Foundation: how waves form, break and move sediment

*Objective:* Describe wave motion and its parts, explain why waves break near the shore, and explain swash, backwash, undertow and longshore drift.

## Waves are energy, not moving water

NCERT: waves are actually the **energy**, not the water as such, that moves across the ocean surface; water particles only travel in a small circle as a wave passes, so things are carried up and forward as the wave approaches and down and back as it passes (kegy213, PDF pp1-2). The motion of surface water seldom affects the still deep bottom water. Wind gives the energy; waves grow larger as they move and absorb more of it, and the largest waves are found in the open ocean.

Most waves are caused by the wind driving against water. A wave's size and shape show its origin: **steep waves are young and made by local wind; slow, steady waves come from far away**, possibly from another hemisphere. The maximum wave height depends on the wind's strength, how long it blows and the area over which it blows in one direction (this area is called the **fetch**, a standard term; PDF p1).

## Parts of a wave (NCERT, PDF p2)

- **Crest** and **trough**: highest and lowest points.
- **Wave height**: vertical distance from trough bottom to crest top. **Amplitude** is half the height.
- **Wavelength**: horizontal distance between two crests. **Period**: time between two crests passing a point. **Frequency**: waves passing a point per second. **Speed**: how fast the wave moves, in knots.


## Why waves break

As a wave approaches the beach it **slows down** because of friction with the sea floor. NCERT says the wave **breaks when the depth of water is less than half the wavelength** (PDF p1). Leong's variant is that shallow water, less than the height of the waves, checks the forward movement so the crests curl over and break as **breakers** (PDF p127). Learn the NCERT rule for examinations and treat Leong's as a looser description of the same event. On approach waves also bend, or **refract**, to follow the alignment of the coast (Leong, PDF p127).

## Swash, backwash, undertow

The water that rushes up the beach and hurls rock debris at the land is the **swash**; the water that drains back is the **backwash**. The **undertow** flows near the bottom away from the shore and can pull a bather out (Leong, PDF p127).

## Longshore drift

If waves reach the coast **at an angle**, the swash carries sand obliquely up the beach, but the backwash runs straight down the slope. Over many waves the sand moves in a zigzag along the shore; this is **longshore drift**, in the direction of the dominant wind (Leong, PDF p131). Currents that run parallel to the coast carry the same sand along (Leong, PDF p127). NCERT's supply of sediment, the 'sediment budget', is the base of beaches and bars (module 4).

## Tides as a working agent

Tides do not attack a cliff directly; by raising and lowering the water level they spread wave attack over a vertical zone instead of a single line. A tide channelled between islands or into bays and estuaries is a **tidal current**, and in estuaries tides help desilt harbours (NCERT kegy213, PDF pp2-4). The causes and types of tides are taught in Day 17 lesson GS-109 module 6.

### 3. Core: marine erosion and its landforms

*Objective:* Name the four processes of marine erosion and trace the sequence cliff, notch, wave-cut platform, cave, arch, stack and stump.

## Four processes (Leong, PDF pp127-128)

1. **Corrasion (abrasion)**: waves armed with rock fragments of all sizes strike the base of cliffs and wear them back.
2. **Attrition**: the moving fragments themselves hit each other and are ground into smaller pieces; the fine sand of beaches is largely made this way.
3. **Hydraulic action**: waves enter joints and crevices and compress the air inside. When the wave retreats the air expands with explosive force and the cracks widen.
4. **Solvent action (corrosion)**: seawater dissolves the calcium carbonate of limestone coasts. Leong says this is limited to limestone coasts.

The rate of erosion depends on the **nature of the rock**, the **amount of rock exposed** to the sea, the work of **tides and currents**, and **human interference** with coast protection; volcanism, glaciation and earth movement also matter (PDF p128).

## Headlands and bays

On an exposed coast of mixed rock, soft bands are cut back into inlets, coves or bays and hard bands stay as headlands or capes (Leong, PDF pp128-129). The pattern is called **differential erosion**.

## Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

A steep rock face by the sea is a **cliff**. The sea cuts a **notch** at its foot, undermining it until it collapses, so the cliff retreats. What is left is a gently seaward-sloping rock floor, the **wave-cut platform**, whose upper part is bare at low tide and which is strewn with debris from the cliff (Leong, PDF p130). The eroded material is dropped as an **offshore terrace**. NCERT describes the same platform in front of the retreating cliff, and calls a platform lying above the average height of waves a **wave-cut terrace**, with later offshore deposits forming a **wave-built terrace** in front (kegy206, PDF pp11-12).

The dip of rock layers matters. If the beds dip **seawards**, large blocks slide into the sea and the cliff rises in steps; if they dip **landwards** the cliff is more resistant (Leong, PDF pp129-130). Where a platform becomes very wide, the cliff is protected and further erosion is small.

## Caves, arches, stacks and stumps

Waves attack weak places at the base of a cliff and excavate **caves**. Two caves cut from opposite sides of a headland can meet to form a **natural arch**. When the arch roof collapses, the seaward end remains as a pillar of rock, a **stack**. Stacks wear down to **stumps**, only just visible above sea level at low tide (Leong, PDF pp130-131). NCERT adds that sea stacks are temporary remnants and that cliffs may finally be reduced to a narrow coastal plain (kegy206, PDF p12).

## Blowholes and geos

Waves force compressed air up a joint in the cave roof; the shaft may reach the surface as a **blow-hole (gloup)**. Collapse of the cave roof leaves a long narrow inlet called a **geo** (Leong, PDF p131).

## A one-line sequence to learn

Headland attack -> notch -> cliff retreat -> **platform** left behind -> cave -> arch -> stack -> stump.

> Leong's illustrations (the Needles, Old Man of Hoy and others) are in the British Isles; the extracts supply no Indian stack or arch example, so name Indian examples only from an atlas.

### 4. Core: marine deposition, bars, spits, lagoons and the sediment budget

*Objective:* Describe how beaches, dunes, bars, spits, tombolos and lagoons form, and explain why the sediment budget and mangroves protect a coast.

## Where the sediment comes from

Deposition needs a supply of sediment. NCERT says most beach sediment comes from land carried by streams and rivers or from wave erosion (kegy206, PDF p12). Eroded cliff debris is broken and rolled to roundness and dropped in the offshore, where longshore currents and waves distribute it as beaches along the shore and as bars in the nearshore zone (PDF p11).

## Beaches and dunes

**Beaches** are characteristic of shorelines dominated by deposition, though they may occur as patches even on rugged shores. They are **temporary**: a sandy beach that looks permanent may shrink to a narrow strip of coarse pebbles in another season (NCERT, PDF p12). Most beaches are sand; **shingle beaches** have small pebbles and even cobbles. Waves sort the load: coarser material is dropped at the top of the beach and finer material is carried down by the backwash (Leong, PDF p132).

Behind the beach, wind lifts sand and drops it as **sand dunes**, forming long ridges parallel to the coast, very common along low sedimentary coasts (NCERT, PDF p12).

## Bars, barriers, spits, tombolos and lagoons

- **Offshore bar**: a ridge of sand and shingle lying in the sea approximately parallel to the coast. It is submerged. When further sand makes it show above water it is a **barrier bar** (NCERT, PDF pp11-12).
- **Spit**: a barrier bar attached at one end to the land, often a headland or the side of a bay; Leong defines it as a ridge with one end joined to land and the other projecting into the sea. Oblique waves can curve it into a **recurved spit (hook)** (PDF pp132-133).
- **Bar across a mouth**: where barrier bars and spits grow across a bay mouth, only a narrow opening is left, and the bay becomes a **lagoon**. Sediment from the land or from the beach fills the lagoon, which becomes a swamp and finally a **coastal plain** (NCERT, PDF pp12-13).
- **Tombolo**: a bar joining an island or rock to the mainland, such as the connecting bar at Lumley Beach, Sierra Leone (Leong, PDF p133).

NCERT gives Indian examples: a satellite picture of part of the **Godavari delta** shows a **spit** (kegy206, PDF p13), and the **Krishna delta**, Andhra Pradesh, is shown as an example of delta building (PDF p4). Standard knowledge (not in the extracts; verify) cites **Chilika** in Odisha and **Pulicat** on the Tamil Nadu-Andhra border as lagoons of the east coast.

## Deltas: river deposition at the coast

A **delta** is formed when the load carried by a river is dumped into the sea and, if not carried away along the coast, spreads as a low cone. Deltas are well sorted and stratified: coarse material settles first, and silt and clay are carried farther out. As the delta grows, **distributaries** lengthen (NCERT, PDF p4). Large sediment-rich rivers build deltas along low sedimentary coasts (PDF p12).

## The sediment budget and the natural buffer

NCERT's box states that **offshore bars give the first defence against storm or tsunami waves** by absorbing most of their force. Next come barriers, beaches, beach dunes and **mangroves**. If human activity disturbs the **sediment budget** (the balance of sediment arriving and leaving) or removes mangroves, these forms erode away and human settlements take the first strike (kegy206, PDF p13). This is the physical basis for the Mains answers on erosion, sand mining and mangroves (module 9). Depositional landforms need a steady supply of material to last, but storm and tsunami waves can change a coast drastically whatever the supply (PDF p12).

### 5. Core: types of coast, emergence and submergence, and India's west and east coasts

*Objective:* Classify coasts as high rocky or low sedimentary (NCERT) and as submergent or emergent (Leong), and place India's west and east coasts correctly with the variant noted.

## NCERT's two coast types

NCERT assumes sea level is constant and uses two types to explain how coastal landforms evolve (kegy206, PDF p11). A **high rocky coast** is highly irregular and indented, with hillsides dropping sharply into the water; erosion dominates, giving cliffs and wave-cut platforms, and later beaches and bars from the eroded material. NCERT labels it 'retreating landward' or 'submerged'. A **low sedimentary coast** is smooth, with a gentle slope into the water; deposition dominates, giving bars, barrier bars, spits, lagoons, marshes and, where large rivers bring sediment, deltas. NCERT labels it 'advancing seaward' or 'emerged'. The feature-by-feature table is in the master notes.

The **west coast of India** is described as a **high rocky retreating coast** where erosional forms dominate; the **east coast** is a **low sedimentary coast** where depositional forms dominate (kegy206, PDF p12). Both statements are teachable directly from NCERT. Leong, however, lists the western coast of the Deccan among his emergent upland coasts (PDF p136). The books use different frameworks; for UPSC, use NCERT's rocky-erosional versus sedimentary-depositional contrast for the west and east coasts and use Leong only for the global names below.

## Leong's classification: submergence and emergence

Leong divides coasts into two basic types (PDF pp133-136).

**Coastlines of submergence** (land sinks or sea rises):

- **Ria coast**: drowned river valleys, long narrow branching inlets, formed where mountains run at right angles (transverse) to the coast; not glaciated and getting deeper seawards. North-west Spain, south-west Ireland and Devon and Cornwall are examples.
- **Fiord (fjord) coast**: submerged U-shaped glacial troughs with steep walls, tributaries joining at right angles, and a shallow **threshold** at the seaward end. Norway, Alaska, British Columbia, southern Chile and the South Island of New Zealand are examples; the Sogne Fiord is 177 km long and almost 1,220 m deep mid-channel.
- **Dalmatian coast**: ridges parallel (concordant) to the coast; submergence leaves elongated islands parallel to the shore.
- **Estuarine coast**: drowned river mouths forming funnel-shaped estuaries that make good ports where not silted.

**Coastlines of emergence** (land rises or sea falls), less common:

- **Uplifted lowland coast**: uplift of the continental shelf gives a smooth gentle lowland, shallow waters with **lagoons, salt marshes and mud-flats**, and old ports become inland towns.
- **Emergent upland coast**: faulting lifts coastal plateaus; **raised beaches**, steep cliffs and deeper offshore water, with few good ports.

Leong's text says sea level rose almost 90 m since the Ice Age, a figure that varies in other sources (verify). The words **eustatic** (global sea-level change) and **relative** (sea level change relative to a coast whose land also moves) are standard terms used to explain why a coast may appear to emerge or submerge; they are not in the extracts, so verify definitions in a standard text.

## Why the 2023 Prelims item fits here

Leong says the uplifted lowland coast has shallow waters with lagoons, salt marshes and mud-flats (PDF p136). A marshland produced by **repeated falls in sea level** is therefore an emergent-coast feature. The 2023 Prelims question lists the Rann of Kutch, Naupada Swamp, Marakkanam salt pans and Bhitarkanika mangroves; the extracts do not describe any of them, so use the classification here and check a map. Standard knowledge (verify): the Rann of Kutch is a vast seasonal salt marsh believed to have been a shallow arm of the sea, Bhitarkanika is a delta mangrove, Marakkanam has human-made salt pans, and Naupada Swamp is a brackish coastal wetland in Srikakulam district, Andhra Pradesh.

### 6. Core: islands and coral reefs as coastal and oceanic landforms

*Objective:* Classify islands as continental or oceanic, describe coral reefs as landforms (fringing, barrier, atoll) and explain Darwin's and Daly's theories.

## Islands (Leong, PDF pp138-139)

An **island** is land surrounded on all sides by water.

**Continental islands** were once part of the mainland and were cut off by a sinking of land or a rise of sea level. They share rock structure, plants and animals with the mainland across the channel. They can be individual islands (Sri Lanka, separated by the Palk Strait; Madagascar by the Mozambique Channel; Tasmania by the Bass Strait), archipelagoes (the British Isles) or island arcs (the Aleutians, the Kurile Islands and other arcs of the Pacific coasts). NCERT's India chapter notes that Sri Lanka is separated from India by the Gulf of Mannar and the Palk Strait (kegy101, PDF p5).

**Oceanic islands** stand in the open ocean with no link to a continent; their flora and fauna differ from the continents'. They are either **volcanic islands** (tops of volcanic cones built up from the sea floor, such as Mauna Loa in Hawaii) or **coral islands** (low islands built by coral animals, such as the Laccadives and Maldives of the Indian Ocean, the Marshall Islands and Bermuda). India's own island groups, Andaman and Nicobar and Lakshadweep, are covered in Day 17 lesson GS-130.

## Coral reefs as landforms

Coral polyps are tiny animals that live in colonies in tropical seas and secrete calcium carbonate; when they die, their limy skeletons cement into **coralline limestone** (Leong, PDF pp139-140). Reef-building coral needs warm water (not below about 20 degrees C), clear, saline, sediment-free water and a depth shallower than about 55 m (30 fathoms; 30 x 1.83 m = about 55 m), so reefs are tropical and generally absent on west coasts of continents where cold currents or upwelling occur. The full conditions, the polyp-algae partnership, bleaching and acidification are taught in Day 17 lesson GS-109 module 8; this module keeps the reef as a landform.

## Three reef types (Leong, PDF pp141-142)

- **Fringing reef**: a coralline platform close to the shore, about a kilometre wide, sometimes cut off by a shallow lagoon; absent at river mouths.
- **Barrier reef**: separated from the coast by a wide, deep channel or lagoon, with gaps that serve as shipping entrances. The Great Barrier Reef off Queensland is 1,930 km long in Leong, with a channel up to 160 km wide; PMF IAS gives about 2,300 km, so quote the length as a range and flag it (verify).
- **Atoll**: a ring-shaped reef enclosing a shallow lagoon with no land in the centre, broken in a few places; Suvadiva in the Maldives has a lagoon over 64 km across.

The table of the three types is in the master notes.

## Origin: two theories (Leong, PDF pp142-143)

- **Darwin's subsidence theory (1842)**: a fringing reef forms round an island or extinct volcano; the island slowly subsides while the coral grows upwards; the fringing reef becomes a barrier reef with a lagoon, and when the island has vanished only the ring remains as an atoll. Later study of the Pacific floor supports subsidence.
- **Daly's glacial control theory (1910)**: in the Ice Age cold water stopped coral growth and waves planed islands down; as the ice melted sea level rose and corals grew upwards on the planed platforms at about one-third of a metre per decade. Boring evidence supports a change of sea level, while the deepest borings reach basalt, matching Darwin's subsided islands.

Leong concludes that a combination of the two theories accounts for the important features. The extract notes that none of the theories is universally accepted.

### 7. Core: ocean currents, their causes, types and circulation

*Objective:* Define ocean currents, list their causes, classify them and describe the main circulations.

## What an ocean current is

An **ocean current** is a continuous flow of a huge amount of water in a definite direction, like a river in the ocean; waves, by contrast, move energy while water stays roughly in place (NCERT kegy213, PDF pp1, 4). Leong calls currents large masses of surface water circulating in regular patterns; those flowing from the equator towards the poles are **warm currents** and those from polar regions towards the equator are **cold currents** (PDF p151).

## Causes

NCERT separates **primary forces** that start the movement from **secondary forces** that shape the flow (PDF p4).

- Primary: **solar heating** (heated water expands, so water near the equator is about 8 cm higher than in the middle latitudes, giving a slight gradient down which water flows); **wind**, which pushes the surface by friction; **gravity**, which pulls the piled water down; and the **Coriolis force**, which turns moving water to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. The great circular flows are called **gyres**.
- Secondary: differences in **density**. Salt water is denser than fresh and cold water denser than warm, so dense water sinks, and cold polar water moves towards the equator at depth while warm water moves poleward on the surface.

Leong lists five factors with planetary winds the dominant one: the **planetary winds**, **temperature**, **salinity**, the **Earth's rotation** (clockwise gyres in the north, anticlockwise in the south) and the **shape of the land masses**, which deflect and split currents (PDF pp151-153). In the North Indian Ocean the direction of the current reverses with the monsoon: the **South-West Monsoon Drift** in summer (June to October) and the **North-East Monsoon Drift** in winter (from December), the clearest proof of wind control (PDF pp155-156).

## Types

- By depth: **surface currents** are about 10 per cent of ocean water, in the upper 400 m; **deep water currents** are the other 90 per cent and move because of density and gravity, sinking at high latitudes where cold raises density (NCERT, PDF p4).
- By temperature: **cold currents** bring cold water into warm water areas, usually on west coasts of continents in low and middle latitudes and on east coasts in higher latitudes in the northern hemisphere; **warm currents** are usually on east coasts in low and middle latitudes and on west coasts in high latitudes of the northern hemisphere (PDF p4).

Currents are strongest at the surface (over five knots at most) and weaken with depth (below 0.5 knots) (PDF p4).

## The main circuits (Leong, PDF pp153-155)

- **North Atlantic**: the North Equatorial Current feeds the Gulf Stream, which becomes the **North Atlantic Drift** to Europe; the cool **Canaries Current** returns south; the cold **Labrador Current** meets the Gulf Stream off Newfoundland; the middle is the Sargasso Sea.
- **South Atlantic**: warm **Brazilian Current**, then the cold **Benguela Current** on the African side.
- **Pacific**: warm **Kuroshio** (Japan Current) and North Pacific Drift; cold **Oyashio**, California Current; in the south, East Australian Current and the cold **Humboldt (Peruvian) Current**.
- **Indian Ocean**: South Indian circuit with the Agulhas, West Australian currents; the monsoon drifts in the north.

### 8. Core: effects of ocean currents on coasts, climate and marine life

*Objective:* Explain how warm and cold currents change coastal climate, rainfall, fog and fisheries, and distinguish currents from water masses.

## Effects (NCERT, PDF p5; Leong, PDF pp150, 155-157)

- **Temperature**: west coasts of tropical and subtropical continents are bordered by cool water with narrow temperature ranges, fog and aridity; mid-latitude west coasts are bordered by warm water, giving a marine climate with cool summers and mild winters. Leong says the British Isles are about 10 to 16 degrees C warmer than the average for their latitude because westerlies carry the warmth of the North Atlantic Drift ashore, while Norway's coast is ice-free even at 60-70 degrees N.
- **Rain and fog**: onshore winds over warm currents bring rain; over cold currents they are dry. Fogs form where warm and cold currents meet, as off Newfoundland and Japan; Leong says the cold Peruvian Current helps make the Chilean and Peruvian coasts practically rainless.
- **Fisheries and coasts**: mixing of warm and cold currents replenishes oxygen and favours plankton, so the best fishing grounds lie in these mixing zones (NCERT). Peru's guano deposits and fish shoals are a Leong example.
- **Geomorphic work**: currents carry the sediment that waves loosen and deposit it along the coast; longshore currents build spits and bars and tidal currents scour estuaries (Leong PDF pp127, 132; NCERT PDF p2).

## Currents and water masses (for the 2019 15-mark question)

A **water mass** is a large body of water with fairly uniform temperature and salinity that forms in a particular source region and then spreads, usually by sinking; a **current** is a flow in a definite direction. The extract shows that deep waters sink at high latitudes where cold raises density (NCERT, PDF p4), but it does not define water masses; standard texts do (verify). Impacts differ: surface currents mainly affect coastal climate and fisheries, while deep water masses affect heat transport, ocean oxygen and nutrient supply to the surface through mixing (standard; verify).

### 9. Master: coastal erosion, the sediment budget and management

*Objective:* Explain coastal erosion as a sediment-budget problem with its natural and human causes, effects and management techniques with their limits; India's NCCR data and answer framework are in Day 17 lesson GS-130.

## Erosion as a sediment-budget problem

A coast erodes when more sediment leaves a stretch of shore than arrives. This is the **sediment budget** of module 4: sediment comes from rivers and from cliff erosion, moves along the shore by longshore drift, and is lost to deeper water or to the land. NCERT adds that on a sedimentary coast the maintenance of depositional features depends on a steady supply of sediment, and that offshore bars, beaches, dunes and mangroves are the natural buffer (kegy206, PDF pp12-13).

## Natural processes

Waves dash along the coast; during cyclone landfall **storm surges** destroy beaches and strip the top layer; in estuaries **tidal bores** damage banks (PMF IAS, PDF p407). Winds, waves, tides, currents, geomorphology and sediment supply together decide whether a coast erodes or builds. Rising sea level adds to the risk (standard knowledge; verify).

## Human causes

- **Ports and construction along the coast** interrupt longshore sediment movement: sand piles up on the up-drift side and the down-drift side is starved.
- **Dredging in ports and dumping the dredged sediment in deep sea** rather than along the coast (PMF IAS, PDF p408).
- Removal of **sand and mangroves** shrinks both the budget and the buffer.
- Standard knowledge (verify): upstream dams and river-sand mining cut the sediment that rivers bring; groundwater and oil extraction can sink land.

Accretion, the widening of a coast, is not inherently destructive, but it can harm deltaic ecosystems by reducing inland water flow (PMF IAS, PDF p408).

## Management techniques (PMF IAS, PDF pp407-409)

- **Groyne**: built perpendicular to the shore to trap sand moving along it; it may starve the down-drift side and move the problem along the coast (standard knowledge).
- **Seawall, revetment**: hard structures that absorb or reflect wave energy.
- **Submerged breakwater**: about 500 m from the shoreline, to break wave energy.
- **Beach nourishment**: dredged sand placed on an eroding beach; needs a continuing supply.

Treat beaches as part of the sea, not the land, and allow natural nourishment; mangrove and shelter-belt planting (standard; verify) complements hard structures. Effects: loss of land, houses and roads, beaches and tourism, damage to fishing livelihoods and habitat, and heavier storm-surge damage when natural buffers go (NCERT, PDF p13).

## Three Mains themes as mechanisms (standard knowledge; verify)

- **Sand mining (2019 Mains GS III)**: removing sand from beaches, dunes and river mouths shrinks the sediment budget, so waves reach the land; dune and mangrove habitat is damaged. Cite state examples only from verified sources.
- **Seawater intrusion (2025 Mains GS III)**: when a coastal aquifer is pumped faster than it is recharged, the freshwater head falls and saline water moves inland; remedies are limiting extraction, recharge structures, subsurface barriers and restoring mangroves and wetlands.
- **Mangroves (2019 Mains GS I)**: depleted by aquaculture, reclamation, urbanisation, pollution and freshwater diversion; important as a storm and tsunami buffer (NCERT, PDF p13), as fish nurseries and as shoreline stabilisers.

## Where the India data sits

PMF IAS reports that more of the east coast than the west eroded in 1990-2018, with West Bengal the most affected state at about 60 per cent, and that about one-third of the national coastline eroded (it prints 32 per cent from the NCCR assessment; the NCCR report is widely quoted as about 33.6 per cent, so verify). The state-wise findings, the east-west reasons and the 2022 Mains GS III Q18 answer framework are taught in Day 17 lesson GS-130.

## Tsunamis (largely standard knowledge; verify)

The extracts mention tsunami waves only as events that cause far-reaching changes quickly and as a danger that offshore bars, barriers and mangroves help absorb (kegy206, PDF pp11, 13). The remaining points are standard knowledge to be checked in a recognised text:

- **What**: a tsunami is a train of very long sea waves produced by a sudden displacement of a large volume of water, usually by an undersea earthquake, but also by submarine landslides and volcanic eruptions. The name is Japanese.
- **How**: a sudden vertical movement of the sea floor pushes up the water column; in the deep ocean the wave is only slightly high but very fast and long; as it enters shallow water it slows (the same friction that makes ordinary waves slow in NCERT's account) and its height builds.
- **Where**: most often around subduction zones of the Pacific Ocean's Ring of Fire; the Indian Ocean event of 26 December 2004 off Sumatra struck the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the Indian east coast, and the 2011 event off Japan is another example. Check dates and casualties before quoting.
- **Consequences**: loss of life; destruction of ports, houses and roads; coastal erosion and landform change; salt contamination of farmland and aquifers; damage to mangroves and reefs; long-term livelihoods and displacement. Natural buffers (offshore bars, dunes, mangroves) reduce the force (kegy206, PDF p13).
- Earthquakes and plate tectonics are taught in other lessons; link to them for the origin.

### Notes: Core coastal terms

- Geomorphic agent: running water, groundwater, wind, glaciers or waves that perform erosion and deposition (NCERT kegy206).
- Swash: water rushing up the beach; backwash: water draining back; undertow: bottom flow away from shore.
- Longshore drift: zigzag movement of sand along a beach when waves arrive at an angle.
- Wave-cut platform: rock floor left as a cliff retreats; stack and stump: remnants of an arch.
- Bar, barrier bar and spit: offshore ridge; ridge exposed above water; barrier attached to land at one end; lagoon: water behind a bank closing a bay.
- Sediment budget: the balance between sediment arriving at a coast and sediment leaving it.
- Gyre: a large circular current system; Coriolis force turns moving water right in the northern and left in the southern hemisphere.

### Notes: Erosion versus deposition landforms

- Landforms by dominant process.

### Notes: Marine erosion processes

- Corrasion: rock fragments hurled by waves wear the base of cliffs.
- Attrition: fragments collide and break into finer pieces, making beach sand.
- Hydraulic action: air compressed in cracks expands explosively as the wave retreats.
- Solvent action: seawater dissolves calcium carbonate on limestone coasts only (Leong).
- Sequence: notch, cliff retreat, platform, cave, arch, stack, stump.

### Notes: Coral reefs and islands

- Coral needs water not colder than 20 degrees C, depth shallower than about 55 m (30 fathoms), and clear, saline, sediment-free moving water (Leong); full conditions and ecology in Day 17 lesson GS-109 module 8.
- Reefs are generally absent on west coasts of continents where cold currents or upwelling occur.

### Notes: Ocean currents: cause and effect

- Solar heating, wind, gravity and Coriolis force start the flow; density differences shape it (NCERT).

- In the north Indian Ocean the current direction reverses with the monsoon (Leong).
- Currents and longshore currents carry sediment and shape coasts; tidal currents scour estuaries.

### Notes: Coastal erosion: mechanism and management

- A coast erodes when sediment leaving a stretch exceeds sediment arriving (the sediment budget).

### Last-week summary

- Erosion processes: corrasion, attrition, hydraulic action, solution (limestone only); headland: notch, cliff retreat, platform, cave, arch, stack, stump.
- Deposition: offshore bar (submerged), barrier bar (above water), spit (attached at one end), tombolo (island to mainland), lagoon behind a bar; sediment budget = arriving minus leaving.
- NCERT: west coast high rocky retreating (erosion), east coast low sedimentary advancing (deposition); Leong: submergence (ria, fiord, Dalmatian, estuarine) vs emergence (uplifted lowland, emergent upland).
- Reefs: fringing, barrier, atoll; Darwin 1842 subsidence, Daly 1910 glacial control.
- Currents: heating, wind, gravity, Coriolis (right in north, left in south), density secondary; surface 10 per cent in upper 400 m, deep 90 per cent; Humboldt, Benguela, Labrador cold; Gulf Stream, Kuroshio warm.
