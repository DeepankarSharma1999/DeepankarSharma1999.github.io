## CT-009: Fractions, decimals, LCM and HCF

Prelims CSAT (qualifying) · CSAT · Quantitative aptitude

### Bridge: Reading fractions and decimals: the minimum notation

A **fraction** a/b says: split a whole into b equal parts and take a of them. The number above the line, a, is the **numerator** (how many parts are taken); the number below, b, is the **denominator** (into how many equal parts the whole is cut). The denominator can never be 0. So 3/4 means three of four equal parts, and its value sits between 0 and 1.

A **decimal** is a fraction whose denominator is 10, 100, 1000 and so on, written with a point. Each place after the point is worth one-tenth of the place before it: in 0.25 the 2 is in the tenths place (2/10) and the 5 in the hundredths place (5/100), so 0.25 = 25/100 = 1/4. Moving the decimal point one place right multiplies by 10; one place left divides by 10. So 7.5 x 100 = 750 and 3.25 x 100 = 325.

The small word **of** between two quantities means multiply: one-third of 90 is 1/3 x 90 = 30, and two-thirds of three-quarters is 2/3 x 3/4 = 1/2. A **whole number** with a fraction attached, such as 3 1/2, is a **mixed number**; it means 3 + 1/2.

The CSAT writes decimals with a raised point (7.5 is printed as 7·5) and sometimes writes fractions as 'one-third' or '1/3rd'. They are the same things written differently; always rewrite the numbers in the form you can calculate with before doing anything else.

### 2. Fractions: types, lowest terms, operations and the fraction that remains

*Objective:* Reduce fractions using divisibility tests, add with the least common denominator, and compute a fraction of what remains after repeated reductions.

## The words

A **proper** fraction has numerator smaller than denominator (3/4, below 1); an **improper** one has numerator at least the denominator (7/4); a **mixed number** is a whole plus a proper fraction (1 3/4 = 7/4, since 1 x 4 + 3 = 7). **Equivalent** fractions have the same value: multiply or divide the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number, so 2/3 = 4/6 = 6/9. A fraction is in **lowest terms** when its numerator and denominator have HCF 1.

## Reducing with divisibility tests

Cancel common factors using the tests of Day 17 lesson CT-008 module 4. Original worked example: reduce 252/378. Both even: 126/189. Digit sums 9 and 18, so divisible by 9: 14/21. Multiples of 7: **2/3**. Check: HCF(252, 378) = 126, and 252 / 126 = 2, 378 / 126 = 3.

## Adding and subtracting

Use the **least common denominator**, the LCM of the denominators, not their product. Original example: 5/12 + 7/18. LCM(12, 18) = 36, so 15/36 + 14/36 = **29/36**. The product 216 gives the same 29/36 after reducing 174/216, at more cost.

## Multiplying and dividing

Multiply tops and bottoms, cancelling first: 2/3 of 3/4 = 1/2. To divide, multiply by the **reciprocal**: 5 / (7/4) = 20/7.

## The fraction that remains

If one-third of what is present leaves each year, **two-thirds stays**, and the stay-fraction multiplies each year. After n years the remainder is (2/3)^n of the original. Official item 2017-q12 (one-third of a community migrates every year; what is left after six years?): (2/3)^6 = 64/729, which is option D by our working (analysis, not the official key). The traps are the subtraction habit, 1 - 6 x 1/3 = -1, which is impossible, and taking one-third of the *original* each year, which would remove 6/3 of it.

$$ remaining after n steps = (1 - f)^n x original, where f is the fraction removed each step $$

Option check: 32/243 = (2/3)^5 is the five-year value (off by one); 16/243 and 32/729 mix powers; only 64/729 = 2^6 / 3^6 is (2/3)^6.

Terminating decimals are fractions over a power of 10 (0.375 = 375/1000 = 3/8); recurring ones need module 4.

### 3. Comparing fractions and the add-the-same-number rule

*Objective:* Order fractions by five methods, prove what happens when the same positive number is added to numerator and denominator, and find which fraction changes least.

## Five ways to compare a/b with c/d (positive denominators)

1. **Cross-multiplication**: compare a x d with c x b; the side with the larger product is the larger fraction.
2. **Common denominator**: rewrite both over the LCM of the denominators and compare numerators.
3. **Decimal conversion**: divide, then compare (best for denominators 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 25).
4. **Benchmarks**: place each against 1/2 or 1 first (5/9 is just above 1/2; 4/9 just below).
5. **Distance from 1**: n/(n+1) is short of 1 by 1/(n+1), so a larger n gives a fraction closer to 1: 5/6 beats 4/5 beats 3/4.

Same numerator: the **smaller** denominator wins (1/3 > 1/5).

Original worked example, ordering 7/12, 5/8, 11/18 and 3/5. LCM(12, 8, 18, 5) = 360, giving 210, 225, 220 and 216 over 360. So 5/8 > 11/18 > 3/5 > 7/12; decimals agree (0.625, 0.611, 0.600, 0.583). Cross-multiplication on the top pair: 5 x 18 = 90 against 11 x 8 = 88. See the figure.

**Edge case for method 1**: it assumes positive denominators; make the denominator positive first.

## Adding the same positive number k to numerator and denominator

Let the fraction be a/b with a >= 0, b > 0, k > 0. Then

$$ (a+k)/(b+k) - a/b = [b(a+k) - a(b+k)] / [b(b+k)] = k(b - a) / [b(b+k)] $$

The bottom is positive, so the sign is the sign of (b - a):

- **proper** fraction (a < b): the fraction **increases**;
- **improper** fraction (a > b): it **decreases**;
- **a = b** (the fraction is 1): no change.

A proper fraction stays proper (a+k < b+k), moving towards 1 without passing it. Edge cases tested: 0/5 with 1 added to both gives 1/6 (up); 7/3 gives 8/4 = 2, below 7/3 (down); 4/4 stays 1. Checked by computer for 0 <= a < 50, 1 <= b < 50, 1 <= k < 30.

Item 2019-q50 asks this for a proper fraction. By the formula the result is always greater (option B in the printed list; analysis, not the official key); the reason is the sign of b - a.

## How much does it change? (item 2020-q76)

The four fractions 2/3, 3/4, 4/5, 5/6 all have b - a = 1, and k = 5. The change is 5 x 1 / [b(b+5)] = 5 / [b(b+5)], so it is **smaller when b is larger**:

| Fraction | New fraction | Change = 5 / [b(b+5)] |
|---|---|---|
| 2/3 | 7/8 | 5/24 = 0.208 |
| 3/4 | 8/9 | 5/36 = 0.139 |
| 4/5 | 9/10 | 1/10 = 0.100 |
| 5/6 | 10/11 | 5/66 = 0.076 |

The minimum change is for 5/6 (option D in the printed list; our working, not the official key).

### 4. Decimals, recurring decimals and the nines-and-zeros rule

*Objective:* Decide whether a fraction terminates, convert any recurring decimal to a fraction by an exact rule, and test it on edge cases.

## Terminating or recurring?

A fraction in **lowest terms** p/q gives a terminating decimal exactly when q has no prime factors except 2 and 5. The reason: a terminating decimal has denominator 10^n = 2^n x 5^n, so q must divide such a number. Edge cases: 3/8 and 7/20 terminate; 1/6 recurs; **3/12 terminates** because it reduces to 1/4 first. Always reduce before testing (checked by long division for numerators 1 to 59, denominators 1 to 199).

## The exact conversion rule

Write the decimal as 0.(non-repeating digits)(repeating block). Let a be the number of non-repeating digits and b the length of the repeating block. Then

$$ value = (N - M) / (b nines followed by a zeros) $$

where N is the whole digit string (non-repeating part then one copy of the block) read as an integer, and M is the non-repeating part read as an integer (0 if there is none).

**Why it works**, shown for 0.1(6). Let x = 0.1666... Then 10x = 1.666... and 100x = 16.666... Subtracting, 100x - 10x = 15, so 90x = 15 and x = 1/6. Here 90 is one nine then one zero; 15 is 16 - 1.

| Decimal | N - M | Denominator | Fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.(27) | 27 | 99 | 3/11 |
| 0.(09) | 9 | 99 | 1/11 |
| 0.1(6) | 16 - 1 = 15 | 90 | 1/6 |
| 0.0(3) | 3 - 0 = 3 | 90 | 1/30 |
| 0.4(27) | 427 - 4 = 423 | 990 | 47/110 |
| 0.(9) | 9 | 9 | 1 |

Each row was confirmed by long division.

## Edge cases

- **0.(9) = 1 exactly.** Let x = 0.999... Then 10x - x = 9, so x = 1. There is no number between 0.999... and 1; they are two writings of the same number.
- A block can start with zero: 0.(09) has b = 2, so 99 in the denominator, not 9.
- 1.2(27) and 1.(27) differ: 1.(27) = 14/11 = 28/22, while 1.2(27) = 27/22.

## Item 2020-q53 and item 2020-q12

The item gives 1.272727... with block 27, so the value is 1 + 27/99 = 1 + 3/11 = **14/11**, option B in the printed list (our working, not the official key). By size, 13/11 is 1.18..., 127/99 is 1.2828... (127 written over 99), 137/99 is 1.3838....

In item 2020-q12 (sticks of 7.5 ft and 3.25 ft), remove the decimals: times 100 gives 750 and 325. HCF(750, 325) = 25, so 0.25 ft. The reading used: the smallest positive length obtainable by adding and subtracting the stick lengths is their HCF (for example 10 x 7.5 - 23 x 3.25 = 0.25 ft), the standard meaning of "minimum length" here; a reading, not a key.

### 5. HCF and LCM structure: exponents, the product rule and fractions

*Objective:* Explain with prime exponents why HCF x LCM = product holds for two numbers and fails for three, find the missing number from HCF and LCM, and find the HCF and LCM of fractions.

## Recap and the exponent view

Day 17 lesson CT-008 module 3 gives the methods. The idea to carry: for every prime p, the **HCF takes the smallest exponent** of p across the numbers and the **LCM takes the largest**. Recap of the example in Day 17 lesson CT-008 module 3: 84 = 2^2 x 3 x 7 and 126 = 2 x 3^2 x 7 give HCF = 2 x 3 x 7 = 42 and LCM = 2^2 x 3^2 x 7 = 252.

## Why the product rule holds for two numbers

For a prime with exponents e and f in the two numbers, **smallest + largest = e + f**. So HCF x LCM has the same exponents as a x b: the rule holds for **two numbers** (tested for all pairs up to 199).

## Why it fails for three

Sort the exponents of a prime as e1 <= e2 <= e3. The HCF contributes e1, the LCM contributes e3, the product of the three numbers contributes e1 + e2 + e3. They differ by the middle exponent e2. The rule therefore holds **only if e2 = 0 for every prime**, that is, no prime divides two of the numbers: the numbers are pairwise coprime. Check: 4, 6, 10 have HCF 2 and LCM 60, so HCF x LCM = 120, not 240. The numbers 6, 10, 15 give 1 x 30 = 30, not 900. For 2, 3, 5 (pairwise coprime) it holds: 1 x 30 = 30. Tested for every triple up to 30.

## Finding the missing number

For two numbers with HCF h and LCM L, with one number a: the other is b = h x L / a. Original example: h = 6, L = 180, a = 36 gives b = 30. Check: HCF(36, 30) = 6 and LCM(36, 30) = 180. Both numbers are multiples of h, and a divides L.

## HCF and LCM of fractions

For fractions **in lowest terms**:

$$ HCF = HCF(numerators) / LCM(denominators);  LCM = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators) $$

Item 2020-q51 (greatest x such that 3 1/2 m and 8 3/4 m are whole multiples of x): the fractions are 7/2 and 35/4, so HCF = HCF(7, 35) / LCM(2, 4) = 7/4 = 1 3/4 m (option D in the printed list; our working). Check: 7/2 = 2 x 7/4 and 35/4 = 5 x 7/4. Common-denominator method: 14/4 and 35/4, HCF(14, 35) = 7, so 7/4. **Edge case**: the formula fails on unreduced fractions. For 6/4 and 9/6 it gives HCF(6, 9) / LCM(4, 6) = 1/4, but both equal 3/2 and their HCF is 3/2. Tested on 3,000 random triples of lowest-terms fractions.

## Three theory items (analysis, not official keys)

- **2026-q59**: for two numbers HCF x LCM = a x b, so if it equals a^3 then **b = a^2**: one number is a perfect square and a^2 - a = a(a - 1) is even. Both statements hold.
- **2025-q47**: QQQ = 3 x 37 x Q and 481 = 13 x 37; 13 never divides a digit Q, so the HCF is 37.
- **2025-q28**: each of 7, 11, 13 must be in some number and missing from some number: 6 patterns per prime, 216 ordered triples, 32 sets of different numbers (40 with repeats): always above 8.

### 6. Word problems: repeated events, equal division, remainders and chains

*Objective:* Recognise which of five word-problem patterns a stem uses, choose HCF or LCM correctly, and handle the period, counting and remainder details that decide the option.

## Pattern 1: repeated events use the LCM

Events with periods p1, p2, p3 from a common start coincide again after **LCM(p1, p2, p3)** and every LCM after that. Watch the wording: "every 5th day" has period 5, but "after a gap of 2 days" can mean 2 days skipped, so period 3. Meetings in a time T: floor(T / LCM) later ones, plus 1 if the start counts. Original: bells ring every 12, 15 and 18 s, together at the start; LCM = 180 s, so in 3600 s they ring together 20 more times, 21 counting the start.

**Day of the week**: add LCM mod 7. Item 2021-q75 (every 5th, 24th and 9th day, together on a Sunday): LCM = 360 = 7 x 51 + 3, so **Wednesday** (option B; our working).

**Item 2019-q35** (swims after a gap of 2 and 3 days, together on 1 January). Reading used: days skipped, periods 3 and 4, LCM 12, so 13 January (option D); periods 2 and 3 would give 7 January, also an option (module 7).

**Item 2023-q50** (signals take 25, 39, 60 s from green to red, equal green and red times): a full green-to-green cycle is **twice** that, 50, 78, 120 s; LCM = 7800 s = 2 h 10 min, so 4:10 p.m. (option B). The uncorrected 25, 39, 60 gives 3:05 p.m., which is no option.

**Item 2026-q10** (jumps of 4, 6, 5 feet from mark 199): common marks 199 + 60k; up to 1000 these are k = 0 to 13, so 199 lies inside the 195 to 1000 window, so it counts: **14** (option D by our working; analysis, not the official key). Counting from 259 only (13) is the trap.

## Pattern 2: equal division uses the HCF

Largest tile, longest equal pieces, largest bottle: **HCF**. Original: a 630 cm by 450 cm floor, HCF 90 cm, 7 x 5 = 35 tiles; ropes of 84, 126, 210 m, HCF 42 m, 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 pieces. "How many sizes are possible" asks for the **factors of the HCF**: item 2024-q19, HCF(399, 532) = 7 x 19 = 133, sizes 1, 7, 19, 133: **4** (option B; our working).

## Pattern 3: remainders

1. Same remainder r with every divisor: **LCM x m + r**. Item 2020-q54: LCM(3, 4, 5, 6) = 60, so 60m + 2; four-digit smallest is 60 x 17 + 2 = **1022**. Item 2022-q65: LCM(6, 9, 12, 15, 18) = 180, above 1000: 180 x 6 + 3 = **1083**.
2. Constant gap: LCM x m minus the gap (2025-q18 gives 1078; CT-008 module 3).
3. Greatest number leaving remainders r1, r2, r3 on a, b, c: **HCF(a - r1, b - r2, c - r3)**, valid only if the result **exceeds every remainder**. Original: 123, 89, 115 leave 3, 5, 7: HCF(120, 84, 108) = 12 > 7. Edge: 245 and 1029 leave 5 and 4, but HCF(240, 1025) = 5 is not greater than 5, so no such divisor exists.

## Pattern 4: counting common multiples

Numbers up to N divisible by both a and b: floor(N / LCM). Item 2019-q15: multiples of 4 and 6 are multiples of 12; floor(100 / 12) = **8**. Trap: 25 + 16, or 4 x 6 = 24.

## Pattern 5: ratio chains with a side condition

Item 2022-q47: A = 2B = 3C = 4D = 5E = 6F, each share an even whole number, so A is a multiple of 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12, that is of 120. Total = 120 x (1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1/5 + 1/6) = 120 x 49/20 = **294** (option C). A = 60 gives 147 but D's share 15 is odd.

### Notes: Definitions

- Fraction a/b: a parts of a whole cut into b equal parts (b not 0). Proper: a < b. Improper: a >= b. Mixed: whole plus proper fraction.
- Lowest terms: HCF of numerator and denominator is 1.
- Equivalent fractions: same value, obtained by multiplying or dividing numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.
- HCF (highest common factor): the largest number dividing all the given numbers. LCM (least common multiple): the smallest positive number divisible by all of them.
- Coprime numbers: HCF 1. Pairwise coprime: every pair has HCF 1.
- Recurring (repeating) decimal: digits after the decimal point repeat forever from some place; the repeating block is written in brackets, 0.1(6) = 0.1666....

### Notes: Formulae and identities (every one checked by computer)

- Two numbers: HCF(a, b) x LCM(a, b) = a x b (checked for all pairs up to 199).
- Three numbers: LCM(a, b, c) = a x b x c x HCF(a, b, c) / [HCF(a, b) x HCF(b, c) x HCF(c, a)] (checked for all triples up to 30; example 12, 18, 30: 6480 x 6 / (6 x 6 x 6) = 180). The simple product rule holds for three numbers only if they are pairwise coprime (checked for all triples up to 30).
- Adding k > 0 to numerator and denominator: (a+k)/(b+k) - a/b = k(b - a)/[b(b + k)] (checked for 0 <= a < 50, 1 <= b < 50, 1 <= k < 30).
- Recurring decimal: value = (N - M) / (b nines then a zeros), a non-repeating digits, b repeating digits.
- Fractions in lowest terms: HCF = HCF(numerators)/LCM(denominators); LCM = LCM(numerators)/HCF(denominators).
- Same remainder r: LCM x m + r. Constant gap g: LCM x m - g. Greatest divisor with remainders r_i: HCF(a_i - r_i), valid only if it exceeds every r_i.
- Multiples of L up to N: floor(N/L). Remaining after n reductions of fraction f: (1 - f)^n.
- Terminating decimal: lowest-terms denominator has only the primes 2 and 5.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- HCF (largest divisor of all) versus LCM (smallest common multiple): equal division and 'greatest' point to HCF; repeated events and 'least number divisible' point to LCM.

- Terminating decimal versus recurring decimal: decided by the lowest-terms denominator.
- 'Every kth day' (period k) versus 'after a gap of k days' (period k + 1).
- Counting meetings including or excluding the start.

### Core concepts

- Fractions compare by cross-multiplication, a common denominator, decimals or benchmarks.
- A recurring decimal equals (N - M) over b nines and a zeros.
- HCF takes minimum prime exponents; LCM takes maximum; so HCF x LCM = product for two numbers only.
- Equal division points to HCF; repeated events and remainders point to LCM.

**Q.** Write 2 3/5 as an improper fraction and 0.35 as a fraction in lowest terms.

**A.** 2 3/5 = (2 x 5 + 3)/5 = 13/5. 0.35 = 35/100 = 7/20.

**Q.** Which is larger, 7/9 or 5/7? Use cross-multiplication.

**A.** 7 x 7 = 49 against 5 x 9 = 45, so 7/9 is larger.

**Q.** Find the HCF and LCM of 12 and 18 and verify the product rule.

**A.** HCF 6, LCM 36; 6 x 36 = 216 = 12 x 18.

**Q.** Convert 0.(45) to a fraction in lowest terms.

**A.** 45/99 = 5/11.

**Q.** Why is 0.(9) exactly 1?

**A.** Let x = 0.999...; then 10x - x = 9.999... - 0.999... = 9, so 9x = 9 and x = 1. They are two ways of writing the same number.

**Q.** Why does HCF x LCM = product fail for three numbers?

**A.** For each prime, HCF takes the smallest exponent and LCM the largest; the product uses all three. The middle exponent is missing, so the rule holds only if it is 0 for every prime (pairwise coprime numbers).

**Q.** Why must the greatest divisor leaving remainders exceed every remainder?

**A.** A remainder on division by d is always less than d, so d must be larger than each remainder; 245 and 1029 leaving 5 and 4 give HCF(240, 1025) = 5, which is not above 5, so no divisor exists.

**Q.** Why do the signals in item 2023-q50 use full cycles of twice the green-to-red time?

**A.** Green and red last equally, so green to red is half of green to green; the signals are green together again only after whole full cycles.

**Q.** Find the least number that leaves remainder 1 on division by 4, 5 and 6 and is divisible by 7.

**A.** LCM(4, 5, 6) = 60, so the numbers are 60m + 1. Since 60 leaves remainder 4 on division by 7, 4m + 1 must be a multiple of 7, giving m = 5: 301 = 7 x 43. Check: 301 leaves 1 on division by 4, 5 and 6.

**Q.** Three ropes of 84 m, 126 m and 210 m are cut into pieces of equal length, as long as possible. How long is each piece and how many pieces are there?
