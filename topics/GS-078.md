## GS-078: Rock-cut architecture and Indian caves

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Rock-cut architecture and Indian caves and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Origins and the Deccan sequence

Artificial caves have a long history: in the third century BCE Asoka had caves cut in the Barabar hills for Ajivika renouncers, and the Lomus Rishi cave's rectangular hall with a circular rear chamber set a template. Western India then produced a dense series of Buddhist caves from the second century BCE at Bhaja, Pitalkhora, Bedsa, Nashik, Karla, Junnar and Kanheri. Junnar alone has over two hundred excavations and Kanheri over a hundred. Because few dated inscriptions survive, Ajanta and other Deccan caves lack a precise chronology. Ajanta's painting survives as the sole example from both the first century BCE and the fifth century CE.

### Ellora, Elephanta and Bagh

Ellora is singled out for stylistic eclecticism: monasteries and shrines of three religions spanning five centuries at one site. Its Buddhist caves contain Vajrayana images such as Tara, Mahamayuri and Avalokiteshwara; the Brahmanical caves depict Ravana shaking Kailash, Andhakasuravadha and Vishnu's avatars; pillar decoration grows increasingly ornate towards the ninth-century Jain caves. The eighth-century Kailashnatha temple was hewn out of one rock, and an inscription records the chief sculptor's astonishment at his own work. Elephanta near Mumbai is Shaivite and contemporary with Ellora. The Bagh caves in Madhya Pradesh survive as five of nine viharas; Cave 4, the Rang Mahal, kept murals, many moved to Gwalior's museum in 1982.

### Eastern and southern rock-cut traditions

In coastal Andhra, Guntapalle combines structural stupas and viharas with caves, including a small circular chaitya with a stupa inside, from the second century BCE. Anakapalli's hillock was carved into a huge stupa surrounded by votive rock-cut stupas. In Odisha the Udaigiri-Khandagiri caves were scattered Jain monastic cells, some boulders shaped like animals, with pillared verandas and chaitya-arch decoration. Further south, rock-cut caves continued at Badami and Aihole under the Chalukyas and at Mahabalipuram under the Pallavas. This spread shows the technique was a shared regional craft serving several faiths rather than a single sect's signature.

### Worked example

Q: 'Indian rock-cut architecture reflects religious pluralism and shifting patronage.' Discuss. Frame: Mauryan Barabar caves for Ajivikas; Buddhist chaitya-vihara sites of western India funded collectively; Jain caves of Udaigiri-Khandagiri with Kharavela inscriptions; Ellora's three-faith complex and monolithic Kailash; Chalukya and Pallava caves showing the turn to royal patronage after the sixth century.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which Ellora cave is the monolithic Kailash temple?
  - **a:** Cave No. 16, Kailash leni.
- - **q:** Why is Bagh Cave 4 called Rang Mahal?
  - **a:** Its walls and ceilings preserved painted murals — the 'Palace of Colours'.
- - **q:** Which dynasties patronised rock-cut caves at Badami/Aihole and Mahabalipuram?
  - **a:** The Chalukyas and the Pallavas respectively.

### Common trap

Labelling Ellora as a purely Buddhist or purely Hindu site. It combines twelve Buddhist caves, Brahmanical Caves 13–28 and ninth-century Jain caves, and guilds from Vidarbha, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu worked there.
