### Bridge: Percentages, ratios and the idea of a season

A **percentage** is a part out of a hundred. If a state grows rice on 25 hectares out of every 100 hectares sown, the share of rice is 25 per cent. NCERT says about one-fourth of India's total cropped area is under rice; one-fourth is 25 per cent. When NCERT writes 'per cent of the reporting area' it means the share is calculated out of the area for which land records exist.

A **ratio** compares two numbers by division. Cropping intensity (module 2) divides the area sown in a year, counting every crop, by the area of land that was actually sown at least once, and then multiplies by 100 to express it as a percentage. If both numbers are equal the answer is 100 per cent; if the first is larger it is above 100.

A **hectare** (ha) is a unit of land area equal to a square 100 m by 100 m (10,000 square metres). **Yield** means the harvest per hectare, for example kilograms per hectare; **production** is the total harvest; **area** is the land used. Yield times area equals production. A crop can have a large production because it has a large area or because it has a high yield; the exam asks which one.

A **season** in agriculture is the part of the year in which a group of crops is sown and harvested. India's crop seasons are tied to the monsoon (the seasonal rain-bearing wind) and winter. A **tropical** crop likes warm weather all year; a **temperate** crop likes cool weather for growth; a **subtropical** crop lies between. These three words appear repeatedly in the crop profiles in later modules.

Finally, 'per cent of total cropped area' for a crop in the extract tells you how widely it is grown (for example wheat about 14 per cent) and does not tell you how much it yields. Keep area, yield and production separate in your mind.

### 1. Orientation: Indian agriculture on one page

*Objective:* Hold the lesson as six questions: what land, which season, how watered, which crop, which zone and what problems.

## The six questions

Agriculture means growing crops (and, more widely, rearing animals) on land for food, fibre and other raw materials. Leong calls it the most widespread form of human activity and a flow resource: soil that is carefully maintained is renewable, unlike a mineral that is mined out. NCERT says land is the most crucial input for people who depend on farming, because agriculture is purely land-based, soil quality directly decides productivity, and land ownership carries social value and acts as security for credit.

Every agriculture question in the exam reduces to six questions. Learn them in this order.

1. **How much land, and in what use?** Net sown area, fallow, wasteland, forest (module 2).
2. **Which season?** Kharif, rabi, zaid, and the exception of the south (module 3).
3. **How watered?** Irrigated, rainfed, dryland, wetland (module 4).
4. **What type of farming?** Subsistence, intensive, plantation, mechanised (module 5).
5. **Which crop, where?** Rice, wheat, pulses, oilseeds, fibre, sugarcane, tea, coffee (modules 6 and 7).
6. **Which zone and what problems?** Factors, agro-climatic planning, Green Revolution, constraints (modules 8 and 9).

## The three words that carry the topic

- **Agro-climatic** means related to both agriculture and climate. An agro-climatic zone is a region whose climate, soil and water make it suited to a particular set of crops and farming methods, so that planning can treat it as a unit.
- **Cropping intensity** is how many times, on average, the same piece of land is cropped in a year (module 2).
- **Green Revolution** is the spurt of foodgrain production from the mid-1960s using high-yielding seeds, fertiliser and irrigation (module 9).

## What you are not asked to memorise

You do not need district-level statistics. Prelims rewards knowing the crop's condition, its season and its two or three leading states. Mains rewards a cause-effect story: why productivity is low, why farmers are indebted, why a zone-wise plan helps. The master notes hold the tables to rebuild.

### 2. Land use, net sown area and cropping intensity

*Objective:* Define the nine land-use categories, compute the composition of cultivable land from NCERT Table 3.1, and calculate cropping intensity.

## Reporting area versus geographical area

Land-use records are kept by the land revenue department. The land-use categories add up to the **reporting area**, which differs a little from the **geographical area** that the Survey of India measures and keeps fixed. NCERT's nine categories are:

1. **Forests.** Area classified as forest (demarcated by the government), which is different from actual forest cover. The category can grow without any rise in real forest cover.
2. **Barren and wastelands.** Barren hilly terrain, desert, ravines that cannot be cultivated with present technology.
3. **Land under non-agricultural uses.** Settlements, roads, canals, industries and shops.
4. **Permanent pastures and grazing lands.** Mostly owned by the village panchayat or government (common property resources); a small share is private.
5. **Miscellaneous tree crops and groves.** Orchards and fruit trees, not included in net sown area, much of it private.
6. **Culturable wasteland.** Land left uncultivated for more than five years; it can be reclaimed.
7. **Current fallow.** Left without cultivation for one agricultural year or less, to rest the land.
8. **Fallow other than current fallow.** Cultivable but left for more than a year and less than five years.
9. **Net area sown.** The physical extent of land on which crops are sown and harvested.

## Total cultivable land: Table 3.1 (NCERT)

NCERT adds net sown area, all fallow lands and culturable wasteland to estimate the total stock of cultivable land (the table is in the master notes). As a percentage of reporting area the total was 59.5 in 1950-51 (8.0 + 6.1 + 3.7 + 41.7) and 57.7 in 2019-20 (3.9 + 3.7 + 4.5 + 45.6). Net area sown rose from 41.7 to 45.6 per cent of reporting area, which as a share of total cultivable land is 70.0 to 79.0 per cent (41.7 divided by 59.5 and 45.6 divided by 57.7). NCERT notes a marginal decline in the stock of cultivable land and limited scope for adding more net sown area, so it recommends land-saving technologies.

## Increases and declines (1950-51 to 2019-20)

Five categories rose (forest, non-agricultural use, permanent pasture, current fallow, net sown area) and four fell (barren and wasteland, culturable wasteland, tree crops and groves, other fallow). Non-agricultural use rose fastest, with the shift to industry and services; the rise in forest is more demarcated area, not more cover; the rise in net sown area came from using culturable wasteland.

## Cropping intensity

Gross cropped area (GCA) counts each crop on each piece of land, so land sown twice counts twice. NCERT writes the formula as cropping intensity = (GCA divided by NSA) times 100, where NSA is net sown area.

Worked example. A district has 100 ha of net sown area and 150 ha of gross cropped area: 150 divided by 100 times 100 = 150 per cent. A value of 100 per cent means every sown hectare is cropped once.

Why it matters: NCERT says that in a land-scarce, labour-abundant country a high cropping intensity makes fuller use of land and reduces rural unemployment, because it raises the demand for labour.

### 3. Cropping seasons: kharif, rabi and zaid

*Objective:* State the three seasons with months, crops and the exception of southern India, from NCERT Table 3.2.

## The three seasons of the north and interior

NCERT: there are three distinct cropping seasons in the northern and interior parts of the country.

- **Kharif.** June to September, largely coinciding with the south-west monsoon. Tropical crops grow: rice, cotton, jute, jowar, bajra, tur. Table 3.2 lists, for northern states, rice, cotton, bajra, maize, jowar, tur; for southern states, rice, maize, ragi, jowar, groundnut.
- **Rabi.** Begins with the onset of winter in October-November and ends in March-April; the table gives October to March. Low temperature favours temperate and subtropical crops such as wheat, gram and mustard. Table 3.2 lists, for northern states, wheat, gram, rapeseed and mustard, barley; for southern states, rice, maize, ragi, groundnut, jowar.
- **Zaid.** April to June, a short-duration summer season that begins after the rabi harvest. Crops are watermelon, cucumber, vegetables, fruits and fodder, grown on irrigated land. Table 3.2 lists vegetables, fruits and fodder in the north and rice, vegetables and fodder in the south.

The full table (months, northern and southern crops) is in the master notes under Cropping seasons (NCERT Table 3.2).

## Why the south is different

NCERT says this three-way distinction does not exist in the southern parts. There, temperatures are high enough to grow tropical crops in any period of the year provided soil moisture is available, so the same crops can be grown **three times** in an agricultural year if there is enough moisture. The deciding factor in the south is moisture, in the north it is temperature and the monsoon.

## Examples from the crop profiles

- Rice is a kharif crop in the Himalayas and north-west under the south-west monsoon; in southern states and West Bengal two or three rice crops a year are possible. West Bengal's three crops are called **aus, aman and boro**.
- Wheat is a rabi crop, a temperate-zone crop grown in winter.
- Jowar is sown in both kharif and rabi in southern states but is a kharif (often fodder) crop in the north.
- Groundnut is largely a kharif crop of drylands but is also grown in rabi in southern India.
- Gram is a rabi, mostly rainfed, subtropical crop; sugarcane is a long-duration tropical crop (about a year in many places; standard, verify).

## Reading the table in an exam

Test a month or season against temperature and water: a cold-loving crop (wheat, mustard) cannot be kharif in the north, and a monsoon crop (rice, cotton, jute) cannot be a dry winter crop without irrigation. Keep the 'except in the south' rule.

### 4. How the crop is watered: irrigated, rainfed, dryland and wetland

*Objective:* Classify farming by moisture source, distinguish protective and productive irrigation, dryland and wetland farming, and recall India's irrigation facts and forms.

## Classification by main source of moisture

NCERT classifies farming, on the basis of the main source of moisture for crops, as **irrigated** and **rainfed (barani)**.

**Irrigated farming** differs by the objective of irrigation:

- **Protective irrigation.** Protects crops from the adverse effects of moisture deficiency; irrigation acts as a supplementary source over and above the rainfall. The strategy is to give soil moisture to the maximum possible area.
- **Productive irrigation.** Provides enough soil moisture in the cropping season to get high productivity. The water input per unit area of cultivated land is higher than in protective irrigation.

**Rainfed farming** is classified by the adequacy of soil moisture during the season:

- **Dryland farming.** In India largely confined to regions with annual rainfall below 75 cm. These regions grow hardy, drought-resistant crops such as ragi, bajra, moong, gram and guar (a fodder crop) and use measures of soil-moisture conservation and rain-water harvesting.
- **Wetland farming.** Rainfall exceeds the soil moisture requirement during the rainy season; flood and soil erosion hazards are present. Water-intensive crops such as rice, jute and sugarcane are grown, and aquaculture is practised in freshwater bodies.

Exam shortcut: sugarcane is a wetland (water-intensive) crop and so is not a dryland crop; ragi, jowar, bajra and groundnut belong with dryland farming. (A NCERT exercise asks exactly which crop is not cultivated under dryland farming.)

## Facts on irrigation in India

NCERT says irrigation covers only about 33 per cent of the cultivated area, so most crop production depends directly on rain; the supply of canal water also depends on the south-west monsoon. Expansion of irrigation allowed modern technology such as high-yielding varieties, fertilisers, pesticides and machinery.

## Forms of irrigation (Leong, world and Indian examples)

- **Canal.** A dam holds a reservoir that feeds canals; perennial canals irrigate all year, while inundation canals of the Ganges and Indus basins fill only with flood water and dry up in winter, so they have been gradually replaced.
- **Tank.** Mud bunds store excess rainy-season water, used mainly at the end of the rains in India and Sri Lanka; some Deccan tanks are centuries old and silted.
- **Well and tube well.** Wells reach the water table; modern tube wells with electric pumps raise water from 92 m or more.

Leong adds that modern works are usually multipurpose projects (irrigation, power, navigation and flood control).

## Why irrigation matters for the next modules

Irrigation is the main reason for state-wise yield differences: Punjab and Haryana rice came with irrigation after the Green Revolution, while rainfed wheat or gram gives low yields.

### 5. Types of agriculture and farming systems

*Objective:* Classify agriculture into the main types (subsistence, intensive, plantation, mechanised, mixed) and describe farming systems such as shifting cultivation, crop rotation and terracing, flagging Leong's world frame.

## Leong's frame, applied with care

Leong divides world agriculture into tropical and temperate types. It is a world text, so use it for the idea and NCERT for Indian facts.

## Tropical types

1. **Extensive subsistence agriculture (shifting cultivation).** Small forest patches are cleared and tilled with crude tools for the family's own need; leaching under heavy rain and heat exhausts the soil, so a new patch is cleared after a few years. The **bush fallow** (land-rotation) system is an improved form.
2. **Intensive subsistence agriculture.** High-intensity peasant farming, normally with wet padi (rice) as the chief crop, in densely populated areas: irrigated fields, terraced hills, little or no surplus for export. Leong names China, India and Japan; NCERT's small-farm rice-and-wheat foodgrain agriculture is the Indian counterpart.
3. **Plantation agriculture.** Large estates of perennial crops such as cocoa and rubber and shrubs such as coffee, commercialised and begun by European colonists; it needs large capital, labour and a waiting period of years before yield. NCERT calls tea and coffee plantation crops; Assam tea began in the 1840s.

## Temperate types

4. **Extensive mechanised cereal cultivation.** Very large farms (Leong gives 240 to 16,200 ha) where all stages are done by machine, in Canada, the USA, Argentina and Australia; yield per hectare is lower than on intensive farms but total output is large.
5. **Intensive and mixed farming.** Crops combined with animal husbandry, rotation and farmyard manure in densely populated countries; it includes orchard farming and market gardening.

## Indian classification

NCERT classifies by moisture (module 4) and by purpose: **subsistence** (family consumption) and **commercial** (for sale). Foodgrains dominate in all parts of the country, and modernisation and commercialisation have taken place mainly in the irrigated areas.

## Soil-protecting practices (Leong)

Crop rotation, contour ploughing, terracing, strip cropping, cover cropping, fallowing, controlled grazing and afforestation protect the soil (the master notes hold the table). The bad practices that cause erosion are overcropping and monoculture, overgrazing, slope-wise ploughing (gullies), deforestation (sheet erosion) and shifting cultivation with a short fallow.

## The Indian exam angle

Prelims asks, for example, which crops belong to plantation agriculture (tea, coffee, rubber). Mains uses the types to show why Indian agriculture is mixed: subsistence in dry rainfed areas, commercial in irrigated tracts and plantation in the hills of Assam, West Bengal and the Western Ghats region.

### 6. Foodgrains: cereals and pulses

*Objective:* State, for rice, wheat, jowar, bajra, maize, gram and tur, the season, conditions, area share and leading states from NCERT.

## Foodgrains in the economy

Foodgrains occupy about **two-thirds** of the total cropped area. They are cereals and pulses. Cereals occupy about 54 per cent of total cropped area; India produces about 11 per cent of the world's cereals and ranks third after China and the USA (NCERT). Cereals are fine grains (rice, wheat) and coarse grains (jowar, bajra, maize, ragi).

## Rice

- A staple food and a tropical humid-area crop with many varieties grown from sea level to about 2,000 m. Two or three crops a year in southern states and West Bengal (aus, aman, boro); a kharif crop in the Himalayas and north-west.
- India contributes 22.07 per cent of world production and ranked second after China in 2018. About one-fourth of the total cropped area is under rice.
- Leading states: West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab. High yields in Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Haryana, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, West Bengal and Kerala; in the first four almost all rice land is irrigated. Rice in Punjab and Haryana was introduced in the 1970s after the Green Revolution. Yield is very low in rainfed Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Odisha.
- Leong's rice rainfall differs inside its own pages (1,270 mm in the table, 1,520 mm in the text), so quote neither as an Indian figure.

## Wheat

- Second cereal after rice; India produces about 12.8 per cent of world wheat (2017). A temperate-zone crop grown in the winter rabi season; about 14 per cent of the total cropped area; about 85 per cent of the area is in the north and central regions (Indo-Gangetic Plain, Malwa Plateau, Himalayas up to 2,700 m).
- Leading states: Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan. Yield above 4,000 kg per hectare in Punjab and Haryana; low where rainfed (Madhya Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir).

## Coarse cereals (together about 16.50 per cent of area)

Jowar (about 5.3 per cent of area; Maharashtra more than half), bajra (about 5.2 per cent; the hot dry north-west and west) and maize (about 3.6 per cent; Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar) are the main coarse cereals; the master notes table holds their conditions and leading states.

## Pulses

- Rich in protein; they are **legume crops that raise soil fertility by nitrogen fixation**. India is a leading producer. Pulses occupy about 11 per cent of cropped area; they are mostly rainfed crops of the dry Deccan and central plateaus and the north-west, so yields are low and fluctuate.
- **Gram** is a subtropical rabi crop needing one or two light showers or irrigations; about 2.8 per cent of area; the main states are Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Rajasthan. After the Green Revolution wheat displaced gram in Haryana, Punjab and northern Rajasthan.
- **Tur (arhar, red gram, pigeon pea)** is the second important pulse, grown on marginal lands in dry central and southern states; about 2 per cent of area; Maharashtra gives about one-third of production.

## Why pulses matter (2017 Mains link)

The extract gives the points for the advantage-of-pulses question: protein for vegetarian diets, fixation of nitrogen, suitability to dry rainfed land and to marginal land, and a role in rotation. Standard additional points (verify): a low water requirement and an income for dryland farmers. The UN declared 2016 the International Year of Pulses (as the question states).

### 7. Oilseeds, fibres, sugarcane, tea and coffee

*Objective:* State conditions, season, share and leading states for groundnut, rapeseed-mustard, soyabean, cotton, jute, sugarcane, tea and coffee.

## Oilseeds (together about 14 per cent of cropped area)

Grown for edible oil in drylands: Malwa plateau, Marathwada, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Telangana, Rayalseema region of Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka plateau.

- **Groundnut.** About 18.8 per cent of world production (2018). Largely a rainfed kharif crop of drylands; also rabi in southern India; about 3.6 per cent of area. Leading: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra. Yield comparatively high in Tamil Nadu where it is partly irrigated.
- **Rapeseed and mustard** (rai, sarson, toria, taramira). Subtropical, rabi, frost sensitive; north-west and central India; about two-thirds of area irrigated; about 2.5 per cent of area. Rajasthan gives about one-third; Haryana and Madhya Pradesh follow.
- **Soyabean and sunflower.** Soyabean mainly in Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra (about 90 per cent of output together); sunflower in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and adjoining Maharashtra.

## Fibre crops

- **Cotton.** Tropical, kharif, semi-arid areas; needs clear sky at flowering. India grows both short staple (Indian) and long staple (American, 'narma') cotton; about 4.7 per cent of area; second in the world after China. Three growing areas: Punjab, Haryana and northern Rajasthan in the north-west; Gujarat and Maharashtra in the west; plateaus of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu in the south. Yield is high under irrigation in the north-west and low under rainfed conditions in Maharashtra. Leong's world requirements: about 200 frost-free days, frost-sensitive, about 1,000 mm rain (irrigation below 500 mm).
- **Jute.** A cash crop of West Bengal and the eastern parts; India produces about three-fifths of world jute; West Bengal about three-fourths of the national output; Bihar and Assam follow; about 0.5 per cent of area. Leong: wet alluvial soil, hot moist climate.

## Sugarcane

Tropical, but largely irrigated in India. About 19.7 per cent of world production, second after Brazil in 2018; only 2.4 per cent of area. Uttar Pradesh produces about two-fifths; Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh are other leading states with high yield; yield is low in northern India.

## Tea

A plantation beverage crop of hilly, well-drained land in humid and sub-humid tropics and subtropics. Plantation began in the 1840s in the Brahmaputra valley of Assam, later in the sub-Himalayan region of West Bengal (Darjeeling, Jalpaiguri and Cooch Behar districts) and on the lower slopes of the Nilgiri and Cardamom hills. NCERT's paragraph reads, in order: India's share of world production is about 21.22 per cent (2018); its share in the international market has declined substantially; it 'ranks second among tea exporting countries in the world after China (2018)'; Assam accounts for about 53.2 per cent of the total cropped area (read as the national tea area) and contributes more than half of production; West Bengal and Tamil Nadu are the other leading producers. A column break around Fig. 3.10 splits these lines in the extraction; that is an extraction artefact, not an NCERT slip. Assam's 53.2 per cent is taught as printed. Only the export-rank sentence is flagged: standard trade data put Kenya at or near the top of tea exporters (verify). Leong: high temperature, heavy rainfall without long drought, no killing frost, acidic soil and cheap abundant labour.

## Coffee

A tropical plantation crop; three varieties (arabica, robusta, liberica). India mostly grows arabica, in great demand; about 3.17 per cent of world production (2018, rank eighth). Grown in the highlands of the Western Ghats in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu; Karnataka gives more than two-thirds of the production.

### 8. Factors of agriculture and the logic of agro-climatic zones

*Objective:* Explain how climate, relief, soil, biotic and economic factors decide crops, and how they define agro-climatic zones: the Planning Commission step of 1988, the 15 regions, the 127 NARP zones and the 20 agro-ecological regions (verify).

## The factors that decide what is grown

Leong lists six groups.

1. **Climate** (the greatest factor): rainfall (amount, distribution, timing) and temperature (growth-season temperature, frost-free period). Rice needs heavy, well-distributed rain; cotton needs a moderately high temperature, sunshine and about 200 frost-free days. Irrigation, greenhouses and plant breeding extend a crop's range, but at higher cost.
2. **Relief.** Lowlands suit a wide range of crops; height removes most. Tea and coffee thrive on well-drained hill slopes, and terracing makes lowland-like fields on slopes.
3. **Soil, the edaphic factor** ('edaphic' means relating to the soil). Sandy soils drain quickly and suit groundnut; clayey soils retain moisture and suit rice; loam is intermediate; humus raises fertility.
4. **Biotic factors:** weeds, parasites, diseases and insect pests.
5. **Economic factors:** cost of land, seed, fertiliser, machinery, marketing and storage.
6. **Other factors:** ownership, social influences, labour, transport and demand. For current Indian detail use NCERT, not Leong's older world account.

## From factors to zones

An **agro-climatic zone** is a region where rainfall, temperature and soils are broadly similar, so the same crops and package of practices suit all of it. If climate, relief and soil decide what can be grown, a country can be cut into zones where these are alike and planned zone by zone rather than state by state: a state boundary is a political line, a zone boundary follows climate and soil.

NCERT gives the Indian steps: rice grows in **different agro-climatic regions** from sea level to about 2,000 m (one crop across several zones, with different varieties); the Planning Commission turned to **rainfed areas in the 1980s** and **initiated agro-climatic planning in 1988** for regionally balanced development, stressing diversification into dairy, poultry, horticulture, livestock rearing and aquaculture.

## How many zones, and which? (outside the extract; verify)

The extract gives no count or list. Three standard layers are commonly reported; verify each against a current source.

- **15 Planning Commission agro-climatic regions**: 1 Western Himalayan, 2 Eastern Himalayan, 3 Lower Gangetic Plain, 4 Middle Gangetic Plain, 5 Upper Gangetic Plain, 6 Trans-Gangetic Plain, 7 Eastern Plateau and Hills, 8 Central Plateau and Hills, 9 Western Plateau and Hills, 10 Southern Plateau and Hills, 11 East Coast Plains and Hills, 12 West Coast Plains and Ghats, 13 Gujarat Plains and Hills, 14 Western Dry Region, 15 Islands. Example: the Trans-Gangetic Plain (Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and part of Rajasthan) is the irrigated wheat and rice belt.
- **127 ICAR agro-climatic zones (NARP)**: finer zones inside states. Example: the rain-shadow 'scarcity' zone of Maharashtra, with jowar, bajra and pulses.
- **20 NBSS&LUP agro-ecological regions** (about 60 sub-regions; soil, climate and growing period). Example: the hot semi-arid region with deep black soils of the Deccan plateau suits cotton, sorghum, soyabean and pulses.

## Using zones in an answer

### 9. Agricultural development, the Green Revolution and the problems

*Objective:* Narrate India's agricultural development from independence to liberalisation, explain the Green Revolution and its regional bias, and list the problems of Indian agriculture with the links between them.

## From independence to the Green Revolution

Indian agriculture was largely subsistence before independence and performed poorly in the first half of the twentieth century, with severe droughts and famines. At partition about one-third of the irrigated land of undivided India went to Pakistan, reducing the irrigated share. NCERT then gives the government's strategy:

1. After independence the immediate goal was more foodgrains by switching from cash crops to food crops, intensifying cropping on cultivated land and bringing cultivable and fallow land under the plough.
2. Production stagnated in the late 1950s, and the **Intensive Agricultural District Programme (IADP)** and **Intensive Agricultural Area Programme (IAAP)** were launched.
3. Two consecutive droughts in the mid-1960s caused a food crisis, and foodgrains were imported.
4. By the mid-1960s new high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of wheat (from Mexico) and rice (from the Philippines) were available. India adopted a **package technology**: HYVs with chemical fertilisers, in irrigated areas of Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat. Assured irrigation was a pre-requisite.
5. The spurt of production is the **Green Revolution**. It made India self-reliant in foodgrains and boosted agro-industries, but it was initially confined to irrigated areas and so created regional disparities until the 1970s, after which the technology spread to the east and central parts.
6. In the 1980s the Planning Commission turned to rainfed areas and launched agro-climatic planning in 1988; in the 1990s liberalisation influenced the course of agriculture.

NCERT adds: production and yield of rice and wheat rose impressively; chemical fertiliser consumption increased 15 times since the mid-1960s; pesticide use grew because HYVs are susceptible to pests. The NMSA promotes sustainable, climate-resilient, location-specific farming, and organic farming through schemes such as Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY); NCERT prints the second scheme as Rashtriya Vikas Yojana, whose usual name is Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (verify).

## The problems of Indian agriculture (NCERT)

NCERT says most problems are region-specific but some are common:

The eight problems are an erratic monsoon (irrigation covers only about 33 per cent of cultivated area), low productivity (yields below the USA, Russia and Japan for rice, wheat, cotton and oilseeds), finance and indebtedness, lack of land reforms (Zamindari the most exploitative), small and fragmented holdings, lack of commercialisation, underemployment (4 to 8 months in unirrigated tracts) and land degradation (salinisation, alkalisation, waterlogging, pesticide build-up, erosion). The master notes hold the table with the core point of each.

Leong adds the general mechanisms behind soil damage: overcropping, overgrazing, deforestation and shifting cultivation (module 5).

## Linking the problems

Draw the chain: erratic rain leads to crop failure, which leads to debt, which blocks purchase of inputs and keeps yields low, which keeps income low and holdings small. Irrigation breaks the chain at the first link but brings salinisation if used without drainage. This chain is the Mains skeleton.

## What is tested

The seven questions test five skills: identifying a crop from its profile, knowing producing states, comparing India and China, knowing crop origins and writing a cause-effect answer. All analyses below are reasoning exercises, not an official answer key.

**2020 Q87 (crop from the profile).** The profile: subtropical, hard frost injurious, at least 210 frost-free days, 50 to 100 cm rainfall, light well-drained moisture-retaining soil. Test the four crops: sugarcane is tropical and largely irrigated; jute is a wet, hot, humid delta crop; tea needs heavy rain and is a hill plantation crop; cotton is frost-sensitive and needs a long frost-free period and moderate rainfall (Leong: about 200 frost-free days, about 1,000 mm; NCERT: tropical kharif, semi-arid). The profile matches cotton. The label variants (200 versus 210 days; NCERT's 'tropical' against 'subtropical') are not contradictions; the decisive features are frost sensitivity and the long frost-free period.

**2022 Q62 (tea-producing states).** From the extract, tea comes from Assam, West Bengal (Darjeeling, Jalpaiguri, Cooch Behar) and the lower slopes of the Nilgiri and Cardamom hills (Tamil Nadu and Kerala). **2023 Q4 (India versus China).** NCERT says Indian yields of most crops are far below international levels (USA, Russia, Japan). Standard comparison, flagged verify: China's cereal yields per hectare are well above India's, and India's arable area is larger than China's. A statement on irrigated share needs FAO or Economic Survey data; look it up. Production rank (second in rice and cotton) measures total output, not yield per hectare, and is not evidence on productivity.

**2019 Q21 (crop origins).** Tobacco, cocoa and rubber are purely New World plants (the Americas). Cotton is different: it was domesticated independently in both worlds, and it was already grown in the Old World (the Indus valley), so it was not 'introduced' there from the New World. Leong gives rubber's home as Brazil, maize as American, coffee as Ethiopian and tea as Chinese; its cacao-in-Africa line is a slip against the standard American origin. Verify.

**2025 Q24 (turmeric) and Q39 (plant forms).** Neither is in the extracts. Turmeric (Spices Board or Economic Survey; verify): India is the largest producer, consumer and exporter of turmeric, with 30-plus varieties and leading states commonly given as Telangana, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. **2017 Mains Q7 (pulses).** Module 6 gives the extract-based points: protein, nitrogen fixation, dryland suitability, marginal land and a role in rotation; add verified extras.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-NCERT-CROPPING-INTENSITY (verified):** NCERT: cropping intensity in per cent = (gross cropped area / net sown area) x 100; for a land-scarce but labour-abundant country like India a high cropping intensity is desirable for fuller use of land and for reducing rural unemployment.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-KHARIF (verified):** NCERT: the kharif season (June-September) largely coincides with the south-west monsoon and allows tropical crops such as rice, cotton, jute, jowar, bajra and tur.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-RABI (verified):** NCERT: the rabi season begins with the onset of winter in October-November and ends in March-April; low temperature suits temperate and subtropical crops such as wheat, gram and mustard (Table 3.2 gives October-March; wheat, gram, rapeseed and mustard, barley in the north).
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-ZAID (verified):** NCERT: zaid (April-June in Table 3.2) is a short-duration summer cropping season beginning after the rabi harvest, when watermelons, cucumbers, vegetables and fodder crops are grown on irrigated land.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-SEASONS-SOUTH (verified):** NCERT: the kharif-rabi-zaid distinction does not exist in southern India, where temperature is high enough to grow tropical crops in any period of the year provided soil moisture is available, so the same crops can be grown thrice in an agricultural year.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-RICE (verified):** NCERT: India contributes 22.07 per cent of world rice production (second after China, 2018); about one-fourth of the cropped area is under rice; West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and Punjab lead; West Bengal grows three crops (aus, aman, boro); rice is kharif in the Himalayas and north-west; yield is very low in rainfed Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Odisha.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WHEAT (verified):** NCERT: wheat is the second most important cereal; India produces about 12.8 per cent of world wheat (2017); it is a rabi, temperate-zone crop on about 14 per cent of cropped area, with about 85 per cent of the area in the north and centre; Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan lead, and yield is above 4,000 kg per hectare in Punjab and Haryana.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-DRYLAND (verified):** NCERT: dryland farming in India is largely confined to regions with annual rainfall below 75 cm and grows hardy, drought-resistant crops such as ragi, bajra, moong, gram and guar, with soil-moisture conservation and rain-water harvesting.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-IRRIGATION-33 (verified):** NCERT: irrigation covers only about 33 per cent of the cultivated area in India; crop production on the rest depends directly on rain, and a poor south-west monsoon also reduces the supply of canal water.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-LOW-PRODUCTIVITY (verified):** NCERT: per hectare output of most crops such as rice, wheat, cotton and oilseeds in India is much lower than in the U.S.A., Russia and Japan, and the vast rainfed areas growing coarse cereals, pulses and oilseeds have low yields.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-INDEBTEDNESS (verified):** NCERT: modern farm inputs are expensive and unmanageable for marginal and small farmers with meagre savings, who borrow from institutions and moneylenders; crop failure and low returns trap them in indebtedness.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-LAND-REFORMS (verified):** NCERT: of the three British revenue systems (Mahalwari, Ryotwari, Zamindari) the Zamindari was the most exploitative; land reforms were not implemented effectively after Independence, and small, fragmented holdings are uneconomic.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-UNDEREMPLOYMENT (verified):** NCERT: there is massive underemployment in Indian agriculture, particularly in unirrigated tracts, where seasonal unemployment ranges from 4 to 8 months.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-LAND-DEGRADATION (verified):** NCERT: faulty irrigation and agricultural strategy has degraded cultivable land, especially in irrigated areas: alkalisation, salinisation and waterlogging, toxic build-up of insecticides and pesticides, and displacement of leguminous crops, which obliterates natural nitrogen fixation.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GR-CONTEXT (verified):** NCERT: agricultural production stagnated in the late 1950s, so the Intensive Agricultural District Programme (IADP) and Intensive Agricultural Area Programme (IAAP) were launched; two consecutive droughts in the mid-1960s caused a food crisis and foodgrains were imported.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GR-HYV (verified):** NCERT: high yielding varieties of wheat (Mexico) and rice (Philippines) were available by the mid-1960s; India introduced a package technology of HYVs with chemical fertilisers in irrigated areas of Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat, with assured irrigation a basic pre-requisite.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GR-GAINS (verified):** NCERT: the spurt of agricultural growth from the package technology is known as the Green Revolution; it gave a fillip to agro-inputs, agro-processing and small-scale industries and made the country self-reliant in foodgrain production.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GR-REGIONAL (verified):** NCERT: the Green Revolution was initially confined to irrigated areas, which led to regional disparities in agricultural development till the 1970s, after which the technology spread to the Eastern and Central parts of the country.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-AGROCLIMATIC-1988 (verified):** NCERT: the Planning Commission focused on the problems of rainfed-area agriculture in the 1980s and initiated agro-climatic planning in 1988 to induce regionally balanced agricultural development, stressing diversification into dairy farming, poultry, horticulture, livestock rearing and aquaculture.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TEA-COFFEE-GHATS (verified):** NCERT: tea is also cultivated on the lower slopes of the Nilgiri and Cardamom hills in the Western Ghats, and coffee is cultivated in the highlands of the Western Ghats in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 3 Land Resources and Agriculture (legy203, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy203.pdf)
