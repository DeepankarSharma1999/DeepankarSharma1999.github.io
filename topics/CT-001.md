### Bridge: Reading vocabulary: the seven words this lesson uses constantly

These seven words appear in question stems, in this lesson and in every explanation you will read about comprehension. Learn them now so that nothing later feels like jargon.

- **Claim**: a statement the author puts forward as true and wants you to accept. 'Decentralisation only works where participation is genuine' is a claim. A passage usually has one organising claim and several smaller ones.
- **Premise**: a reason or piece of evidence offered in support of a claim. 'Complaints fell by half in wards with active committees' is a premise supporting the claim above.
- **Inference**: something you work out from the passage that the passage does not state in so many words, but which must be true if the passage is true. If a text says 'in some years the subsidy exceeded the 10 per cent limit India accepted', you can infer that in those years the subsidy was above the agreed limit, even though the word 'breach' never appears. A further step such as 'India will be penalised' is not an inference; it is a guess. An inference stays close to the text, and denying a valid inference contradicts the passage.
- **Assumption**: an unstated belief the author must hold for the argument to work. If the author argues 'warnings failed, so taxes are needed', the author assumes that the goal is to reduce consumption, not merely to inform. Deny the assumption (the goal is only to inform) and the argument collapses. Assumptions sit *underneath* the argument; inferences sit *after* it.
- **Corollary**: a consequence that follows almost automatically from what has been established. In CSAT stems, 'the most logical corollary' means: if the passage is true, what else must be true? It is a tight inference, not a loose implication.
- **Qualify**: to limit a statement so that it is not absolute. 'Information matters, *but rarely changes behaviour on its own*' is a qualified claim. Authors who qualify their claims are not contradicting themselves; they are being precise. Options that drop the qualification are 'too strong'.
- **Concede**: to admit that an opposing point has some truth before continuing with one's own argument. 'Both habits matter, but they account for only part of the problem' concedes a point. A conceded point is never the main idea; it is the thing the author moves past.

A quick habit: whenever you read 'but', 'yet', 'however', 'although' or 'rather', mark it. The author is usually conceding or qualifying on one side of that word and stating the real claim on the other side.

### Bridge: What 'qualifying paper' and 'negative marking' mean for your strategy

UPSC Preliminary Examination has two papers on the same day. Paper I (General Studies) decides your rank for the Mains cut-off. Paper II, called CSAT (Civil Services Aptitude Test), is **qualifying**: its marks are not added to your total, but you must score at least 33 per cent in it, or your Paper I score is not even considered. Failing CSAT by one mark ends the attempt exactly as surely as failing Paper I by fifty.

The arithmetic: 80 questions, 2.5 marks each, 200 marks total. Thirty-three per cent of 200 is 66 marks. Net scores move in steps of 5/6 of a mark (2.5 for a right answer, 0.833 lost for a wrong one), so no score lies between 66 and 66.67 and the lowest passing score is 66.67 (for example 27 right and 1 wrong: 67.5 - 0.83 = 66.67). About 27 fully correct answers with nothing wrong already give 67.5. Every wrong answer costs one-third of the question's marks, that is 0.833 marks. So three wrong answers wipe out one right one. An unattempted question costs nothing. (Registry provisions Plan-4, Plan-8 and Plan-12 give the one-third penalty, the 33 per cent bar and the two-hour paper; the 80-question count and the 2.5 marks per question are not in the registry and still need an official source.)

What this means in practice:

- You do not need to attempt all 80 questions. You need a comfortable margin above 66. A commonly given piece of advice is to aim at 90-100 marks so that a bad day still clears the bar; treat this as advice, not as a rule or a statistic.
- Comprehension questions are the safest marks in the paper for a careful reader, because the answer is inside the text; nothing has to be remembered. That is why this topic is Day 1.
- Guessing and expected value. A right answer earns 2.5 and a wrong one costs 0.833, so the expected value of a guess is 2.5p - 0.833(1 - p), where p is your chance of being right. It is positive exactly when p is above 25 per cent. A blind guess among four options has an expected value of exactly 0 (0.25 x 2.5 - 0.75 x 0.833 = 0): it gains nothing on average and only adds risk. Among three options it is about +0.28 marks, and among two options about +0.83. So elimination is what makes a guess worthwhile. Two cautions: the figures hold only if your eliminations are sound (candidates often overrate them), and a positive average still carries variance, which matters when your margin over 66 is thin. The method in this lesson is designed to get you to two options honestly, and usually to one.
- Time is two hours for 80 questions: 90 seconds per question on average. A passage question that you can answer in three minutes is worth attempting; one that would take eight minutes should be skipped and returned to only if time remains.

Hold these numbers in mind whenever the lesson says 'skip' or 'safe'.

### 1. What a CSAT comprehension question actually looks like

*Objective:* Recognise the physical shape of a CSAT comprehension item and the recurring stem phrases so that nothing on the exam page is unfamiliar.

## The shape of the item

A CSAT comprehension item has three parts printed one after another: a **passage**, a **stem** (the question sentence) and **four options** labelled (a) to (d). The passage is usually 80 to 200 words, sometimes up to 350. Since 2020 most passages carry exactly one question; the 2017-2019 papers sometimes attached two or three questions to a longer passage. The instructions printed in the paper tell you to answer on the basis of the passage *only*, even when you know better. That single instruction is the whole game.

## The stems, year by year

Across the 71 verified questions mapped to this topic (2017-2026), the stems cluster into a small family of phrases. Learn them so that you read the stem in two seconds, not ten:

- 'The main idea of the passage is that ...' (2017, 2022)
- 'Which among the following is the most crucial message conveyed by the above passage?' (2017, 2018)
- 'Which of the following is the most logical, rational and crucial message given by the passage?' (2018, 2019)
- 'Which one of the following statements best implies / best reflects the **crux** of the passage?' (2020 onwards, every year)
- 'Which one of the following statements best reflects the **critical message** of the passage?' (2020, 2021, 2025)
- 'Which one of the following statements best reflects the **central idea** of the passage?' (2021, 2024, 2025)
- 'most logical, rational and **practical / pragmatic** message conveyed by the author' (2023, 2025)
- 'The passage seems to argue ...', 'The author holds the view that ...', 'the point of view of the given passage', 'the intent of the writer' (2019, 2018, 2026)

The words *crux*, *central idea*, *critical message*, *crucial message* and *main idea* are used interchangeably by the examiner. Do not look for subtle differences between them. The word **practical / pragmatic** is a small signal: it means the examiner expects the answer to be a workable position, not an extreme one. The phrase 'logical and rational' is also a signal: it tells you that one or more options will be emotionally appealing but not logically supported.

## Two formats that look different

Most items offer four statements and ask for one. Two variants appear occasionally. First, a 'which of the following are consequences / which reflect the intent' item lists numbered statements and offers combination codes such as '(a) 1 and 2'; the 2018 desertification passage and the 2026 HMPV passage used this format. Second, a very short passage of one or two sentences (the 2017 'pearls and thread' item) asks which view *corroborates* the statement. These test the same skill with a different surface.

## Why this is Day 1 material

Nothing in a comprehension item depends on memory. The passage is in front of you. What the examiner tests is whether you can hold the author's argument in your head without adding to it or shrinking it. That is a skill, and skills improve with deliberate, slow practice before they improve with speed.

### 2. Topic, main idea, theme, message, assumption, inference, corollary

*Objective:* Separate the seven things a stem can ask for, so that you never answer 'what is the passage about' when the question is 'what does the passage claim'.

## The ladder from topic to corollary

Think of a passage as a person speaking. You can describe the conversation at different depths.

| Term | Question it answers | Typical form | Example (Arctic passage, 2017) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic | What is the passage about? | A noun phrase | Arctic sea ice and polar bears |
| Main idea / central idea / crux | What is the author claiming about the topic? | One full sentence | Lengthening summer melt threatens the Arctic food web and so the bears |
| Theme | What larger human concern does the claim belong to? | An abstract noun | Climate change endangering species |
| Message / critical message | What does the author want the reader to take away or do? | A sentence, often with 'should' or a warning | The threat to polar bears comes from climate change |
| Assumption | What must the author believe for the argument to hold, though it is not written? | A sentence | Polar bears depend on the ice-based food web |
| Inference | What follows from the passage that is not written? | A sentence close to the text | If melt lengthens further, bear numbers will fall |
| Logical corollary | What must be true if the passage is true? | A tight consequence | Protecting bears requires addressing the melt |

The **topic** is never the answer to a main-idea question, but it is the commonest wrong option: a phrase that correctly names the subject without saying anything about it. The **theme** is usually too broad to be the answer; 'climate change is dangerous' is a theme, and an option at that level of generality is almost always a distractor. The examiner's 'crux', 'central idea' and 'critical message' all sit at the main-idea level: one sentence that explains why every other sentence in the passage was written.

## Main idea versus message

These overlap but are not identical. The main idea is descriptive: what the passage *says*. The message is directed: what the passage *wants*. In many CSAT passages the author argues towards a position, so the main idea and the message coincide. When a passage is purely descriptive (a study found X, which affects Y), the 'message' stem still expects the central finding, not an invented recommendation. If the passage never says 'should', be suspicious of options that say 'should'.

## Assumption versus inference

An **assumption** comes *before* the argument; the author stands on it. An **inference** comes *after*; the reader walks forward from the text. Both are unstated, which is why learners confuse them. Test: an assumption is a necessary unstated premise, so if you deny it the author's argument collapses. An inference is what must be true if the passage is true, so if you deny it you contradict the passage itself. CSAT stems rarely use the bare word 'assumption' in this topic, but 'the passage is based on the idea that' (2022) asks for exactly that.

## Corollary

'Most logical corollary' is the examiner's phrase for a conclusion that follows *necessarily*. Treat it as the strictest form of inference. An option that is merely consistent with the passage is not a corollary; it must be forced by the passage.

## One-line test for each term

- Topic: can I say it in three words with no verb? Then it is a topic, not an idea.
- Main idea: does it explain why the first sentence, the examples and the last sentence are all there?
- Theme: is it general enough to fit ten other passages? Then it is a theme.
- Assumption: if I deny it, does the author's argument fall?
- Inference: does it stay within one step of the text, so that denying it would contradict the passage?

### 3. Reading once for structure: claim, support, contrast, qualification, conclusion

*Objective:* Read a passage a single time while tagging each sentence by its job, then write the organising claim in one sentence before looking at any option.

## Why structure, not words

Beginners read a passage for its words and then look for an option containing the same words. The examiner knows this and writes distractors that reuse the passage's vocabulary. Experts read for the passage's **structure**: what job each sentence is doing. Once you know the jobs, the main idea is the sentence that the other sentences serve.

## The five jobs

Every sentence in a short argumentative passage does one of five jobs:

1. **Claim**: states the author's position. Often (not always) the first or last sentence.
2. **Support**: gives a reason, example, statistic or quotation for the claim. Support is never the main idea, however vivid. '90 per cent cheaper during the 2010s' is support.
3. **Contrast**: introduces an opposing view or the common belief the author will reject. Signalled by 'but', 'yet', 'however', 'although', 'in this view', 'is often described as'. Whatever comes *before* the contrast word is usually the view being pushed aside.
4. **Qualification**: limits the claim. 'Not that information is worthless' is a qualification. Options that ignore qualifications are too strong.
5. **Conclusion / implication**: tells you what follows. Signalled by 'therefore', 'as a result', 'this is why', 'the lesson is', 'it seems'.

## The one-pass routine

Read at normal speed, and as you finish each sentence mentally tag it with one letter: C (claim), S (support), X (contrast), Q (qualification), N (conclusion). Do not re-read yet. When you reach the end, ask: which C or N do the S sentences point to? That is the organising claim. Write it in one sentence in your own words, in the margin. Only then read the options.

This costs about fifteen seconds and saves a minute of option-wrestling, because you now compare each option against *your* sentence rather than against a vague memory of the passage.

## A slow illustration

Take the 2018 WTO passage. Sentence 1: India is bound by WTO agreements (S, background). Sentence 2: under the Agriculture Agreement minimum support prices are treated as distorting and limited (S). Sentence 3: the limit is 10 per cent of production value for developing countries (S, detail). Sentence 4: India's PDS involves minimum support prices and stockholding (S). Sentence 5: in some years the subsidy may exceed 10 per cent (N, implication). No sentence contains the word 'conflict', yet every sentence builds towards one: India's food-security machinery can breach a trade commitment India has accepted. That is the organising claim, and it was never stated outright. Reading for structure found it; reading for words would not have.

## When the passage is descriptive

Some passages (the 2021 Cauvery study, the 2020 agriculture-and-poverty passage) describe findings rather than argue. The routine still works: the N sentence is the one that gathers the findings ('this has driven farmers to depend more on groundwater'; 'all this led to an unprecedented fall in poverty'). The main idea is then the cause-to-effect chain the passage traces, not any single link in it.

## Where the claim hides

- Often the **last sentence** after a 'therefore' or 'the lesson is'.
- Often the **first sentence after the contrast word** ('The metaphor is useful but incomplete' leads into the real claim).
- Rarely the opening sentence when the opening sentence presents the common view the author will reject. Beginners pick the opening sentence reflexively; check whether a contrast word follows it.

### 4. The seven classic wrong-option types

*Objective:* Name the type of every distractor you reject, so that elimination becomes a repeatable procedure instead of a feeling.

## Why naming matters

If you reject an option because 'it feels off', you will accept a similar option next time when it feels right. If you reject it because 'it is too strong', you have a rule you can apply again. Each wrong option in a well-made CSAT item belongs to one of seven families. Learn them with the official examples below (the examples are analysed, not keyed).

## 1. Too narrow

The option is true and taken from the passage, but it is one piece of support, not the claim the support serves. In the 2025 renewable-energy passage, an option about *economies of scale* names one mechanism mentioned in the first sentence, while the passage goes on to argue about timing of the transition. A detail dressed as a conclusion.

## 2. Too broad

The option is a theme rather than an idea: it would fit many passages. 'Modern technological progress is intensely linked to path-dependent innovations' (same passage) stretches a claim about green energy to all technology. If you can imagine the option as the answer to a different passage, it is too broad.

## 3. Too strong (absolute)

The option drops the author's qualification and makes the claim absolute: 'only', 'never', 'all', 'no', 'must', 'cannot', 'the sole'. In the 2018 monoculture passage, 'monoculture is the only way to ensure food security' and 'genetically modified crops only can save the world' are both absolute statements the passage never makes. In the 2022 opinions passage, 'people will succeed in any undertaking if they do not accept any opinion at all' converts a warning into a guarantee.

## 4. Outside knowledge

The option may be true in the world and may even sound sophisticated, but the passage does not contain it. The 2023 nutrition passage has options naming universal basic income, genetically modified crops and food fortification, none of which the passage mentions; it speaks only of placing nutrition at the centre of how food systems work. These options are bait for candidates who know current affairs well, which is to say for every serious aspirant.

## 5. Reversal

The option states the opposite of the passage, or swaps cause and effect. In the 2017 Arctic passage, 'without polar bears the food chains will disappear' reverses the passage's direction, which runs from melting ice to the food web to the bears at its top. In the 2026 customer-service passage, an option saying that AI, *unlike* previous technologies, raised alarm about job losses contradicts the opening sentence, which says earlier waves raised the same concern.

## 6. Distortion

The option keeps the passage's words but bends the meaning. 'Climate change has caused Arctic summer to be short' uses the passage's vocabulary while the passage says the summer melt is *lengthening*. Distortions are the hardest to catch because they pass the vocabulary test. Catch them by checking the verb and the direction of change.

## 7. Partially true

The option correctly captures one half of the passage and ignores the other half, or captures a conceded point rather than the claim. In the 2018 WTO passage, 'India provides food security to its poor' is true according to the passage but is background, not the tension the passage builds. In the 2026 customer-service passage, an option about voice menus failing to make humans redundant repeats the concession, not the point of view.

## The order in which to apply them

Eliminate in this order, because it is fastest: first reversal and distortion (check direction and verbs); then outside knowledge (search the passage for the key noun; a one-step paraphrase or ordinary inference, such as melting ice leading to climate change, is not outside knowledge); then too strong (scan for absolutes); then too narrow versus too broad (ask which option explains the *most* sentences). What remains is usually one option. If two remain, module 5 shows how to break the tie.

## The attractive-wrong-option rule

Almost every CSAT item contains one option that a thoughtful, well-read, morally serious person would agree with. That is precisely why it is there. Agreement is not the test; the passage is.

### 5. The six-step method, demonstrated on four official questions

*Objective:* Run a fixed six-step routine on real questions, watching the reasoning modelled in full, so that you can reproduce it under time pressure.

## The six steps

1. **Read the stem first** (two seconds). Note the key noun (crux / message / intent) and any signal adjective (practical, logical).
2. **Read the passage once**, tagging sentence jobs (module 3). Mark contrast words.
3. **Write the organising claim** in one sentence, in your own words, before reading options.
4. **Read all four options** without judging; a hasty choice of (a) blinds you to (d).
5. **Eliminate by type** in the order given in module 4, writing the type letter beside each rejected option (N, B, S, O, R, D, P).
6. **Confirm** the survivor by checking that it explains the most sentences and contains no word the passage does not support. If two survive, prefer the one with fewer unsupported additions and the less absolute wording.

What follows is analysis of four official questions. It shows how the steps work; it does not claim to be the official key. UPSC releases official Prelims answer keys only after the final result, and this project has verified none, so none is asserted.

## Demonstration 1: Arctic sea ice (2017, Q3)

Stem: 'most crucial message'. Passage structure: S (ice melting earlier, freeze later), S (30 per cent decline in three decades), N (lengthening melt 'threatens to undermine the whole Arctic food web, atop which stand polar bears'). Organising claim in my words: *a warming-driven melt is undermining the Arctic food web and so threatens the bears at its top.*

Options: (a) says summer became 'short' with high temperature: the passage says the melt period is lengthening, so this is a distortion (D). (b) relocating bears to the South Pole: nothing in the passage (O). (c) says the food chain depends on the bears' presence: the passage runs the other way, from web to bears (R). (d) says climate change threatens the bears' survival: this matches the cause-to-effect chain. Analysis points to (d) as the only option consistent with the passage's direction.

## Demonstration 2: WTO and minimum support prices (2018, Q53)

Stem: 'crucial message'. Structure, as worked in module 3: background, rule, limit, India's practice, possible breach. Organising claim: *India's food-security arrangements (MSP, stockholding) may collide with the trade limits India has accepted.*

(a) 'India should revise its PDS': a prescription; the passage recommends nothing (O, and too strong). (b) 'India should not be a member of WTO': absolute and unsupported (S, O). (c) 'food security collides with trade': names the tension every sentence builds. (d) 'India provides food security to its poor': true per the passage but background only (P). Analysis points to (c).

## Demonstration 3: the natural state and the civil state (2021, Q41)

Stem: 'central idea'. Structure: C (nothing is good or bad by common assent in a natural state), S (each man consults his own advantage, answerable to no law), N ('therefore' sin exists only in a civil state, which decrees by common consent what is good or bad). Organising claim: *right and wrong exist only once a civil state is formed by common consent.*

(a) conceptions of right and wrong exist due to the formation of a state: matches the 'therefore' sentence. (b) 'unless a ruling authority decides ... no man would be morally right': shifts from 'common consent' to 'ruling authority' and adds a claim about individuals' moral standing (D, S). (c) 'man is inherently immoral and selfish': the passage says in the natural state there is *no* good or bad, so 'immoral' imports a judgment the passage denies (D). (d) right and wrong are 'necessary for the survival of the species': survival is nowhere in the passage (O). Analysis points to (a). Notice that (c) is the attractive-wrong option: it feels like a fair summary of 'consults only his own advantage', but the passage withholds the moral label.

## Demonstration 4: learning curves in renewable energy (2025, Q3)

Stem: 'central idea'. Structure: C (steep learning curve: costs fall as more is produced), S (firms' green innovation is path-dependent), S (solar 90 per cent cheaper in the 2010s), N ('moving early and gradually' lets economies adjust; 'a late, more chaotic transition is costlier'). Organising claim: *because green costs fall with experience, starting early and gradually is cheaper than a late rush.*

(a) economies of scale 'essential': one mechanism from sentence 1, not the point (N for narrow). (b) 'modern technological progress' linked to path dependence: generalises beyond green energy (B). (c) 'countries with large economies' are better placed: size of economy is never mentioned (O). (d) timing plays a crucial role: matches the two concluding sentences. Analysis points to (d).

## What the four have in common

In each case the survivor was the option that explained the *last* movement of the passage (the N sentence) while staying inside its vocabulary. In each case at least one rejected option was true in the world and false to the passage.

### 6. Time strategy, risk and the traps that catch good readers

*Objective:* Decide, under the 33 per cent bar and one-third penalty, which comprehension items to attempt, when to skip, and which habits of a thoughtful reader must be switched off in the exam hall.

## The time budget

Two hours, 80 questions, 90 seconds average. Comprehension passages in CSAT typically take two to four minutes each including the options, so they run above average; the arithmetic works because many quantitative items take under a minute. A workable plan for a beginner: allot about 60-70 minutes to the 25-30 passage items, in two sweeps.

- **First sweep**: attempt every passage of under 150 words, and any longer passage whose structure you can tag on one reading. If after one reading you cannot write the organising claim, mark the item and move on. Do not re-read a third time in the first sweep.
- **Second sweep**: return to marked items with the remaining time. Re-read once with fresh eyes; often the contrast word you missed is obvious now.

## Reading pace

Aim at roughly 150-200 words per minute with structural tagging; a 150-word passage then takes about a minute to read and a further minute for the options. Faster than this, you lose qualifications; slower, you lose questions. Pace is built in practice, not in the hall. In the hall, read at the speed at which you can still hear the author's 'but'.

## The 33 per cent safety logic

Because CSAT is qualifying, your target is a margin, not a maximum. Attempt the comprehension items you have reduced to one option, and the ones you have honestly reduced to two. Where three options still look possible, a guess is worth about +0.28 marks on average (1/3 x 2.5 - 2/3 x 0.833), so it is not an average loss. The reason to leave such items in the first sweep is risk control and time control: candidates overrate their eliminations (if your true chance is below one in four, the guess does lose on average), and a thin margin over the 66 line is the wrong place to take variance. Once you are comfortably above the line, a guess among three whose eliminations you trust is acceptable. Thirty confident comprehension answers alone put you above the 66 line with room to spare.

## Traps that catch the well-read

1. **The true statement that is not the message.** The option is correct in the world and consistent with the passage, but it is support, background or a concession. You are being asked what the passage *argues*, not what is *true*. Rule: ask 'is this what the other sentences are for?'
2. **The morally attractive option.** Passages on poverty, environment and rights invite you to pick the kindest option. In the 2021 Cauvery passage, 'farmers adopt unsustainable livelihoods and risky coping strategies' carries a sympathetic judgment the passage does not make; the passage records cause and effect without blame. Rule: strip the adjectives and ask whether the bare claim is in the text.
3. **The option that reuses passage words.** Word overlap is the examiner's cheapest distractor. The correct option is frequently a paraphrase; the distortion is frequently a quotation with one verb changed. Rule: check verbs and direction, not nouns.
4. **The opening sentence reflex.** Beginners assume the first sentence is the thesis. When the first sentence states the common view ('X is often described as', 'policymakers frequently treat'), the thesis is after the contrast word.
5. **The last-sentence reflex.** The opposite error. In the 2017 work-and-leisure passage the final sentence asks how much leisure we can afford, but the sentences before it argue that freedom from work is unnatural and that we cannot escape work. Whether the main idea is the necessity of work or the balance it implies is exactly the kind of judgment that requires the whole structure, not the last line alone.
6. **The practical-but-unsupported fix.** When the stem says 'practical' or 'pragmatic', candidates reach for the most actionable option. The 2025 climate-by-State passage offers 'essentially the responsibility of each State' and 'strategies at the State/region level'. The first allocates responsibility, which the passage does not; the second describes the level of planning, which the passage does. Practical means workable *and* supported.
7. **Over-inferring in statement-combination items.** The 2026 HMPV item lists three statements of intent and offers combinations including 'None of the above'. Statement 1 ('evolve methodologies') and statement 3 ('remedies of HMPV') name things the passage does not do; statement 2 (compare the two viruses) is what the passage does. Since no combination offers '2 only', the item forces a judgment about whether partially matching combinations count. This lesson records that analysis as contested; the lesson for you is that you must not invent a methodology or a remedy to make a combination fit.

## When to abandon a passage

Abandon (for the first sweep) when: the passage is over 250 words and you cannot tag it in one reading; or three options survive elimination; or the stem asks for an inference and two options are each one step from the text. Return later. An abandoned item costs nothing; a wrong guess costs 0.83.

## What to carry forward

Three habits transfer to the rest of CSAT comprehension (inference, assumption and tone questions in later topics) and to Mains: read the stem first; write the claim before the options; name the reason for every rejection. Everything else in this lesson is detail in service of those three.

### Notes: Definitions

- Topic: the subject of the passage, a noun phrase with no claim (for example, 'Arctic sea ice and polar bears').
- Main idea / central idea / crux: the single sentence that explains why every other sentence in the passage was written.
- Theme: the broad human concern the main idea belongs to; usually too general to be a correct option.
- Message / critical message: what the author wants the reader to take away; coincides with the main idea in argumentative passages.
- Assumption: a necessary unstated premise the author must hold for the argument to work; denying it collapses the argument.
- Inference: an unstated statement that must be true if the passage is true, within one step of the text; denying it contradicts the passage.
- Logical corollary: a consequence forced by the passage; the strictest kind of inference.
- Qualify: to limit a claim with 'but', 'rarely', 'only partly'; concede: to admit an opposing point before moving past it.

### Notes: CSAT paper facts that drive strategy

- CSAT (General Studies Paper II) is qualifying: 33 per cent of 200 marks, that is 66 marks, is required (net scores move in steps of 5/6, so the lowest passing score in practice is 66.67).
- 80 questions, 2.5 marks each, two hours; wrong answers lose one-third of the question's marks (0.833).
- Three wrong answers cancel one right answer; an unattempted question costs nothing.
- About 27 fully correct answers with no errors clear the bar; a commonly given piece of advice is to aim at a margin of 90-100 marks (advice, not a rule).
- Comprehension items are usually 25-30 of the 80 questions and are the safest marks for a careful reader.

### Notes: The seven wrong-option types

- Apply in order: reversal and distortion, then outside knowledge, then too strong, then narrow versus broad.

### Notes: The six-step method

- Step 1: read the stem first; note key noun (crux, message, intent) and signal adjective (practical, logical).
- Step 2: read the passage once, tagging each sentence C (claim), S (support), X (contrast), Q (qualification), N (conclusion).
- Step 3: write the organising claim in one sentence in your own words before reading options.
- Step 4: read all four options without judging.
- Step 5: eliminate by type, writing the letter beside each rejected option.
- Step 6: confirm the survivor explains the most sentences and adds no unsupported word; if two survive, prefer fewer additions and less absolute wording.

### Notes: Stem phrases by year (verified questions)

- 2017: 'The main idea of the passage is that'; 'most crucial message conveyed'; 'Which one of the following views corroborates the above statement?'
- 2018: 'What is the essential message'; 'most logical, rational and crucial message given by the passage'; 'The author of the passage holds the view that'.
- 2019: 'most logical, rational and crucial message'; 'The passage seems to argue'.
- 2020: 'best implies the crux'; 'best reflects the critical message'.
- 2021: 'best reflects the crux / critical message / central idea'; 'best sums up the passage'.
- 2022: 'message of the author'; 'The passage is based on the idea that'; 'What is the main idea of the passage?'
- 2023: 'most logical, rational and practical message conveyed by the author'; 'what the author intends to say'; 'which term best expresses the ultimate goal of the state'.
- 2024: 'central idea of the above passage'; 'crux of the above passage'.
- 2025: 'most logical, rational and pragmatic message'; 'critical message conveyed by the author'; 'central idea conveyed by the passage'.
- 2026: 'Which of the following reflect the intent of the writer' (numbered statements with codes); 'most appropriately reflects the point of view'.

### Notes: Signal words and the sentence jobs they announce

- Contrast (X): but, yet, however, although, rather, 'is often described as', 'in this view', 'critics predicted'. The real claim usually follows.
- Qualification (Q): 'not that ... is worthless', 'only part of', 'rarely', 'on its own', 'but incomplete'. Options that drop these are too strong.
- Conclusion (N): therefore, as a result, this is why, the lesson is, it seems, 'all this led to'. Often carries the main idea.
- Support (S): for example, research reveals, a study showed, statistics, named cases. Never the main idea.

### Notes: Concepts commonly confused

- Main idea versus topic: the topic has no verb; the main idea is a full claim.
- Main idea versus theme: the theme fits many passages; the main idea fits only this one.
- Assumption versus inference: an assumption is a necessary unstated premise (deny it and the argument collapses); an inference is what must be true if the passage is true (deny it and you contradict the passage).
- Message versus true statement: a statement can be true, in the passage, and still not be what the passage argues.
- Practical option versus supported option: 'practical' in the stem means workable and in the passage, not merely actionable.
- Paraphrase versus quotation: the correct option is often a paraphrase; the distortion often quotes the passage with one verb altered.

### Notes: Exceptions to the usual rules

- The first sentence is not the claim when it states the common view that a contrast word then rejects.
- The last sentence is not always the claim: in the 2017 work-and-leisure passage the last sentence poses a question that depends on the argument before it.
- A descriptive passage (a study found X) has no 'should'; a 'message' stem then expects the central finding, not a recommendation.
- In statement-combination items (2018 desertification, 2026 HMPV) the task is to test each numbered statement against the passage separately; the codes come last.
- A one-sentence passage ('pearls and thread', 2017) asks which view corroborates it: look for the option that restates the relationship, not one that adds a judgment.

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - The main idea is the one sentence that explains why every other sentence in the passage exists.
  - Read for sentence jobs (claim, support, contrast, qualification, conclusion), not for words.
  - Write the organising claim before reading the options.
  - Every rejected option belongs to one of seven types: too narrow, too broad, too strong, outside knowledge, reversal, distortion, partially true.
  - CSAT is qualifying with one-third negative marking, so the target is a safe margin above 66 marks, not a maximum.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - CSAT: 80 questions, 200 marks, 2.5 marks each, two hours, 33 per cent qualifying (66 marks), one-third negative marking (0.833 per wrong answer).
  - Three wrong answers cancel one right answer; a blank costs nothing.
  - The stems 'crux', 'central idea', 'critical message', 'crucial message', 'main idea' are used interchangeably across 2017-2026.
  - Passages are typically 80-200 words and since 2020 usually carry one question each.
  - The paper instructs that answers be based on the passage only.
- **criticalDistinctions:**
  - Topic (noun phrase) versus main idea (full claim) versus theme (broad concern).
  - Assumption (before the argument) versus inference (after it) versus corollary (forced consequence).
  - What the passage says versus what is true in the world.
  - A concession (admitted then moved past) versus the claim (what the author moves to).
- **traps:**
  - Choosing a statement that is true and in the passage but is support or background rather than the claim.
  - Being drawn to the morally sympathetic option that adds a judgment ('unsustainable', 'exploitative') the passage never makes.
  - Picking the option that reuses the passage's nouns while changing the verb or the direction of change.
  - Taking the opening sentence as the thesis when it states the view the author is about to reject.
  - Treating a guess among three surviving options as automatically wrong or automatically fine: it is worth about +0.28 marks only if the eliminations are sound, and a thin margin over 66 should not carry avoidable variance.

### Last-week summary

- Main idea: the one sentence explaining why every other sentence exists; the topic makes no claim, the theme fits many passages.
- Stem first; tag C/S/X/Q/N in one reading; write the claim yourself before the options.
- Eliminate reversal and distortion, then outside knowledge, too strong, narrow versus broad; partially true last.
- Survivor explains the most sentences and adds no unsupported word; prefer fewer additions, softer wording.
- Traps: true-but-support options, sympathetic added judgments, reused nouns with changed verbs, an opening view the author rejects.
