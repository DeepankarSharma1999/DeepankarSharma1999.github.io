### Bridge: Components, squares and the average of cos squared

A vector in a plane can be split into two perpendicular components. If a vector of length E makes angle θ with an axis, the component along that axis is E cos θ and the component across it is E sin θ. For a 3-4-5 triangle with E = 5, cos θ = 3/5 and sin θ = 4/5, so the components are 3 and 4, and 3 squared plus 4 squared equals 5 squared. The intensity of light is proportional to the square of the amplitude of E, so a component of amplitude E cos θ carries a fraction cos squared θ of the intensity. The average of cos squared θ over a full circle is 1/2, because cos squared θ = (1 + cos 2 θ)/2 and cos 2 θ is positive as often as negative. Two identities are used repeatedly: cos(A + B) = cos A cos B − sin A sin B, and sin(A − B) = sin A cos B − cos A sin B. Finally, a quantity such as cos(ωt − δ) means the same oscillation as cos(ωt), delayed by the phase δ: it reaches each peak later by δ/ω seconds.

### Bridge: Optical path, phase difference and refractive index

Light travels at speed c in vacuum and at speed v = c/μ in a medium of refractive index μ (the refractive index is the ratio c/v; see lesson PH-042 module 6). In a slab of thickness d the number of wavelengths is d divided by the wavelength inside, which is λ/μ, so it is μ d / λ. The product μ d is called the optical path. Two waves that cross the same thickness d in media of different index μ₁ and μ₂ therefore differ in optical path by Δ = (μ₁ − μ₂) d. A path difference of one whole wavelength λ corresponds to a phase difference of 2 π radians (360 degrees), so a path difference Δ corresponds to a phase difference δ = 2 π Δ / λ. A path difference of λ/4 is a phase difference of π/2 (90 degrees), λ/2 is π (180 degrees), and adding any whole number of wavelengths changes nothing. Here λ is always the wavelength in vacuum, and the path difference is measured in the same unit as λ (metres, nanometres or micrometres, not mixed).

### 1. Orientation: what polarisation is, the five states and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Name the five states of polarisation, say what the tip of the electric vector does in each, and list the three things this syllabus clause asks you to do.

## What this topic is about

Light is a transverse wave: its electric field E vibrates at right angles to the direction of travel (waves, wavefronts and rays are set up in lesson PH-042; the addition of collinear vibrations is in PH-035 to PH-038). Because E points sideways, one more question arises that a longitudinal wave such as sound never raises: **in which sideways direction does E point, and how does that direction change with time?** The answer is the **state of polarisation**. Interference and diffraction cannot tell you that light is transverse; polarisation can, which is why Brijlal and Subrahmanyam open the chapter with it (PDF p. 498).

## The five states

| State | What the tip of E does at one point | Turn a polariser in front of it |
|---|---|---|
| Unpolarised | jumps about at random in all sideways directions | intensity constant, half of the input |
| Linear (plane) | moves back and forth along one straight line | zero twice and maximum twice per turn |
| Circular | goes round a circle at constant speed | intensity constant |
| Elliptical | goes round an ellipse | a maximum and a smaller minimum that is not zero |
| Partially polarised | a mixture of linear and unpolarised | a maximum and a smaller minimum that is not zero |

Read the last column carefully: it is the heart of the *detection* part of the syllabus. Two different states give the same pattern in two of the rows, and the only way to separate them is to add a quarter-wave plate (module 10).

### 2. Foundation: E vector, unpolarised light, coherence and degree of polarisation

*Objective:* Define plane of vibration, unpolarised, partially polarised, coherent and incoherent components, and compute the degree of polarisation from Imax and Imin.

## The electric vector and the plane of vibration

A light wave carries an electric field E and a magnetic field B at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. Only E is drawn, since it is E that acts on electrons (Brijlal PDF pp. 499 to 500). At a fixed point, E is a vector in the plane at right angles to the beam. The **state of polarisation** is the shape that the tip of this vector traces in that plane as time goes on. Brijlal and Subrahmanyam call the plane that contains E and the direction of travel the **plane of polarisation** (PDF p. 500). Older books call the plane at right angles to E the plane of polarisation (standard knowledge, verify), so in an answer say 'plane of vibration of E' and name the direction.

## Why ordinary light is unpolarised

An ordinary source is a huge crowd of atoms. Each atom emits a short **wave-train**, a burst lasting about 10⁻⁸ s according to the extract (PDF p. 500). Every wave-train is polarised, but its direction is unrelated to the next one. At any point the direction of E therefore wanders at random, far faster than any eye or detector can follow. The extract elsewhere quotes 10⁻¹⁴ s for the random change in a bulb (PDF p. 499); the numbers differ, but both are far too fast to follow, so on average there is no preferred sideways direction.

## The two-component description

Any sideways vector can be resolved along two perpendicular axes, say y and z (bridge module B1). Two words are essential:

- **Coherent**: the two components keep a fixed phase difference.
- **Incoherent**: the phase difference changes at random.

Then:

- **Linear light** is two coherent components with phase difference 0 or π, so the sum stays on one straight line (Brijlal PDF p. 501).
- **Natural light** is equivalent to two components of equal intensity whose phase difference changes at random (PDF p. 500, Fig. 20.4 b), a convenient picture only.
- **Partially polarised light** is the same picture with unequal intensities, or equivalently a mixture of linear and natural light (PDF pp. 504 to 505).

Incoherent components do not interfere, so their intensities add. Coherent components add as amplitudes, and the phase difference decides the result (module 3).

## Degree of polarisation

Pass the beam through a polariser and rotate it. The transmitted intensity swings between Imax and Imin, and the swing from one to the other takes a quarter-turn of 90° (PDF p. 505). Define

$$ P = (Imax − Imin) / (Imax + Imin) $$

For linear light Imin = 0 and P = 1. For natural light Imax = Imin and P = 0. If Imax = 2 Imin, then P = 1/3, or about 33 per cent (the extract's own example). The extract states that P is not defined for circular and elliptical light (PDF p. 505).

For a mixture of linear light I_p and natural light I_n, a polariser along the linear direction passes all of I_p and half of I_n, and a polariser across it passes only half of I_n. So

$$ Imax = I_p + I_n/2,  Imin = I_n/2,  P = I_p / (I_p + I_n) $$

Check with the numbers in the check box: this reproduces P = 0.8.

## Dots and arrows

For light meeting a surface, the plane of incidence contains the incident ray and the normal. The component of E perpendicular to it is called **s** (from the German senkrecht, drawn as dots) and the component in it is **p** (drawn as arrows; PDF pp. 502 and 506). Both are used in module 5.

### 3. Core derivation: two perpendicular coherent vibrations give a line, a circle or an ellipse

*Objective:* Derive the ellipse equation for perpendicular components with phase difference δ, deduce the linear, circular and elliptical cases, and fix the handedness rule with a stated viewpoint.

## Setting and assumptions

Light travels along +x. The electric field has two parts in the y–z plane (x, y, z a right-handed set), with the same ω and k and a fixed phase difference δ, so they are coherent (module 2). Write φ = kx − ωt:

$$ E_y = E₁ cos φ,   E_z = E₂ cos(φ + δ) $$

This is Brijlal equations 20.37 and 20.38 (PDF pp. 529 to 530). The task is to remove φ and find the curve traced by the tip of (E_y, E_z).

## Derivation 1: the general equation

1. Expand: E_z/E₂ = cos φ cos δ − sin φ sin δ, and use cos φ = E_y/E₁.
2. Isolate the sine: E_z/E₂ − (E_y/E₁) cos δ = −sin φ sin δ.
3. Square, using sin²φ = 1 − E_y²/E₁²:

$$ (E_z/E₂ − (E_y/E₁) cos δ)² = (1 − E_y²/E₁²) sin²δ $$

4. Expand the left side and move (E_y²/E₁²) sin²δ across; with cos²δ + sin²δ = 1 the E_y² terms combine:

$$ E_y²/E₁² + E_z²/E₂² − 2 (E_yE_z / E₁E₂) cos δ = sin²δ $$

This is Brijlal equation 20.43 (PDF p. 530). Squaring loses the sign of sin φ, but the curve contains both signs because φ runs through a full cycle.

5. **Ellipse.** Written as A E_y² + B E_yE_z + C E_z², with A = 1/E₁², C = 1/E₂², B = −2 cos δ/(E₁E₂), it has B² − 4AC = −4 sin²δ/(E₁²E₂²) < 0 unless sin δ = 0: an ellipse.
6. **Tilt.** The standard rotation-of-axes result tan 2α = B/(A − C) gives

$$ tan 2α = 2 E₁E₂ cos δ / (E₁² − E₂²) $$

with α measured from the y-axis (matches PDF p. 530). The axes lie along y and z exactly when cos δ = 0.

## The special cases

- **δ = 0 (or 2mπ):** the equation becomes (E_y/E₁ − E_z/E₂)² = 0, so E_z = (E₂/E₁) E_y: **linear light** at angle tan⁻¹(E₂/E₁) to the y-axis. (The extract prints tan⁻¹(E₂ − E₁) on PDF p. 531, a slip.)
- **δ = π (or (2m + 1)π):** E_z = −(E₂/E₁) E_y, linear along the other diagonal.
- **δ = ±π/2, E₁ ≠ E₂:** E_y²/E₁² + E_z²/E₂² = 1, **elliptical light** with axes along y and z.
- **δ = ±π/2, E₁ = E₂ = E₀:** E_y² + E_z² = E₀², **circular light**.
- **Other δ:** a tilted ellipse (Brijlal Fig. 20.41: δ = 0, π/4, π/2, 3π/4, π with equal amplitudes gives line, thin ellipse, circle, thin ellipse tilted the other way, line).

## Which way does it turn?

At x = 0: E_y = E₁ cos ωt, E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ), so E_z lags E_y by δ. Form L = E_y (dE_z/dt) − E_z (dE_y/dt), positive when the tip turns from +y towards +z:

$$ L = ωE₁E₂ [cos(ωt − δ) sin ωt − cos ωt sin(ωt − δ)] = ωE₁E₂ sin δ $$

using sin A cos B − cos A sin B = sin(A − B) with A = ωt, B = ωt − δ. So **sin δ > 0 turns y → z** and sin δ < 0 turns z → y (a numerical sweep over δ confirms this).

Now the viewpoint. An observer looking back at the source sees the x-axis pointing at the eye, and for a right-handed set y → z is **anticlockwise** to this observer. Brijlal Chapter 20 calls clockwise, as seen by an observer looking back towards the source, **right** (PDF p. 503). So here sin δ > 0 is **left** and sin δ < 0 is **right**. Chapter 13 (PDF p. 296) instead uses a right-hand rule with the thumb along the propagation, which can name the same motion the opposite way. The physics agrees; only the names differ. In every answer state the convention and the viewpoint.

### 4. Core: polarisers, Malus's law and what a rotating analyser shows

*Objective:* Derive Malus's law and the factor one half for natural light, solve multi-polariser problems, and predict what a rotating polariser shows for each state.

## The polariser and its transmission axis

A **polariser** is any element that turns natural light into linear light. A polaroid sheet or a Nicol prism has a **transmission axis**: the component of E along it passes, the component across it is removed (absorbed in a polaroid, sent away in a Nicol prism; modules 5 to 7). The same device used to examine light is called an **analyser**. The extract stresses that polariser and analyser are made alike; only their roles differ (Brijlal PDF p. 512).

## Derivation 2: Malus's law

Setting: linear light of intensity I₁ and amplitude E₁ is incident on an ideal polariser. Its E makes an angle θ with the transmission axis. Sign convention: θ is measured from the axis to E, and only θ² matters.

1. Resolve E₁ along the axis and across it: E_parallel = E₁ cos θ, E_perpendicular = E₁ sin θ.
2. Only E_parallel passes. The transmitted amplitude is E₁ cos θ.
3. Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude (bridge module B1), so

$$ I = I₁ cos²θ $$

This is **Malus's law** (found by E. L. Malus in 1809), Brijlal equation 20.10 (PDF p. 513). Special cases: θ = 0° or 180° gives I = I₁; θ = 90° or 270° gives I = 0. In one full turn of the analyser there are therefore **two maxima and two zeros**, which is the test for linear light.

## Derivation 3: why natural light loses exactly half

1. In natural light every angle θ is equally likely, so the fraction transmitted is the average of cos²θ over a full circle.
2. Use cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2. The average of the constant 1/2 is 1/2, and the average of (cos 2θ)/2 over a full circle is zero, since cos 2θ is positive as often as negative.
3. Hence the average is 1/2:

$$ I = I₀/2 $$

The extract does the same with an integral from 0 to 2π (PDF p. 511). The transmitted light is linear and does not change if the polariser is turned, since the input has no preferred direction.

## What a rotating polariser shows for each state

Turn the polariser through 360° and watch the transmitted intensity (Brijlal PDF p. 520):

- **Natural:** constant, I₀/2.
- **Partially polarised:** between Imax and Imin (not zero), two of each per turn.
- **Linear:** from 0 to Imax, two zeros and two maxima per turn.
- **Circular:** constant. Proof: take components E₀ cos ωt along y and E₀ sin ωt along z (a circle). A polariser at angle φ to y passes E₀ cos ωt cos φ + E₀ sin ωt sin φ = E₀ cos(ωt − φ). The amplitude E₀ does not depend on φ, so the intensity is independent of φ. The total time-averaged intensity is proportional to the mean of E₀²(cos²ωt + sin²ωt) = E₀², while the transmitted one is proportional to the mean of E₀² cos²(ωt − φ) = E₀²/2: exactly half, at every φ.
- **Elliptical:** with semi-axes a and b, the maximum is proportional to a² when the axis lies along the major axis and the minimum to b² along the minor axis (PDF p. 521). Neither is zero.

## Chains of polarisers

For a chain, apply Malus's law at each step with the angle between **successive** axes, and the first element halves natural light. The three-polariser example in the check box shows that adding a middle polariser at 45° lets light through two crossed polarisers; this is a favourite viva question. Limiting case: a very large number N of polarisers each turned by 90°/N relative to the previous one pass (cos²(90°/N))^N → 1 of the polarised intensity, so the plane can be rotated 90° with almost no loss.

### 5. Core: producing linear light by reflection, scattering and selective absorption

*Objective:* Derive Brewster's law tan θ_B = μ, compute the reflected fraction at that angle, and describe the pile of plates, scattering and the polaroid sheet.

## The idea

When light reaches the boundary between air and a transparent dielectric (glass, water), part is reflected and part is refracted. Natural light has equal s and p components (module 2). At one special angle of incidence, the **polarising angle** or **Brewster angle** θ_B, the reflected beam contains no p-component and is completely linearly polarised, with E perpendicular to the plane of incidence (Brijlal PDF p. 506). Metals do not obey this (PDF p. 507).

## Derivation 4: Brewster's law

Setting: light in a medium of index μ₁ meets a medium of index μ₂ at angle of incidence θ_B; refraction angle r. Sign convention: all angles from the normal.

1. Brewster found that the polarisation of the reflected beam is complete when the reflected and refracted rays are at right angles (PDF p. 506): θ_B + r = 90°.
2. Hence r = 90° − θ_B and sin r = cos θ_B.
3. Snell's law: μ₁ sin θ_B = μ₂ sin r = μ₂ cos θ_B.
4. Divide by cos θ_B:

$$ tan θ_B = μ₂ / μ₁ $$

For air (μ₁ = 1) this is the textbook form tan θ_B = μ (equation 20.3 and 20.6, PDF pp. 506 to 507).

**Why the p-component vanishes.** The Fresnel result for p-light (Brijlal equation 13.69a, PDF p. 309) is Γ_p = tan(θ_i − θ_t)/tan(θ_i + θ_t). It is zero when tan(θ_i + θ_t) is infinite, i.e. θ_i + θ_t = 90°, the same condition. Chapter 13 gives tan θ_B = √(ε₂/ε₁) for p-light (PDF p. 311); non-magnetic s-light has no such angle.

**Numbers (recomputed):**

| Surface | μ | θ_B | r = 90° − θ_B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 1.33 | 53.06° | 36.94° |
| Glass | 1.5 | 56.31° | 33.69° |
| Diamond | 2.42 | 67.55° | 22.45° |

The extract's 57° for glass is rounded; 56.31° is exact for μ = 1.5.

**How strong is the reflected beam?** At θ_B the s-reflection coefficient is Γ_s = −sin(θ_i − θ_t)/sin(θ_i + θ_t) (equation 13.60a, PDF p. 306) = −sin(2θ_B − 90°) = cos 2θ_B, whose magnitude |cos 2θ_B| equals (μ² − 1)/(μ² + 1). For μ = 1.5 the reflectance is R_s = (1.25/3.25)² = 0.148. Natural light carries half its power as s, so the reflected beam has 0.074 of the incident power: fully polarised, but weak. The source calls the method 'not advantageous' for this reason (PDF p. 506).

## Pile of plates, scattering, dichroism

- **Pile of plates:** one surface at θ_B transmits all p but only 0.852 of s, so the transmitted beam is just 8 per cent polarised. The extract says about 15 plates leave p only (PDF pp. 507 to 508); the standard stack formula P = m/(m + 5.76) for μ = 1.5 (verify) gives P = 0.72 for 15 plates, so 'completely polarised' is an idealisation. In that formula m counts plates (two surfaces each) and multiple reflections are included; a single-pass count without them gives 0.98 for 15 plates (recomputed).
- **Scattering:** a vibrating electron radiates nothing along its own axis, so light scattered through about 90° from a beam is strongly polarised, with E perpendicular to the plane containing the beam and the line of sight. Blue sky is a case, but multiple scattering spoils it (PDF p. 508).
- **Selective absorption (dichroism):** tourmaline absorbs one of the two perpendicular components much more than the other (Biot, 1815). E. H. Land's polaroid sheet (the extract gives 1928; the PVA-iodine H-sheet dates from 1938; verify) is a stretched polyvinyl-alcohol (PVA) sheet dyed with iodine so that long conducting chains line up. E along the chains drives currents and is absorbed; E across them passes. Polaroids are cheap, large and used for sunglasses and filters (PDF pp. 508 to 510).

Sunglasses and the Brewster window of a gas laser are in the master notes.

### 6. Core: double refraction in calcite, the o-ray and e-ray, and positive and negative crystals

*Objective:* Describe the o-ray and e-ray, define optic axis and principal section, and compare positive and negative crystals.

## What happens in an anisotropic crystal

In glass the speed of light is the same in every direction (the material is **isotropic**). In a crystal whose atoms are packed differently along different directions, the electrons respond differently to E, so the speed, and hence the refractive index, depends on direction (**anisotropic**; Brijlal PDF p. 513). Calcite (Iceland spar) is the standard case. A beam of natural light entering it splits into two linearly polarised rays, the **ordinary ray (o-ray)** and the **extraordinary ray (e-ray)**. This is **double refraction** or **birefringence** (PDF p. 509).

## Key words

- **Optic axis:** a direction, not a single line, along which both rays travel together at the same speed, so no splitting occurs. It runs through a blunt corner of the calcite rhomb, making equal angles with the three edges that meet there (PDF p. 514).
- **Uniaxial crystal:** one optic axis (calcite, quartz); a biaxial crystal (mica, topaz) has two (PDF p. 513).
- **Principal section:** a plane that contains the optic axis and is perpendicular to a pair of opposite faces; it cuts calcite in a parallelogram with angles 71° and 109° (PDF p. 514).

## The two rays compared

1. The o-ray obeys the ordinary laws of refraction; the e-ray does not.
2. Both are linearly polarised, at right angles. In the o-ray E is **perpendicular** to the principal section; in the e-ray E is **parallel** to it (PDF p. 515).
3. The o-ray has the same speed in all directions in the crystal, so one constant index μ_o. The e-ray's speed depends on direction, so μ_e depends on direction; its extreme value is called μ_e (PDF p. 516).
4. Along the optic axis both speeds are equal. The distinction between o and e exists only inside the crystal (PDF p. 517).

Huygens explained this with two wavelets: a **sphere** for the o-wave and an **ellipsoid** of revolution about the optic axis for the e-wave, touching on the axis (PDF pp. 515 to 516). The cuts with the axis inclined to, parallel to or perpendicular to the face give different ray directions (PDF pp. 521 to 524); with the axis parallel to the face and the beam normal to it, both rays go straight on at different speeds, the geometry of wave plates.

## Positive and negative crystals

| | Positive (quartz) | Negative (calcite) |
|---|---|---|
| Wave surfaces | e-ellipsoid inside the o-sphere | e-ellipsoid outside the o-sphere |
| Speeds | v_e ≤ v_o (slower e) | v_e ≥ v_o (faster e) |
| Indices | μ_e > μ_o | μ_e < μ_o |
| Δμ = μ_e − μ_o | positive | negative |
| Fast ray | o | e |

Equality holds along the optic axis (PDF pp. 517 to 518). Equation 20.13 on PDF p. 517 prints μ_o where μ_e is meant; the table follows the surrounding text.

### 7. Master: the Nicol prism and the Rochon and Wollaston prisms

*Objective:* Explain how a Nicol prism removes the o-ray by total internal reflection at the balsam layer, check the 69.02° condition, and contrast the Rochon and Wollaston prisms.

## The Nicol prism (builds on module 6)

A Nicol prism turns natural light into one linear beam by throwing the o-ray out (Brijlal PDF p. 519). Construction: a calcite rhomb about three times as long as it is thick has its end faces ground to make 68° (instead of 71°) with the long edges; it is cut in two along a plane perpendicular to the new end faces and cemented again with Canada balsam, μ = 1.55 (data from the extract: μ_o = 1.66, μ_e = 1.486).

Why it works: the balsam index 1.55 lies between 1.486 and 1.66. For the o-ray, balsam is optically rarer (1.55 < 1.66), so it can be totally internally reflected if the angle of incidence exceeds sin⁻¹(1.55/1.66) = 69.02° (computed). For the e-ray, whose index inside the crystal lies between 1.486 and the balsam value for the directions the prism is designed to use, balsam is denser, so it is transmitted. The o-ray is absorbed on a blackened face. The emerging e-ray is linear with E in the principal section.

Two Nicols, one as polariser P and one as analyser A, behave like two polaroids: parallel principal sections give maximum, crossed give zero (Malus's law). Limitation: Canada balsam absorbs ultraviolet light (PDF p. 542). **Rochon** and **Wollaston** prisms (two cemented prisms with perpendicular optic axes) instead send out *both* rays, widely separated, with the Wollaston pair also usable for measuring the polarisation percentage of a partly polarised beam (PDF pp. 542 to 543).

## Numbers (recomputed)

sin⁻¹(1.55/1.66) = 69.02° is the smallest angle of incidence on the balsam layer at which the o-ray is totally reflected. The e-ray, with an index near 1.486 to 1.55 in this geometry, meets a denser layer and is transmitted. The prism therefore passes one beam, linear with E in the principal section, and wastes the other.

### 8. Core: quarter-wave and half-wave plates, thickness formulas and optic-axis geometry

*Objective:* Derive the phase difference of a birefringent plate, the quarter-wave and half-wave thicknesses, and the 2θ rotation of a half-wave plate, with worked numbers for quartz and calcite.

## Setting and assumptions

A **wave plate** (also called a **retarder**) is a flat plate of a uniaxial crystal cut so that the optic axis lies in the plane of the plate (Brijlal PDF p. 532). Light falls at normal incidence. Take the beam along +x, the optic axis along z and the thickness d. Inside, the o-wave (E along y) and the e-wave (E along z) travel in the **same direction** with different speeds c/μ_o and c/μ_e. They stay together, so there is no lateral shift (module 6).

## Derivation 5: phase difference and the thicknesses

1. Optical path of the o-ray: μ_o d. Optical path of the e-ray: μ_e d.
2. Optical path difference Δ = (μ_o − μ_e) d (Brijlal equation 20.35, PDF p. 528). The sign depends on which ray is slower: positive when o lags (calcite), negative for quartz.
3. Phase difference δ = (2π/λ) Δ = 2π (μ_o − μ_e) d / λ (equation 20.36).
4. **Quarter-wave plate:** require |Δ| = λ/4, i.e. δ = π/2:

$$ d = λ / (4 |μ_o − μ_e|) $$

(equations 20.48 and 20.49, PDF p. 533). For a negative crystal the absolute value is μ_o − μ_e; for a positive crystal it is μ_e − μ_o.
5. **Half-wave plate:** require |Δ| = λ/2, i.e. δ = π:

$$ d = λ / (2 |μ_o − μ_e|) $$

(equations 20.50 and 20.51), exactly twice as thick.
6. **Order.** A phase difference larger by 2π behaves the same, so thicker plates also work: d = (4m + 1) λ / (4 |Δμ|) for a quarter-wave plate of order m = 0, 1, 2, … (standard result, not in the extract; verify). The (4m + 3) orders also give circular light, but with the opposite hand. The m = 0 plate is the **zero-order** plate, the thinnest.

**Numbers (recomputed):**

| Crystal | |Δμ| | QWP at 589 nm | HWP at 589 nm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quartz (μ_o = 1.544, μ_e = 1.553) | 0.009 | 16.36 μm | 32.72 μm |
| Calcite (μ_o = 1.66, μ_e = 1.486) | 0.174 | 0.846 μm | 1.693 μm |

Calcite at 0.85 μm is impractically thin, so calcite plates are made in a higher order (m = 10 gives 34.70 μm). The 'quarter-wave' property is wavelength-specific: the same quartz plate gives 117.8° at 450 nm and 75.7° at 700 nm if Δμ is taken as constant.

## Derivation 6: the half-wave plate rotates the plane by 2θ

Setting: linear light of amplitude E whose vibration makes an angle θ with the optic axis (z), measured from the axis towards y. Sign convention: θ positive from z toward y.

1. Resolve: e-component E cos θ along z, o-component E sin θ along y. They are in phase on entry because they come from one wave.
2. After the plate the o-component is delayed by δ = π relative to the e-component (the sign of δ only decides which component carries the extra π, which gives the same line):
E_z = E cos θ cos ωt, E_y = E sin θ cos(ωt − π) = −E sin θ cos ωt.
3. Combine: (E_z, E_y) = E cos ωt (cos θ, −sin θ). This is linear light at angle **−θ** to the axis.
4. The vibration has been turned from +θ to −θ, a rotation of **2θ** (the mirror image of the input about the optic axis). For θ = 45° the turn is 90° (Brijlal PDF p. 534).
5. For circular or elliptical light the same δ = π reverses the sense of rotation, as module 9 shows. The extract states this (PDF p. 534).

Check: θ = 0 or 90° is unchanged; an input at 20° returns −20°, a 40° rotation.

### 9. Core: producing circular and elliptical light, handedness and left-right conversion

*Objective:* Derive the elliptical and circular output of a quarter-wave plate, fix the handedness rule for positive and negative crystals, and list three ways to reverse circular light.

## Setting and assumptions

Natural light passes through a polariser and becomes linear (module 4). It then falls at normal incidence on a quarter-wave plate whose optic axis lies in the plane of the plate (module 8). The incident vibration makes angle θ with the optic axis, measured from z towards y as in module 8. Take a **negative** crystal such as calcite first, where the e-component (along z, the axis) is faster and the o-component (along y) is delayed by 90° (Brijlal PDF p. 534).

## Derivation 7: output of a quarter-wave plate

1. Entry components, in phase: E_z = E cos θ cos ωt, E_y = E sin θ cos ωt.
2. After the plate the o-component lags by π/2: E_y = E sin θ cos(ωt − π/2) = E sin θ sin ωt.
3. Divide by the amplitudes and add the squares: (E_z / E cos θ)² + (E_y / E sin θ)² = cos²ωt + sin²ωt = 1.

$$ E_z² / (E cos θ)² + E_y² / (E sin θ)² = 1 $$

4. This is an **ellipse with axes along the optic axis and across it**, semi-axes E cos θ and E sin θ.
5. If θ = 45°, both semi-axes are E/√2, a **circle**. If θ = 0° or 90° the ellipse collapses to a line. The intensity is unchanged: E²(cos²θ + sin²θ) = E².
6. Sense of rotation: form L′ = E_z (dE_y/dt) − E_y (dE_z/dt) = ωE² sin θ cos θ (cos²ωt + sin²ωt) = ωE² sin θ cos θ. For 0° < θ < 90° this is positive: the tip goes z → y.

The viewpoint is the same as in module 3: for an observer looking back at the source, y → z is anticlockwise, so z → y is **clockwise**, which in Brijlal's convention is **right**. Sampling confirms clockwise at 30°, 45°, 60° and anticlockwise at −45°.

| Crystal | θ > 0 (between +z and +y) | θ < 0 |
|---|---|---|
| Negative (calcite) | right | left |
| Positive (quartz) | left | right |

Reason: in a positive crystal the e-component is slower, so the delay swaps and the sense reverses.

## Production, step by step

- **Elliptical light:** polariser, then a quarter-wave plate with the incident vibration at θ other than 0°, 45° and 90°. Axis ratio tan θ. For example θ = 30° gives semi-axes in the ratio cos 30° : sin 30°, so intensities 3 : 1 along the two axes (Brijlal PDF pp. 534 to 535).
- **Circular light:** the same, with θ = 45°, so the two components have equal amplitude and a 90° phase difference (PDF p. 535).
- Fresnel's rhomb does the same job for all wavelengths; see the master notes ('Devices and applications').

## Turning left into right (2019 Q3(b)(i))

Take right-circular light E_z = E₀ cos ωt, E_y = E₀ sin ωt. Three ways to reverse it:

1. **Half-wave plate, any orientation.** Circular light has equal amplitudes 90° apart along **any** pair of perpendicular axes, so the plate's axes can be anywhere. The plate adds π to one component: E_y → −E₀ sin ωt, giving (cos ωt, −sin ωt), the opposite sense. The extract states this on PDF p. 534.
2. **Two identical quarter-wave plates with parallel axes**, which together are a half-wave plate. (If the second plate is turned through 90° it undoes the first.)
3. **Mirror reflection at normal incidence** reverses the named sense, since the direction of travel reverses while the sense of rotation in space does not (PDF p. 545). This is why a **circular polariser** hides reflections on a display: ambient light becomes circular, returns reversed, passes the plate again (acting as a half-wave plate overall, 2θ = 90°) and is blocked by the polariser.

### 10. Master: detection and analysis of the state of polarisation with an analyser and a quarter-wave plate

*Objective:* Run the two-step analyser and quarter-wave plate scheme that separates all five states, justify each branch by calculation, and state the one condition the extract leaves out.

## Why the eye cannot tell

Your eye responds to intensity and colour, not to polarisation (Brijlal PDF p. 501), so any test must turn a difference of polarisation into a difference of intensity. The only tools are a polariser used as an analyser and a quarter-wave plate. The scheme is Brijlal Section 20.22 with Fig. 20.51 (PDF pp. 536 to 538).

## Step 1: the analyser alone

Turn the analyser through 360° and watch (module 4):

1. Two zeros and two maxima: **linear**.
2. Constant intensity: **circular** or **natural**.
3. A maximum and a minimum that is not zero: **elliptical** or **partially polarised**.

## Step 2: add the quarter-wave plate

**Derivation 8: elliptical light becomes linear.** Let the ellipse have its axes along the plate axes: E_z = a cos ωt along the optic axis and E_y = b sin ωt across it. A calcite plate delays the o-component (along y) by a further π/2:

1. E_y = b sin(ωt − π/2) = −b cos ωt.
2. The pair becomes (E_z, E_y) = (a, −b) cos ωt: **linear light** along a line of slope −b/a.
3. The analyser extinguishes it twice per turn, when its axis is perpendicular to that line.

For circular light a = b, so the output is linear at 45° to the plate axes. The extract states these results in words (PDF pp. 535 to 537).

**The condition the extract omits.** This only works if the plate axes coincide with the ellipse axes. For an ellipse tilted with respect to the plate, the extra 90° produces another ellipse, not a line. Practical rule (standard optics, verify): find the analyser angle for Imax in step 1; the major axis of the ellipse is along it; set the plate's axis there. For circular light there is no special direction, so any orientation works.

**Partially polarised light** is a mixture of a linear part and natural light. A plate can only reshape the linear part. The natural part (I_n) always contributes I_n/2 in every analyser direction, so Imin ≥ I_n/2 > 0, and no extinction occurs.

**Natural light** passes the plate unchanged in character: still constant intensity.

## Worked comparison

Elliptical light of intensity ratio 3 : 1 (from a quarter-wave plate with θ = 30°, module 9) and partially polarised light with I_p = I_n = 4 (Imax = 6, Imin = 2) look alike in step 1: both give Imax/Imin = 3, with P = 0.5 in the second case. In step 2, with the plate axis along the Imax direction, the first gives Imin = 0, the second still gives Imax/Imin = 3.

## Telling right from left

A rotating analyser cannot separate the senses either: both give constant intensity. A calcite quarter-wave plate (axes z, y) sends right-circular light (E_z = cos, E_y = sin) to linear at −45° and left-circular light to +45° (angle from the optic axis toward y). So noting which way the analyser must be set for extinction identifies the hand (module 9).

### 11. Master: the Babinet compensator

*Objective:* Describe the Babinet compensator, state why it works at any wavelength, and use d₁ − d₂ = λ/|Δμ| to relate band shift to path difference.

## The Babinet compensator

A **compensator** is a wave plate of variable thickness, for measuring unknown ellipses (Brijlal PDF pp. 538 to 541). The Babinet type has two quartz wedges with optic axes perpendicular to each other and to the beam; one wedge slides on a micrometer screw. The net path difference is Δ = (μ_e − μ_o)(d₁ − d₂), zero at the centre where d₁ = d₂, so dark bands appear between crossed polarisers wherever d₁ − d₂ is a whole number of wavelengths. It works at any wavelength, whereas a quarter-wave plate works for one. It gives the phase difference, axis direction and axis ratio of an elliptical beam.

## Numbers (recomputed)

For quartz (|μ_e − μ_o| = 0.009) at 589 nm, one wavelength of path difference needs d₁ − d₂ = 589 nm / 0.009 = 65.4 μm. A quarter-wave of path difference is 16.4 μm. Moving the wedge changes d₁ − d₂ smoothly, so the dark bands shift by one band for every 65.4 μm change and any phase difference can be dialled in; reading the band shift gives the ellipse's phase difference, and the analyser setting for extinction gives its axis direction.

### Notes: Definitions

- State of polarisation: the shape traced by the tip of the electric vector E, in the plane across the beam, at one point as time passes.
- Linear (plane) polarisation: the tip moves along a straight line. Circular: along a circle. Elliptical: along an ellipse. Partially polarised: a mixture of linear and natural light. Natural (unpolarised): no preferred direction.
- Coherent components keep a fixed phase difference; incoherent ones do not. Natural light behaves like two equal incoherent perpendicular components.
- Degree of polarisation P = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin); P = 1 for linear light, 0 for natural light; not defined for circular and elliptical light (Brijlal PDF p. 505).
- Optic axis: a direction in a uniaxial crystal along which o and e speeds are equal. Principal section: a plane containing the optic axis and perpendicular to a pair of faces.
- Positive crystal: μ_e > μ_o (quartz). Negative crystal: μ_e < μ_o (calcite). Birefringence is μ_e − μ_o.
- Quarter-wave plate: optic axis in the plane of the plate, thickness giving a path difference λ/4 (phase π/2) between o and e. Half-wave plate: λ/2 (phase π).
- Handedness (Brijlal Chapter 20): right-circular means the tip turns clockwise as seen by an observer looking back towards the source; Chapter 13 uses a right-hand rule with the thumb along the propagation, so the names can be opposite. State the convention.

### Notes: Equations to know

- Perpendicular components: E_y = E₁ cos(kx − ωt), E_z = E₂ cos(kx − ωt + δ) give E_y²/E₁² + E_z²/E₂² − 2 (E_y E_z/E₁E₂) cos(δ) = sin²(δ).
- Axis tilt: tan 2 α = 2 E₁ E₂ cos(δ)/(E₁² − E₂²). Turning sense: L = ω E₁ E₂ sin(δ) (positive = y toward z).
- Malus: I = I₁ cos²(θ). Natural light through one polariser: I = I₀/2.
- Brewster: tan(θ_B) = μ₂/μ₁, θ_B + r = 90 degrees; reflectance of s at θ_B: R_s = ((μ² − 1)/(μ² + 1))².
- Phase difference of a plate: δ = 2 π |μ_o − μ_e| d / λ. Quarter-wave: d = λ/(4|μ_o − μ_e|); half-wave: d = λ/(2|μ_o − μ_e|); order m: d = (4m + 1) λ/(4|μ_o − μ_e|) (standard, verify).
- Half-wave plate: input at θ to the axis leaves at -θ (rotation 2 θ). Quarter-wave plate: ellipse (E_z/E cos θ)² + (E_y/E sin θ)² = 1; θ = 45 degrees gives a circle.
- Phase from path: δ = 2 π Δ / λ.

### Notes: Derivation summary: ellipse of perpendicular vibrations

- Write φ = kx − ωt, E_y = E₁ cos(φ), E_z = E₂ cos(φ + δ). Expand and use cos(φ) = E_y/E1.
- Isolate sin(φ) sin(δ) = (E_y/E₁) cos(δ) − E_z/E₂; square it, using sin²(φ) = 1 − E_y²/E₁².
- Collect terms with cos²(δ) + sin²(δ) = 1 to get the general conic; B² − 4AC = −4 sin²(δ)/(E₁² E₂²) < 0 so it is an ellipse.
- δ = 0 or π: a line; δ = π/2 with E₁ = E₂: a circle; otherwise an ellipse. Sense: L = ω E₁ E₂ sin(δ).

- Step 1, analyser alone: two zeros means linear; constant means circular or natural; max and min with min not zero means elliptical or partial.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Polarised light versus unpolarised light with a polariser in front: a polariser turns natural light into linear light and halves it; it cannot change linear light's intensity except by Malus's law.
- Circular versus natural light: both give constant intensity through a rotating analyser; only a quarter-wave plate separates them.
- Elliptical versus partially polarised light: both give max and nonzero min; the plate separates them.
- Quarter-wave plate versus half-wave plate: π/2 gives circular or elliptical; π rotates the plane by 2 θ and reverses handedness.
- o-ray versus e-ray: the labels exist only inside the crystal; the e-ray does not follow Snell's law in general.
- Brewster angle versus critical angle: tan(θ_B) = μ₂/μ₁ exists for incidence from either side and picks out complete polarisation of the reflected beam; the critical angle sin(c) = μ₂/μ₁ exists only from the denser to the rarer medium and marks total internal reflection.

      - Quarter-wave plate: |Δ| = λ/4, δ = π/2, so d = λ/(4|μ_o − μ_e|). Half-wave plate: |Δ| = λ/2, δ = π, d = λ/(2|μ_o − μ_e|). Order m: d = (4m + 1) λ/(4|Δμ|) (standard).
      - Half-wave rotation: input at angle θ from the axis towards y. Components E cos θ along z and E sin θ along y, in phase.
      - After the plate the o-component gains π: E_y = E sin θ cos(ωt − π) = -E sin θ cos(ωt), E_z = E cos θ cos(ωt).
      - Resultant (E_z, E_y) = E cos(ωt) (cos θ, -sin θ): linear at -θ; the plane has turned through 2 θ.
      - Example: quartz at 589 nm gives d_QWP = 16.36 micrometres, d_HWP = 32.72 micrometres.
    - **checks:**
      - Units: λ and d in the same unit; δ is dimensionless.
      - Limit θ = 0 or 90 degrees: no rotation, as expected for light already along an axis.
      - Check: 2 π (0.009)(16.36 micrometres)/589 nm = 1.5707 rad = π/2.
    - **traps:**
      - Using (μ_o − μ_e) without absolute value for a positive crystal.
      - Using the half-wave formula for a quarter-wave plate (factor 2).
  - - **id:** PH-207-D4
    - **task:** Show how a polariser and a quarter-wave plate produce elliptical and circular light, and how the same plate with an analyser detects them (15 marks; practice target about 18 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Set-up: calcite-type plate (e fast), optic axis along z. Linear light of amplitude E at angle θ from z towards y falls on it.
      - Entry components in phase: E_z = E cos θ cos(ωt), E_y = E sin θ cos(ωt).
      - Exit: the o-component (y) is delayed by π/2: E_y = E sin θ sin(ωt).
      - Eliminate ωt: (E_z/(E cos θ))² + (E_y/(E sin θ))² = cos²(ωt) + sin²(ωt) = 1, an ellipse with semi-axes E cos θ (along the axis) and E sin θ.
      - θ = 45 degrees: equal semi-axes E/√2, a circle. Sense: E_z dE_y/dt − E_y dE_z/dt = ω E² sin θ cos θ > 0, so z towards y, clockwise to an observer looking back at the source: right in Brijlal's convention.
      - Detection: place the same plate in front of the analyser with its axis along an ellipse axis; ellipse E_z = a cos(ωt), E_y = b sin(ωt).
      - The plate delays y by a further π/2: E_y = -b cos(ωt). Resultant (a, -b) cos(ωt): linear, so the analyser has two zeros per turn.
      - Circular light (a = b) gives linear light at 45 degrees to the axes; a partially polarised beam retains its natural part, so its minimum is never zero.
    - **checks:**
      - Intensity is conserved: E² (cos² θ + sin² θ) = E².
      - Limits: θ = 0 or 90 degrees gives a line; θ = 45 degrees gives a circle.
      - Numerical: θ = 30 degrees gives intensity ratio 3 : 1 along the axes, and a numerical sampling confirms the clockwise sense.
    - **traps:**
      - Using a plate with the incident vibration along an axis and expecting circular light.
      - Claiming the plate straightens any ellipse without aligning its axes.
