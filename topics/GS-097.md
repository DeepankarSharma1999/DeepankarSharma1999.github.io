## GS-097: Plate tectonics and continental drift

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Geological time and slow rates: million years and centimetres a year

Tectonic processes are slow, so the numbers in this topic are large and the speeds are tiny. A **million years ago** (written Ma in many books) means one million years before today. 200 million years ago is 200 Ma. The earth is about 4,600 million years old (NCERT kegy102 p2), so 200 Ma is only a small part of its history.

A plate speed is given in **centimetres per year (cm/yr)**, about as fast as a fingernail grows. The trick is to convert it. There are 100 cm in a metre and 1,000 m in a km, so 100,000 cm make a km. One centimetre per year therefore means 1 cm x 1,000,000 years = 1,000,000 cm = 10 km in one million years. Use this rule: **1 cm/yr = 10 km per million years**.

Worked example. NCERT says the slowest ridge, the Arctic Ridge, spreads at less than 2.5 cm/yr and the fastest, the East Pacific Rise, at more than 15 cm/yr. At 2.5 cm/yr: 2.5 x 10 = 25 km per million years. At 15 cm/yr: 15 x 10 = 150 km per million years. Over 100 million years the slow one gives 2,500 km and the fast one 15,000 km (each is only a unit conversion, not a book figure).

Two more habits. First, **latitude** is the angle north or south of the equator; one degree of latitude is about 111 km on the ground. Second, when a map is described in words, say which way something moves *from where to where*. Both habits are used again in module 7.

### 1. Orientation: a moving surface in three steps

*Objective:* Say in one minute how the idea of drifting continents grew into plate tectonics, and name the one thing that really moves.

## The picture first

Continents cover about 29 per cent of the earth's surface and oceans the rest (NCERT kegy204 p1). On a world map their positions look fixed. They are not. NCERT's opening claim is that the positions of continents and oceans were different in the past and will be different in the future.

The story of how scientists learned this has **three steps**. Each step fixed a weakness of the one before.

1. **Continental drift (1912).** Alfred Wegener argued that the continents were once joined and have drifted apart. His evidence was good; his proposed force was not.
2. **Sea-floor spreading (1961).** After the Second World War, mapping of the ocean floor showed new crust being made at mid-ocean ridges. Harry Hess proposed that the sea floor spreads and is destroyed again at trenches.
3. **Plate tectonics (1967).** The earth's rigid outer shell is cut into plates that move slowly over a softer layer. This joined the first two ideas and is the modern theory.

## The one correction to remember

Wegener thought the *continents* move through the ocean floor. Plate tectonics says the continents are *passengers*: **the plate moves, and the continent is a part of the plate** (NCERT p7). The Pacific plate is mostly ocean; the Eurasian plate is mostly continent.

## Why it matters for the examination

The same machinery explains where earthquake and volcano belts run, why the longest mountain chain is under the ocean, why the Himalaya is young and still rising, and why ocean crust is younger than continental rock.

## Link to the previous lesson

Plates are made of the **lithosphere**, which is the crust plus the rigid top of the mantle. The soft layer beneath is the **asthenosphere**. Both were defined in Day 18 lesson GS-096 (section 'Core: crust, mantle and core with their boundaries and figures'). This lesson does not re-teach the earth's interior.

### 2. Foundation: the words, and the ocean floor in three parts

*Objective:* Define the ten terms the rest of the lesson uses and name the three divisions of the ocean floor.

## Ten words to fix first

- **Plate (tectonic or lithospheric plate)**: a massive, irregularly shaped slab of solid rock, usually made of both continental and oceanic lithosphere, that moves horizontally as a rigid unit over the asthenosphere (NCERT p6).
- **Oceanic crust and continental crust**: the thin dense crust under the oceans and the thicker lighter crust of the continents. Thicknesses are in Day 18 lesson GS-096.
- **Mid-oceanic ridge**: an interconnected chain of submerged mountains; the longest mountain chain on earth, with a central **rift** at its crest where volcanic activity is intense (NCERT p3).
- **Trench (deep-oceanic trench)**: a very deep, narrow furrow on the ocean floor, mostly close to the continental margins. Plates sink here.
- **Subduction**: sinking of one plate under another. The place is a **subduction zone** (NCERT p7).
- **Spreading site**: a place where plates move apart.
- **Fold mountains**: mountains made by crustal layers being squeezed into folds (Leong p36). The young ones (Alps, Himalayas, Andes, Rockies) are the highest.
- **Convection cell**: a loop in which hot material rises, spreads, cools and sinks (NCERT p7).
- **Palaeomagnetism**: the magnetic direction recorded in rocks when they cooled. It lets scientists read the past positions of continents.
- **Radiometric dating**: finding a rock's age from the steady decay of radioactive elements in it.

## Three more words from the evidence

**Tillite**: sedimentary rock made of glacier deposits. **Placer deposit**: heavy mineral, such as gold, concentrated by water. **Fossil**: preserved remains of an ancient living thing.

## The ocean floor in three parts

NCERT p3 divides the floor by depth and relief into **continental margins, deep-sea basins and mid-ocean ridges**.

| Part | What it is | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| Continental margins | The transition between shore and deep sea: shelf, slope, rise and the deep trenches | The trenches show where plates sink |
| Deep-sea basins (abyssal plains) | Extensive plains between the margins and the ridges where continental sediments settle | Sediment here is thin, which surprised scientists |
| Mid-ocean ridges | The submerged mountain chain with a central rift | Where new crust is made |

The details of ocean-floor relief come in a later ocean lesson; here only the three parts matter.

## Two mix-ups to avoid

First, **lithosphere is not the same as crust**: in NCERT the lithosphere is the crust plus the top of the mantle, 5 to 100 km thick under oceans and about 200 km under continents (kegy204's figures; Day 18 lesson GS-096, section 'Core: crust, mantle and core with their boundaries and figures', quotes 10-200 km from another NCERT chapter). Second, the word **continent** and the word **plate** are not interchangeable. A plate can carry both a continent and an ocean floor.

### 3. Core: continental drift and its five evidences

*Objective:* State Wegener's hypothesis, list the five NCERT evidences with their data, and say why the hypothesis was not accepted.

## The hypothesis

The Dutch map maker Abraham Ortelius first noticed, as early as 1596, that the coasts of the Atlantic fit together; Antonio Pellegrini drew a map showing the continents joined. But the comprehensive argument came from **Alfred Wegener**, a German meteorologist, in **1912** (NCERT p1). (Leong p29 says 'nineteenth century'; that is a slip, 1912 is the twentieth.)

Wegener held that all the continents once formed one mass, **Pangaea** ('all earth'), surrounded by one ocean, **Panthalassa** ('all water'). About **200 million years ago** Pangaea began to split into two: **Laurasia** (north) and **Gondwanaland** (south). These then broke into the continents we see.

## The five evidences (NCERT pp1-2)

1. **Jig-saw fit.** The coasts of Africa and South America match. Bullard in 1964 used a computer to find the best fit and it was 'quite perfect'. The fit was tried not at the present shoreline but at the **1,000-fathom line** (a fathom is 1.83 m, so that is about 1,830 m depth), because the true edge of a continent is under water.
2. **Rocks of the same age across the ocean.** Radiometric dating shows a belt of ancient rocks of about **2,000 million years** on the Brazil coast matches rocks in **western Africa**. The earliest marine deposits on the coasts of South America and Africa are of the **Jurassic** age, which suggests the Atlantic did not exist before then.
3. **Tillite.** The **Gondwana system** of sediments in India has a base of thick tillite, showing long and widespread glaciation, and has counterparts in six southern landmasses by NCERT's count, but NCERT names only five: Africa, Falkland Island, Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia. Standard accounts add South America (verify). The 2025 Prelims statement III repeats NCERT's six. Similar histories mean the lands were once together; tillite also records old climates.
4. **Placer deposits.** The Ghana coast has rich placer gold but no source rock nearby. The gold-bearing veins are in **Brazil**, so the gold came from the Brazil plateau when the two continents lay side by side.
5. **Fossils.** Identical land or freshwater species on both sides of a sea need explaining. The small reptile **Mesosaurus**, adapted to shallow brackish water, is found only in the Southern Cape province of South Africa and in Brazil, now **4,800 km** apart.

## The force, and why drift was set aside

Wegener proposed the **pole-fleeing force** (from the earth's rotation, which makes the equator bulge) and the **tidal force** (from the moon and sun). He believed they would work when applied over millions of years. Most scholars thought both **totally inadequate** (NCERT p2). The evidence was accepted by some; the engine was not.

## Limits of the evidence

All five come from the **continents**. They show that lands were once close but not how they moved or why. That gap was filled by the ocean floor (module 4).

Africa and South America as two jigsaw pieces with arrows labelling the four evidence spots: the Brazil to West Africa belt, the Ghana to Brazil gold, the Mesosaurus localities, and the tillite regions of the south.

### 4. Core: what the ocean floor revealed, and sea-floor spreading

*Objective:* List the five post-war ocean-floor facts, explain Hess's sea-floor spreading and say why crust must be consumed somewhere.

## Why the story restarted

For continental drift almost all the evidence came from the continents. After the Second World War, expeditions mapped the ocean floor and palaeomagnetic studies were made on oceanic rocks. They showed that the floor is full of relief: submerged mountain ranges and deep trenches (NCERT p2-3). New facts followed.

## The five facts (NCERT p4)

1. **Ridge volcanism.** Volcanic eruptions are common all along the mid-oceanic ridges and bring huge amounts of lava to the surface.
2. **Symmetry.** Rocks at equal distances on either side of the crest are alike in age, chemistry and magnetic properties. The age **increases with distance from the crest**; rocks closest to the ridge are the youngest. The magnetic stripes alternate between normal and reverse polarity and mirror each other across the crest. (NCERT p4: the rocks at the crest have normal polarity because they formed in the present magnetic field, which is normal; going outwards the strips alternate reverse, normal, reverse, mirrored on both sides, NCERT p7.)
3. **Young ocean crust.** Oceanic crust rocks are nowhere more than about **200 million years** old, whereas some continental rock formations are as old as about **3,200 million years**.
4. **Thin sediments.** If ocean floors were as old as the continents they should carry a long sediment record. The sediment column is thin and nowhere older than 200 million years.
5. **Earthquake depth.** Deep trenches have deep-seated earthquakes; the ridge areas have shallow foci.

## Hess's idea (1961)

**Harry Hess** reasoned from these facts. Constant eruptions at the crest rupture the oceanic crust and new lava wedges in, **pushing the older crust on either side**. The sea floor spreads. But the earth is not growing, and spreading in one ocean does not shrink another, so crust must be destroyed somewhere. Hess argued that the floor pushed away from the crest **sinks at the oceanic trenches and is consumed** (NCERT pp4-5).

The diagram below shows the cross-section: youngest crust at the crest, older crust outwards, sinking at the trenches.

## Holmes's convection idea

In the 1930s **Arthur Holmes** suggested that radioactive heat makes convection currents in the mantle. This was an early answer to the 'force' problem that had sunk Wegener (NCERT p2). Module 9 returns to it.

## Rates of spreading

The strips of normal and reverse magnetic field parallel to the ridges are also used to measure speed. The slowest is the Arctic Ridge (under 2.5 cm/yr) and the fastest the East Pacific Rise near Easter Island (over 15 cm/yr), which is about 3,400 km west of Chile (NCERT p7). In the unit rule of bridge GS-097-B1, 1 cm/yr is 10 km per million years.

### 5. Core: the plate model and the named plates

*Objective:* Define a plate, name the seven major and five minor plates NCERT lists with their positions, and explain what bounds a plate.

## From spreading to plates

With sea-floor spreading, interest in the distribution of oceans and continents revived. In **1967**, McKenzie and Parker, and independently Morgan, combined the available ideas into **plate tectonics** (NCERT p5). The lithosphere is broken into rigid slabs that move horizontally over the asthenosphere.

## What a plate is

A plate may carry continental crust, oceanic crust or both. A plate is called **continental** or **oceanic** according to which occupies the larger part: the Pacific plate is largely oceanic; the Eurasian plate is called a continental plate. The lithosphere in a plate is **5 to 100 km** thick in oceanic parts and about **200 km** in continental parts (NCERT p6).

The major plates are bounded by **young fold mountain ridges, trenches and/or faults**.

## The seven major plates (NCERT p6)

| No. | Plate (as NCERT describes it) |
|---|---|
| I | Antarctica and the surrounding oceanic plate |
| II | North American plate (with the western Atlantic floor, separated from the South American plate along the Caribbean islands) |
| III | South American plate (with the western Atlantic floor) |
| IV | Pacific plate |
| V | India-Australia-New Zealand plate |
| VI | Africa with the eastern Atlantic floor |
| VII | Eurasia and the adjacent oceanic plate |

## Five important minor plates (NCERT pp6-7)

- **Cocos**: between Central America and the Pacific plate.
- **Nazca**: between South America and the Pacific plate.
- **Arabian**: mostly the Saudi Arabian landmass.
- **Philippine**: between the Asiatic and Pacific plates.
- **Caroline**: between the Philippine and Indian plates, north of New Guinea.

Antarctica is therefore a **major** plate, not a minor one; this is a standard NCERT exercise trap.

## What moves, and for how long

All plates have moved in the geological past and will continue to move; Pangaea itself was a result of continental masses, each on a plate, converging earlier. Past positions are worked out from palaeomagnetic data (NCERT p7; the Indian subcontinent was traced from rocks near Nagpur).

## Reading a plate map in words

When you describe a figure in an answer, say which plate lies on which side. Example: along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge the American plate(s) lie to the west, the Eurasian and African plates to the east (NCERT p7). The Pacific plate is bordered on the north-east by the North American plate; further south-east the Cocos and Nazca plates separate it from Central and South America; on the west lie the Philippine plate, the Eurasian (Asiatic) plate and the India-Australia-New Zealand plate; on the south the Antarctic plate (map wording from NCERT's lists; positions standard, verify).

### 6. Core: the three kinds of plate boundary

*Objective:* Define divergent, convergent (three forms) and transform boundaries, say whether crust is created, destroyed or neither, and give an example and a typical feature for each.

## The rule that sorts every boundary

Ask two questions of any pair of neighbouring plates: **are they moving apart, together or sideways, and is crust made, destroyed or neither?** NCERT p7 gives three types.

## 1. Divergent boundary

The plates **pull away** from each other and **new crust is generated**. The sites are called **spreading sites**. NCERT's best-known example is the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**, where the American plate(s) are separated from the Eurasian and African plates. On land, the East African Rift Valley (Leong: a system about 4,800 km long from East Africa through the Red Sea towards Syria, formed by tension) is a rift of the same kind (this link is standard knowledge, verify). NCERT traces a spreading site from the Red Sea rift south-eastwards past the Chagos Archipelago, which the Indian plate's western margin joins (module 7). The Red Sea rift itself lies between the African and Arabian plates (standard; verify).

## 2. Convergent boundary

One plate **dives under another** and **crust is destroyed**. The place where the sinking happens is a **subduction zone**. There are three ways:

- **Ocean-continent.** An oceanic plate sinks beneath a continental plate. Standard outcome (verify; NCERT lists the type only): a deep trench and a belt of volcanic fold mountains on the continental edge. The Andes, which Leong lists among the Circum-Pacific fold mountains with many active volcanoes (p36-37), are the classic case.
- **Ocean-ocean.** One oceanic plate sinks beneath another. Outcome (standard, verify): a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, an **island arc**. Standard examples (verify): the Mariana, Aleutian and Tonga trench-arc systems. NCERT names the island arc along the Java Trench at the Indian plate's eastern edge but not its crust pair.
- **Continent-continent.** Both plates carry continental crust, which is too light to sink easily, so the colliding margins are crumpled and thrust up into a great fold-mountain belt. NCERT's example: the Indian plate along the **Himalayas** (continent-continent convergence). Leong notes the Himalayas have 'surprisingly' no active volcano; the reason is that there is no ocean plate sinking to melt, which is an inference here, not a book sentence.

## 3. Transform boundary

The plates **slide horizontally past each other**; crust is **neither produced nor destroyed**. Transform faults are the planes of separation, generally **perpendicular to the mid-oceanic ridges**, and offset the ridge into segments; NCERT links them to eruptions not occurring along the whole crest at the same time (p7). The San Andreas Fault of California is the standard continental example (not in the extracts; verify).

## One-line memory frame

Trap: attach the verb to the type, **diverge, create; converge, destroy; transform, neither**. Exception: in continent-continent convergence little crust is destroyed; it is crumpled, thickened and uplifted (Himalaya). The ocean floor between the continents (Tethys) was consumed earlier by subduction.

### 7. Core: the Indian plate, its boundaries and the rise of the Himalaya

*Objective:* Describe the Indian plate's boundaries in the four directions, give NCERT's chronology of its journey, and link the collision to the Himalaya.

## The plate and its four boundaries (NCERT kegy204 p8)

The Indian plate includes Peninsular India and the Australian continental portion (kegy204; but see the variant below). Go round it in words:

- **North.** A **subduction zone along the Himalayas** forms the northern boundary: a **continent-continent convergence**.
- **East.** The boundary runs through the **Rakinyoma Mountains** of Myanmar (NCERT's spelling) towards the island arc along the **Java Trench**. Further east, NCERT places a spreading site **east of Australia** as an oceanic ridge in the south-west Pacific (as NCERT states; standard maps show trenches and New Zealand's Alpine transform fault; verify).
- **West.** It follows the **Kirthar Mountain** of Pakistan, then the **Makrana coast** (NCERT's spelling; the Makran coast), and joins the spreading site running from the **Red Sea rift** south-eastwards along the **Chagos Archipelago**. NCERT names no type; standard maps show it mainly as a sliding (transform) margin, the Chaman fault on land and the Owen fracture zone at sea (verify).
- **South.** The boundary with the **Antarctic plate** is an oceanic ridge, a divergent boundary, running roughly west to east and merging into the spreading site a little south of New Zealand.

**Variant.** kegy102 p2 says the Indian plate was once much larger, with the Australian plate part of it, and that the Australian plate later moved towards the south-east while the Indian plate moved north. kegy204's major-plate list prints one India-Australia-New Zealand plate. Both are reported; for a list question follow the list.

From 50 degrees south to 23.5 degrees north is 73.5 degrees of latitude, about 8,170 km; over 140 million years that is about 58 km per million years, **about 5.8 cm/yr**, inside NCERT's 2.5 to 15 cm/yr range.

## The Himalaya in structure

kegy102 p3 calls the Himalayas **young, weak and flexible**, still shaped by faults, folds and thrust plains, unlike the rigid Peninsular Block (block faulting; rift valleys of Narmada, Tapi and Mahanadi). The Indo-Ganga-Brahmaputra plain was a depression that reached its maximum development about 64 million years ago and has been filled with sediments. Do not mix this 64 Ma marker with the collision's 40-50 Ma or Leong's Alpine orogeny at about 30 Ma (p35).

### 8. Core: the world pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes as evidence

*Objective:* Describe the three belts of earthquake and volcano concentration in words and show that they trace the plate boundaries.

## What NCERT's map shows (kegy204 p3)

On the map of earthquakes and volcanoes you can read two patterns.

**Pattern 1: a line of dots along the ridges.** In the central Atlantic there is a line of dots roughly parallel to the coasts; it continues into the Indian Ocean and **splits a little south of the Indian subcontinent**: one branch goes into East Africa, the other meets a similar line running from Myanmar to New Guinea (NCERT's 'New Guiana'). This line **coincides with the mid-oceanic ridges**.

**Pattern 2: a shaded belt** that coincides with the **Alpine-Himalayan system** and the **rim of the Pacific Ocean**. The Pacific rim is called the **Rim of Fire** because of its active volcanoes.

## Depth of focus (NCERT p3)

The **focus** is the point underground where an earthquake begins. Along the mid-oceanic ridges foci are at shallow depths; along the Alpine-Himalayan belt and the Pacific rim the earthquakes are deep-seated. The deep trenches also have deep-seated earthquakes (p4). This is an observed fact that also tests sea-floor spreading: shallow at spreading ridges, deep where plates dive.

## What Leong adds (pp52-55)

- Volcanoes follow regions of folding or faulting: coastal ranges, offshore islands and mid-ocean, with few in continental interiors.
- The Circum-Pacific chain runs from the Aleutian Islands into Kamchatka, Japan, the Philippines and Indonesia (Java and Sumatra), then south to the Solomon Islands, New Hebrides, Tonga and North Island of New Zealand; on the other side it continues from the Andes through Central America and Mexico to Alaska.
- The Atlantic coasts have few active volcanoes, but Iceland and the Azores are active; the Mediterranean volcanoes (Vesuvius, Etna, Stromboli) go with the Alpine folds; the Himalayas have no active volcano.
- Earthquake distribution coincides closely with that of volcanoes. Book estimates (verify): about two-thirds of volcanoes and 70 per cent of earthquakes in the Circum-Pacific belt, 20 per cent of earthquakes in the Mediterranean-Himalayan belt. The printed 'almost 3200 km' chain length is too small: check, do not memorise.

## Why this is evidence

Quakes and volcanoes are **concentrated in narrow belts**, not scattered, and the belts match the places where plates diverge (ridges), converge (trenches and fold mountains) and slide (faults). The map tests plate tectonics without needing Wegener's continents.

## Mountain alignment (supports the 2021 Mains question)

Leong's map of the earth's structure (Fig. 2.8, p36) divides land by folding age: Pre-Cambrian shields, then Caledonian, Hercynian and Alpine fold belts. The loftiest ranges, the young **Alpine** folds, include the Alps, Himalayas, Andes and Rockies (p35). Standard alignment (verify): the Rockies and Andes run north-south along the Pacific rim of the Americas; the Alps and Himalaya run broadly east-west across Eurasia. Weather effects (barriers, rain shadow) are standard ideas, verify; four worked examples are in the master notes.

### 9. Master: what drives the plates, and hotspots and mantle plumes

*Objective:* Explain the driving force as NCERT presents it, and define a hotspot and a mantle plume with the caution they need.

## The force problem, solved in outline

Wegener's pole-fleeing and tidal forces were judged inadequate (module 3). The modern answer in NCERT p7 is the **convection cell**: the mobile rock beneath the rigid plates is believed to move in a circle. Heated material rises, spreads and cools, then sinks to depth and the cycle repeats. The earth's heat comes from two main sources, **radioactive decay** and **residual heat**. Arthur Holmes first suggested this in the 1930s and it influenced Hess. NCERT concludes that the **slow movement of hot, softened mantle below the rigid plates is the driving force**. Leong p36 lists 'flow movements in the mantle' among the stresses that can fold mountains. The physics idea is the same as heat transport by a rising and sinking fluid; you need only the picture here.

**Caution (standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify).** Later textbooks add that the sinking weight of a cold plate at a trench ('slab pull') and the push from the elevated ridge ('ridge push') help move plates. Treat these as extensions to the NCERT picture, and in an NCERT-based answer put convection first.

## Hotspots and mantle plumes (needed for the 2018 Mains question)

The extracts do not define these; the following is standard knowledge to verify. See Day 18 lesson GS-096, section 'Core: volcanoes and magma as messengers from the mantle'.

- A **mantle plume** is a narrow, hot column of rock rising from deep in the mantle. At the surface the place it reaches is a **hotspot**, a volcanic area that stays put while the plate drifts over it.
- As the plate moves, one volcano after another forms, so a **chain of volcanoes** appears, youngest above the plume and older along the plate's direction of motion. Hawaii in the Pacific is the standard example.
- Role in plate tectonics: hotspot tracks work like a fixed reference that **records plate motion** (direction and rate); a large plume head can cause huge lava outpourings. The **Deccan Traps** have been linked to such an event as India moved north (NCERT gives the outpouring about 60 million years ago; the link to the Reunion plume is standard knowledge, verify).
- Contrast: most volcanoes sit on plate boundaries (module 8); plume volcanoes can sit in the middle of a plate. Leong's volcanoes 'in the midst of oceans' (p52) include both ridge volcanoes (Iceland, Azores) and mid-plate ones such as Hawaii (Mauna Loa, Leong's Fig. 3.12).

## Limits of the theory worth stating in a Mains answer

Plume theory itself is debated (verify). The exact balance of forces and the depth of the flow are less certain, so say 'believed' as NCERT does. Past motion rests on indirect data (magnetic strips, rock ages), and ocean crust older than about 200 million years has been consumed.

## A figure worth drawing

A hotspot diagram: a plate moving left to right over a fixed plume, a row of volcano cones with ages increasing along the direction of motion, and an arrow showing the plume rising from the mantle.

## Traps to carry into every question

- **Plate versus continent**: it is the plate that moves.
- **Antarctica is a major plate**; Nazca, Cocos, Arabian, Philippine and Caroline are the minor ones listed.
- Say 'believed' for convection and mark standard-knowledge additions with 'verify'.

## Answer-writing tip

Definition first, mechanism in the middle, a map or Indian example near the end; one labelled figure saves words.

### Notes: Core terms

- Continental drift: Wegener's 1912 hypothesis that the continents were once one mass (Pangaea) and have drifted apart (NCERT kegy204 p1).
- Sea-floor spreading: Hess's 1961 hypothesis that new oceanic crust forms at the crest of mid-oceanic ridges, spreads outwards and sinks at trenches (kegy204 pp4-5).
- Plate (lithospheric plate): a massive, irregular slab of solid rock, usually of both continental and oceanic lithosphere, moving horizontally as a rigid unit over the asthenosphere (kegy204 p6).
- Subduction zone: the location where one plate sinks beneath another (kegy204 p7).
- Spreading site: a place where plates move away from each other (kegy204 p7).
- Transform fault: a plane of separation, generally perpendicular to the mid-oceanic ridges, along which plates slide past (kegy204 p7).
- Tillite: sedimentary rock formed from glacier deposits (kegy204 p2). Placer deposit: a mineral concentration carried and dropped by water (here gold, Ghana coast).
- Convection cell: a circuit in which heated material rises, spreads, cools and sinks (kegy204 p7).

### Notes: Chronology: from Ortelius to the Himalaya

- 1596: Abraham Ortelius first proposes that the Americas, Europe and Africa were joined (kegy204 p1).
- 1912: Alfred Wegener's continental drift theory, with Pangaea and Panthalassa (kegy204 p1).
- About 200 million years ago: Pangaea begins to split into Laurasia and Gondwanaland (kegy204 p1); India starts moving north (p8).
- 1930s: Arthur Holmes proposes mantle convection currents (kegy204 p2).
- After the Second World War: ocean-floor mapping and palaeomagnetic studies (kegy204 pp2-4).
- 1961: Harry Hess proposes sea-floor spreading (kegy204 p4). 1964: Bullard's computer fit of the Atlantic margin (kegy204 p1).
- 1967: McKenzie and Parker, and independently Morgan, formulate plate tectonics (kegy204 p5).
- About 140 million years ago: Indian subcontinent as far south as 50 degrees south (kegy204 p8).
- About 60 million years ago: Deccan Traps outpouring begins (kegy204 p8).
- 40-50 million years ago: India collides with Asia; the Himalayas are still rising (kegy204 p8).

### Notes: Numbers to remember

- Continents cover 29 per cent of the earth's surface (kegy204 p1).
- Oceanic crust is nowhere more than about 200 million years old; some continental rocks are about 3,200 million years old (kegy204 p4).
- Lithosphere: 5 to 100 km oceanic, about 200 km continental (kegy204 p6).
- Seven major plates and five listed minor plates (kegy204 pp6-7).
- Spreading rates: slowest Arctic Ridge under 2.5 cm/yr; fastest East Pacific Rise over 15 cm/yr, about 3,400 km west of Chile (kegy204 p7).
- 1 cm/yr = 10 km per million years (derived, bridge GS-097-B1).
- East African Rift system about 4,800 km long (Leong p38); Mesosaurus localities 4,800 km apart (kegy204 p2): two different 4,800 km figures, do not confuse.

### Notes: Indian plate boundaries (words for a map)

- North: subduction zone along the Himalayas, continent-continent convergence.
- East: Rakinyoma Mountains of Myanmar towards the island arc along the Java Trench; further east a spreading site east of Australia (oceanic ridge, south-west Pacific).
- West: Kirthar Mountain of Pakistan, along the Makrana (Makran) coast, joining the spreading site from the Red Sea rift south-eastwards along the Chagos Archipelago.
- South: oceanic ridge (divergent boundary) with the Antarctic plate, running roughly west to east and merging into the spreading site a little south of New Zealand.
- Direction of motion: the plate has moved northwards since about 200 million years ago and is still moving north towards Eurasia.

### Notes: Easily confused pairs

- Continental drift versus plate tectonics: continents move versus plates move.
- Lithosphere versus crust: NCERT lithosphere = crust plus top of mantle; Leong uses lithosphere in a narrower sense in places.
- Laurasia versus Gondwanaland: Laurasia is the northern part; Gondwanaland the southern part of Pangaea.
- Pangaea versus Panthalassa: the land versus the ocean.
- Gondwanaland (supercontinent) versus Gondwana system (the sediment sequence with tillite at the base).
- Cocos plate (between Central America and the Pacific plate) versus Nazca plate (between South America and the Pacific plate).
- Spreading site versus subduction zone: where crust is made versus where it sinks.

### Notes: Worked example: reading a plate-map statement

- Statement: 'The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a convergent boundary where the American plate subducts under Africa.' Test: the ridge is a spreading site, a divergent boundary, where crust is created and the American plate(s) separate from the Eurasian and African plates (kegy204 p7). The statement is wrong on the type and on subduction.
- Statement: 'The northern boundary of the Indian plate along the Himalayas is a continent-continent convergence.' Test: matches kegy204 p8. Correct.
- Statement: 'Transform faults are generally perpendicular to the mid-oceanic ridges.' Test: matches kegy204 p7. Correct.
- Statement: 'Crust older than 500 million years is common on the ocean floor.' Test: oceanic crust is nowhere more than about 200 million years old (kegy204 p4). Wrong.

### Notes: Mountain alignment and local weather: worked examples (2021 Mains)

- Standard examples, all to verify. Andes: wet windward Chilean south against the dry Patagonian lee; the Atacama lies on the dry coast.
- Rockies: a north-south barrier, with the Chinook wind and the dry Great Basin on the lee; with no east-west barrier, polar and Gulf air meet over the plains (Day 19 lesson GS-104, section on the twister question).
- Alps: east-west, so Atlantic westerlies reach deep into Europe; the Foehn blows on the lee.
- Himalaya: an east-west wall that blocks cold Central Asian air and holds the monsoon rain on the Indian side.

### Core concepts

- Drift evidence came from continents; spreading evidence came from the ocean floor; plates joined the two.
- Crust is created at divergent boundaries, destroyed at convergent boundaries and neither at transform boundaries.
- The plate moves; the continent is a passenger.
- The Indian plate moved north and its collision with Asia raised the Himalaya, which is still rising.
- Earthquakes and volcanoes cluster along ridges, trenches, fold-mountain belts and the Pacific rim.
- Convection in the mantle is the book's driving force; hotspots and plumes are standard-knowledge additions.

### Practice

**Q.** Who first proposed, in 1596, that the Americas, Europe and Africa had once been joined, and who put forward the comprehensive continental drift theory in 1912?

**A.** Abraham Ortelius (Dutch map maker) in 1596; Alfred Wegener (German meteorologist) in 1912.

**Q.** List the seven major plates of NCERT.

**A.** Antarctica with the surrounding oceanic plate; North American; South American; Pacific; India-Australia-New Zealand; Africa with the eastern Atlantic floor; Eurasia with the adjacent oceanic plate.

**Q.** What are the three kinds of plate boundary and what happens to crust at each?

**A.** Divergent (created), convergent (destroyed at a subduction zone) and transform (neither).

**Q.** Name the force NCERT calls the driving force of plate movement.

**A.** The slow movement of hot, softened mantle (convection) beneath the rigid plates.

**Q.** Why did continental drift fail to win acceptance even though its evidence from the continents was strong?

**A.** Its proposed forces, pole-fleeing and tidal, were judged totally inadequate to move continents; there was no mechanism, and the ocean floor was not yet mapped.

**Q.** How do the youth of oceanic crust and the thinness of ocean sediment support sea-floor spreading?

**A.** If the floor were as old as the continents it would carry a long sediment record; crust nowhere older than about 200 million years and thin sediment mean the floor is renewed and old floor is consumed at trenches.

**Q.** Why does a continent-continent collision build a mountain belt rather than a trench with volcanoes?

**A.** Both plates carry light continental crust, so neither sinks easily; the margins are crumpled and thrust up (the Himalaya). Leong notes the Himalayas have no active volcano (the reason given is an inference).

**Q.** What does the shallow-to-deep pattern of earthquake foci tell us about plate boundaries?

**A.** Shallow foci at mid-oceanic ridges mark spreading sites; deep-seated foci at trenches, the Pacific rim and the Alpine-Himalayan belt mark places where plates dive.

**Q.** A plate sketch shows two plates with arrows pointing away from a ridge crest. Name the boundary, the crust verb, the rock-age pattern and an NCERT example.
