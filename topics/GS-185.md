## GS-185: Constitutional offices and appointment process

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Union government

### Bridge: Constitutional, statutory and executive bodies

A public body gets its authority from one of three sources, and the source decides how easily the government of the day can change or abolish it.

1. **Constitutional body.** It is created directly by an Article of the Constitution. To change its basic features Parliament must amend the Constitution, a much harder process than passing an ordinary law. Examples: the Election Commission (Article 324), the Union Public Service Commission (Article 315), the Finance Commission (Article 280), the CAG (Article 148).
2. **Statutory body.** It is created by an **Act** of Parliament (or of a State legislature). Parliament can amend or repeal the Act by ordinary law. Laxmikanth's examples: the National Commission for Women (1992), the National Commission for Minorities (1993), the National Human Rights Commission (1993) and the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (2007).
3. **Executive (non-statutory) body.** It is created by a resolution or order of the government. The Central Vigilance Commission began in 1964 by an executive resolution and was given statutory status by the CVC Act, 2003 (Laxmikanth: October 2003; verify).

Some bodies change category. The National Commission for Backward Classes began as a statutory body in 1993 and became constitutional in 2018 (module 6). Note also that a **constitutional office** (such as the Attorney General) is a single post, while a **constitutional body** (such as the Election Commission) is an institution; exams use both words loosely, so read the Article.

### Bridge: Reading a removal rule: pleasure, 'like a Supreme Court judge' and the Supreme Court's inquiry

Every constitutional office has some rule about how its holder leaves. Four patterns recur.

1. **At pleasure.** The Constitution says the holder serves 'during the pleasure' of the appointing authority (President or Governor). It means the holder can be removed at any time and no procedure or grounds are laid down. Examples: the Attorney General and the Advocate General (module 7).
2. **Same manner and grounds as a Supreme Court judge.** The holder can be removed by the President only on an **address (resolution) of both Houses of Parliament passed by a special majority** (a majority of the total membership of each House and at least two-thirds of those present and voting), on **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**. Examples: the Chief Election Commissioner and the CAG. This is the strongest protection. (The judges' own removal is in GS-186.)
3. **Removal by the President on stated grounds, with a Supreme Court inquiry for misbehaviour.** Used for the chairman and members of the UPSC and the State Public Service Commissions (module 4).
4. **Constitution silent.** The Constitution does not specify the procedure or term; in practice the appointing authority controls. Example: the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (module 6).

Two more words. A **warrant under hand and seal** is the formal written order by which the President appoints some office-holders (the CAG, and the members of the national commissions). **Charged on the Consolidated Fund** means an expense is paid directly from the government's main account and is not put to the annual vote of Parliament (GS-184 bridge module 2).

### 1. Orientation: what a constitutional office is and the four questions to ask of every one

*Objective:* Say why the Constitution creates certain offices and bodies directly, list the main ones with their Articles, and learn the four questions that organise this lesson.

## Why some offices are in the Constitution

Most public bodies exist because a government created them and a government can change them. A few jobs are too important for that: the body that runs elections, the body that recruits civil servants, the auditor of public money, the adviser on how tax money is shared. If the government of the day could dismiss or reshape them, they could not check it fairly. The framers therefore placed them in the Constitution, with Articles fixing how they are appointed, how long they serve and how they can be removed. Laxmikanth calls the CAG, the Supreme Court, the Election Commission and the UPSC the 'bulwarks' of the democratic system.

## The main offices and the Articles that create them

From Laxmikanth's Table 53.2 and the individual chapters: Attorney General (Article 76); CAG (148); Advocate General (165); State Finance Commission (243-I) and State Election Commission (243-K), which are met in the local-government lessons; Inter-State Council (263); GST Council (279-A); Finance Commission (280); UPSC and SPSC (315); Election Commission (324); National Commissions for SCs (338), STs (338-A) and Backward Classes (338-B); the commissions on Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes (339), Backward Classes (340) and Official Language (344); and the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (350-B). Many of these were not in the original Constitution but were added by amendment.

## The four questions

For every office or body, ask:

1. **Who appoints?** (the President, or the Governor)
2. **For how long?** (a fixed term, an age limit, or no fixed term)
3. **How is the holder removed, and by whom?** (use the four patterns in bridge module B2)
4. **What protects independence?** (security of tenure, service conditions that cannot be worsened, expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund, and a bar on later government jobs)

Two more questions turn facts into marks: what the body **does**, and whether its **advice or recommendation is binding**.

### 2. The Election Commission I: Article 324, composition, tenure and removal

*Objective:* State what the Election Commission controls, how it is composed and how its members hold office and can be removed, as printed in Laxmikanth, and note where the position changed.

## What it is

The **Election Commission** is a permanent and independent body established by the Constitution to ensure free and fair elections. **Article 324** vests the power of superintendence, direction and control of elections to **Parliament, State legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President** in it. It is an all-India body, common to the Centre and the States. It does **not** run elections to panchayats and municipalities, for which the 73rd and 74th Amendments created separate State Election Commissions.

## Composition

Under Article 324: the Commission consists of the **Chief Election Commissioner (CEC)** and such number of other Election Commissioners as the **President** fixes from time to time; the President appoints them; when other Commissioners are appointed, the CEC is the chairman; the President may appoint regional commissioners after consulting the Commission; and the conditions of service and tenure are determined by the President (Article 324(2) and 324(5) both carry the words 'subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament', which matters after 2023; verify the exact text).

The history shows how flexible this is. From 1950 until 15 October 1989 the Commission was a one-member body. On 16 October 1989 two more Commissioners were appointed because lowering the voting age to 18 increased the work. In January 1990 those posts were abolished and it reverted to one member. In October 1993 two more were appointed, and it has been a **three-member body** since.

The CEC and the two other Commissioners have **equal powers** and, on Laxmikanth's text, equal salary and allowances similar to a Supreme Court judge. If they differ, the Commission decides **by majority**.

## Tenure and removal

They hold office for **six years or until age 65**, whichever is earlier, and may resign at any time. The six-year/65 rule came from a statute, the Election Commission (Conditions of Service of Election Commissioners and Transaction of Business) Act, 1991, not from Article 324; the 2023 Act replaced that law (verify). The **CEC** has security of tenure: he can be removed **only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a Supreme Court judge** (pattern 2 of bridge module B2). His service conditions cannot be changed to his disadvantage after appointment. **Any other Election Commissioner or regional commissioner can be removed only on the recommendation of the CEC.** So the CEC is protected from the executive, and the Commissioners are protected by the CEC.

## Weaknesses Laxmikanth lists

The Constitution (as in the 2020 text) did not prescribe the qualifications of the members, did not itself specify their term (the 1991 Act supplied it, see Tenure above), and did not bar retiring Commissioners from further government appointments. Module 3 shows what the 2023 Act changed on qualification, term and the selection committee.

### 3. The Election Commission II: functions, machinery and the 2023 appointment law

*Objective:* Classify the Commission's functions, describe its machinery, and explain the appointment debate and the 2023 Act with the caution it needs.

## Functions in three groups

Laxmikanth sorts the powers into **administrative**, **advisory** and **quasi-judicial**. A **quasi-judicial** function is one where a body decides a dispute somewhat like a court without being a court.

- **Administrative:** determine the areas of constituencies on the basis of the Delimitation Commission Act (the boundaries themselves are drawn by the Delimitation Commission; GS-178 module 3); prepare and revise electoral rolls; notify dates and schedules of elections and scrutinise nominations; recognise political parties and allot election symbols; fix the model code of conduct; prepare the roster for party broadcasts; cancel polls in case of rigging, booth capturing or violence; supervise the machinery of elections.
- **Advisory:** advise the President on disqualification of members of Parliament and the Governor on disqualification of members of a State legislature (their opinion is what module 5 of GS-178 called for); certify whether elections can be held in a State under President's Rule: continuing a Proclamation under Article 356 beyond one year needs, among other conditions, the Election Commission's certificate that elections there are difficult (Article 356(5)). Laxmikanth's word 'emergency' here means the President's Rule proclamation.
- **Quasi-judicial:** act as a court in disputes about recognition of parties and allotment of symbols; appoint officers to inquire into disputes about electoral arrangements.

## Machinery

The Commission is assisted by deputy election commissioners drawn from the civil service, and secretaries and other officers. In each State a **chief electoral officer** is appointed by the CEC in consultation with the State government; at district level the **collector** is the district returning officer, who appoints a returning officer for each constituency and a presiding officer for each booth.

## The appointment problem and the 2023 Act

Under Article 324 the President appoints the Commissioners, which in practice meant the Council of Ministers chose them, and Parliament had made no law laying down a selection process. This was a long-standing criticism.

**Flagged as of 31 October 2026, from public reports; neither the Act nor the judgment was read here (verify before use).** In March 2023 a Constitution Bench in **Anoop Baranwal v Union of India** is reported to have ruled that, until Parliament makes a law, the President should appoint the CEC and Election Commissioners on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India. Parliament then passed the **Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023**. As reported: a selection committee of the Prime Minister (chair), the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and a Union Cabinet minister nominated by the Prime Minister, which replaces the Chief Justice with a minister; a search committee headed by the Law Minister that prepares a panel; eligibility for persons who hold or have held a post equal to Secretary to the Government of India; six-year term or age 65; and salary and service conditions the same as a Supreme Court judge (the Bill as introduced proposed Cabinet-Secretary parity, dropped by amendment before passage; verify). The Act is also reported to protect Commissioners from legal proceedings for acts done in office (added by amendment; verify). Challenges to the Act were reported to be before the Supreme Court. Whether they have been decided must be checked.

For exams: the Constitution's text (Article 324) is unchanged, the 2023 Act is a statute, and the debate is about whether an executive-majority selection committee protects independence as well as one with a judicial member.

### 4. UPSC and the State Public Service Commissions (Articles 315 to 323)

*Objective:* State the composition, tenure, removal and independence provisions of the UPSC, and the points on which the State Public Service Commission and Joint Commission differ.

## The UPSC

The **Union Public Service Commission** is the central recruiting agency, created directly by the Constitution (Articles 315 to 323 in Part XIV). It has a **chairman and other members appointed by the President**. The Constitution does not fix the number; usually it is **nine to eleven including the chairman**. The only qualification: **half the members must have held office for at least ten years** under the Government of India or a State government. The President fixes conditions of service.

**Tenure:** six years or age **65**, whichever is earlier; resignation is addressed to the President. The President can appoint a member as **acting chairman** if the chairman's office is vacant or he cannot function.

**Removal:** the President can remove the chairman or a member if he (a) is adjudged **insolvent**, (b) engages in **paid employment** outside his duties, or (c) is, in the President's opinion, **unfit by infirmity of mind or body**. For **misbehaviour** the President must **refer the matter to the Supreme Court**; if the Court upholds the cause and advises removal, its advice is **binding**. The President can suspend the member during the inquiry. Misbehaviour is defined as being concerned or interested in a contract with the Government of India or a State, or sharing in its profit other than as a member of an incorporated company.

**Independence:** removal only as above; service conditions cannot be varied to disadvantage; the commission's expenses are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**; the chairman is **not eligible for further employment** under the Government of India or a State after ceasing to hold office; a member is eligible to become chairman of the UPSC or of a State Commission but for no other job; and a chairman or member **cannot be reappointed** to the same office (Article 316(3)), though a member may become chairman (Article 319). (Laxmikanth notes that the Supreme Court held in 1979 that being Governor is a constitutional office, not government employment.)

**Functions:** conducts examinations for all-India and Central services and for centrally administered territories; helps States on joint recruitment if two or more States request; serves a State's needs at the Governor's request with the President's approval; and is **consulted** on recruitment methods, principles of appointment, promotion and transfer, suitability of candidates and disciplinary matters. The Supreme Court has held that consultation is **directory, not mandatory**, and that selection gives no right to the post. UPSC's advice is not binding; non-acceptance cases must be explained in the annual report laid before Parliament and approved by the **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet**. It does not handle classification, pay, cadre management or training: those belong to the **Department of Personnel and Training**.

## The State Public Service Commission (SPSC)

Same Articles. Differences: the **Governor appoints** the chairman and members, but the **President removes** them (the Governor can suspend during the Supreme Court inquiry); the age limit is **62** (raised from 60 by the 41st Amendment, 1976); expenses are charged on the **State** Consolidated Fund; the Chairman of an SPSC is eligible to be chairman or member of the UPSC or chairman of any SPSC. A **Joint SPSC** for two or more States is created by an **Act of Parliament** on the States' request, so it is **statutory**; Punjab and Haryana had one briefly after 1966. In the judicial service the Governor consults the SPSC and the High Court on rules, except for district judges.

### 5. The Finance Commission and the GST Council

*Objective:* Recall the Finance Commission's constitution and advisory role, and learn the composition, voting rule and functions of the GST Council with a worked weighted-vote example.

## Finance Commission: a recap

**Article 280** provides a Finance Commission as a quasi-judicial body, constituted by the **President every fifth year** or earlier. It has a **chairman and four other members**, who hold office for the period the President specifies and are eligible for reappointment. Parliament decides their qualifications (the Finance Commission Act, 1951): the chairman has experience in public affairs; the four members come from a High Court judge or one qualified to be one, a person with specialised knowledge of government finance and accounts, a person with wide experience of financial matters and administration, and a person with special knowledge of economics. Its recommendations go to the President, who lays them before Parliament with an explanatory memorandum on the action taken. They are **advisory, not binding**. For devolution and grants, see GS-193 modules 3 to 7. As of October 2026: the 16th Commission (constituted on 31 December 2023 under Arvind Panagariya): award period 2026-27 to 2030-31, which began on 1 April 2026; its report was reported as submitted in November 2025 and laid before Parliament in February 2026 (verify dates and the headline share against the official report before quoting).

## The GST Council

Full treatment: GS-194 module 8. Recap: the **101st Amendment Act, 2016** inserted **Article 279-A**, and the Council was constituted by Presidential order of 15 September 2016. The Union Finance Minister chairs it and each State nominates a minister; half the members form the quorum. The Chairperson of the Central Board of Excise and Customs (renamed CBIC in 2018) is a permanent, non-voting invitee. Every decision needs **not less than three-fourths of the weighted votes** of members present and voting: the **Centre carries one-third** and **all States together two-thirds**.

**Worked check.** With total weight 100 the Centre has 33.33 and the States 66.67. The States alone cannot reach 75, and the Centre alone holds more than the 25 that blocks a decision, so the **Centre has an effective veto**. The Centre needs 75 - 33.33 = 41.67 more, which is 41.67 / 66.67 = 0.625 of the States' weight: **five-eighths**.

**Flag.** In Union of India v Mohit Minerals (2022) the Supreme Court is reported to have held that the Council's recommendations are persuasive, not binding on Parliament or State legislatures (verify). The special-provision list in Article 279-A includes Jammu and Kashmir, now a Union Territory (list as printed in the source). Rate slabs and the end of the compensation period are later developments outside Laxmikanth's text; verify them before use.


### 6. The national commissions for SCs, STs and Backward Classes and the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities

*Objective:* Trace how the commissions became constitutional, state their composition and powers, and distinguish them from statutory commissions and from the linguistic minorities officer.

## Constitutional and statutory commissions

The National Commission for **Scheduled Castes** is a **constitutional body**, established directly by **Article 338**. Laxmikanth contrasts the National Commission for Women (1992), for Minorities (1993), the National Human Rights Commission (1993) and for Protection of Child Rights (2007), which are **statutory**.

## Evolution (SCs and STs)

Originally Article 338 provided a **Special Officer** for SCs and STs. In 1978 the Government set up a non-statutory multi-member commission, and in 1987 renamed it the National Commission for SCs and STs. The **65th Amendment (1990)**, in force from 12 March 1992, replaced the single officer with a constitutional multi-member **National Commission for SCs and STs**. The **89th Amendment (2003)**, in force from 19 February 2004, split it into the **National Commission for SCs (Article 338)** and the **National Commission for STs (Article 338-A)**, because the problems of STs differ and a Ministry of Tribal Affairs had been created in 1999.

## Backward Classes

In the **Mandal case (Indra Sawhney, 1992)** the Supreme Court told the Centre to set up a permanent statutory body to examine complaints of under-inclusion, over-inclusion or non-inclusion in the list of backward classes. The National Commission for Backward Classes was set up in **1993** under an Act. The **102nd Amendment (2018)** inserted **Article 338-B**, making it constitutional (the 1993 Act was repealed), and also inserted **Article 342-A**, letting the President specify the socially and educationally backward classes. Standard knowledge, flagged: the 105th Amendment (2021) restored the States' power to make their own lists; verify. Since the 2018 Amendment the SC Commission no longer handles OBC matters.

## Composition, functions and powers (common to all three)

Each has a **chairperson, a vice-chairperson and three other members**, appointed by the **President by warrant under hand and seal**. The President decides their conditions of service and tenure; under the Rules, the tenure is **three years**. The Constitution lays down no removal grounds for these commissions; tenure and removal follow Rules made by the President (verify current Rules).

Functions: investigate and monitor constitutional and legal safeguards and evaluate their working; inquire into specific complaints of deprivation of rights; take part in and advise on planning for socio-economic development; report annually to the President; recommend measures; and perform any other function the President specifies. They have the **powers of a civil court** (summoning and examining witnesses on oath, discovery of documents, evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records) and the Centre and States must consult them on major policy affecting the group. Reports go to the President, who lays them before Parliament with a memorandum of action taken and reasons for non-acceptance; State matters go to the Governor for the legislature. The ST Commission has extra functions fixed in 2005 on forest produce, mineral and water rights, displacement and rehabilitation, land alienation, PESA and shifting cultivation. Laxmikanth adds that the SC Commission also handled the Anglo-Indian community (Article 338(10)); its present status after 2020 is flagged verify.

## Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Article 350-B)

Added by the **7th Amendment (1956)** after the States Reorganisation Commission. The **President appoints** the officer, who investigates safeguards for linguistic minorities and reports to the President, who places reports before Parliament and sends them to the States. The Constitution **does not specify** qualifications, tenure, pay or removal. The office was created in 1957 as the Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities; it now functions under the Ministry of Minority Affairs (created 2006).

### 7. Law officers and the CAG: one serves the government, the other checks it

*Objective:* Compare the Attorney General, the Advocate General and the CAG on appointment, tenure, removal and role, and explain why their protections differ.

The details are in GS-184 modules 2 to 5 (law officers) and module 6 (CAG). This module gives the comparison logic only.

## Three offices side by side

- **Attorney General (Article 76):** appointed by the **President**; qualified to be a Supreme Court judge, which includes the jurist route (the President's opinion). Holds office **during the President's pleasure**; no fixed term and no stated removal procedure.
- **Advocate General (Article 165):** appointed by the **Governor**; qualified to be a High Court judge (no jurist route). Holds office during the Governor's pleasure. By convention both law officers resign when the council of ministers changes; that is convention, not a constitutional rule.
- **CAG (Article 148):** appointed by the **President by warrant**; **six years or 65**; removed **only like a Supreme Court judge**; no further office; salary fixed by Parliament and protected; expenses **charged on the Consolidated Fund**.

## Why the difference?

The Attorney General and Advocate General **advise and defend the government**; its confidence in its own lawyer is part of the job, so tenure is at pleasure. The CAG **audits the government for Parliament**; he must be able to criticise ministers, so he is protected like a judge. The same logic separates the **Election Commission, UPSC and CAG** (checkers: protected removal) from the **law officers** (servers: pleasure). The 2018 Mains question on the CAG's 'method and terms of appointment' and 'range of powers' asks exactly this: appointment by warrant, six-year tenure, removal like a judge and the bar on later office (terms), and audit of the Consolidated Fund, Contingency Fund, Public Account and government companies (powers; GS-184 module 6).


### 8. Master layer: the independence toolkit and the appointment-process debate

*Objective:* Use the independence toolkit to compare bodies, identify where safeguards are incomplete, and write a comparison of judicial appointments in India and the United States with appropriate flags.

## The toolkit

Across the bodies in this lesson, independence is built from five tools. Test each body against them (the master table is in the notes).

1. **Security of tenure:** removal only by a special procedure on stated grounds (CEC, CAG, UPSC, SPSC).
2. **Service conditions that cannot be worsened** after appointment (CEC, UPSC, SPSC, CAG).
3. **Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund** so Parliament does not vote on them (UPSC, CAG, and the salaries of judges and presiding officers).
4. **A bar on later government jobs** (UPSC chairman, CAG; a UPSC member may become chairman).
5. **Insulation in the appointment** itself: who chooses.

The fifth tool is the weakest in the text. For nearly every body above, **the President appoints**, which means the Union Cabinet chooses. The Constitution rarely names a consultative process. That is why reforms keep asking for committee-based selection.

## Where safeguards are thin

- **Election Commission:** in the 2020 text, no qualifications, no fixed term and no bar on post-retirement office; the other Commissioners depend on the CEC. The 2023 Act (flagged, module 3) addresses qualification and term and sets a committee, but its composition is disputed.
- **UPSC and SPSC:** advice is not binding; consultation is directory; the government can narrow its role by regulations laid before Parliament for 14 days; the Central Vigilance Commission can give conflicting advice in discipline cases, though the UPSC, as a constitutional body, has an edge.
- **Finance Commission and national commissions:** recommendations are advisory; the safeguard is that the President must lay the report before Parliament with reasons for non-acceptance.
- **Law officers:** pleasure tenure makes them dependent on the government; the safeguard is that they check legality, not policy.

## Judicial appointments compared (2025 Mains)

The verified 2025 question asks for the evolution of the collegium and a comparison with the United States. India: from GS-186 module 2, the President appoints; the First Judges case (S.P. Gupta, decided 1981 and usually cited as 1982) held that 'consultation' is not concurrence, which gave the executive primacy; the Second Judges case (1993) made the Chief Justice's view binding; the Third Judges case (1998) set a collegium of the CJI plus the four senior-most judges; the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act (2014) tried to replace it and were struck down in 2015 (Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India, the Fourth Judges case). Details: GS-186 module 2. United States, standard knowledge, flagged: the President nominates and the Senate confirms by simple majority (advice and consent); justices serve for life during good behaviour and are removable only by impeachment; the number of seats is fixed by statute. 

Points for an answer. **India advantages:** insulation from party politics; seniority and peer knowledge; continuity. **India disadvantages:** opacity; no secretariat or published criteria; judges choosing judges lacks external accountability; concerns about narrow professional networks are often raised. **US advantages:** democratic legitimacy and public hearings. **US disadvantages:** politicised confirmations, strategic timing of vacancies, and polarised appointments. A balanced conclusion asks for a transparent, accountable system with judicial primacy retained for independence.

### 9. How UPSC tests this topic: the two verified questions, Prelims patterns and traps

*Objective:* Plan answers to the verified Mains questions on the CAG and the collegium, recognise the Prelims patterns on constitutional bodies and avoid the standard traps.

## The 2018 Mains question (10 marks): the CAG

'The CAG has a very vital role to play.' Explain how this is reflected in the method and terms of his appointment as well as the range of powers. The directive **Explain** asks for reasons. A plan for about 150 words:

- **Introduction:** Ambedkar called the CAG the most important officer under the Constitution; he is guardian of the public purse (Article 148).
- **Method and terms:** appointed by the President by warrant (method); six years or 65; removal like a Supreme Court judge; no further office; salary fixed by Parliament and equal to a judge's, not alterable to his disadvantage; expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund (terms).
- **Powers:** audit of the Consolidated Funds of the Union and States, Contingency and Public Accounts, government companies and bodies substantially financed by government; certify net proceeds of taxes (Article 279); three audit reports; guide of the Public Accounts Committee (GS-184).
- **Limits as a conclusion:** an auditor, not a comptroller, in practice; propriety audit is discretionary; secret service expenditure is outside.

## The 2025 Mains question (15 marks): collegium and the American system

'Discuss the evolution' and 'critically examine the advantages and disadvantages' are two tasks. Part one: the Judges cases and NJAC (GS-186 module 2). Part two: the comparison in module 8 above (the United States details flagged). Budget about half the answer for each, and end with a reform view.

## Prelims patterns

1. **Who appoints, who removes** for the CEC, UPSC, SPSC, CAG, Attorney General, Advocate General. The trap swaps President and Governor, or says 'same as a Supreme Court judge' for the wrong office.
2. **Constitutional versus statutory** (NHRC, NCW, NCM versus NCSC, NCST, NCBC).
3. **Numbers:** six years or 65 (CEC, UPSC, CAG), 62 (SPSC), 14 days for regulations, five members for the Finance Commission, three-fourths weighted for the GST Council.
4. **Binding or advisory:** UPSC, Finance Commission and national commissions advise; the Supreme Court's removal advice for UPSC misbehaviour is binding on the President.
5. **Post-2023 traps:** a statement on how the CEC is appointed must be answered with the date in mind. Older keys use the executive-appointment position; newer ones use the 2023 Act.

## Distractor habits

Exams alter 'Governor' to 'President', '62' to '65', 'misbehaviour or incapacity' to 'any ground', and describe the Attorney General as fixed-term. Words such as 'only' and 'always' are risky because UPSC and SPSC have multiple grounds.

### 10. Guided practice: one MCQ thought aloud and one Mains skeleton

## Worked MCQ (thought aloud)

**Q.** Consider the following statements:
1. The Chairman of a State Public Service Commission is appointed by the Governor and can be removed only by the President.
2. The Attorney General of India can be removed only by a resolution of both Houses of Parliament passed by a special majority.
3. The Finance Commission is constituted by the President every fifth year or earlier.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct? (A) 1 only (B) 1 and 3 only (C) 2 and 3 only (D) 1, 2 and 3

**Thinking.** Statement 1: matches module 4 (Governor appoints, President removes). Correct. Statement 2: the Attorney General holds office during the pleasure of the President (pattern 1 in bridge module B2); the special-majority rule belongs to the CEC, the CAG and judges (pattern 2). Wrong. Statement 3: matches module 5 (Article 280). Correct. Answer: **B**. Tick each statement against a module, never against a feeling.

## Mains skeleton (15 marks, 250 words): 'Constitutional bodies are only as independent as the process that appoints them. Critically examine.'

1. **Introduction:** the framers protected tenure and removal; appointment is left to the executive.
2. **Safeguards that work:** tenure, protected service conditions, charged expenses, bar on later office (CEC, CAG, UPSC).
3. **Where they fail:** no qualifications and no committee in the text for the Election Commission (as of 2020); pleasure tenure of law officers; advisory nature of recommendations.
4. **Reforms and comparisons:** committee selection, the 2023 Act and its dispute (flag verify), collegium versus United States.
5. **Way forward:** transparent criteria, plural selection panels, published reasons, parliamentary reports.
6. **Conclusion:** independence needs both protection after appointment and neutrality in it.

### Notes: Constitutional and statutory bodies at a glance

- Constitutional: Election Commission, UPSC and SPSC, Finance Commission, GST Council, CAG, NCSC, NCST, NCBC (since 2018), Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities, Attorney General, Advocate General.
- Statutory: NCW (1992), NCM (1993), NHRC (1993), NCPCR (2007), Joint SPSC, Central Vigilance Commission (statutory status by the CVC Act, 2003, Laxmikanth: October 2003, verify; created by executive resolution in 1964).
- Evolution of SC and ST commissions: Special Officer; 1978 non-statutory commission; 1987 National Commission for SCs and STs; 65th Amendment (1990, in force 1992); 89th Amendment (2003) split into Articles 338 and 338-A (separate commissions from 2004).
- Backward Classes: Indra Sawhney (1992); National Commission for Backward Classes Act, 1993; 102nd Amendment (2018): Articles 338-B and 342-A; the Act of 1993 repealed.
- The Commission for Linguistic Minorities: Article 350-B from the 7th Amendment (1956); office from 1957.

### Notes: Election Commission functions

- Administrative: delimitation by the Delimitation Commission Act, electoral rolls, schedules, nominations, recognition of parties and symbols, code of conduct, broadcast roster, cancelling polls, supervision.
- Advisory: disqualification of members of Parliament (President) and of State legislatures (Governor); certifying whether elections can be held in a State under President's Rule, needed among other conditions to continue a Proclamation beyond one year (Article 356(5)).
- Quasi-judicial: disputes about recognition of parties and allotment of symbols; officers to inquire into disputes about electoral arrangements.
- Machinery: deputy election commissioners; chief electoral officer in each State; district returning officer (the collector); returning officer per constituency; presiding officer per booth.
- Not covered: elections to panchayats and municipalities (State Election Commissions, Articles 243-K and 243-ZA).

### Notes: Confused pairs and exceptions

- President appoints, Governor appoints: SPSC (Governor appoints, President removes); Advocate General (Governor appoints and removes).
- 'Like a Supreme Court judge' applies to the CEC and CAG; it does not apply to the UPSC chairman, the Attorney General or the other Election Commissioners.
- Attorney General (Union, Article 76, at pleasure) versus Solicitor General (not in Article 76) versus Law Minister (in the Cabinet).
- CAG: Comptroller (control of issue of money) versus Auditor General (audit after spending). In India, only the second in practice.
- National Commission for Women and Human Rights are statutory; the SC, ST and BC commissions are constitutional.
- Finance Commission (Article 280, recommends tax sharing) versus the Planning Commission and NITI Aayog (non-constitutional) versus the GST Council (Article 279-A, recommends GST matters).
- Exception: the UPSC's advice is not binding on the government, yet the Supreme Court's advice on misbehaviour is binding on the President.
- Exception: a member of the UPSC can become its chairman (Article 319), but a chairman or member cannot be reappointed to the same office (Article 316(3)).

### Core concepts

- Constitutional bodies are created by Articles and are harder to change than statutory bodies.
- Each office is defined by who appoints, for how long, how removed and what protects independence.
- Checkers (Election Commission, UPSC, CAG) are protected in tenure; servers (law officers) serve at pleasure.
- The Finance Commission, UPSC and national commissions give advice, not binding orders.
- The GST Council is a federal body whose three-fourths weighted rule gives the Centre a veto.

### Practice

**Q.** What does it mean for a body to be constitutional rather than statutory?

**A.** It is created directly by an Article of the Constitution, so changing its basic features needs a constitutional amendment; a statutory body is created by an Act.

**Q.** How long do the CEC, UPSC members and the CAG hold office?

**A.** Six years or until age 65, whichever is earlier.

**Q.** Who appoints the Attorney General and the Advocate General?

**A.** The President appoints the Attorney General; the Governor appoints the Advocate General.

**Q.** How many members does the Finance Commission have?

**A.** A chairman and four other members.

**Q.** Why are the Election Commission, UPSC and CAG protected from removal while the Attorney General serves at pleasure?

**A.** The first three check the government and need independence; the Attorney General advises and represents the government, so it may choose its own lawyer.

**Q.** Why does the Centre have an effective veto in the GST Council?

**A.** Decisions need three-fourths of weighted votes and the Centre holds one-third, more than the one-fourth needed to block.

**Q.** Why does Laxmikanth say the UPSC's role is limited?

**A.** Its advice is not binding, consultation is directory, the government can exempt matters, and it handles recruitment but not pay, cadre or training.

**Q.** Why did the 89th Amendment split the SC and ST commissions?

**A.** The problems of STs differ from those of SCs, and a Ministry of Tribal Affairs had been created, so a separate commission could safeguard their interests more effectively.

**Q.** In a GST Council meeting the total weight is 100. States holding 45 of their 66.67 and the Centre vote yes. Does the proposal pass?

**A.** Yes: 33.33 + 45 = 78.33, which is at least 75.

**Q.** A State PSC chairman is accused of misbehaviour. Who refers the matter, to whom, and who may suspend him?

**A.** The President refers it to the Supreme Court; the Governor can suspend him during the inquiry; the President removes him only if the Court upholds the cause and advises removal.

**Q.** A candidate is a citizen with nine years as a High Court advocate. Is he qualified to be Advocate General? To be Attorney General?

**A.** Advocate General: no (ten years as a High Court advocate or in judicial office is required; High Court qualification has no jurist route). Attorney General: not on the advocate route; only if the President considers him a distinguished jurist.

**Q.** State who appoints and who removes the CEC, UPSC chairman, SPSC chairman, CAG and Attorney General, and the tenure of each.

**A.** CEC: President appoints, removed like a Supreme Court judge, 6 years or 65. UPSC chairman: President appoints and removes on stated grounds, 6 years or 65. SPSC chairman: Governor appoints, President removes, 6 years or 62. CAG: President by warrant, removed like a judge, 6 years or 65. Attorney General: President appoints, pleasure, no fixed term.

**Q.** Describe the GST Council's composition and voting rule.

**A.** Finance Minister (chair), the Union Minister of State for Revenue or Finance and one minister from each State; decisions need three-fourths of weighted votes of those present and voting, with the Centre at one-third and the States together at two-thirds; quorum is half the members.

**Q.** Trace the Amendments that shaped the SC, ST and Backward Classes commissions.

**A.** 65th Amendment (1990) created a National Commission for SCs and STs; the 89th (2003) split it under Articles 338 and 338-A; the 102nd (2018) inserted Article 338-B for the Backward Classes Commission and Article 342-A.

**Q.** List the five independence tools and apply them to the CAG.

**A.** Security of tenure, protected service conditions, charged expenses, bar on later jobs, insulated appointment; the CAG has the first four, with appointment left to the President.
