### Bridge: Vectors and the cross product: the minimum for angular momentum and centre of mass

The full introduction to vectors is bridge PH-001-B1 in the Newton-laws lesson (PH-001); read it first if components, dot products or cross products are unfamiliar. Here is the minimum needed for this lesson.

A **position vector** r1 locates particle 1 from a chosen origin O; r2 locates particle 2. The **difference** r = r1 − r2 is the vector that points from particle 2 to particle 1, and its length |r| is their separation. A **weighted average** of position vectors, with the masses as weights, is a vector as well: R = (m1 r1 + m2 r2)/(m1 + m2). It lies on the line joining the particles, nearer to the heavier one.

The **cross product** of two vectors is a third vector perpendicular to both; its size is |A||B| sin θ and its direction comes from the right-hand rule (fingers from A to B, thumb gives A × B). The order matters: B × A = −A × B, and A × A = 0. The **angular momentum** of a particle of momentum p at position r about the origin is L = r × p. Because a cross product is linear (A × (B + C) = A × B + A × C), angular momentum of a collection of particles can be split into parts by splitting each position vector into parts.

Worked check. If r = (3, 0, 0) m and p = (0, 2, 0) kg m s⁻¹, then L = r × p = (0·0 − 0·2, 0·0 − 3·0, 3·2 − 0·0) = (0, 0, 6) kg m² s⁻¹, along +z, as the right-hand rule predicts for anticlockwise motion seen from above.

Dimensions to remember: [L] = M L² T⁻¹; [r × p] = L × M L T⁻¹, consistent.

### Bridge: Derivative of a rotating unit vector and polar coordinates

For a particle moving in a plane, polar coordinates (r, φ) are often easier than x and y. Let r̂ = (cos φ, sin φ) be the unit vector pointing from the origin to the particle and φ̂ = (−sin φ, cos φ) the unit vector at right angles to it, in the direction of increasing φ. Both have length 1.

If the angle φ changes with time, differentiate with the chain rule: dr̂/dt = φ̇ (−sin φ, cos φ) = φ̇ φ̂, and dφ̂/dt = φ̇ (−cos φ, −sin φ) = −φ̇ r̂. So the derivative of a unit vector that merely turns is perpendicular to it (the same fact as in the rotating-frame lesson PH-002, bridge B2).

The position is r = r r̂. By the product rule, the velocity is

$$ v = ṙ r̂ + r φ̇ φ̂ $$

The radial part ṙ is the rate of change of distance, and r φ̇ is the speed due to turning. Because r̂ and φ̂ are perpendicular, the speed squared is

$$ v² = ṙ² + r² φ̇² $$

Check with uniform circular motion: r constant so ṙ = 0, hence v = r φ̇, the familiar v = r ω. The angular momentum about the origin, for mass μ, is L = r × (μ v) = μ r² φ̇ ẑ (only the φ̂ part of the velocity contributes, since r̂ × r̂ = 0 and r̂ × φ̂ = ẑ).

Units: φ̇ is s⁻¹ (radians are dimensionless), so r φ̇ has units of speed.

### Bridge: Calculus of vector-valued functions and the algebra of sums of squares

A vector that depends on time, r(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t)), is differentiated component by component: ṙ = (ẋ, ẏ, ż) is the velocity, and r̈ = (ẍ, ÿ, z̈) the acceleration. The usual product rule works: d(f A)/dt = ḟ A + f Ȧ for a number f(t) and a vector A, and d(A · B)/dt = Ȧ · B + A · Ḃ. For a cross product d(A × B)/dt = Ȧ × B + A × Ḃ with the order kept.

Two further tools matter in the two-body problem.

**Sums and linearity.** The derivative of a sum is the sum of derivatives. So if r1 = R + a r with a constant number a, then ṙ1 = Ṙ + a ṙ, and r̈1 = R̈ + a r̈. This lets us move between particle coordinates and centre-of-mass and relative coordinates.

**The square of a vector sum.** For any vectors A and B, |A + B|² = A·A + 2 A·B + B·B. If A = V + a v and B = V − b v the cross terms are proportional to V·v, and they cancel only if the weights satisfy a condition (we shall see it holds for the masses of the two particles). The dot product of a vector with itself is the square of its length.

Worked check. Let V = (1, 0, 0), v = (0, 2, 0), a = 2. Then |V + a v|² = |(1, 4, 0)|² = 17, and A·A + 2A·B + B·B with A = V, B = a v gives 1 + 0 + 16 = 17: the dot product V · v is zero, so there is no cross term here. Another check: V = (1, 0, 0), v = (1, 0, 0), a = 2 gives |(3, 0, 0)|² = 9 = 1 + 2·2 + 4 = 9.

Notation: a dot over a letter is d/dt; a double dot is d²/dt².

### 1. Orientation: what 'Two-body problem; Reduced mass' asks of you

*Objective:* State what the two-body problem is, why reduction to one body is the key idea, and where it appears in the Optional paper.

## Where this sits in the syllabus

The Physics Paper I syllabus, section 1(a) 'Mechanics of particles', contains the two phrases 'Two-body problem; Reduced mass'. This lesson is topic PH-009. It uses ideas from PH-001 (Newton's laws and conservation laws): centre of mass, momentum and angular momentum conservation, and collisions in the centre-of-mass frame. It also borrows one idea from PH-002 (a frame that accelerates needs a fictitious force), used in an alternative derivation in module 4.

## What the problem is

Two particles, of masses m1 and m2, interact with each other and with nothing else: no walls, no outside force. Examples you will meet: the Earth and the Moon, two stars of a binary system, the electron and the proton of a hydrogen atom, the two atoms of a diatomic molecule such as HCl. We call such a pair an **isolated two-particle system**, and the force between them an **internal force**.

## Why it looks hard and why it is easy

Each particle has three coordinates, so the pair has six unknown functions of time, and each particle's equation of motion contains the position of the other. The remarkable result of this lesson is that the six coordinates split into two independent groups: three coordinates of the **centre of mass**, which moves as if free, and three coordinates of the **relative position**, which obeys the equation of a single particle of mass μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2), called the **reduced mass**, moving under the same force. The two-body problem is therefore reduced to a one-body problem.

## The vocabulary for first use

- **Centre of mass (CM):** the mass-weighted average position of the system.
- **Relative position:** r = r1 − r2, the vector from particle 2 to particle 1.
- **Reduced mass:** μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2); equivalently 1/μ = 1/m1 + 1/m2.
- **Central force:** a force along the line joining the particles whose size depends only on their separation.
- **Barycentre:** another name for the CM in astronomy.

## What the examiner asks

Typical parts: derive the reduction and the form of the kinetic energy and angular momentum; compute reduced masses and their effects (spectral shifts of hydrogen and deuterium, molecular vibration and rotation frequencies, binary-star periods); find where the CM of the Earth–Moon pair lies. The project has no image-verified past question attached to this topic, so this lesson does not quote one; module 10 explains how to practise anyway.

Modules 2 to 5 are the core of the first pass. Modules 6 to 8 are applications you should be able to reproduce; module 9 (collisions in the CM frame) is a deeper dive that links to PH-001. Each module has one objective and one stopping point.

### 2. Foundation: centre of mass, relative position and the reduced mass

*Objective:* Define R, r, M and μ, invert the definitions to get r1 and r2, and describe the basic properties of μ.

## Definitions

Work in an inertial frame with origin O (the laboratory). Particle 1 has mass m1 and position r1; particle 2 has mass m2 and position r2. Define

$$ M = m1 + m2 ,  R = (m1 r1 + m2 r2)/M ,  r = r1 − r2 $$

R is the position of the **centre of mass**; r is the **relative position** (from 2 to 1); M is the total mass. Differentiating: the CM velocity is V = Ṙ = (m1 ṙ1 + m2 ṙ2)/M, the total momentum is P = m1 ṙ1 + m2 ṙ2 = M V, and the relative velocity is v = ṙ = ṙ1 − ṙ2.

## Inverting the definitions

We can recover each particle's position from R and r. Start from r1 − r2 = r and M R = m1 r1 + m2 r2. Multiply the first by m2 and add to the second: m2 r + M R = m2 r1 − m2 r2 + m1 r1 + m2 r2 = M r1, so

$$ r1 = R + (m2/M) r ,  r2 = R − (m1/M) r $$

Check the second: subtracting gives r1 − r2 = (m2/M + m1/M) r = r, correct. Check the first against the definition: m1 r1 + m2 r2 = M R + (m1 m2/M − m2 m1/M) r = M R, correct. Interpretation: particle 1 sits at distance (m2/M)|r| from the CM and particle 2 at (m1/M)|r| on the opposite side, so **the lighter body is farther from the CM**; the distances are inversely proportional to the masses.

Worked example. m1 = 2 kg at x = 0 and m2 = 3 kg at x = 5 m. R = (2·0 + 3·5)/5 = 3 m; r = 0 − 5 = −5 m. Check: R + (3/5)(−5) = 0 = x1, and R − (2/5)(−5) = 5 m = x2.

## Reduced mass

$$ μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2) ,  1/μ = 1/m1 + 1/m2 $$

Properties, each easy to check:

- Dimension of mass; [μ] = kg.
- μ is smaller than either mass: μ = m1/(1 + m1/m2) < m1, and likewise μ < m2.
- Limit m2 ≫ m1: μ ≈ m1 (1 − m1/m2), so μ tends to the smaller mass. For the hydrogen atom, the proton is 1836 times heavier than the electron, so μ ≈ m_e (1 − 1/1836).
- Equal masses m1 = m2 = m: μ = m/2.
- For a 2 kg and a 3 kg particle μ = 6/5 = 1.2 kg.

## Sign and coordinate conventions used from now on

An inertial frame; r points from particle 2 to particle 1; a force F(r) r̂ acting on particle 1, with F < 0 meaning attraction and F > 0 repulsion (r̂ = r/|r|). Particle 2 feels the equal and opposite force, as required by the third law.

### 3. Core: reduction to a one-body problem, μ r̈ = F(r)

*Objective:* Derive the equation of motion of the relative coordinate and the motion of the centre of mass from Newton's second and third laws.

## Assumptions

Isolated pair: no external force. The force on particle 1 due to particle 2 is F12 = F(r) r̂, a central force (along the line joining the particles, size dependent on r = |r| only). By the third law the force on particle 2 due to particle 1 is F21 = −F12. The frame is inertial.

## Derivation

Step 1: write Newton's second law for each particle.

$$ m1 r̈1 = F12 = F ,  m2 r̈2 = F21 = −F $$

where F = F(r) r̂ denotes the force vector on particle 1.

Step 2: centre of mass. Add the two equations; the internal forces cancel:

$$ m1 r̈1 + m2 r̈2 = 0 ,  i.e.  M R̈ = 0 $$

So the CM moves with constant velocity V (it may be at rest). The total momentum P = M V is conserved. This is the statement of PH-001 that internal forces cannot change the motion of the CM.

Step 3: relative coordinate. Divide the first equation by m1 and the second by m2:

$$ r̈1 = F/m1 ,  r̈2 = −F/m2 $$

Subtract:

$$ r̈ = r̈1 − r̈2 = F (1/m1 + 1/m2) = F/μ $$

because 1/m1 + 1/m2 = (m1 + m2)/(m1 m2) = 1/μ. Multiply by μ:

$$ μ r̈ = F(r) r̂ $$

Step 4: interpretation. This is the equation of a single particle of mass μ at position r from a fixed centre, acted on by the force F(r) r̂. Solve it for r(t), then use module 2 to recover r1 = R + (m2/M) r and r2 = R − (m1/M) r.

## Checks

- Dimensions: μ r̈ has units kg m s⁻², a force.
- m2 → ∞: μ → m1, the heavy particle stays at rest (R → r2; and r → r1 if particle 2 sits at the origin, since r = r1 − r2) and particle 1 moves under F as in the familiar one-body problem.
- The six equations of motion (two vector equations) have been replaced by M R̈ = 0 and μ r̈ = F, which are independent.

## The caution that examiners use

In the one-body equation μ replaces the mass in the *kinematic* side (the inertia), but **the force law keeps the actual masses**. For gravity, F = −G m1 m2/r² and not −G μ²/r². Mathur stresses exactly this point: one cannot replace m1 by μ inside the force expression, because the force depends on both masses. For an orbit, the equation reads μ r̈ = −G m1 m2 r̂/r², or equivalently r̈ = −G M r̂/r², since m1 m2/μ = M.

The CM result and the relative result are the whole of the reduction; energy and angular momentum are separated in the next module.

### 4. Core: energy and angular momentum separate into CM and relative parts

*Objective:* Show that T = ½ M V² + ½ μ v² and L = R × P + r × p_rel, and list the conserved quantities of an isolated pair.

## Kinetic energy

From the inverted coordinates, the velocities are ṙ1 = V + (m2/M) v and ṙ2 = V − (m1/M) v, where V = Ṙ and v = ṙ. The kinetic energy is

$$ T = ½ m1 |ṙ1|² + ½ m2 |ṙ2|² $$

Expand each square (bridge B3):

$$ ½ m1 [ V² + 2 (m2/M) V·v + (m2/M)² v² ] + ½ m2 [ V² − 2 (m1/M) V·v + (m1/M)² v² ] $$

Group terms. The V² terms: ½ (m1 + m2) V² = ½ M V². The cross terms: (m1 m2/M − m2 m1/M) V·v = 0. The v² terms: ½ v² (m1 m2² + m2 m1²)/M² = ½ v² m1 m2 (m2 + m1)/M² = ½ v² m1 m2/M. Therefore

$$ T = ½ M V² + ½ μ v² $$

The first part is the kinetic energy of the CM motion, the second the kinetic energy of the relative motion. The cross terms vanish because the weights are the masses.

## Angular momentum about the origin

L = m1 r1 × ṙ1 + m2 r2 × ṙ2. Substitute r1 = R + (m2/M) r, ṙ1 = V + (m2/M) v and the corresponding forms for particle 2 (with −m1/M):

$$ L = M R × V + [ m1 (m2/M) − m2 (m1/M) ] (R × v + r × V) + [ m1 (m2/M)² + m2 (m1/M)² ] r × v $$

The bracket in the middle is zero. The last bracket equals m1 m2 (m2 + m1)/M² = μ. Hence

$$ L = R × P + r × (μ v) $$

with P = M V. The first part is the angular momentum of the CM (as if all the mass M were at R), the second is the angular momentum of the relative motion. For L about the CM only the second part survives, because R = 0.

## Conservation laws

- P is conserved (module 3, M R̈ = 0).
- The CM angular momentum R × P is then constant as well, so r × μ v = L_rel is separately conserved when L is conserved. For a central force the torque on the relative motion about the origin is r × F = 0, because F is along r, so **L_rel is conserved**, the motion lies in a plane and the area law follows (later topics).
- Energy: E = ½ M V² + ½ μ v² + U(r), with U the potential energy of the internal force; the CM part is constant, so ½ μ v² + U(r) = E_rel is conserved.

**Mid-module stopping point (about 30 minutes in):** if you must pause, stop here, once you can write T = ½ M V² + ½ μ v² and L = M R × V + μ r × v with the cross terms cancelled. The alternative method below can wait for the next sitting.

## Alternative method with a non-inertial frame (link to PH-002)

Attach axes to particle 2 without rotating them. Particle 2 accelerates with a2 = −F/m2, so in this non-inertial frame particle 1 feels, besides F, the fictitious force −m1 a2 = +(m1/m2) F. Then m1 r̈ = F + (m1/m2) F, i.e. r̈ = F (1/m1 + 1/m2) = F/μ, the same result. This shows that reduced mass is the price of working relative to a body that itself recoils.

## Checks

Dimensions: ½ μ v² is energy; μ r × v is angular momentum. Limits: m2 → ∞ gives μ → m1 and T → ½ m1 v² with the CM at rest.

### 5. Core: central forces and the effective potential (a first look)

*Objective:* Obtain the radial equation for the relative motion, introduce the effective potential, and derive the circular-orbit condition; full orbit theory is deferred.

## Setting

The relative motion is a single particle of mass μ at position r under a central force F(r) r̂ with potential energy U(r), F = −dU/dr. Because L_rel = r × μ v is conserved (module 4), the motion stays in the plane perpendicular to it. Use polar coordinates (r, φ) in that plane (bridge B2): v² = ṙ² + r² φ̇², and the angular momentum has magnitude

$$ ℓ = μ r² φ̇ $$

This is a constant of the motion. (Only the symbol ℓ is used here; the full central-force theory, orbit equation and Kepler's laws are separate topics.)

## Energy equation

The relative energy is E_rel = ½ μ ṙ² + ½ μ r² φ̇² + U(r). Eliminate φ̇ using φ̇ = ℓ/(μ r²):

$$ E_rel = ½ μ ṙ² + ℓ²/(2 μ r²) + U(r) $$

Define the **effective potential** U_eff(r) = U(r) + ℓ²/(2 μ r²). Then E_rel = ½ μ ṙ² + U_eff(r): a one-dimensional problem in r. The term ℓ²/(2μ r²) is called the centrifugal barrier; it is the kinetic energy of the angular motion, written as a function of r, and it is large at small r, keeping the particle away from the centre if ℓ ≠ 0. (The name is traditional: in the inertial frame it is simply the angular kinetic energy rewritten in terms of r, not a new force.)

## Radial equation of motion

Differentiating E_rel with respect to time (E constant, ℓ constant):

$$ μ r̈ = − dU/dr + ℓ²/(μ r³) = − dU_eff/dr $$

The radial acceleration is therefore the true radial force plus the outward term ℓ²/(μ r³) = μ r φ̇².

## Circular orbit

A circular orbit has r = r₀ constant, so r̈ = 0 and dU_eff/dr = 0 at r₀: −dU/dr + ℓ²/(μ r₀³) = 0. For gravitation, U = −k/r with k = G m1 m2, we have −dU/dr = −k/r₀², so the condition reads

$$ k/r₀² = ℓ²/(μ r₀³) ,  r₀ = ℓ²/(μ k) $$

With ℓ = μ r₀² ω this is μ ω² r₀ = k/r₀², i.e. the angular frequency of the relative orbit satisfies

$$ ω² = k/(μ r₀³) = G M/r₀³ $$

because k/μ = G m1 m2/μ = G M. This is Kepler's third law for the circular case (module 6).

## Checks and limits

Dimensions: ℓ²/(μ r²) is energy (ℓ is M L² T⁻¹). For ℓ = 0 the barrier vanishes and the motion is radial (a head-on fall). The effective potential for U = −k/r has a minimum at r₀ = ℓ²/(μk) with value −k/(2r₀); circular orbits have E = −k/(2r₀), a negative energy, indicating a bound state.

### 6. Core: gravitating pairs - Kepler's third law with total mass, binary stars, Earth-Moon

*Objective:* Show that both bodies orbit the centre of mass with the same period, derive T² = 4π² a³/(G(m1 + m2)) for circular relative orbits, and use it for a binary star and the Earth-Moon system.

## Both bodies circle the centre of mass

For an isolated gravitating pair take the CM at rest (choose the frame moving with V). Each body then describes a path about the CM. In the circular case, the separation is constant, a = |r|, and the line joining the bodies turns at a constant angular speed ω. By module 2, body 1 is at distance r1 = (m2/M) a from the CM and body 2 at r2 = (m1/M) a on the opposite side, so both circles have the same period T = 2π/ω and the speeds are v1 = ω r1 and v2 = ω r2.

## Third law with total mass

Newton's second law for body 1, with the gravitational force G m1 m2/a² providing the centripetal acceleration ω² r1:

$$ m1 ω² r1 = G m1 m2 /a² $$

Substitute r1 = (m2/M) a:

$$ ω² (m2/M) a = G m2/a² ⟹ ω² = G M/a³ $$

which agrees with the reduced-mass result of module 5 (ω² = k/(μ a³) = G M/a³ with k/μ = G M). In terms of the period T = 2π/ω:

$$ T² = 4π² a³ / ( G (m1 + m2) ) $$

For an elliptical relative orbit the same formula holds with a the semi-major axis of the relative orbit (a standard result from the reduced-mass Kepler problem, derived in the central-force topics; the textbook derivation is by solving μ r̈ = −G m1 m2 r̂/r², see Upadhyaya §5.16). Check the dimensions: G M/a³ is (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²)(kg)/m³ = s⁻², as ω² should be. Limit m2 ≫ m1: M ≈ m2 and the familiar T² = 4π² a³/(G m2) of a planet round a fixed Sun.

## Solar units

Measure T in years, a in astronomical units (AU) and masses in solar masses: T² = a³/(m1 + m2). Check with the Earth: T = 1 yr, a = 1 AU, M ≈ 1.

## Example 1: a binary star

Stars of 2 M☉ and 1 M☉ separated by 3 AU: T² = 27/3 = 9 yr², so T = 3.00 yr. The distances from the CM are r1 = (1/3)(3 AU) = 1 AU and r2 = (2/3)(3 AU) = 2 AU; the heavier star is nearer. The speeds are v1 = 2π(1 AU)/(3 yr) = 9.93 km s⁻¹ and v2 = 19.9 km s⁻¹. Check: m1 v1 = m2 v2 (2 × 9.93 = 1 × 19.86), as zero total momentum in the CM frame demands.

## Example 2: Earth and Moon

With M_E = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, m_M = 7.35 × 10²² kg and a = 3.84 × 10⁸ m, the CM is at (m_M/M) a = 4.67 × 10⁶ m = 4670 km from the Earth's centre, i.e. about 0.73 of the Earth's radius: the barycentre lies **inside** the Earth, so the Earth wobbles about it rather than orbiting it. With these rounded data the formula gives a period of 27.2 days (numerical N6, with more precise data, gives 27.3 days; the observed sidereal month is 27.32 days). If the Earth were taken fixed, the same rounded data would give 27.41 days, 0.6 % longer (27.45 days with N6's data). The reduced mass is 0.988 m_M. For the Sun–Jupiter pair (standard data, not in the extracts) the barycentre is about 7.4 × 10⁵ km from the Sun's centre, slightly outside the Sun's surface.

## What to take from this

Two bodies in orbit orbit their CM, not each other; Kepler's third law uses the **sum** of the masses, which matters when the masses are comparable and is negligible (a few parts per million) for the Earth round the Sun.

### 7. Core: the hydrogen atom, deuterium and positronium - reduced mass in spectra

*Objective:* Show that the Bohr energy levels depend on the reduced mass, obtain the corrected Rydberg constant, and compute the hydrogen-deuterium and positronium shifts.

## Why reduced mass appears

In the hydrogen atom the electron (mass m_e) and the proton (mass M_p ≈ 1836 m_e) attract each other with the Coulomb force. By module 3, the relative motion behaves as a particle of mass μ = m_e M_p/(m_e + M_p) in the same force. A nucleus is not fixed: both particles circle their common CM, which lies very close to the proton.

## Bohr model with reduced mass

Assumptions: circular relative orbit of radius r; Coulomb attraction k_e/r² with k_e = e²/(4π ε₀); relative angular momentum quantised, μ v r = n ħ (n = 1, 2, 3, ...), where v is the relative speed and the angular momentum is that of the relative motion in the CM frame (module 4).

Step 1 (dynamics, module 5): μ v²/r = k_e/r².

Step 2 (quantisation): v = n ħ/(μ r). Substitute: μ n² ħ²/(μ² r² r) = k_e/r², so n² ħ²/(μ r³) = k_e/r², giving

$$ r_n = n² ħ²/(μ k_e) $$

Step 3 (energy): E = ½ μ v² − k_e/r = ½ k_e/r − k_e/r = − k_e/(2r), so

$$ E_n = − μ k_e² /(2 ħ² n²) $$

Both the orbit radius and the energy depend on μ: E_n ∝ μ and r_n ∝ 1/μ. For an infinitely heavy nucleus μ = m_e.

## The Rydberg constant

The spectral wavenumbers 1/λ = R_M (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) therefore carry R_M = R_∞ μ/m_e = R_∞/(1 + m_e/M_nucleus), where R_∞ is the constant for an infinitely heavy nucleus. With the standard values R_∞ = 1.09737 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (an external constant, not derived in the extracts) and M_p/m_e = 1836.15:

$$ R_H = R_∞ /(1 + 1/1836.15) = 1.09678 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ $$

so μ_H = 0.99946 m_e, a decrease of 0.054 %. For deuterium, nuclear mass 3670.48 m_e: R_D = 1.09707 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (μ_D = 0.99973 m_e).

## Isotope shift of H-alpha (n = 3 → 2)

1/λ = R × (1/4 − 1/9) = 5R/36, so λ = 36/(5R). Hydrogen: λ_H = 656.47 nm. Deuterium: λ_D = 656.29 nm. The difference is 0.18 nm: the deuterium line is shorter in wavelength. This small shift is how deuterium was first recognised (standard history, flagged) and it is a common exam application. Check: the relative shift Δλ/λ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴ equals the relative difference of μ (0.99973/0.99946 − 1 = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴).

## Positronium

Positronium is an electron and a positron (same mass m_e). The reduced mass is μ = m_e/2, so all energy levels are half those of an infinitely heavy hydrogen (E_1 = −6.80 eV against −13.6 eV), the orbit radii are twice as large, and the wavelengths of the spectral lines are about twice those of hydrogen: for H-alpha about 1312 nm. This is the point used in the textbooks to confirm that μ_Ps is half the electron mass.

## Checks

Dimensions: μ k_e²/ħ² has units kg (J m)² /(J s)² = J. Every level scales linearly with μ: doubling the reduced mass doubles each binding energy.

Note on the wavelengths: the H-alpha values used in this module and in numerical N2 (656.47 nm for hydrogen, 656.29 nm for deuterium) are vacuum wavelengths. The commonly quoted 656.28 nm for hydrogen is the air value, so do not compare the two directly. The difference between H and D (0.18 nm) is what matters.

### 8. Core: diatomic molecules and the two-mass spring

*Objective:* Obtain the vibration frequency ν = (1/2π)√(k/μ) and the moment of inertia I = μ r₀² of a diatomic molecule, and use them with numbers.

## Model

A diatomic molecule such as HCl or CO is modelled as two point masses m1 and m2 held at an equilibrium separation r₀ by a force that behaves like a spring of force constant k (the 'bond'), with no external force. Mathur treats exactly this picture (an isolated two-body harmonic oscillator).

## Vibration

Take the line of the molecule as the x-axis with x1, x2 the positions, x = x1 − x2 > 0 the separation and ξ = x − r₀ the stretch. The spring force on particle 1 is −k ξ (restoring) and on particle 2 is +k ξ. Newton's laws: m1 ẍ1 = −k ξ, m2 ẍ2 = +k ξ. Divide and subtract exactly as in module 3: ξ̈ = ẍ1 − ẍ2 = −k ξ (1/m1 + 1/m2) = −k ξ/μ, so

$$ μ ξ̈ = − k ξ ,  ω = √(k/μ) ,  ν = (1/2π) √(k/μ) $$

The molecule vibrates as a single oscillator of mass μ. It is wrong to use m1 alone, or the total mass: the relative coordinate is what oscillates, and the CM can drift or accelerate without changing the relative motion.

Uniform gravity does not change this: if each particle also feels m_i g downward, r̈1 − r̈2 = (F/m1 + g) − (−F/m2 + g) = F/μ, the g's cancel.

## Numbers: HCl

Take m1 = 1 u and m2 = 35.5 u (Mathur's masses). μ = 35.5/36.5 = 0.973 u = 1.615 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. The fundamental frequency is ν = 8.7 × 10¹³ Hz, so

$$ k = 4π² ν² μ = 4π² (8.7 × 10¹³)² (1.615 × 10⁻²⁷) = 4.83 × 10² N m⁻¹ $$

(Mathur prints about 485 N m⁻¹, rounding the masses slightly differently.) For DCl, m1 = 2 u: μ = 2 × 35.5/37.5 = 1.893 u. If the force constant is the same for the two isotopic molecules, ν ∝ 1/√μ, so ν_DCl = 8.7 × 10¹³ × √(0.973/1.893) = 6.2 × 10¹³ Hz, about 0.72 times the HCl frequency. (The statement that k is unchanged by isotopic substitution is a standard physical assumption since the electronic binding is the same; flagged as outside the extracts.)

## Rotation

For rotation about an axis through the CM perpendicular to the bond, the moment of inertia of the rigid pair is I = m1 r1² + m2 r2² with r1 = m2 r₀/M and r2 = m1 r₀/M:

$$ I = (m1 m2² + m2 m1²) r₀²/M² = m1 m2 r₀²/M = μ r₀² $$

Dimension: kg m². The rotational kinetic energy is L²/(2I) = ½ μ r₀² ω². In quantum theory the rotational levels are E_J = J (J + 1) ħ²/(2I) (a standard result quoted without derivation), so the J = 0 → 1 transition has frequency ν = h/(4π² I). For CO take μ = 12 × 16/28 = 6.857 u = 1.139 × 10⁻²⁶ kg and r₀ = 0.1128 nm (standard value, flagged): I = 1.449 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m², ν = 1.158 × 10¹¹ Hz = 115.8 GHz, wavelength 2.59 mm. The observed millimetre line of CO is at a similar frequency, near 115 GHz; the rigid-rotor model is accurate to about half a percent.

## A two-mass spring

Masses 2 kg and 3 kg joined by a spring k = 120 N m⁻¹: μ = 1.2 kg and ω = √(120/1.2) = 10 rad s⁻¹ (f = 1.59 Hz). Using only the 2 kg mass would give 7.75 rad s⁻¹, which is wrong.

### 9. Master: collisions in the centre-of-mass frame and the energy that can be converted

*Objective:* Use T = ½ M V² + ½ μ u² to show that only ½ μ u² is available for conversion in a collision, and compute the maximum energy loss.

## Setup

This module connects the two-body reduction to the collisions of PH-001 (module 8 there). Two particles of masses m1 and m2 have lab velocities u1 and u2 before the collision. The CM velocity is V = (m1 u1 + m2 u2)/M and the relative velocity is u = u1 − u2. No external force acts during the collision, so V is the same before and after.

## Kinetic energy split

By module 4, the total kinetic energy is T = ½ M V² + ½ μ u². The first term belongs to the motion of the CM and **cannot be changed by the collision**, because V is constant. All energy conversion (heat, deformation, excitation, particle creation) comes out of the second term, ½ μ u², the kinetic energy in the CM frame.

## CM-frame velocities

In the CM frame particle 1 has velocity u1′ = u1 − V = (m2/M) u and particle 2 has u2′ = u2 − V = −(m1/M) u. Check: m1 u1′ + m2 u2′ = (m1 m2/M − m2 m1/M) u = 0. Each has momentum of size μ u in opposite directions, since m1 (m2/M) = μ.

## The maximum loss

The most kinetic energy that can be lost is the whole CM-frame energy, which happens when the particles stick together (perfectly inelastic): both then move with V and the final kinetic energy is ½ M V². So

$$ ΔT_max = ½ μ u² $$

In an elastic collision the relative speed is unchanged (only its direction rotates), so ½ μ u² is unchanged and no energy is converted.

## Worked example

A 1 kg block moving at 6 m s⁻¹ hits a 2 kg block at rest. V = 6/3 = 2 m s⁻¹, u = 6 m s⁻¹, μ = 2/3 kg. Initial T = ½ (1)(36) = 18 J = ½ (3)(4) + ½ (2/3)(36) = 6 + 12 J. Maximum loss ½ μ u² = 12 J (two-thirds of the initial energy); final kinetic energy 6 J for the stuck-together blocks. Check the dimension: kg m² s⁻² = J.

## Limits

If m2 ≫ m1: μ ≈ m1, and the whole ½ m1 u1² can be converted when the target is at rest and heavy (a ball sticking to a wall). For a target at rest the convertible fraction is μ/m1 = m2/(m1 + m2): it tends to 1 for a very heavy target, equals 1/2 for equal masses, and tends to 0 for a very light target, which is the least effective absorber.

## Scattering

The same reduction underlies Rutherford scattering (a separate topic): the scattering of a light projectile by a nucleus is the relative motion of one particle of mass μ in the Coulomb field, viewed in the CM frame, and the laboratory angles follow from the transformation back using V (PH-001 module 8).

## The ten-mark derivation: structure

For 'Show that the two-body problem reduces to a one-body problem with reduced mass' a complete answer has the following parts (this is a plan for your own effort, not an official marking scheme):

1. Statement of assumptions: isolated pair, central force, inertial frame, r = r1 − r2 from 2 to 1.
2. Newton's laws for each particle: m1 r̈1 = F, m2 r̈2 = −F.
3. Centre of mass: M R̈ = 0.
4. Relative equation: r̈ = F/μ, with the algebra 1/m1 + 1/m2 = 1/μ shown.
5. Interpretation: one particle of mass μ, with the force law keeping the true masses.
6. Recovery of r1 and r2 and a limit (m2 ≫ m1) and a dimension check.

For kinetic energy and angular momentum, show the cross-term cancellation line by line (module 4); the examiner looks for it.

## Guided problem A: reading μ in a limit

Question. Find how much smaller the reduced mass of the Earth–Moon system is than the Moon's mass (m_M/M_E = 0.0123).

Thinking aloud. Use μ = m_M/(1 + m_M/M_E). The ratio m_M/M_E = 0.0123, so μ = m_M/1.0123 = 0.9878 m_M; the reduction is 1.2 %. Limit check: m_M/M_E → 0 gives μ → m_M. So the Moon's motion about the Earth is described, to 1.2 % accuracy in inertia, by treating the Earth as fixed; but the CM shift (0.73 of the Earth's radius from the centre) is large in absolute terms.

## Guided problem B: two equal stars

Question. Two stars of 2 × 10³⁰ kg each are 1.5 × 10¹¹ m apart in circular motion about their CM. Find the period and each star's speed (G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ SI).

Thinking aloud. Equal masses: M = 4 × 10³⁰ kg, each star at a/2 from the CM. T = 2π √(a³/(G M)): a³ = 3.375 × 10³³, G M = 2.67 × 10²⁰; ratio 1.264 × 10¹³; square root 3.556 × 10⁶ s; T = 2.234 × 10⁷ s = 258.6 days = 0.708 yr. Speed v = ω (a/2) = (2π/T)(7.5 × 10¹⁰) = 2.81 × 10⁻⁷ × 7.5 × 10¹⁰ = 21.1 km s⁻¹. Check: centripetal acceleration v²/(a/2) = 5.94 × 10⁻³ m s⁻² should equal G m/a² = 5.94 × 10⁻³. Both agree. Interpretation: each star is pulled by the other at distance a (not a/2), a common error being to use a/2 in the force.

## Thinking sequence for any two-body question

Decide the frame; choose R and r; write both equations; add for R, subtract for r; say what μ is; identify the force law with true masses; check limits and dimensions.

### Notes: Definitions

- Isolated two-particle system: two particles interacting only with each other, with no external force.
- Centre of mass R = (m1 r1 + m2 r2)/M, M = m1 + m2; relative position r = r1 − r2 (from particle 2 to particle 1).
- Reduced mass μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2), equivalently 1/μ = 1/m1 + 1/m2.
- Central force: force along the line joining the particles, size depending only on the separation.
- Barycentre: the centre of mass of an astronomical system.
- Effective potential: U_eff(r) = U(r) + ℓ²/(2μ r²), with ℓ = μ r² φ̇ the (constant) relative angular momentum.

### Notes: Governing laws and equations

- M R̈ = 0 (isolated pair): P = M V constant.
- μ r̈ = F(r) r̂, with the force law containing the true masses (for gravity F = −G m1 m2/r²).
- r1 = R + (m2/M) r and r2 = R − (m1/M) r.
- T = ½ M V² + ½ μ v² and L = R × P + r × (μ v).
- E_rel = ½ μ ṙ² + ℓ²/(2μ r²) + U(r), μ r̈ = −dU_eff/dr.
- T_orbit² = 4π² a³/(G(m1 + m2)).
- Maximum energy lost in a collision: ½ μ u² with u the relative speed.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: reduction to one body

- m1 r̈1 = F, m2 r̈2 = −F (third law, central force).
- Add: M R̈ = 0, so the CM moves uniformly.
- Divide by masses and subtract: r̈ = F(1/m1 + 1/m2) = F/μ.
- Interpretation: one particle of mass μ in the force F(r) r̂; recover r1 and r2 from R and r.
- Alternative: work in a frame attached to particle 2 and add the fictitious force m1 F/m2 (link PH-002).

### Notes: Limits and standard values of μ

- Reduced mass in common cases; always less than either mass.

### Notes: Applications: formulae

- Binary: r1 = (m2/M) a, r2 = (m1/M) a; ω² = G M/a³.
- Hydrogen-like: r_n = n² ħ²/(μ k_e), E_n = −μ k_e²/(2 ħ² n²), R_M = R_∞ μ/m_e = R_∞/(1 + m_e/M_nucleus).
- Diatomic vibration: ν = (1/2π) √(k/μ). Rotation: I = μ r₀², ν_{0→1} = h/(4π² I).
- Two-mass spring: ω = √(k/μ); amplitudes of the two masses are in the inverse ratio of the masses.

### Notes: Worked numbers to remember

- Hydrogen: R_H = 1.09678 × 10⁷ m⁻¹; deuterium: R_D = 1.09707 × 10⁷ m⁻¹; H-alpha 656.47 nm versus 656.29 nm (vacuum wavelengths; the commonly quoted 656.28 nm for H is the air value).
- HCl (μ = 0.973 u, ν = 8.7 × 10¹³ Hz): k ≈ 483 N m⁻¹; DCl frequency ratio 0.717.
- Binary 2 M☉ + 1 M☉ at 3 AU: T = 3 yr; radii 1 AU and 2 AU from the CM.
- Earth–Moon CM at 4670 km from the Earth's centre, 0.73 of the Earth's radius.
- 1 kg at 6 m s⁻¹ hitting 2 kg at rest: maximum loss 12 J of 18 J.

### Notes: Commonly confused ideas

- Replacing the masses inside the force law by μ: the force keeps the true masses; μ replaces only the inertia in μ r̈ = F.
- Taking the distance of a star from the CM as the separation in the gravitational force.
- Thinking the heavier body stays at rest: both orbit the CM, with radii inversely proportional to the masses.
- Using m1 or M instead of μ in the vibration frequency of a molecule.
- Treating the effective-potential barrier as a new force: it is the angular kinetic energy written in terms of r.

### Notes: Limits and exceptions

- The reduction needs an isolated pair with an internal force obeying the third law along the line joining the particles.
- With a third body or an external force the CM equation changes; the relative equation changes too unless the external field is uniform (acceleration the same for both).
- The Bohr-model results are non-relativistic and neglect nuclear size; the reduced mass is the leading correction only.
- The rigid-rotor and harmonic-oscillator models of a molecule ignore stretching and anharmonicity.

### Memory skeleton

- Isolated pair, central force obeying the third law
- CM R = (m1 r1 + m2 r2)/M; relative r = r1 − r2
- Add: M R̈ = 0; divide and subtract: μ r̈ = F(r) r̂, μ = m1 m2/(m1 + m2)
- T and L split into CM and relative parts; effective potential
- Gravitating pairs: Kepler III with m1 + m2; radii inverse to masses
- Spectra (R_M = R_∞ μ/m_e), molecules (√(k/μ), I = μ r₀²), collisions (½μu²)

### Last-week summary

- m1 r̈1 = F, m2 r̈2 = −F; add: M R̈ = 0; divide and subtract: μ r̈ = F, 1/μ = 1/m1 + 1/m2.
- Force keeps true masses (F = −G m1 m2/r²); only inertia becomes μ. μ < either mass; equal masses m/2.
- r1 = R + (m2/M) r, r2 = R − (m1/M) r; T = ½MV² + ½μv²; T_orbit² = 4π²a³/(G(m1 + m2)).
- E_n ∝ μ; ν = (1/2π)√(k/μ); I = μ r₀²; maximum collision loss ½μu².
