## GS-198: Fifth and Sixth Schedules

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · States, local governance and federalism

### Bridge: Tribe, Scheduled Tribe, Scheduled Area, tribal area: four different words

Four words look alike and are not.

- **Tribe** is an everyday and anthropological word for a community with its own culture, customs and, often, its own way of holding land and forest. By itself it has no legal effect.
- **Scheduled Tribe (ST)** is a legal status. A community becomes a Scheduled Tribe when it is listed under the Constitution (standard: the President notifies the list under Article 342 and Parliament can change it by law; verify). The status belongs to the community, not to the address: an ST family living in a big city is still ST.
- **Scheduled Area** is a *place*: an area declared by the President under the **Fifth Schedule**. Only in such places do the special Fifth Schedule rules operate.
- **Tribal area** is the *Sixth Schedule's* word for a place in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura or Mizoram that has been constituted as an autonomous district.

A **Schedule** is a list or table placed at the end of the Constitution to hold detail that would clog the Articles. An **Article** is a numbered provision; a **Part** is a group of Articles. Articles 244 and 244A sit in **Part X**; the Fifth and Sixth Schedules are the detail behind Article 244.

A note on language: the old passage that Laxmikanth quotes uses the word 'aboriginals' and calls some areas 'anthropological specimens'. That is wording from an older period and should not be copied into answers. Write 'Scheduled Tribes', 'tribal communities' or 'Adivasi communities' and give the positive reason: distinct institutions, customary law and land systems.

### Bridge: Autonomy, self-rule and secession

**Autonomy** means that a group or region has the power to decide certain matters for itself while remaining inside a larger constitutional system. **Self-rule** means that a community governs its own affairs, especially customs, land and village administration, through its own institutions. **Secession** means leaving the country. NCERT's federalism chapter asks the learner to separate autonomy from secession; the idea in its text is that demands for autonomy are political demands to share and exercise power within the Constitution, which a responsive federation should be able to meet.

The key point for this topic is the source of the power. The powers of a Sixth Schedule council are not inherent or sovereign; the Constitution grants them, and Laxmikanth stresses that autonomous districts 'do not fall outside the executive authority of the state concerned'. So the Sixth Schedule is a *delegation of self-rule inside the State*, not a separate State. The same Constitution lets the Governor reorganise the districts, assent to council laws and, after a commission reports, dissolve a council (module 6).

Why offer autonomy at all? NCERT's reading is that national unity cannot be built by streamlining differences, and that forced unity generates alienation. It also warns that special provisions for north-eastern tribal communities have not fully stemmed alienation or insurgency. Keep both halves for Mains: autonomy is the Constitution's response to diversity, and it succeeds only where the institutions work and people trust them.

### 1. Orientation: Article 244 and the two systems

*Objective:* Say in four sentences what Article 244 does, which Schedule serves which States, and which neighbouring lessons already cover related ground.

## The problem the Constitution is solving

India has one standard machinery of State government: the Governor, the Council of Ministers, the State legislature and, since the 1990s, panchayats and municipalities. Some regions, however, are home to communities with their own customs, languages and ways of holding land and forest. If the standard machinery is applied unchanged, those communities can lose land to outsiders and lose a voice in decisions. The Constitution therefore sets up a **special system of administration** for such regions.

Laxmikanth opens Chapter 41 with the key sentence: **Article 244 in Part X** envisages a special system for areas designated as 'scheduled areas' and 'tribal areas'. Two Schedules then do the work.

## Two systems, one Article

- **Fifth Schedule**: the administration and control of **Scheduled Areas** and **Scheduled Tribes** in any State *except* Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. It works mainly through the **Governor and the President**. (In the Constitution's own numbering this is Article 244(1); standard, verify.)
- **Sixth Schedule**: the administration of **tribal areas** in exactly those four north-eastern States. It works through elected **autonomous district and regional councils**. (Article 244(2); standard, verify.)

The simplest picture: under the Fifth Schedule the State machinery stays in charge but is *adjusted and supervised* (the Governor can change how laws apply and the Centre carries a larger responsibility). Under the Sixth Schedule a *new local layer* of self-government is created, with its own law-making, courts and taxes.

## The Articles to keep in view

| Article | Subject (Laxmikanth Table 41.2 and Chapter 14) |
|---|---|
| 244 | Administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas |
| 244A | Formation of an autonomous State comprising certain tribal areas in Assam |
| 275 | Grants-in-aid from the Centre, including for ST welfare and for raising the level of administration of Scheduled Areas |
| 339 | Control of the Union over the administration of Scheduled Areas and the welfare of Scheduled Tribes |

## What this lesson does not repeat

Part XXI special provisions (Articles 371A to 371J), such as Nagaland's protection of customary law, are in **GS-200**, modules 4 and 5, which also give a one-paragraph outline of the two Schedules. The Governor's discretion is in **GS-189**, module 6. The Panchayat extension to Scheduled Areas (PESA) is in **GS-195**, module 7. Here we go deeper on the two Schedules themselves and on how to compare them.

**Study order.** Modules 1 to 7 are the first pass. Module 8 (the comparison) is the most testable single page. Modules 9 and 10 are the examination layer and can be deferred to your first revision cycle.

### 2. Fifth Schedule I: how an area becomes a Scheduled Area

*Objective:* Explain who declares a Scheduled Area, how the declaration can change, the four commonly cited criteria (flagged), and the ten States on Laxmikanth's 2019 list.

## Meaning and reason

A **Scheduled Area** is an area that the **President** has declared under the Fifth Schedule. Laxmikanth explains that such areas are treated differently because the communities there need special effort to improve their condition, so the whole of the normal administrative machinery of a State is not extended to them and the Central government has somewhat greater responsibility. (The older quotation uses outdated words; the stable idea is that normal machinery is adjusted and the Centre shares responsibility.)

## Declaration and change

Source (p. 898): the President is empowered to declare an area a Scheduled Area. He can **increase or decrease** its area, **alter its boundary lines**, **rescind** the designation or make **fresh orders** redesignating an area, in **consultation with the Governor** of the State concerned. Table 41.3 adds a legal milestone: the **Fifth Schedule to the Constitution (Amendment) Act, 1976** empowered the President to increase the area of a Scheduled Area after consulting the Governor, and to rescind earlier orders or make fresh orders redefining the area. Note the pattern: a **Presidential order**, not a Parliamentary Act and not a Governor's decision, creates the area. The Governor's role is consultation.

## Criteria (not in the extract; flagged)

The extracts do not list criteria. The administrative criteria commonly cited, drawn from the early Scheduled Area orders and the Dhebar Commission and repeated in Ministry of Tribal Affairs material, are four (standard; verify against the Ministry's current text):

1. **Preponderance of tribal population** (some secondary sources add 'not less than 50 per cent'; the official criterion says only preponderance; verify).
2. **Compactness and reasonable size** of the area.
3. A **viable administrative unit** such as a district, block or taluk.
4. **Economic backwardness** of the area compared with neighbouring areas.

These are guidelines, not wording inside the Constitution; the Constitution supplies the power and the procedure. That is why an examiner can ask about the 'unit' of a Scheduled Area (the 2023 PYQ in module 9).

## States with Scheduled Areas

Laxmikanth's note (p. 905, 'at present (2019)') lists **ten States**: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha and Rajasthan. *Flag: verify the present list; it is a snapshot and Presidential orders can change it.* Two reading rules:

- **Scheduled Areas are areas, not whole States.** In many States only some districts or blocks are Scheduled (standard; verify). A State is on the list because it has at least one such area.
- **Having Scheduled Tribes does not make an area Scheduled.** For example, Karnataka and Kerala have Scheduled Tribe populations but do not appear on Laxmikanth's list of ten (verify).

The ten States have a rough map pattern: central-eastern India (Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh), the west (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan), the south (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana) and one Himalayan State (Himachal Pradesh). None of the four Sixth Schedule States is on the list, which follows from the Schedule's own scope.

### 3. Fifth Schedule II: executive power, the Governor's tools and the Centre

*Objective:* State what the State and the Centre can each do in a Scheduled Area, and describe the Governor's tools: directions on Acts, regulations for peace and good government, and land and money-lending regulation.

## Who holds executive power

A common wrong belief is that Fifth Schedule status takes an area away from the State government. The source says the reverse (p. 898): **the executive power of the State extends to the Scheduled Areas**. What is added is:

- a **special responsibility of the Governor**: he submits a **report to the President on the administration of such areas**, annually or whenever the President requires; and
- a power of the **Centre to give directions to the State** about the administration of such areas (Article 339, listed in Table 41.2 as 'control of the Union over the administration of Scheduled Areas and the welfare of Scheduled Tribes'; the clause number 339(2) is standard knowledge, verify).

So the picture is: **State administers, Governor reports, Centre can direct.** The Centre does not 'take over the total administration', and a local body does not 'assume total administration'. Both are wrong pictures that appear as distractors (the 2025 PYQ in module 9).

## The Governor's tools

The source (pp. 898-899) gives the Governor three connected powers:

**Tool 1: Direction on Acts.** He can direct that a particular **Act of Parliament or of the State legislature** does not apply to a Scheduled Area, or applies with specified **modifications and exceptions**. This lets general law be adapted to local conditions.

**Tool 2: Regulations for peace and good government.** After **consulting the Tribes Advisory Council** (module 4), he can make **regulations for the peace and good government** of a Scheduled Area. A regulation may even **repeal or amend an Act of Parliament or State law** that applies there.

**Tool 3: Land and money-lending regulation (the most-quoted use of Tool 2).** Such regulations may **prohibit or restrict the transfer of land** by or among members of Scheduled Tribes, **regulate the allotment of land** to Scheduled Tribe members, and **regulate the business of money-lending** in relation to Scheduled Tribes.

**The safeguard.** All such regulations need the **assent of the President**. A regulation is not complete merely because the Governor has made it.

## Two points of care

- The Schedule *enables* the Governor to restrict land transfer through regulations (State regulations and laws on tribal land exist; verify the State examples you meet). It does not, merely by declaring an area Scheduled, freeze every land transaction in that area. This matters for the 2022 PYQ (module 9).
- The extract does not say whether the Governor must follow ministerial advice when making these regulations. Laxmikanth's lists of the Governor's discretion (GS-189, module 6) include the royalty dispute under the Sixth Schedule (paragraph 9(2)) and, among the special responsibilities listed on p. 696, Assam's tribal areas, but not a general Fifth Schedule discretion. Treat the question as open and verify before asserting either way.

Separately, Article 19(5), as Laxmikanth explains it, permits reasonable restrictions on the freedom to move and settle in India for the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe. This is why outsiders' entry and settlement in tribal areas can be restricted.

### 4. Fifth Schedule III: Tribes Advisory Council, commissions, PESA, land law and rights context

*Objective:* Describe the Tribes Advisory Council and the two commissions, place PESA and the Forest Rights Act in the picture without repeating GS-195, and state the Samatha point as a flagged context.

## The Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)

The source (p. 898) says each State having Scheduled Areas **has to establish** a Tribes Advisory Council 'to advise on the welfare and advancement of the Scheduled Tribes'. It consists of **20 members, three-fourths of whom are representatives of the Scheduled Tribes in the State legislative assembly**. A similar council **can also be established in a State having Scheduled Tribes but no Scheduled Areas, if the President so directs**. Three points follow:

- The TAC is **advisory**: it advises; it does not legislate. The Governor must *consult* it before making regulations (module 3).
- The 20 is Laxmikanth's number; the Fifth Schedule's own text is commonly read as 'not more than twenty' (standard; verify).
- The condition for the second kind of TAC is a Scheduled Tribe population without Scheduled Areas, so TACs are not confined to the ten States of module 2.

## Administration commissions

The Constitution requires the President to appoint a **commission to report on the administration of Scheduled Areas and the welfare of Scheduled Tribes**. He may do so at any time but **compulsorily after ten years of the commencement of the Constitution**. Laxmikanth records two: the **Dhebar Commission** (appointed 1960, report 1961) and, after four decades, the **Bhuria Commission** (appointed 2002, chaired by Dilip Singh Bhuria, report 2004). Notice what the dates show: the ten-year duty was met once and the next commission came after four decades, so the rule is not an 'every ten years' rule.

## PESA, in one paragraph

Part IX of the Constitution (Panchayats) does not extend of its own force to Scheduled Areas; Parliament extended it with modifications through the **Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996**. The Gram Sabha is the nucleus; its list of powers (minor forest produce, intoxicants, land alienation, money-lending) is in **GS-195, module 7**. The link to this lesson: PESA works inside Fifth Schedule areas and supplements the Governor's regulation power with village-level rights.

## Context to know, flagged (not in the extracts; verify each)

- **Samatha v State of Andhra Pradesh (1997)**: the Supreme Court is reported to have held that, in Scheduled Areas, transfer of land to non-tribals, including government land leased to private parties for mining, is not allowed, and to have treated such transfers as null and void. This is the judgment commonly paired with the 2019 PYQ (module 9). Verify the holding before quoting it in an answer.
- **Forest Rights Act, 2006** (Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act): recognises individual and community forest rights and gives a role to the Gram Sabha. It is a national law, not limited to Scheduled Areas (verify).
- **Land law**: several States have their own regulations or Acts restricting transfer of tribal land (verify the specific State Act before citing it).

Why list these? They show the *legal ladder* against land alienation: the Constitution enables (Governor's regulation), State laws prohibit, courts enforce, and the Gram Sabha consents. The ladder fails mostly at enforcement, a point used in the Mains framework of module 10.

### 5. Sixth Schedule I: the idea, the four States and the ten tribal areas

*Objective:* Explain why only four States have the Sixth Schedule, what an autonomous district is, how the Governor can reshape districts, and list the ten tribal areas State by State.

## Why only four States?

The Sixth Schedule contains special provisions for the **tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram**. Laxmikanth quotes an older justification: in these four States the tribal communities had not assimilated much of the life and ways of other people around them and still had their roots in their own culture and customs, whereas tribal people elsewhere had, more or less, adopted the culture of the majority around them; so a 'sizeable amount of autonomy' was given for self-government. (The quotation's word 'anthropological specimens' is outdated and should not be reused. Use the positive reason: distinct, intact customary institutions.) Standard background, flagged: the Constituent Assembly's sub-committee for the north-east, led by Gopinath Bordoloi, shaped this design (verify).

## The autonomous district

The central idea (p. 900) is that the tribal areas in the four States are **constituted as autonomous districts**, but they **do not fall outside the executive authority of the State** concerned. So an autonomous district is a district of the State that has a special council with its own powers; the State government does not vanish.

The **Governor** is the shaper of this map. He can **organise and reorganise** the autonomous districts: increase or decrease their areas, change their names, define their boundaries. If an autonomous district has **different tribes**, he can divide it into several **autonomous regions**, each with its own **regional council**.

## The ten tribal areas (Table 41.1, 2019)

| State | Tribal areas (autonomous districts) |
|---|---|
| Assam | North Cachar Hills District; Karbi Anglong District; Bodoland Territorial Areas District |
| Meghalaya | Khasi Hills District; Jaintia Hills District; Garo Hills District |
| Tripura | Tripura Tribal Areas District |
| Mizoram | Chakma District; Mara District; Lai District |

That is 3 + 3 + 1 + 3 = **10** areas, which matches Laxmikanth's note that 'there are a total of ten tribal areas'. Flag: in later usage North Cachar Hills is known as Dima Hasao (standard; verify), and the list is a 2019 snapshot.

## Three quick contrasts with the Fifth Schedule

- **Name**: 'tribal areas' (Sixth) against 'Scheduled Areas' (Fifth).
- **Creation**: autonomous districts are named in the Schedule's own table and reshaped by the Governor; Scheduled Areas are declared and altered by the President.
- **Body**: elected district council (Sixth) against an advisory Tribes Advisory Council (Fifth).

Remember also that being in the north-east is not enough. **Nagaland, Manipur and Arunachal Pradesh** have special provisions under Articles 371A, 371C and 371H (GS-200, module 4) but do not appear in Table 41.1. A guess by geography ('north-east means Sixth Schedule') fails on exactly these States.

### 6. Sixth Schedule II: councils, their four kinds of power and the Governor

*Objective:* State the composition and term of a district council as Laxmikanth gives them, list its legislative, judicial, executive and financial powers, and explain how Acts of Parliament and the State apply and how the Governor supervises.

## Composition and term (Laxmikanth p. 900)

Each autonomous district has a **district council of 30 members**: **26 elected on adult franchise** and **4 nominated by the Governor**. Elected members hold office for **five years** (unless the council is dissolved earlier); nominated members hold office **at the Governor's pleasure**. Each autonomous region also has a **separate regional council**.

Flag, because this is a rule with exceptions: the 26 + 4 pattern is Laxmikanth's description of the general scheme. The Schedule's own text is commonly read as 'not more than thirty' members with 'not more than four' nominated, and individual councils differ (the Bodoland Territorial Council is larger, and Tripura's council is reported to have a different split; verify each before quoting a number). Flagged figures for the named exceptions (standard knowledge; verify): the Bodoland Territorial Council has up to 46 members (40 elected, 6 nominated); the Tripura Tribal Areas Autonomous District Council has 30 members (28 elected, 2 nominated).

## The four kinds of power

**1. Legislative.** The district and regional councils administer their areas and can make laws on **specified matters**: land, forests, canal water, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance of property, marriage and divorce, social customs and so on. **All such laws need the Governor's assent.**

**2. Judicial.** Within their territories the councils can constitute **village councils or courts** to try suits and cases between members of the tribes, and they **hear appeals** from them. The **jurisdiction of the High Court** over these cases is specified by the Governor.

**3. Executive.** The district council can **establish, construct or manage** primary schools, dispensaries, markets, ferries, fisheries, roads and so on. It can make **regulations controlling money-lending and trading by non-tribals**, but such regulations **need the Governor's assent**.

**4. Financial.** The councils can **assess and collect land revenue** and impose **certain specified taxes**. A share of mining royalties is also due to them: Table 41.3 records that the 1988 amendment fixed a time-limit for making over the share of royalties, and Laxmikanth's Governor chapter (pp. 694 and 697) records that if the amount is disputed, **the Governor decides it in his discretion and his decision is final** (paragraph 9(2)).

## How Acts apply

The Acts of Parliament or of the State legislature **do not apply** to autonomous districts and regions, **or apply with specified modifications and exceptions** (p. 901). Laxmikanth's note 5 on p. 905 says *who* gives the direction:

- **Assam**: the **Governor**, for both Acts of Parliament and Acts of the State legislature.
- **Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram**: the **President** for Acts of Parliament; the **Governor** for Acts of the State legislature.

## Supervision

The **Governor can appoint a commission** to examine and report on any matter relating to the administration of the autonomous districts or regions, and **may dissolve** a district or regional council on the commission's recommendation. So the Governor reshapes, assents, supervises and can dissolve; the council governs day to day.

## One more tool you may meet

Laxmikanth's 1988 amendment row says the Governors of Tripura and Mizoram act 'in their discretion' in some functions, and the 1995 row makes consultation with the two Assam councils compulsory when the Governor uses his discretionary powers there. Quote these only with the Act in the table.

### 7. Sixth Schedule III: amendments, Bodoland, Article 244A and the grants in Article 275(1)

*Objective:* Trace the seven Parliamentary amendments in Table 41.3, explain the Bodoland Territorial Council, define Article 244A, and say what Article 275(1) adds.

## The amendments in Table 41.3

Laxmikanth lists seven Parliamentary laws on these Schedules. Learn them in date order.

| Year | Law | What it did |

Standard background, flagged: each Schedule contains a paragraph allowing Parliament to amend it by ordinary law, which the text says is not an Article 368 amendment (verify the paragraph). Second, do not mix the **Bodoland Act of 2003** with the **92nd Constitution Amendment Act 2003**, which concerned the Eighth Schedule languages (standard; verify).

## The Bodoland Territorial Council (2003)

Laxmikanth (p. 904) says the Act of 2003 gave effect to the **Memorandum of Settlement of 10 February 2003** between the Centre, the Government of Assam and the Bodo Liberation Tigers. It (i) added the **Bodoland Territorial Areas District** to Assam's tribal areas; (ii) created an autonomous self-governing body, the **Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC)** within Assam; (iii) gave it **legislative, administrative and financial powers** over specified subjects; and (iv) provided **adequate safeguards for non-tribals** in the area. Flag: the later 2020 Bodo accord and the present size of the council are post-extract developments (verify).

## Article 244A: an autonomous State inside Assam

Table 41.2 lists **Article 244A**: formation of an autonomous State comprising certain tribal areas in Assam, with a local legislature or council of ministers or both. Standard background, flagged: Article 244A was added by the 22nd Amendment of 1969 (the same Amendment that added Article 371B, which Laxmikanth's Chapter 37 note 6 credits to the 22nd Amendment), and Meghalaya was first formed as an autonomous State within Assam before becoming a full State (verify the dates). It is a rarely used device; Mains answers should mention it as the Constitution's 'higher rung' beyond district councils.

## Money: Article 275(1)

Laxmikanth's Chapter 14 (p. 350) says the Constitution provides **specific grants** under Article 275 for promoting **the welfare of Scheduled Tribes** in a State and for **raising the level of administration of Scheduled Areas**, 'including the State of Assam'. These statutory grants are made on the Finance Commission's recommendation (see GS-193). Chapter 3 (p. 116) shows the Sixth Schedule as linked to both **Articles 244 and 275**. So grants link both systems.

### 8. Master view: Fifth against Sixth Schedule, with edge-case tests

*Objective:* Reproduce the comparison table from memory and test each shortcut rule against at least three edge cases before trusting it.

## The comparison table

| Feature | Fifth Schedule | Sixth Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Article hook | Article 244(1) (standard; verify); Article 275 grants | Article 244(2) (standard; verify); Article 275 grants |
| Where | Scheduled Areas in States other than the four (ten States on the 2019 list) | Tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram (ten areas) |
| How created | President declares, alters or rescinds by order, consulting the Governor | Autonomous districts named in the Schedule; Governor organises and reorganises them |
| Special body | Tribes Advisory Council: advisory, 20 members, three-fourths ST (Laxmikanth; Schedule text: not more than twenty, as nearly as may be three-fourths ST; verify) | District council (30 members: 26 elected, 4 nominated; Schedule text: not more than thirty, not more than four nominated; the BTC differs; verify) and regional councils |
| Law-making | Governor's directions on Acts and regulations; President's assent to regulations | Council laws on specified matters; Governor's assent |
| Justice | No special tribal courts in the extract | Village councils or courts for suits between tribes; councils hear appeals |
| Money | Article 275(1) grants | Land revenue and specified taxes; royalty share; Article 275 grants |
| Centre's role | Governor's annual report; Article 339 directions; Presidential commission | Governor's commission; Parliament's Acts apply with modifications (President directs in three States) |
| State executive | Extends to the area | Autonomous districts remain within the State's executive authority |
| Panchayats (Part IX) | Extended with modifications by PESA 1996 (GS-195) | Exempt from Part IX (GS-195, module 7) |

## Edge-case tests of the shortcut rules

**Rule A: 'Fifth means Governor and President; Sixth means elected councils.'** *Edge 1:* the Governor gives assent to council laws in the Sixth Schedule, so he is central there too. *Edge 2:* the President directs the application of Parliament's Acts in Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram (note 5, p. 905). *Edge 3:* Scheduled Areas also have elected panchayats under PESA. Verdict: useful as a first sorting rule, wrong if used to answer 'who gives the direction'. Better rule: ask what the document says about *the council's own law-making*; only the Sixth Schedule has it.

**Rule B: 'Sixth means north-east; Fifth means the rest.'** *Edge 1:* Nagaland, Manipur and Arunachal Pradesh are north-eastern but have neither Sixth Schedule areas in Table 41.1 nor the Fifth Schedule's ten-State list. *Edge 2:* Sikkim is a north-eastern State with neither Sixth Schedule tribal areas nor a Scheduled Area on the 2019 list; its protection is Article 371F (GS-200 module 4; standard knowledge, verify). *Edge 3:* a State's Scheduled Tribes do not make a Scheduled Area (Karnataka, module 2). Verdict: replace with 'Sixth means the four named States; Fifth means a State with a declared Scheduled Area'.

**Rule C: 'Fifth Schedule areas leave the State government's control; Sixth gives self-government.'** *Edge 1:* Source p. 898: the State's executive power extends to Scheduled Areas. *Edge 2:* Source p. 900: autonomous districts do not fall outside the State's executive authority. *Edge 3:* the 2025 PYQ statement that a local body assumes total administration. Verdict: false in both systems; both are adjustments inside the State.

**Rule D: 'The Governor's assent applies only to Sixth Schedule laws.'** *Edge 1:* every ordinary State Bill, including a State law restricting tribal land transfer that operates in Scheduled Areas, needs the Governor's assent under Article 200 (GS-189 module 4), so the rule is false for State laws anywhere. *Edge 2:* a Fifth Schedule regulation is made by the Governor but is completed only by the President's assent. *Edge 3:* in the Sixth Schedule both council laws and the money-lending and trading regulations need the Governor's assent. Verdict line: Fifth Schedule regulation: the President assents; Sixth Schedule council law or regulation: the Governor assents; ordinary State law anywhere: the Governor under Article 200. The rule fails at Edges 1 and 2.

**Rule E: 'Both have an advisory body.'** *Edge 1:* the Tribes Advisory Council is advisory only, while a Sixth Schedule district council is an elected body with its own law-making power, so the two are not the same kind of body. *Edge 2:* the Sixth Schedule has no Tribes Advisory Council; its supervision is through the Governor's commission and the power to dissolve a council. *Edge 3:* a Tribes Advisory Council can be required by the President's direction in a State that has no Scheduled Area, so a council may exist without a Scheduled Area (module 4). Verdict: false as a statement about the Sixth Schedule; the Tribes Advisory Council belongs to the Fifth Schedule system.

Use the table as the answer-sheet; use the tests when an option sounds neat.

### 9. Examination application: PYQ patterns and thought-aloud elimination

*Objective:* Recognise the three Prelims patterns in the five verified Prelims PYQs and the Mains pattern in the 2026 Ladakh question, and practise elimination without treating any analysis as an official key.

The six verified PYQs fall into three patterns. Every discussion below is *analysis*, not an official answer key; check the official key afterwards.

## Pattern 1: 'Which Schedule?' (2019 Q50)

The question asks under which Schedule a transfer of tribal land to private parties for mining can be declared null and void. Eliminate by purpose: the Third Schedule holds oaths, the Ninth holds laws protected from challenge, the Twelfth lists municipal functions, so only the Fifth concerns tribal land. The question also draws on the Samatha judgment (module 4; verify). Lesson: a vocabulary of Schedules scores marks.

## Pattern 2: 'What does Fifth Schedule status actually do?' (2022 Q73, 2025 Q56)

2022: option (b) describes the Sixth Schedule's council; (c) UT and (d) 'Special Category State' are unrelated constitutional ideas (Special Category is a financial classification, not in this lesson). That leaves (a), the land-transfer protection, which the Governor's regulation power enables (module 3). The question says 'best reflects', so the careful reading is: option (a) is the only option consistent with the Schedule's tools.

### 10. Mains frameworks: effectiveness, land alienation, development against rights

*Objective:* Use a fixed structure to plan three kinds of Mains answers on the Schedules, using only supported facts and flagged context.

## A structure that works for all three

1. **Introduce** the constitutional design in one sentence (Article 244, two systems).
2. **Explain** the mechanism relevant to the question (Governor's regulation, council powers, Gram Sabha).
3. **Evaluate** with one strength and one gap, each tied to a fact.
4. **Resolve** with two or three precise reforms.

## Task type 1: 'How effective have the Fifth and Sixth Schedules been?'

*Intro*: two systems for tribal areas. *Strengths*: Sixth Schedule councils have real law-making, courts and taxes, and the Bodoland Territorial Council (2003) shows the model can accommodate an insurgency settlement; Fifth Schedule keeps tribal land protection, a Tribes Advisory Council and annual reports. *Gaps*: NCERT notes that provisions have not stemmed alienation and insurgency; the Fifth Schedule depends on Governor and Union attention, and Laxmikanth records only two commissions in decades; council finance depends on grants and the Governor's controls (assent, dissolution). *Way forward*: regular commissions, empowered councils with finance, enforced land laws, PESA compliance (GS-195). Do not quote court cases or statistics you have not verified.

## Task type 2: 'Tribal land alienation'

*Mechanism*: Governor's regulations prohibit or restrict transfer, regulate allotment and money-lending (module 3); Sixth Schedule councils make laws on land and regulate money-lending and trading by non-tribals; Article 19(5) allows restriction on outsiders' settlement; the Samatha judgment (flagged). *Why it persists*: enforcement is weak, records are poor, land is wanted for mining and projects (standard; verify), and moneylenders and non-tribals use loopholes. *Remedy*: record updating, Gram Sabha consent (PESA), fast tribunals, restoration orders.

## Task type 3: 'Development against rights'

*Tension*: Scheduled Areas hold forests and minerals, so the same area is both a rights zone and a resource zone. *Instruments*: Article 275(1) grants fund tribal welfare; PESA gives the Gram Sabha a say in land acquisition and minor minerals (GS-195, module 7); Forest Rights Act 2006 (flagged). *Evaluation*: development that bypasses consent fuels alienation (NCERT's warning); development with consent and benefit-sharing builds trust. Offer a balanced conclusion.

## Thought-aloud: planning in six lines

Question: 'Examine the role of the Governor in the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.' *Plan*: (1) Fifth: report to President, directions on Acts, regulations (President's assent); (2) Sixth: organises districts, nominates four members, assents to laws, appoints commission, dissolves councils, decides royalty disputes in discretion; (3) both: acts as the constitutional link between State and Centre; (4) strength: flexible adjustment to local needs; (5) concern: personal discretion and dependence on the Centre's nominee (GS-189 debate); (6) reform: consultation with councils, transparent commissions.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Article 244 (Part X): provides a special system of administration for Scheduled Areas and tribal areas, detailed in the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.
- Scheduled Area: an area declared by the President under the Fifth Schedule, in a State other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura or Mizoram.
- Tribal area: an area in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura or Mizoram constituted as an autonomous district under the Sixth Schedule.
- Autonomous district: a tribal area with a district council; autonomous region: part of a district with several tribes, with a regional council.
- Tribes Advisory Council: the advisory body for Scheduled Tribe welfare, 20 members with three-fourths ST representatives of the State assembly (Laxmikanth).
- Regulation (Fifth Schedule): a rule made by the Governor for peace and good government of a Scheduled Area; it needs the President's assent.
- Scheduled Tribe: a community listed under the Constitution; status belongs to the community, not to the address (standard; verify Article 342).

### Notes: Articles at a glance

- Article 244: administration of Scheduled Areas and tribal areas (Part X).
- Article 244A: formation of an autonomous State comprising certain tribal areas in Assam (standard: added by the 22nd Amendment 1969; verify).
- Article 275: grants-in-aid; specific grants for ST welfare and the administration of Scheduled Areas, on the Finance Commission's recommendation.
- Article 339: control of the Union over the administration of Scheduled Areas and the welfare of Scheduled Tribes; the Union may give directions and the President appoints a commission.
- Article 19(5): reasonable restrictions on free movement and settlement for the protection of the interests of Scheduled Tribes (Laxmikanth, p. 210).
- Article 371A, 371G, 371H, 371C: special provisions for Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur (GS-200, module 4).

### Notes: Governor's role across the two Schedules

- Fifth: reports to the President on Scheduled Areas; directs that Acts do not apply or apply with modifications; makes regulations after consulting the Tribes Advisory Council; regulations need Presidential assent.
- Sixth: organises and reorganises autonomous districts; divides a district into regions; nominates four members; assents to council laws and money-lending regulations; specifies High Court jurisdiction over tribal courts; appoints commissions; dissolves councils on a commission's recommendation; decides disputed royalty shares in his discretion.
- Sixth: the direction on Acts of Parliament is the Governor's in Assam but the President's in Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram; State Acts are directed by the Governor in all four.
- See GS-189, module 6, for the discretion framework.

### Notes: Cases and rights context (flagged)

- Samatha v State of Andhra Pradesh (1997): reported to bar transfer of land in Scheduled Areas to non-tribals, including mining leases of government land to private parties; verify before quoting.
- Forest Rights Act 2006: recognises individual and community forest rights and gives the Gram Sabha a role; verify provisions before citing.
- PESA 1996: Gram Sabha powers in Scheduled Areas (GS-195, module 7).

### Notes: Exceptions to neat rules

- Not every north-eastern State is under the Sixth Schedule: Nagaland, Manipur and Arunachal Pradesh have Article 371 provisions instead.
- Having Scheduled Tribes does not make an area Scheduled; a presidential order is required.
- The 26 elected plus 4 nominated pattern is a general statement; the Bodoland Territorial Council and some other councils differ (verify).
- The Governor is not the assenting authority for Fifth Schedule regulations; the President is.
- The compulsory commission is a once-after-ten-years requirement; the practice has been two commissions in decades, not one per decade.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Scheduled Area (Fifth) against tribal area (Sixth).
- Tribes Advisory Council (advisory) against district council (law-making).
- Governor reports to the President (Fifth) against Chief Minister reports (wrong).
- Sixth Schedule (Amendment) Act 2003 (Bodoland) against the 92nd Constitution Amendment Act 2003 (Eighth Schedule languages; verify).
- Autonomy (inside the Constitution) against secession (leaving).
- Article 339 (Union directions on Scheduled Areas) against Article 356 (President's Rule).

### Core concepts

- Article 244 creates two systems: Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas, Governor and President) and Sixth Schedule (tribal areas, elected councils).
- A Scheduled Area is declared by the President, in consultation with the Governor; the State's executive power continues.
- The Governor's three tools in the Fifth Schedule: direction on Acts, regulations, land and money-lending regulation, with Presidential assent.
- Sixth Schedule councils have legislative, judicial, executive and financial powers, but laws need the Governor's assent.
- Autonomous districts remain under the executive authority of the State.

**A.** The Sixth Schedule.

**A.** No; the State's executive power extends to it, while the Governor reports to the President and the Union can give directions.

**A.** A tribal area in the four named States, constituted with a district council, but remaining under the State's executive authority.
