## GS-189: Governor and Chief Minister

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · States, local governance and federalism

### Bridge: Head of state versus head of government at State level

A **head of state** is the person who symbolises and formally represents the State and in whose name government is carried on. A **head of government** is the person who actually runs the government, heads the ministers and answers to the legislature. In a **parliamentary system** these jobs are separated. At the Centre, the **President** is head of state and the **Prime Minister** is head of government. In a State, the **Governor** is the head of the State (in Laxmikanth's words the nominal, titular or constitutional head) and the **Chief Minister** is the head of the government.

Two Latin labels appear in Laxmikanth: **de jure** means 'in law' (the Governor is the executive on paper) and **de facto** means 'in fact' (the Chief Minister really decides). 'Executive' means the part of government that carries out laws and runs administration. 'Nominal' means in name only.

Why does this matter for exams? Because many statements test the swap. 'All executive action of the State is taken in the name of the Governor' is true; 'the Governor decides policy on his own' is false in ordinary matters, because he acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers under Article 163, except in the matters where the Constitution lets him use his own discretion.

### Bridge: 'Pleasure of the President' and 'aid and advice'

The phrase **'holds office during the pleasure of the President'** appears in Article 156 for Governors. It means there is **no guaranteed term**: even though the usual period is five years, the President can end the term at any time, without giving reasons in the Constitution and without a formal trial. A Governor therefore cannot be 'impeached' (the formal trial of the President under Article 61 is not available for a Governor). In practice the President acts on the advice of the **Union Council of Ministers** (standard; verify), so the phrase really means 'at the pleasure of the Central government'. Laxmikanth records that the Supreme Court has held this pleasure to be not justiciable; that is, a court will not examine the President's reasons in the ordinary case.

**Aid and advice** (Article 163) means the head of state acts on the recommendation of the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister. The Governor must follow such advice **except** where the Constitution says he can act in his **discretion**. Ministers also hold office 'during the Governor's pleasure' (Article 164) but, for the Chief Minister, the Supreme Court said in S. R. Bommai that the Governor cannot use that pleasure to dismiss a Chief Minister who has a majority.

### 1. Orientation: the State executive and its two heads

*Objective:* Name the four parts of the State executive and explain, in one sentence each, who is head of the State and who is head of the government.

## Where this topic sits

The Constitution gives every State the same **parliamentary system** that the Centre has. Part VI of the Constitution deals with the government in the States. In it, **Articles 153 to 167** deal with the **State executive**. (An **Article** is a numbered section of the Constitution; a **Part** is a group of Articles. If these words are new, see GS-174 and GS-158.)

The State executive has four parts: the **Governor**, the **Chief Minister**, the **Council of Ministers** and the **Advocate General** of the State. There is no vice-governor. At the Centre there is a Vice-President who is ready to step in; in a State nobody is lined up in advance to replace a Governor (Article 160 lets the President make a temporary arrangement).

## Two heads, two jobs

In a parliamentary system the work of a head of state and the work of a head of government are split between two people (bridge module B1 explains the vocabulary).

| | Governor | Chief Minister |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Head of the **State**; nominal, titular or constitutional head (*de jure* executive) | Head of the **government**; real executive (*de facto* executive) |
| How he gets the job | Appointed by the **President** (not elected) | Appointed by the **Governor**, but in practice the leader of the Assembly majority |
| Answerable to | The President (holds office at his pleasure) | The State Legislative Assembly, through the Council of Ministers |
| Mirror at the Centre | President | Prime Minister |

NCERT puts it the same way: the State executive comprises the Governor, the Chief Minister and the Council of Ministers, and the main principles of the parliamentary system operate at the State level too. Its warning is that the Governor has more discretionary power than the President does.

## The dual role: the idea that runs through the whole lesson

Laxmikanth describes the Governor as the chief executive head of the State who is, like the President, a **nominal** head, but who also acts as an **agent of the Central government**. So the office has a **dual role**: constitutional head of the State and representative of the Centre. Every controversy in the next eight modules (appointment, removal, assent to Bills, ordinances, Article 356) is a question about which half of that dual role should win.

A small administrative fact: the 7th Constitutional Amendment (1956) allows the **same person** to be Governor of two or more States.

Modules 7 (the federal debate) and 9 (Council of Ministers) can be deferred to the second pass, and module 10 (examination layer) can wait for the revision cycle. ### 2. Appointment, qualifications, conditions of office, term and removal of the Governor

*Objective:* State how a Governor is appointed, the two written qualifications, the conditions of office, the term and why 'removal' is not impeachment.

## Appointment: nominated, not elected

The Governor is neither elected by the people nor chosen by an electoral college (the way the President is). He is **appointed by the President by a warrant under his hand and seal**. In effect he is a nominee of the Central government (the Union Council of Ministers advises the President; see B2). Yet the Supreme Court held in 1979 (case not named in the extract) that the office is **not an employment under the Central government**; it is an independent constitutional office.

The Draft Constitution provided for a **directly elected** Governor. The Constituent Assembly rejected that and copied the **Canadian** model (appointment by the Centre) instead of the **American** one (direct election). Laxmikanth lists the reasons: direct election does not fit a parliamentary system; it would breed conflict between Governor and Chief Minister; a nominal head does not justify an expensive election; an elected Governor would belong to a party and not be neutral; and nomination lets the Centre keep control over the States.

## Qualifications (Article 157) and two conventions

The Constitution lays down only **two** qualifications: **citizen of India** and **at least 35 years of age**. Two **conventions** have grown: the Governor should be an **outsider** (not from the State he serves) and the President should **consult the Chief Minister** of that State. These are conventions, not constitutional rules, and Laxmikanth records that both have been violated.

## Conditions of office (Article 158)

- He must not be a member of Parliament or of a State Legislature; if he is, he is **deemed to have vacated** the seat on the day he enters office.
- He must not hold any other office of profit.
- He gets the **Raj Bhavan** without paying rent; emoluments are fixed by Parliament and **cannot be diminished during his term**. If one person governs two States, they share the cost as the President decides. (Laxmikanth mentions a 2018 rise from Rs 1.10 lakh to Rs 3.50 lakh a month; treat as dated, verify.)
- **Immunities** (Article 361, standard; verify the number): he is not answerable to any court for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of his office (Article 361(1), verify); no criminal proceedings shall be **instituted or continued** against him during his term, even for personal acts; no arrest or imprisonment; civil proceedings for personal acts only after **two months' notice**.
- **Oath** (Article 159): to faithfully execute the office, to preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law, and to serve the people of the State. The **Chief Justice of the High Court** administers it (or the senior-most judge available).

## Term and removal (Article 156)

A Governor holds office for **five years** from entering office, but this is **subject to the pleasure of the President**: he has **no fixed term and no security of tenure**. The Supreme Court held that the pleasure of the President is not justiciable (*Surya Narain*, 1982, the case given in Laxmikanth's note). The Constitution gives **no grounds** for removal and no impeachment: removal is by the President acting on Union ministers' advice. In 1989 the V. P. Singh government asked all Governors to resign, and in 1991 the Narasimha Rao government changed fourteen. Standard knowledge, verify: in *B. P. Singhal v Union of India* (2010) the Supreme Court is reported to have held that no cause need be stated, but removal must not be arbitrary or malafide.

He may resign by writing to the President, may be **transferred** to another State and may be **reappointed**. After his term he continues until his **successor takes charge**, so a State is never without a Governor. If a Governor dies suddenly, the President may arrange temporary charge, for example by asking the High Court's Chief Justice to act (Article 160).

### 3. Powers of the Governor: executive, legislative, financial and judicial

*Objective:* Sort the Governor's powers into four heads, recall at least five items under each, and name the three powers he lacks.

## The headline

The Governor has executive, legislative, financial and judicial powers 'more or less analogous' to the President's. But he has **no diplomatic, military or emergency powers**. (Why? Foreign affairs, defence and emergencies belong to the Union.)

## Executive powers (selected)

- All executive action of the State is formally taken **in his name**; he makes rules for authenticating orders and for the allocation of business among ministers.
- He **appoints the Chief Minister** and, on the Chief Minister's recommendation, the other ministers, who hold office during his pleasure. In **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha** a Tribal Welfare minister is provided for; Bihar was removed from that list by the **94th Amendment (2006)**.
- He appoints the **Advocate General**, the **State Election Commissioner** (removable only like a High Court judge) and the chairman and members of the **State Public Service Commission** (who can be removed only by the **President**, not by him).
- He can seek information from the Chief Minister and require a matter decided by one minister to be placed before the Council.
- He can **recommend** President's Rule, and during it acts as the President's agent. He is **chancellor** of State universities and appoints vice-chancellors.

## Legislative powers (selected)

He is an **integral part** of the State legislature. He **summons, prorogues** and **dissolves the Assembly**; addresses the first session after each general election and the first session each year; sends messages on Bills; appoints a member to preside if both presiding offices are vacant; nominates **one-sixth** of the Legislative Council from literature, science, art, the cooperative movement and social service; and decides questions of disqualification of members **in consultation with the Election Commission**. Laxmikanth also lists the nomination of one Anglo-Indian member; that provision is dated because the 104th Amendment (2019) ended Anglo-Indian seats (verify). He lays the reports of the State Finance Commission, the State Public Service Commission and the Comptroller and Auditor-General before the legislature. His two biggest legislative powers (assent to Bills and the ordinance power) have their own modules (4 and 5).

## Financial powers

He sees that the **annual financial statement (budget)** is laid before the legislature; **Money Bills** can be introduced only on his **prior recommendation**; **no demand for a grant** can be made except on his recommendation; he can advance money from the **Contingency Fund**; and he constitutes a **finance commission every five years** to review the finances of **panchayats and municipalities** (see GS-195 and GS-196).

## Judicial powers

He can **pardon, reprieve, respite, remit, suspend or commute** the sentence of a person convicted under a **State law** (Article 161); he is **consulted** by the President on the appointment of judges of the State's High Court; and he appoints, posts and promotes **district judges** (in consultation with the High Court) and appoints other members of the State judicial service in consultation with the High Court and the State Public Service Commission.

## Sorting trick

Ask 'What does the Governor do to the legislature?' (summon, prorogue, dissolve, address, message, nominate) and 'What does he do to money?' (budget laid, prior recommendation, demand for grant, contingency fund, finance commission). The pardon comparison with the President is in the master notes.

### 4. Assent, reservation and the veto on State Bills (Articles 200 and 201)

*Objective:* Trace what the Governor and then the President can do with an ordinary Bill and a Money Bill passed by a State legislature.

## First, the word 'assent'

A Bill passed by a State legislature is not a law until it is **assented to**: that is, formally approved. Article 200 deals with assent by the Governor; Article 201 with Bills **reserved** (kept back) by him for the President.

## Ordinary Bill: four options for the Governor

1. **Give assent**: the Bill becomes an Act.
2. **Withhold assent**: the Bill ends.
3. **Return** the Bill (if it is not a Money Bill) for reconsideration. If the House passes it again, with or without amendments, he **must** assent. This is only a **suspensive veto**: it delays but cannot finally defeat the legislature.
4. **Reserve** the Bill for the President.

**Reservation is compulsory** when the Bill would endanger the position of the **High Court**. Laxmikanth adds that the Governor may also reserve a Bill that is against the Constitution, opposed to the Directive Principles, against the larger interest of the country, of grave national importance, or concerned with compulsory acquisition of property (Article 31A).

## Money Bill: three options

Assent, withhold, or reserve. He **cannot return** a Money Bill, because it was introduced with his own prior recommendation. (The President is in the same position with a Money Bill of Parliament.)

## After reservation

Once he reserves a Bill, the Governor has **no further role**. The President may assent, withhold or return it (an ordinary Bill only). If returned and passed again by the House within six months, the President is **not bound** to assent, unlike the Governor, who is bound after the second passage. Assent by the President is enough; the Governor's assent is no longer needed. Compare the President's veto under Article 111 in GS-174 and the table in the master notes.

## Fault lines: what is 'standard' and what is 'verify'

The extract does not say how long a Governor may take. Standard knowledge (verify against the Article text): the first proviso to Article 200 says the Governor may return a Bill 'as soon as possible' after it is presented to him. Disputes in 2023 to 2025 between several States (reported for **Tamil Nadu, Punjab and Kerala**) and their Governors over Bills left pending produced Supreme Court rulings and a Presidential reference. The reported outcomes (an April 2025 ruling setting time limits and 'deemed assent' for Tamil Nadu Bills; an advisory opinion in November 2025 reported to hold that courts cannot fix such timelines or declare deemed assent while delay can still be reviewed) must be **checked from a current source before you write them as law** (see also GS-174, GS-202). The 2026 Mains question asked exactly about 'the nature of his power while giving assent'.

## What you can say safely

The Governor's power over assent is not a free personal discretion: ordinarily he acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers; among Laxmikanth's five express discretions, the one that touches Bills is **reservation for the President**; and a pending Bill cannot be used to defeat the legislature indefinitely without raising a constitutional question.

### 5. The Governor's ordinance-making power (Article 213)

*Objective:* State the conditions, limits and life of a Governor's ordinance and use them on a dated worked example.

## What an ordinance is

An **ordinance** is a temporary law issued by the executive when the legislature is not sitting. Laxmikanth calls it the Governor's **most important legislative power** (Article 213).

## Nine rules to know (from the comparison with the President)

1. **When**: only when the Assembly (in a one-House State) is not in session, or in a two-House State when both Houses are not in session or **either** is not in session. So an ordinance can be issued even if one House is sitting, because a law needs both Houses.
2. **Why**: only when he is satisfied that circumstances make **immediate action** necessary.
3. **Subject**: only on matters on which the **State legislature** can make laws (co-extensive with its power).
4. **Force and limits**: the same force and effect as an Act of the State legislature, and subject to the same limits: it is invalid to the extent it goes beyond what the legislature could do.
5. **Withdrawal**: he can withdraw it at any time.
6. **Not discretionary**: he acts on the advice of the Council headed by the Chief Minister.
7. **Laid before** the Assembly (or both Houses) when it reassembles.
8. **Life**: it ceases **six weeks after reassembly**, or earlier if a resolution disapproving it is passed by the Assembly and agreed to by the Council (where there is one).
9. **Instructions of the President** are needed in **three** cases: (a) a Bill with the same provisions would have required the President's previous sanction for introduction; (b) he would have thought it necessary to reserve such a Bill for the President; (c) an Act with the same provisions would be invalid without the President's assent.

The President needs no such instructions for his own ordinances, so the Governor's ordinance power carries one more control from the Centre.

## Worked example

The Assembly is prorogued on 1 February. The Governor issues an ordinance on 15 February. The Assembly reassembles on **1 April**. The ordinance must be laid before the House, and it will cease to operate on **13 May** (six weeks, that is 42 days, after 1 April) unless the Assembly disapproves it earlier or passes a Bill replacing it. If the Assembly passes a resolution of disapproval on 20 April, the ordinance ceases on 20 April if the Council also agrees where there is a Council.

The **maximum** life is longer in calendar time: the legislature cannot go more than six months between sittings (Article 174, standard; verify), so the ordinance can in the longest case survive about six months plus six weeks.

## Case law (standard knowledge: verify)

*D. C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar* (1987): repeated re-promulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature was held a fraud on the Constitution. *Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar* (2017) was a larger bench decision that treated the satisfaction of the executive as reviewable and held that laying the ordinance before the legislature is mandatory (GS-179 covers this). This is the base for the 2022 Mains question on re-promulgation (module 10).

### 6. Discretion: Article 163, the hung Assembly and the Article 356 report

*Objective:* Separate constitutional discretion from situational discretion, list the express cases and explain how a Governor picks a Chief Minister when no party has a majority.

## The three Articles

- **Article 154**: the executive power of the State is vested in the Governor, exercised directly or through subordinate officers.
- **Article 163**: there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister as head to **aid and advise** the Governor, **except** so far as he is required to act in his **discretion**.
- **Article 164**: the Council is **collectively responsible** to the State Legislative Assembly (the foundation of the parliamentary system in the State; this is Article 164(2)).

## Two differences from the President

First, the Constitution envisages the Governor acting at times in his **discretion**; no such possibility is written for the President. Second, since the 42nd Amendment (1976), ministerial advice is **binding on the President**, but no such rule exists for the Governor. If a question arises whether something is in his discretion, **his decision is final** and cannot be challenged on that ground.

## Constitutional (express) discretion: five cases in Laxmikanth

1. Reserving a Bill for the President.
2. Recommending **President's Rule**.
3. Acting as administrator of an adjoining Union Territory (additional charge).
4. Fixing the **royalty share** payable to an autonomous tribal district council in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram (Sixth Schedule, paragraph 9(2)).
5. Seeking information from the Chief Minister.

Also he has **special responsibilities** under the President's directions in eight States (list in master notes; link GS-200): he consults the Council but acts finally on his own judgment.

## Situational (hidden) discretion

'Situational' is a **textbook** label, not a constitutional term. It describes three situations: (1) choosing a Chief Minister when **no party has a clear majority** or when the Chief Minister dies suddenly with no obvious successor; (2) dismissing a Council that cannot prove confidence in the Assembly; (3) dissolving the Assembly when the Council has lost its majority.

## The hung Assembly

The Constitution says only that the Governor appoints the Chief Minister. By convention he calls the leader of the majority party. In a hung Assembly he usually invites the leader of the **largest party or coalition** and asks him to prove a **vote of confidence within a month**. Laxmikanth's notes give examples of both patterns (leader of the largest party in Tamil Nadu 1951, Rajasthan 1967, Haryana 1982; leader of a coalition in Punjab 1967, West Bengal 1970, Maharashtra 1978). A person need not prove a majority before being appointed. Punchhi's order of preference for hung Assemblies is in GS-201 (module 6).

## The Article 356 report

Article 356 lets the President proclaim President's Rule in a State on the **Governor's report** or otherwise, if the State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution (standard; verify). The Governor's report is therefore the usual trigger, and it is one of his express discretions. Because he is a Centre-appointed nominee, a report that suits the party ruling at the Centre is the commonest charge against the office (module 7).

The limits: in *S. R. Bommai* (1994) the Supreme Court is reported to have held that the proclamation is **subject to judicial review**, that the Assembly should not be dissolved until Parliament has approved the proclamation, and that the majority is tested on the **floor of the House** (verify). In *Rameshwar Prasad* (2006) the Bihar dissolution was struck down because the report rested on malafide reasons (verify). The full account of Article 356, the approval and duration rules and the case law is in GS-201.

## Judgments (check each)

- **S. R. Bommai v Union of India (1994)**: the extract says only that a Chief Minister cannot be dismissed by the Governor while he enjoys majority support in the Assembly, and that many violations occurred. The floor of the House is the test (standard; verify).
- *Rameshwar Prasad v Union of India* (2006): Bihar Assembly dissolution held unconstitutional because the Governor's report was based on malafide reasons (standard; verify).
- *Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker* (2016): the Constitution Bench held that the Governor's power to summon or advance an Assembly session is not a general discretion (standard; verify).

### 7. Agent of the Centre or constitutional head? Reforms and the President comparison

*Objective:* Build a two-sided argument on the Governor's position in the federal polity and recall at least four reform proposals.

## The debate in one line

Laxmikanth says the Constitution gave the office a **dual role**: constitutional head of the State and representative of the Centre. NCERT says the role of Governors has always been controversial between States and the Centre. The question for Mains is which half dominates in practice.

## Case for 'agent of the Centre'

- **Appointment and removal**: appointed by the Centre, holds office during the President's pleasure, no fixed tenure; in 1989 and 1991 new governments changed Governors wholesale.
- **Who is chosen**: NCERT notes that many Governors have been retired military officers, civil servants or politicians; when the Centre and the State are ruled by different parties, the Governor's actions look like interference.
- **Reports and reservation**: he can recommend President's Rule (Article 356) and reserve State Bills for the President. NCERT records dismissals of State governments with a majority (Kerala 1959) and others without testing the majority (several States after 1967), and the removal of elected governments in Andhra Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir in the 1980s.
- **Special roles**: chancellor of universities (Punchhi advised against making Governors chancellors).

## Case for 'constitutional head'

- The Supreme Court held (1979) that the office is not an employment under the Central government; it is an independent constitutional office.
- He swears to preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law, and ordinarily acts on the **aid and advice** of the Chief Minister's Council (Article 163).
- A five-year term is the norm, and a reported Supreme Court view (*B. P. Singhal*, 2010; verify) is that removal at pleasure must not be arbitrary.
- *Shamsher Singh v State of Punjab* (1974) (standard; verify): the Governor must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except where the Constitution requires him to use discretion.
- Judicial review: the Supreme Court has held that the validity of President's Rule can be examined by the judiciary (NCERT).

## Reform recommendations

- **Sarkaria (1983-88)**: NCERT: appointments should be strictly non-partisan. Sarkaria also set out the selection criteria (eminent in some walk of life, from outside the State, a detached person without deep involvement in local politics, not recently active in politics) and Punchhi reiterated them (verify against the reports). In GS-201: consult the Chief Minister; no dismissal of a Council with a majority; a five-year term should not be disturbed except for compelling reasons.
- **Punchhi (2007-10)**: selection guidelines (eminent, from outside the State, detached from local politics, not recently active in politics); five-year tenure; removal by an impeachment-like procedure; Article 163 gives **no general discretion**; decide on Bills within **six months**; floor test before dismissal; guidance for a hung Assembly (GS-201, module 6).
- **The 2019 PYQ** is an 'identify the body' test: Administrative Reforms Commission (1966), Rajamannar Committee (1969), Sarkaria (1983) and the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (2000) are the options. Learn which phrase belongs to which body from the original reports (verify).

## A balanced verdict

A strong answer does not choose a side. It says the Constitution **designed** the office with two roles; weak tenure and the reservation and Article 356 powers make the 'agent' reading easy to find in practice; judicial review (Bommai, Rameshwar Prasad, Nabam Rebia: verify) has narrowed the room; and the long-term fix is fixed tenure, non-partisan selection and clear guidelines for assent and for a hung Assembly.

## Governor against President (table in the master notes)

The main contrasts: the President is elected by an electoral college, the Governor is nominated; the President can be impeached (Article 61), the Governor serves at pleasure; the President has no discretion in the Constitution, the Governor does; the President can pardon death sentences and court-martial sentences, the Governor cannot (Laxmikanth view; State of Haryana v Raj Kumar, 2021, is reported to hold that the Governor's Article 161 power covers death-row prisoners too: verify; the court-martial point is not in dispute).

### 8. The Chief Minister: appointment, term, oath and powers

*Objective:* Explain how the Chief Minister is appointed (including the six-month rule), and group his powers under four heads.

## Appointment (Article 164)

Article 164 says only that the Chief Minister is **appointed by the Governor**. No procedure is laid down. By convention the Governor appoints the **leader of the majority party** in the Assembly. In a hung Assembly he exercises personal discretion (module 6). If a Chief Minister dies and the party has no obvious successor, the Governor again uses his judgment; usually the ruling party elects a leader and he must appoint that person.

The Constitution does **not** require the person to prove a majority **before** being appointed: the Governor may appoint him and then ask him to prove it within a reasonable time (the extract cites G. M. Shah in Jammu and Kashmir, who proved it, and Bhasker Rao in Andhra Pradesh, who could not).

## The six-month rule

A person who is **not a member of the State legislature** can be appointed Chief Minister for **six months**; within that time he must be elected to the legislature or he ceases to be Chief Minister (Laxmikanth cites Bansilal in Haryana and S. B. Chavan in Maharashtra). The Chief Minister may belong to **either House**; most have been from the Assembly, but C. Rajagopalachari in Madras (1952), Morarji Desai in Bombay (1952), C. B. Gupta in UP (1960) and B. P. Mandal in Bihar (1968) were members of the Legislative Council. Standard knowledge, verify: in *S. R. Chaudhuri v State of Punjab* (2001) the Supreme Court is reported to have held that a non-member cannot be re-appointed again and again to dodge the six-month limit.

## Oath, term and salary

The Governor administers the **oath of office** (true faith to the Constitution; uphold sovereignty and integrity of India; faithfully discharge duties; do right to all people without fear or favour) and the **oath of secrecy**. The term is **not fixed**: he holds office during the Governor's pleasure, but the Governor **cannot dismiss him while he has majority support** (Bommai). If he loses the Assembly's confidence he must resign, or the Governor can dismiss him. Salary and allowances are fixed by the **State legislature**, plus a sumptuary allowance, free accommodation and similar benefits.

## Powers: four heads

1. **With the Council**: the Governor appoints only those persons the Chief Minister recommends; he allocates and reshuffles portfolios; he can ask a minister to resign or advise the Governor to dismiss him; he presides over Council meetings; he guides and coordinates ministers; his resignation or death **dissolves the whole Council**, whereas another minister's exit only creates a vacancy.
2. **With the Governor**: he is the **principal channel of communication** (Article 167). He must communicate Council decisions on administration and legislation, furnish information the Governor asks for, and, if the Governor requires, submit for the Council a matter decided by one minister alone. He advises on appointments of the Advocate General, State Public Service Commission members and State Election Commissioner.
3. **With the legislature**: he advises on summoning and proroguing, can recommend **dissolution of the Assembly** at any time, and announces policy on the floor.
4. **Other roles**: chairman of the **State Planning Board**; vice-chairman by rotation (one year at a time) of the concerned **zonal council**; member of the **Inter-State Council** and the **Governing Council of NITI Aayog**; chief spokesman, crisis manager and political head of the services.

Laxmikanth adds that the Governor's discretion reduces, to some extent, the Chief Minister's authority.

### 9. Council of Ministers: collective responsibility, size and removal; Chief Minister against Prime Minister

*Objective:* Explain collective responsibility, the 91st Amendment size cap, the three ways a Chief Minister goes, and four contrasts between a Chief Minister and a Prime Minister.

## Council of Ministers: appointment and responsibility

Under Article 164 the Governor appoints ministers **on the advice of the Chief Minister**, and they hold office during the Governor's pleasure. In practice the pleasure is the Chief Minister's. The Council is **collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly** (Article 164(2)).

**Collective responsibility** means: (1) all ministers share responsibility for the Council's decisions and defend them in public; (2) if the Assembly passes a vote of no confidence, the **whole Council** must go, not only the minister concerned; (3) a minister who disagrees must resign or be dismissed. **Individual responsibility** adds that each minister answers for his own department. NCERT explains the logic for the Centre: the Council can exist only as long as it enjoys the majority support of the Lok Sabha (or Assembly); the Assembly can remove it through a no-confidence vote.

## Size: the 91st Amendment (2003)

Before the 91st Amendment, there was no limit on the Council's size; NCERT says that in the absence of a limit, jumbo Councils were formed to win over legislators with ministerial posts. After the Amendment, the Council must not exceed **15 per cent of the total number of members of the Assembly**. NCERT reports this for States and the House of the People. Standard knowledge (verify with the Article 164 text): the Amendment also fixes a minimum of 12 ministers (including the Chief Minister) in a State and bars a legislator disqualified for defection from being a minister.

## How a Chief Minister goes

1. **Resignation** (which dissolves the Council).
2. **Defeat** in a vote of no confidence or loss of majority: he must resign or the Governor may dismiss him. Bommai: the test is on the **floor of the House**.
3. **Death.**
Laxmikanth notes that many Governors broke this rule by dismissing Chief Ministers without testing their majority.

## Chief Minister against Prime Minister (core contrasts)

- **Appointment**: the Governor appoints the Chief Minister; the President appoints the Prime Minister. In both cases the leader of the majority party is called.
- **Head of state's freedom**: the President cannot use discretion in the Constitution and is bound by ministerial advice; the Governor has express discretion. NCERT: the Governor has more discretionary powers than the President.
- **Houses**: the Chief Minister may sit in either House of the State legislature; a Prime Minister is a member of either House of Parliament (standard).
- **Bodies chaired**: the Prime Minister heads the Governing Council of NITI Aayog and the Inter-State Council, of which Chief Ministers are members (Laxmikanth); the Chief Minister chairs the State Planning Board and is, by rotation, vice-chairman of a zonal council (its chairman is the Union Home Minister).
- **Domain**: the Prime Minister leads on Union List subjects, including defence and foreign affairs; the Chief Minister leads on State List subjects and shares the Concurrent List, with the Governor's discretion and the President's role over Bills above him.

## Where the two meet the Governor

Article 167 makes the Chief Minister the Governor's information channel; Article 163 makes the Council his adviser; Article 164 makes the Chief Minister his appointee; and the Governor's discretion is the exception. If you keep these three Articles straight, most Chief Minister questions can be answered.

### 10. How UPSC tests this topic: patterns, thought-aloud and frameworks

*Objective:* Recognise the five recurring question types on the Governor and Chief Minister and use a fixed method to answer a statement-based MCQ and a Governor Mains question.

## Pattern 1: immunities and conditions (Prelims 2018, 2025)

These questions copy Constitution language: 'No criminal proceedings shall be instituted **or continued** against the Governor during his term', 'emoluments shall not be **diminished**', 'not answerable to any court'. The trap is a word swap ('civil' for 'criminal', 'increased' for 'diminished', 'permanent' for 'during term'). **Method**: read the verb and the time clause; match it to module 2.

*Thought-aloud on the 2018 question.* Statement 1 says no criminal proceedings can be instituted against the Governor during his term. Module 2 says he is immune from any criminal proceedings during his term. Statement 2 says emoluments cannot be diminished during the term; module 2 says the same. So both statements match the source, and on this analysis 'both' is the likely answer. Treat that as analysis, not the official key.

## Pattern 2: Governor's position and discretion (Prelims 2019, 2025; Mains 2026)

The 2025 question asks whether the Constitution **explicitly** mentions discretion (Article 163 and the Sixth Schedule, paragraph 9(2)) and whether the President can reserve a State Bill **on his own** (Article 201 begins with a Bill that the **Governor** has reserved). **Method**: for 'can X do Y on his own', look for the Article that starts the process.

## Pattern 3: Chief Minister provisions (Prelims 2020)

The 2020 question combines the six-month rule for non-members with the disqualification period under the Representation of the People Act 1951. Treat the second as standard knowledge: disqualification after conviction for a sentence of two years or more runs from the date of conviction and continues for six years after release, so 'permanently' conflicts with it (verify).

## Pattern 4: Governor's legislative power (Mains 2022)

Question: essential conditions for the Governor's legislative powers; legality of re-promulgating ordinances without placing them before the legislature. **Skeleton**: define (1 line); conditions (module 5: not in session; immediate action; instructions of the President in three cases; laid before the legislature; six weeks); cases (Wadhwa 1987; Krishna Kumar Singh 2017: verify); conclusion on legality.

## Pattern 5: Governor in the federal polity (Mains 2026)

'Discuss the position of the Governor... What is the nature of his power while giving assent...? Is he bound by the aid and advice...?' Three sub-questions: structure your answer with three headings. Use Article 163 and the Council's advice; list the exceptions (module 6); handle assent with the safe statements in module 4; avoid unverified recent rulings.

## Directive words

**Discuss**: both sides and conclusion. **Critically examine**: weigh strengths and weaknesses and reach a view.

## Elimination habits

Reject any option that says the Governor is elected, has a fixed tenure, can be impeached, or may return a Money Bill. Reject any statement that the Chief Minister is appointed by the President or needs a majority proved before being sworn in.

### Notes: Definitions in one place

- Governor: appointed by the President; nominal head of the State; also agent of the Centre (dual role).
- Chief Minister: head of the government; de facto executive; appointed by the Governor.
- Assent: formal approval that turns a Bill passed by the legislature into an Act.
- Reservation of a Bill: the Governor keeps a State Bill back for the President's consideration (Article 200 and 201).
- Suspensive veto: a veto that only delays; a Bill passed again by the House must then be assented to (Governor on ordinary Bills).
- Ordinance: a temporary law made by the Governor when the legislature is not in session (Article 213).
- Constitutional discretion: matters the Constitution expressly lets the Governor decide without advice; situational discretion: a textbook label for matters where political circumstances force his judgment.
- Collective responsibility: the Council of Ministers is responsible as one body to the Legislative Assembly (Article 164(2)).

### Notes: Chain: who appoints, advises and answers

- The President appoints the Governor; the Governor appoints the Chief Minister; the Chief Minister recommends the other ministers; the Governor appoints them.
- The Council is collectively responsible to the Assembly; the Assembly can remove it by a no-confidence vote; the Governor then asks for a new government.
- The Governor acts on the Council's advice (Article 163) except in discretion; the Chief Minister must keep him informed (Article 167); discretion therefore sits where the Constitution or the political situation leaves no minister to advise.
- If a Bill is reserved, the chain moves to the President (Article 201); if an ordinance touches the three listed matters, instructions come from the President (Article 213).

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- Reservation is compulsory (not merely allowed) when a Bill would endanger the position of the State High Court.
- The Governor cannot return a Money Bill, but can reserve it.
- A Governor may issue an ordinance while one House is sitting (in a two-House State) because a law needs both Houses.
- The Governor cannot pardon a death sentence, but may suspend, remit or commute it [Laxmikanth view; 2021 SC ruling reported otherwise (verify)].
- State Public Service Commission members are appointed by the Governor but removed only by the President; the State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Governor but removable only like a High Court judge.
- The same person can be Governor of two or more States (7th Amendment 1956).
- A non-member can be Chief Minister for six months; the Chief Minister can belong to the Legislative Council.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Reservation of a Bill (Governor to the President) is not the same as returning a Bill (Governor to the House).
- Withholding assent (Bill ends) is not the same as returning a Bill (Bill continues).
- Pleasure of the President (Governor) is not the same as pleasure of the Governor (ministers).
- Constitutional discretion (written in the Constitution) is not the same as situational discretion (textbook label).
- Head of state (Governor) is not the same as head of government (Chief Minister).
- Collective responsibility (whole Council to the Assembly) is not the same as individual responsibility (one minister for his department).

### Core concepts

- Dual role of the Governor: constitutional head and agent of the Centre.
- Aid and advice (Article 163) with a short list of discretions.
- Four options on an ordinary Bill (assent, withhold, return, reserve); three on a Money Bill.
- Ordinance (Article 213): not in session, immediate action, laid before the legislature, six weeks.
- Chief Minister: appointed by the Governor, leader of the majority, six-month rule.
- Collective responsibility of the Council to the Assembly (Article 164(2)).

**A.** It would not fit a parliamentary system, would breed conflict with the Chief Minister, would make a nominal head costly and partisan and would weaken the Centre's control; the Canadian model was preferred.

**A.** Because it is introduced on his own prior recommendation; he can only assent, withhold or reserve it.
