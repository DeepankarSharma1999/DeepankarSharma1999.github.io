## GS-117: Major river basins and tributaries

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: River words

A **river** is a natural stream of water flowing in a channel to a lake, sea or another river. Its **source** is where it begins (a spring, glacier or lake) and its **mouth** is where it ends. A **tributary** joins a larger river; a **distributary** leaves the main river and flows on separately, typical of a delta. The place where two rivers meet is a **confluence**. The land that drains into a river is its **catchment** or **drainage basin**, and the line separating two basins is the **watershed**. A river that always has water is **perennial**; one that flows only in the rainy season is **ephemeral**. **Snow-fed** rivers receive melt water from glaciers and snow; **rain-fed** rivers depend on rainfall. A **delta** is a fan of deposited sediment at the mouth, with distributaries; an **estuary** is a funnel-shaped mouth with no delta. A **gorge** is a deep narrow valley. **Length** is measured along the river, **catchment area** in square kilometres (sq km), and 1 lakh equals 100,000.

### Bridge: Facing downstream: left and right bank

Rivers have two banks, and the names left and right always refer to the view **facing downstream**, the direction in which the water flows. If you stand on a bridge looking the way the water is going, your left hand points to the left bank. A map does not help unless you know the flow direction. The Ganga flows from west to east across the plains, so when you face east your left hand points north: the **north bank is the left bank** and the **south bank is the right bank**. Tributaries from the Himalaya in the north therefore join the Ganga on the left; tributaries from the Peninsula in the south (Son) join on the right. In Assam the Brahmaputra flows towards the west, so facing west your left hand points south and your right hand points north. Always decide the flow direction first, then the bank.

### 1. Orientation: India's rivers on one page

*Objective:* State what a drainage basin and a watershed are, and describe the main divisions of India's drainage by sea, by basin size and by origin.

## What this topic covers

Rain and melting snow run off the land through channels. NCERT calls the flow through well-defined channels **drainage** and the network of channels a **drainage system**. The area from which a river and its tributaries collect water is its **drainage basin** (the catchment); the line separating one basin from the next is the **watershed**. NCERT adds that watersheds are small in area and basins larger, and that both are natural units of planning because what happens in one part affects the whole.

## Three ways to divide India's rivers

1. **By the sea they reach.** The Arabian Sea drainage and the Bay of Bengal drainage are separated by the Delhi ridge, the Aravalis and the Sahyadris (the Western Ghats). Nearly 77 per cent of the drainage area (Ganga, Brahmaputra, Mahanadi, Krishna and others) goes to the Bay of Bengal and 23 per cent (Indus, Narmada, Tapi, Mahi, Periyar systems) to the Arabian Sea.
2. **By size of basin.** Major basins have more than 20,000 sq km of catchment (14 basins, such as the Ganga, Brahmaputra, Krishna, Tapi, Narmada, Mahi, Pennar, Sabarmati and Barak); medium basins have 2,000 to 20,000 sq km (44 basins, such as the Kalindi, Periyar and Meghna); minor basins are below 2,000 sq km.
3. **By origin and nature.** The **Himalayan drainage** (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra systems) and the **Peninsular drainage**. NCERT admits the scheme is imperfect, since the Chambal, Betwa and Son are older than the Himalayan rivers yet belong to the Ganga system, but it is the most accepted.

## The big picture in one sketch

Himalayan rivers rise in snow-capped mountains and are perennial, carrying water all year; the Peninsular rivers mostly rise in the Western Ghats and flow east to the Bay of Bengal, with the Narmada and Tapi as the great exceptions flowing west. The Western Ghats lie close to the west coast, so the west-flowing rivers are short; the east-flowing ones are long. This pattern is the first thing to draw, and it links back to the Day 17 lessons GS-116 (the Ghats) and GS-130 (coasts and deltas).

### 2. Foundation: river words, banks and drainage patterns

*Objective:* Use the vocabulary of rivers correctly, identify left and right bank, and name the four drainage patterns with an Indian example of each where the sources give one (trellis has none in the extracts).

## Words you will need

A **tributary** is a smaller river joining a larger one; a **distributary** is a branch that leaves the main river, typical of a delta. A **confluence** is the meeting point of two rivers, and **prayag** is the Sanskrit-derived word NCERT uses for one on the Ganga headstreams. A river is **perennial** if it carries water all year and **ephemeral** (non-perennial) if it flows only in the rainy season. A **gorge** is a deep narrow valley with steep sides; **rapids** are fast, rough stretches. An **antecedent** river is one that kept its course while the land rose across it; NCERT labels the Satluj, the Kosi and the Subansiri as antecedent. The **regime** of a river is its seasonal pattern of flow (Leong), controlled by rainfall, snow, rock and basin size.

## Left bank and right bank

Always stand facing **downstream**, the way the water flows. The bank on your left is the left bank, the other the right bank. The Ganga flows west to east in the plains, so its **north bank is the left bank**: the Himalayan tributaries (Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi) join on the left, and the Peninsular Son joins on the right (south) bank. In Assam the Brahmaputra flows west, so north-bank tributaries such as the Subansiri are right-bank and south-bank ones such as the Burhi Dihing are left-bank. Check the direction first, never guess from the map's top.

## Drainage patterns (NCERT)

- **Dendritic**: branches like a tree; the rivers of the northern plain.
- **Radial**: rivers spread out in all directions from a hill; those from the Amarkantak range.
- **Trellis**: main tributaries run parallel and smaller ones join at right angles.
- **Centripetal**: rivers drain from all sides into a lake or depression (Loktak in Manipur is the usual Indian example; standard knowledge, verify).

Leong explains how patterns arise: uniform rock gives dendritic drainage, alternating hard and soft rock gives trellis (rectangular) drainage, and a dome or volcanic cone gives radial drainage. The Day 17 lesson GS-095 (maps) has a short section on reading drainage patterns on a map.

## Three common confusions

- **Basin versus watershed**: NCERT says basin is the larger unit and watershed the smaller; a watershed line is also the *boundary* between basins.
- **Delta versus estuary**: a delta is a fan of deposits with distributaries; an estuary is a funnel-shaped mouth with no delta (module 9).
- **Himalayan versus Peninsular**: the grouping is by origin and nature, not simply by position.

### 3. Core: the Himalayan drainage, its features and its evolution

*Objective:* Describe the features of Himalayan rivers in the mountains and the plains, and summarise NCERT's account of the Indo-Brahma river and its dismemberment.

## Features

The Himalayan drainage is mainly the **Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra** basins. They are fed by snow melt and rain, so they are **perennial**. In the mountains the rivers cut deep **gorges** and V-shaped valleys and tumble over rapids and waterfalls, doing erosional work while the Himalaya rose. On entering the plains they form depositional features: flat valleys, **ox-bow lakes**, flood plains, **braided channels** and, near the mouth, **deltas**. The mountain course is highly tortuous, but on the plains the rivers meander strongly and often shift their courses.

NCERT's example is the **Kosi**, the "sorrow of Bihar". It brings a huge quantity of sediments from the upper reaches and drops them in the plains; the channel gets blocked and the river changes course. Its flow also swings greatly with the season, being highest when snow melt and monsoon rain peak. Floods bring both fertile silt and damage.

## Antecedent rivers

The Satluj, the Kosi and the Subansiri are antecedent in NCERT's text: they existed before the land rose and cut gorges as the ranges lifted. The Brahmaputra is usually grouped with them (standard; the NCERT extract uses the label for the other three).

## Evolution: the Indo-Brahma (Shiwalik) river

Geologists, NCERT says, believe a mighty river called the Shiwalik or **Indo-Brahma** once flowed the whole length of the Himalaya from Assam through Punjab to Sind and reached the Gulf of Sind near lower Punjab in the Miocene period, some 5 to 24 million years ago. Support: the continuity of the Shiwalik deposits (sands, silt, clay, boulders, conglomerates) of lacustrine and alluvial origin. NCERT then says the river was dismembered into three systems: the Indus with its five tributaries in the west, the Ganga with its Himalayan tributaries in the centre, and the Brahmaputra in Assam with its tributaries in the east.

Causes given by NCERT: the **Pleistocene upheaval** of the western Himalayas, including the uplift of what it calls the Potwar Plateau (Delhi ridge), which became the divide between the Indus and Ganga systems; and the down-throwing of the **Malda gap** between the Rajmahal hills and the Meghalaya plateau in the mid-Pleistocene, which turned the Ganga and Brahmaputra toward the Bay of Bengal. A caution: the Potwar Plateau is in northern Pakistan while the Delhi ridge is an extension of the Aravalis, so NCERT's equation of the two is loose (verify). NCERT's physiography chapter adds that Haryana and Delhi form the water divide between the Indus and Ganga systems.

Diagram idea: three arrows from one long river, labelled Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra, with the Delhi ridge and the Malda gap marked.

### 4. Core: the Indus system and its Panjnad tributaries

*Objective:* State the course of the Indus, list its tributaries with bank and confluence points, and work out the order in which the five Punjab rivers join.

## The Indus

NCERT (India: Physical Environment, chapter 3) gives the Indus (Sindhu) a basin of about 11,65,000 sq km, of which 3,21,289 sq km lie in India, and a total length of 2,880 km, of which 1,114 km are in India. Recomputed, India holds about 27.6 per cent of the area and 38.7 per cent of the length. It rises near Bokhar Chu (31 degrees 15 minutes N, 81 degrees 40 minutes E) in Tibet at 4,164 m in the Kailash range, where it is called Singi Khamban (Lion's mouth). It flows north-west between the Ladakh and Zaskar ranges, through Ladakh and Baltistan, cuts a gorge near Gilgit, enters Pakistan near Chilas and emerges from the hills near Attock. In India it flows through the Union Territories of Ladakh and Jammu and Kashmir. It receives **Panjnad** a little above Mithankot and falls into the Arabian Sea east of Karachi.

Its Himalayan tributaries include the Shyok, Gilgit, Zaskar, Hunza, Nubra, Shigar, Gasting and Dras. At Attock the **Kabul** joins on the **right bank**, and the Khurram, Tochi, Gomal, Viboa and Sangar, all from the Sulaiman ranges, also join the right bank.

## The five rivers of the Panjab (Panjnad)

| River | Source and key places (NCERT) |
|---|---|
| Jhelum | spring at Verinag, foot of the Pir Panjal; Srinagar and the Wular lake; joins the Chenab near Jhang |
| Chenab | Chandra and Bhaga meet at Tandi near Keylong (hence Chandrabhaga); largest tributary; about 1,180 km in India |
| Ravi | west of Rohtang pass, Kullu hills; Chamba valley; joins the Chenab near Sarai Sidhu |
| Beas | Beas Kund near Rohtang pass (4,000 m); Kullu valley; gorges at Kati and Largi; meets the Satluj near Harike |
| Satluj | Raksas tal near Mansarovar (4,555 m), called Langchen Khambab; Shipki La; gorge at Rupar; antecedent; feeds the Bhakra Nangal canals |

## Working out the order of joining

Read the table as a chain: the **Jhelum and Ravi flow into the Chenab**, the **Beas flows into the Satluj**, and then the Satluj and Chenab unite as the Panjnad, which joins the Indus above Mithankot. The final Chenab-Satluj junction is standard knowledge beyond the extract (verify); NCERT says only that the Panjnad is the name for the five rivers together. For a question that asks which of Chenab, Jhelum, Ravi and Sutlej receives the other three before the Indus, the chain suggests the Chenab (module 10).

## Ancient names (standard knowledge, verify)

The Rigveda names the rivers: Sindhu (Indus), Vitasta (Jhelum), Asikni (Chenab), Parushni (Ravi), Vipasa (Beas), Shutudri (Sutlej). NCERT does not list them.

### 5. Core: the Ganga from its headstreams to the plains, and the Yamuna

*Objective:* Trace the Ganga from Gaumukh to the delta, name the prayags, recall its length by state, and list the Yamuna and its right-bank and left-bank tributaries.

## The headstreams and the prayags

NCERT: the Ganga rises in the Gangotri glacier near Gaumukh (3,900 m) in Uttarkashi district, Uttarakhand, as the **Bhagirathi**. It cuts narrow gorges through the Central and Lesser Himalayas. The **Alaknanda** rises in the Satopanth glacier above Badrinath. Going down the Alaknanda, the sequence is:

1. **Vishnu Prayag** (near Joshimath): the Dhauli and the Vishnu Ganga meet.
2. **Karna Prayag**: the Pindar joins.
3. **Rudra Prayag**: the Mandakini (Kali Ganga) joins.
4. **Devprayag**: the Bhagirathi meets the Alaknanda and the river is called the **Ganga** from here.

The Ganga enters the plains at **Haridwar**, flows first south, then south-east and east, and finally splits into two distributaries, the Bhagirathi (Hugli) and the Padma (the Bhagirathi is also the headstream's name; do not confuse the two). Beware a name clash: the Mandakini is also called Kali Ganga, and the Sarda is called Kali too.

## Length and basin

NCERT gives 2,525 km, shared by Uttarakhand 110 km, Uttar Pradesh 1,450 km, Bihar 445 km and West Bengal 520 km. Recomputed: 110 + 1,450 + 445 + 520 = 2,525 km. The Ganga basin covers about 8.6 lakh sq km in India alone and is the largest river system in India, with perennial rivers from the Himalaya and non-perennial ones from the Peninsula. The Namami Gange Programme, approved as a flagship programme in June 2014, aims at pollution abatement and rejuvenation (NCERT).

## The Yamuna

The Yamuna is the **westernmost and longest tributary** of the Ganga. It rises in the Yamunotri glacier on the western slopes of the Banderpunch range (6,316 m) and joins the Ganga at Prayag (Allahabad in NCERT, now Prayagraj). Its irrigation canals include the western and eastern Yamuna canals and the Agra canal.

| Bank | Tributaries (NCERT) |
|---|---|
| Right bank | Chambal, Sind, Betwa, Ken (all from the Peninsular plateau) |
| Left bank | Hindan, Rind, Sengar, Varuna |

NCERT's physiography chapter adds that most of the Yamuna's tributaries rise in the Vindhyan and Kaimur ranges, and that the Banas is the only significant Chambal tributary rising in the Aravalli. The Varuna is listed by NCERT as a Yamuna tributary although a Varuna also joins the Ganga at Varanasi (verify).

## The Chambal

It rises near Mhow in the Malwa plateau of Madhya Pradesh, flows north through a gorge above Kota in Rajasthan, then through Bundi, Sawai Madhopur and Dholpur before joining the Yamuna. It is famous for the **Chambal ravines** (badlands). NCERT's sentence about the Gandhisagar dam is ambiguous (it reads 'a gorge upwards of Kota in Rajasthan, where the Gandhisagar dam has been constructed'); the dam is in Mandsaur district, Madhya Pradesh (verify).

Diagram idea: draw the Ganga from Gaumukh with the four prayags marked in order, then Haridwar, then Prayag with the Yamuna joining from the south-west.

### 6. Core: the Ganga's left-bank Himalayan tributaries, the Son and the Damodar

*Objective:* Name the Ganga's left-bank and right-bank tributaries below Prayagraj, state each one's source and confluence as NCERT gives it, and put them in west-to-east order.

## The tributaries

|---|---|---|

The Sarda is also called Saryu, rising as the Goriganga in the Milam glacier; along the Indo-Nepal border it is Kali or Chauk. The Gandak rises in Nepal and drains the central part of that country.

## The sequence below Prayagraj

NCERT lists the left-bank tributaries in the order Ramganga, Gomati, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi, Mahananda. Combining that order with the confluence points it states (Kannauj; Chhapra; Sonpur near Patna) and the fact that Mahananda is the last, the west-to-east order of **Himalayan left-bank tributaries joining downstream of Prayagraj** is **Gomati, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi** (then the Mahananda). The Ramganga joins above Prayagraj at Kannauj. The Gomati's exact confluence and the Kosi's confluence near the Katihar-Kursela area are standard knowledge not given in the extract (verify), and the Gomati rises from a Terai marsh rather than the high Himalaya (verify). The Son, a right-bank river, joins near Arrah west of Patna, which on standard maps lies between the Ghaghara and Gandak confluences (verify).

## Why the pattern matters

The tributaries on the left bank come from the high Himalaya with heavy sediment and snow-melt, so they bring floods and shift channels (Kosi, Gandak); those on the right bank come from the Peninsular plateau and are rain-fed, with a smaller snow-melt contribution (Son, Chambal, Betwa; a general contrast, verify the load comparison).

### 7. Core: the Brahmaputra, the Teesta and the great delta

*Objective:* Trace the Brahmaputra from Tibet to the Bay of Bengal, name its main tributaries by bank, and explain its flood problem and the course of the Teesta.

## The Brahmaputra's journey

NCERT: the Brahmaputra, one of the largest rivers of the world, rises in the Chemayungdung glacier of the Kailash range near Mansarovar. It runs east for nearly **1,200 km** through a dry, flat part of southern Tibet, where it is the **Tsangpo** ("the purifier"); the Rango Tsangpo is its main right-bank tributary there. It carves a deep gorge in the Central Himalayas near Namcha Barwa (7,755 m) and emerges from the foothills as the **Siang or Dihang**. It enters India west of Sadiya town in Arunachal Pradesh and flows south-west. After the Dibang (Sikang) and Lohit join on the left, it is known as the Brahmaputra. It travels about **750 km** through the Assam valley.

NCERT's physiography chapter says the river flows north-east to south-west and then makes an almost 90-degree turn southward at Dhubri before entering Bangladesh.

| Bank | Tributaries in Assam (NCERT) |
|---|---|
| Left bank | Burhi Dihing, Dhansari (South) (and the Dibang and Lohit above) |
| Right bank | Subansiri (rises in Tibet; antecedent), Kameng, Manas, Sankosh |

In Bangladesh the **Teesta** joins on its right bank, after which the river is known as the **Jamuna**; it finally merges with the **Padma** (the Ganga's distributary), which falls into the Bay of Bengal. The three countries on its course are China (Tibet), India and Bangladesh.

## Floods and shifting

The Brahmaputra is known for floods, channel shifting and bank erosion because most of its tributaries are large and carry a great load of sediment from heavy rain in the catchment. Its plains are known for riverine islands and sand bars, and braided channels (NCERT physiography).

## The Teesta

NCERT mentions the Teesta only as joining the Brahmaputra in Bangladesh. Standard knowledge (verify): it rises in the Sikkim Himalaya, the Rangeet is its tributary, it flows through Sikkim and West Bengal into Bangladesh and joins the Brahmaputra (Jamuna), not the sea directly. Note that the Teesta's source is in Sikkim, not at the Brahmaputra's source.

## The delta

The mouths of these great rivers form some of the largest deltas of the world, the famous **Sundarban delta** (NCERT physiography). The Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is shared by India and Bangladesh. Leong calls the Ganges delta an arcuate (fan-shaped) delta as large as West Malaysia; in the standard classification the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is often called tide-dominated or mixed, a variant to note (verify).

### 8. Core: the Peninsular drainage - east-flowing and west-flowing rivers

*Objective:* State why Peninsular drainage is older and different from Himalayan drainage, and give the source, length, basin and states of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Narmada and Tapi.

## Character

The Peninsular system is **older** than the Himalayan, as the broad, largely graded, shallow valleys and mature rivers show. The Western Ghats, close to the west coast, are the divide (Day 17 lesson GS-116 teaches the Ghats and this west/east divide in detail): most major rivers run west to east to the Bay of Bengal, and only small rivulets reach the Arabian Sea. The **Narmada and Tapi are the exceptions**, flowing west through rift valleys. Peninsular rivers are characterised by **fixed courses, absence of meanders and non-perennial flow**; the Narmada and Tapi differ. The Chambal, Sind, Betwa, Ken and Son belong to the Ganga system. NCERT's generalisation that large Peninsular rivers all begin in the Western Ghats is loose: the Mahanadi rises in Chhattisgarh.

## Three events that shaped it (NCERT)

1. Subsidence of the western flank into the sea in the early Tertiary, which spoilt the symmetry of rivers about the original watershed.
2. Himalayan upheaval, when the northern flank subsided and trough faulting formed the rift valleys of the Narmada and Tapi; those rivers fill the cracks with their detritus and so form no deltas.
3. A slight tilt of the plateau from north-west to south-east that oriented the drainage to the Bay of Bengal.

## The east-flowing rivers (all form deltas, GS-130 module 4)

- **Mahanadi**: rises near Sihawa, Raipur district (now Dhamtari; verify), Chhattisgarh, runs through Odisha; 851 km; catchment 1.42 lakh sq km; 53 per cent in Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh and 47 per cent in Odisha; some navigation in its lower course.
- **Godavari**: largest Peninsular river system, the Dakshin Ganga; rises in Nasik district, Maharashtra; 1,465 km; catchment 3.13 lakh sq km; 49 per cent in Maharashtra and 20 per cent in Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh; tributaries the Penganga, Indravati, Pranhita and Manjra; a gorge south of Polavaram; a large delta after Rajamundry.
- **Krishna**: second largest east-flowing; rises near Mahabaleshwar in the Sahyadri; 1,401 km; tributaries Koyna, Tungabhadra, Bhima; basin 27 per cent Maharashtra, 44 per cent Karnataka and 29 per cent Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.
- **Kaveri**: rises in the Brahmagiri hills (1,341 m), Kodagu district, Karnataka (NCERT prints Kogadu); 800 km; basin 81,155 sq km; carries water all year because the upper basin gets south-west monsoon rain and the lower part gets north-east monsoon rain; tributaries Kabini, Bhavani, Amravati; 3 per cent Kerala, 41 per cent Karnataka and 56 per cent Tamil Nadu.

## The west-flowing rivers

- **Narmada**: rises on the western flank of the Amarkantak plateau at about 1,057 m; flows in a rift valley between the Satpura and the Vindhyas; marble gorge and the Dhuandhar waterfall near Jabalpur; 1,312 km; catchment about 98,796 sq km; reaches the Arabian Sea south of Bharuch through a broad 27 km estuary; the Sardar Sarovar Project is on it.
- **Tapi**: rises at Multai, Betul district, Madhya Pradesh; 724 km; basin 65,145 sq km; 79 per cent in Maharashtra, 15 per cent in Madhya Pradesh and 6 per cent in Gujarat.
- **Luni**: the largest river system of Rajasthan, west of the Aravali; from Pushkar, where the Saraswati and Sabarmati branches join at Govindgarh; ephemeral; ends in the Rann of Kuchchh.

A slip to note: NCERT's Godavari percentages (49, 20 and "the rest" in Andhra Pradesh) omit the other states its basin crosses, including Telangana and Odisha (verify).

### 9. Master: deltas and estuaries, water use, trans-boundary rivers and a map-recall method

*Objective:* Contrast a delta with an estuary using Indian rivers, state NCERT's picture of how usable river water is, and apply a six-step sketch method to recall the basins and confluences.

## Delta versus estuary

A **delta** is a fan-shaped area of deposits at the river mouth, where the river drops the fine material it still carries and splits into **distributaries** (Leong). Leong lists favourable conditions: abundant sediment from active erosion upstream and a sheltered coast; it distinguishes bird's-foot (Mississippi), arcuate (Nile, Niger, Ganges, Mekong), estuarine (Amazon, Ob) and cuspate deltas. An **estuary** is a funnel-shaped river mouth with no delta where fresh and salt water mix (Day 17 lesson GS-130, bridge 1).

In India: the **Ganga-Brahmaputra delta with the Sundarbans**, and the deltas of the **Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri** on the east coast (NCERT); the west-flowing rivers do not form deltas, the **Narmada** (broad 27 km estuary, NCERT) being the example, and the Tapi also reaches the sea through an estuary (standard; verify). NCERT's reason for the Narmada and Tapi: they flow in trough faults and fill the cracks with detritus, so there is a lack of alluvial and deltaic deposits. Add the coast: a narrow submerged west coast, where short steep rivers meet the sea (Day 17 lesson GS-130 modules 3 and 4).

## How usable is the water?

NCERT: Indian rivers carry huge volumes of water, but it is uneven in time and space; in the rainy season much is wasted in floods and runs to the sea; floods in one area coincide with drought in another. NCERT's list of problems: insufficient quantity, pollution, silt load, uneven seasonal flow, disputes between states, and shrinking channels as settlements push toward the river. It names inter-basin transfer schemes for teachers to explain: Periyar Diversion Scheme, Indira Gandhi Canal Project, Kurnool-Cuddapah Canal, Beas-Satluj Link Canal and Ganga-Kaveri link. Moving water from the plains to the plateau meets the problem of uneven terrain and of lifting water. For pollution NCERT mentions the Ganga Action Plan, the Yamuna campaign at Delhi and Namami Gange (2014 flagship programme); for the Narmada it mentions the conservation mission Namami Devi Narmade.

## Trans-boundary rivers (Mains background)

The Indus system is shared by India and Pakistan; the Ganga by India, Nepal and Bangladesh; the Brahmaputra by China, India and Bangladesh; the Teesta by India and Bangladesh. The treaties and their current status (the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, the Ganga water treaty of 1996, reports in 2025 on the Indus treaty) are standard knowledge outside the extracts: verify before quoting.

## The six-step sketch method

1. Draw the outline and the divide: Delhi ridge, Aravalis, Sahyadris.
2. In the north-west draw the Indus and fan the five rivers in: Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Satluj from west to east.

4. In the north-east draw the Brahmaputra loop with Namcha Barwa, Sadiya, Dhubri and the Teesta.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Drainage: flow of water through well-defined channels; the network is a drainage system (NCERT).
- Drainage basin: area drained by a river and its tributaries; watershed: boundary between basins (NCERT).
- Perennial river: carries water all year; ephemeral: flows only in the rainy season.
- Antecedent river: keeps its course across land that rises; NCERT labels the Satluj, Kosi and Subansiri antecedent.
- Dendritic, radial, trellis and centripetal: tree-like, spoke-like from a hill, parallel with right-angle tributaries, and into a lake or depression.
- Delta: fan of deposits with distributaries; estuary: funnel-shaped mouth without a delta.
- River regime: the seasonal variation in volume of water in a river (Leong).
- Left and right bank: named facing downstream.

### Notes: Process chains

- Kosi shifting: heavy sediment from the upper reaches, deposition in the plain, blocked channel, river changes course, flood.
- Delta formation: rivers drop fine sediment at the mouth on a sheltered coast, the deposit builds out, the river splits into distributaries.
- Why Narmada and Tapi have estuaries: they flow in trough faults, fill cracks with detritus, so deltaic deposits are lacking.
- Why Himalayan rivers flood: snow melt plus monsoon rain, large sediment loads, gentle gradient on the plain, meandering and shifting channels.
- Why Peninsular rivers vary seasonally: rain-fed, fixed courses, no meanders; the Kaveri is steadier because both monsoons feed it.
- Water stress: uneven rain in time and space, pollution and silt, inter-state disputes, shrinking channels (NCERT).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Left bank versus right bank: always facing downstream.
- Ghaghara versus Gandak: Ghaghara joins at Chhapra, Gandak at Sonpur near Patna.
- Sarda (Kali, Saryu) versus Mandakini (Kali Ganga): different rivers.
- Bhagirathi (headstream at Gaumukh) versus Bhagirathi-Hugli (distributary): same name, different reach.
- Chambal (right-bank Yamuna tributary, Malwa plateau) versus Chambal ravines (badlands).
- Delta versus estuary: Godavari versus Narmada.
- Basin versus watershed: larger unit versus smaller or the dividing line.
