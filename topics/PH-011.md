### Bridge: Relative velocity

Two cars travel on a straight road in the same direction: A at 30 m/s and B at 20 m/s, both measured by an observer standing on the road. A passenger in B sees A pulling away at 30 − 20 = 10 m/s. This is the **relative velocity** of A with respect to B: it is the **velocity of A minus the velocity of B**, written v_AB = v_A − v_B (vectors, so direction matters; in one dimension use signs, with the direction of travel positive).

Notice what the road observer's choice does not change. If another observer rides in a third car moving at 25 m/s along the same road, that observer sees A at 5 m/s and B at −5 m/s, but their difference is still 5 − (−5) = 10 m/s. Subtracting the same velocity from both leaves the difference unchanged: (v_A − c) − (v_B − c) = v_A − v_B.

This simple fact is the heart of the lesson. Two observers whose frames move relative to each other at a constant velocity disagree about every velocity, yet they agree on every **relative** velocity, and therefore on the **separation** of two particles as a function of time. The relative velocity of two particles in a collision is also the quantity that decides whether they are approaching (negative closing rate) or separating.

Worked check. Two particles have velocities (4, 1) and (−2, 3) m/s. Relative velocity v_1 − v_2 = (6, −2) m/s, speed √(36 + 4) = 6.32 m/s. If both are seen from a frame moving at (1, 1) m/s, their velocities become (3, 0) and (−3, 2); the difference is still (6, −2).

### Bridge: Velocity addition (Galilean)

Imagine you walk at 1.5 m/s along the aisle of a train that moves at 20 m/s relative to the platform, in the same direction. Relative to the platform you move at 20 + 1.5 = 21.5 m/s. In symbols: let S be the platform frame (the **laboratory frame** in physics), S′ the train frame, and V the velocity of S′ relative to S. A particle with velocity v′ in S′ has velocity

$$ v = v′ + V   or   v′ = v − V $$

in S. These are the **Galilean velocity transformations**. They are vector equations, so the same rule applies component by component, and they hold for speeds much less than the speed of light (for speeds close to light the relativistic rule replaces them; that is outside this lesson).

For positions, if the origins coincide at time zero, r = r′ + V t. Since V is constant, differentiating gives v = v′ + V, and differentiating once more gives a = a′: **both frames measure the same acceleration**, so Newton's second law (with forces that depend on separations) takes the same form in both. This is why a frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial.

Careful use: V is the velocity of the moving frame as measured in the first frame. To go from the lab to the moving frame, subtract V from every velocity; to go back, add V.

Worked check. A ball moves at (6, 2) m/s in the lab. A frame moves at (2, 0) m/s. In that frame the ball has v′ = (6 − 2, 2) = (4, 2) m/s. Going back: (4 + 2, 2) = (6, 2) m/s.

### Bridge: Vector triangle method

To add two vectors a and b graphically, draw a, then draw b starting from the tip of a; the sum a + b runs from the start of a to the tip of b. The three vectors form a **triangle**, and ordinary triangle geometry then applies.

Two rules are used repeatedly. The **cosine rule** gives the length of the sum: |a + b|² = a² + b² + 2ab cos α, where α is the angle between a and b when drawn from the same point (not the interior angle of the triangle; the interior angle is π − α, which is why the sign is plus). The **sine rule** relates sides to the angles opposite them: in a triangle with sides p, q, r opposite angles P, Q, R, p/sin P = q/sin Q = r/sin R.

In collision problems the first vector is the velocity V of the centre-of-mass frame and the second is the particle's velocity in that frame; their sum is the lab velocity. As the second vector turns through all directions at constant length, its tip traces a **circle**, and the lab velocity is the line from the origin to a point on that circle. Everything about lab angles can then be read from a picture of a circle and a point.

Worked check. a = 3 m/s along x and b = 4 m/s at 90° to a: |a + b|² = 9 + 16 + 2·3·4·cos 90° = 25, so |a + b| = 5 m/s. For a = b = 2 at α = 60°: |a + b|² = 4 + 4 + 8 cos 60° = 12, so |a + b| = 3.46 m/s.

### 1. Orientation: what 'centre of mass and laboratory reference frames' asks of you

*Objective:* State what the toolkit is for, how it differs from PH-001, PH-009 and PH-010, and which two past questions it answers.

## What the topic is

Experiments are done in the **laboratory (lab) frame**, where the target is at rest and the detector is fixed. Theory is easiest in the **centre-of-mass (CM) frame**, where the total momentum is zero and the collision looks symmetrical. The skill examined is the translation between the two: velocities, angles, energies and cross-sections. Mathur notes that collisions in nuclear physics are customarily treated in the CM frame (section 6.8).

### 2. Foundation: the two frames, the CM velocity and why separation is the same

*Objective:* Define the lab and CM frames, derive the CM velocity and the zero-momentum property, and prove that the separation of two particles is the same in both frames.

## Definitions

The **lab frame** is attached to the laboratory (taken as inertial). The **centre-of-mass (CM) frame**, or C-frame, is a frame whose origin is the centre of mass of the system. For an isolated system (no external forces) the CM moves at constant velocity, so the CM frame is also inertial (Mathur, section 6.8). It is also called the **zero-momentum frame**.

## Velocity of the CM and the transformation

For particles of masses m₁, m₂ at lab positions r₁, r₂ the CM position is R = (m₁r₁ + m₂r₂)/M and its velocity is V = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/M, M = m₁ + m₂. Since the CM frame moves at V relative to the lab, the Galilean transformations (bridge B2) give, for each particle,

$$ r_i′ = r_i − R,   v_i′ = v_i − V $$

## Zero total momentum in the CM frame

$$ m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′ = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) − (m₁ + m₂)V = M V − M V = 0 $$

So **the momenta of the two particles in the CM frame are equal and opposite**: p₁′ = −p₂′ = p′. For a target at rest in the lab with projectile velocity u: V = m₁u/M. The projectile's CM velocity is u₁′ = u − V = m₂u/M and the target's is u₂′ = −V = −m₁u/M. Check: m₁u₁′ + m₂u₂′ = m₁m₂u/M − m₂m₁u/M = 0.

## Separation is invariant (2023 Q3(b)(i))

Define the separation vector r = r₁ − r₂ (the position of particle 1 relative to 2). In the CM frame,

$$ r′ = r₁′ − r₂′ = (r₁ − R) − (r₂ − R) = r₁ − r₂ = r $$

The CM position R cancels exactly. Hence the separation vector, and therefore its length, is the same in the two frames at every instant. Differentiating, the relative velocity is also the same: v₁′ − v₂′ = v₁ − v₂ (bridge B1).

**Assumption** used: the frames are related by a pure shift of origin (Galilean, non-relativistic, no rotation), and times are the same in both. The same argument holds for a CM moving at constant or changing velocity as long as both particles are referred to the same R(t).

## Why this matters

The force between the two particles depends only on their separation, so the **interaction is described identically in both frames**. Only the kinematics of the observer changes. This is also why the relative motion (PH-009) can be solved once and used in either frame.

## Numerical illustration

Particles of 2.0 kg at (5, 1) m/s and 3.0 kg at (−2, 3) m/s: V = (10 − 6, 2 + 9)/5 = (0.8, 2.2) m/s. In the CM frame, v₁′ = (4.2, −1.2) and v₂′ = (−2.8, 0.8); momenta 2(4.2, −1.2) = (8.4, −2.4) and 3(−2.8, 0.8) = (−8.4, 2.4), summing to zero. The relative velocity (7, −2) m/s is the same in both frames.

### 3. Core: the kinetic energy split T = T_cm + T_rel (recap) and the available energy

*Objective:* Derive the split of the total kinetic energy into the CM motion and the motion about the CM, show T_rel = (1/2) μ v_rel², and identify the energy available for inelastic processes.

## Recap (derived in PH-009 modules 4 and 9)

For lab velocities v_i = V + v_i′ the cross term in T = ½ Σ m_i (V + v_i′)² is V · Σ m_i v_i′, which vanishes because Σ m_i v_i′ = 0 (module 2). Hence

$$ T = ½ M V² + T′,   T′ = ½ Σ m_i v_i′² = ½ μ v_rel² $$

for two particles, with v₁′ = (m₂/M) v_rel and v₂′ = −(m₁/M) v_rel and μ = m₁m₂/M. PH-009 module 4 gives the full algebra and PH-009 module 9 the maximum energy loss; they are not repeated. **Meaning**: for a collision V is fixed by momentum conservation, so **only T′ can be changed by the collision**. Dimensional check: μ v² is M L² T⁻².

## Target at rest

With the target at rest in the lab, v_rel = u, V = m₁u/M, and

$$ T = ½ m₁u²,   ½ M V² = T · m₁/M,   T′ = T · m₂/M = ½ μ u² $$

Check: m₁/M + m₂/M = 1. **Available energy**: for an inelastic collision or a nuclear reaction, at most T′ can be converted into excitation, heat, mass or new particles; the CM energy ½ M V² must remain as the motion of the products, by conservation of momentum. For a heavy target (m₂ ≫ m₁), T′ ≈ T and almost all of the energy is available. For a light target (m₂ ≪ m₁) T′ ≈ T m₂/m₁ is small.

## Perfectly inelastic collisions

If the two particles stick together, the final velocity is V, so the final kinetic energy is ½ M V² and the loss is exactly T′ = ½ μ u² (the maximum loss consistent with momentum conservation, as Mathur notes and PH-009 module 9 shows). Worked: 2 kg at 6 m/s striking 4 kg at rest has T = 36 J, T′ = 36 × 4/6 = 24 J, final kinetic energy 12 J (check ½ × 6 × 2² = 12 J).

## Symmetric beams versus fixed targets

A fixed target gives T′ = T m₂/M; two beams of equal masses with equal and opposite velocities already have V = 0, so all the energy is T′. For two deuterons: a 10 MeV beam on a stationary target gives only 5 MeV; two head-on 5 MeV beams give 10 MeV (non-relativistic estimate).

## Checks

Limiting case: m₂ → ∞: V → 0, T′ → T. Equal masses: T′ = T/2. Dimensions agree term by term.

### 4. Core: elastic collisions in the CM frame and the lab velocities

*Objective:* Show that in an elastic collision the CM speeds are unchanged and only the direction rotates through Θ, then write the final lab velocities of both particles.

## Speeds do not change

Assumptions: isolated pair, elastic collision (kinetic energy conserved), non-relativistic. In the CM frame the total momentum is zero before and after, so after the collision p₁′ = −p₂′ = p_f. Before the collision, p₁′ = −p₂′ = p_i. Kinetic energy in the CM frame is T′ = p²/(2m₁) + p²/(2m₂) = p²/(2μ) for either state. Energy conservation T′_i = T′_f then gives p_i²/(2μ) = p_f²/(2μ), so

$$ |p_f| = |p_i|  and hence  |v₁′| and |v₂′| are unchanged. $$

(Mathur reaches the same conclusion by algebra, section 6.10.) **Only the direction rotates.** Take x along the incident direction; before the collision the CM velocities are (u₁′, 0) and (−V, 0), where u₁′ = m₂u/M and V = m₁u/M. After the collision, with the CM scattering angle Θ:

$$ v₁′ = u₁′ (cos Θ, sin Θ),   v₂′ = −V (cos Θ, sin Θ) $$

Θ is unrestricted: 0 to π (Mathur, section 6.11). The two particles move in opposite directions in the CM frame, which is why the CM picture is simple.

## Back to the lab frame

Add V along x (bridge B2):

$$ v₁ = (V + u₁′ cos Θ,  u₁′ sin Θ) $$
$$ v₂ = (V − V cos Θ,  − V sin Θ) = V (1 − cos Θ, −sin Θ) $$

**Speeds.** |v₁|² = V² + u₁′² + 2 V u₁′ cos Θ (cosine rule, bridge B3). With u = V + u₁′ and γ = V/u₁′ = m₁/m₂ this is |v₁|² = u₁′² (1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ). For the target, |v₂|² = V² [(1 − cos Θ)² + sin² Θ] = 2V² (1 − cos Θ) = 4V² sin²(Θ/2), so

$$ |v₂| = 2V sin(Θ/2) = (2 m₁u/M) sin(Θ/2) $$

## Checks

Θ = 0 (no scattering): v₁ = (V + u₁′, 0) = (u, 0) and v₂ = 0: nothing happens. Θ = π (head-on, bounce back in the CM frame): v₁ = ((V − u₁′), 0) = ((m₁ − m₂)u/M, 0), which is the one-dimensional elastic result, and |v₂| = 2V = 2m₁u/M, again the one-dimensional elastic result. Momentum: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ has x component m₁(V + u₁′cos Θ) + m₂V(1 − cos Θ) = (m₁ + m₂)V + (m₁u₁′ − m₂V)cos Θ = m₁u, since m₁u₁′ = m₂V. y component: m₁u₁′ sin Θ − m₂V sin Θ = 0.

## Number

m₁ = 3 kg at 4 m/s on m₂ = 1 kg at rest; Θ = 120°. V = 3 m/s, u₁′ = 1 m/s. v₁ = (3 + 1 × (−0.5), 0.866) = (2.50, 0.866) m/s, speed 2.646 m/s; v₂ = 3(1.5, −0.866) = (4.50, −2.598) m/s, speed 5.196 m/s. Kinetic energy: ½ × 3 × 7 + ½ × 27 = 10.5 + 13.5 = 24.0 J, equal to the initial ½ × 3 × 16 = 24.0 J.

### 5. Core: the velocity circle, the sine-rule identity and the tangent construction

*Objective:* Recall tan θ and the recoil angle (PH-010 module 8), then derive the sine-rule identity sin(Θ − θ) = γ sin θ and the tangent construction from the velocity circle.

## The picture

Draw the velocity of the CM frame, V, from the origin O to a point C on the x axis (OC = V, along the beam). The projectile's CM velocity u₁′ is a vector of fixed length w = u₁′ starting at C and pointing in any direction (angle Θ from +x). Its tip P traces a **circle of radius w centred at C**; the lab velocity v₁ is the vector OP. So **the angle θ of OP from the x axis is the lab scattering angle**. The ratio of the two fixed lengths is OC/CP = V/w = γ = m₁/m₂.

## Recap: tan θ and the recoil angle (derived in PH-010 module 8)

From the components of module 4, v₁x = V + w cos Θ and v₁y = w sin Θ, so

$$ tan θ = sin Θ/(γ + cos Θ) $$

(Upadhyaya's problem list, PDF 154, states it as tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + 1/A) with A = m₂/m₁). For the target, v₂ = V(1 − cos Θ, −sin Θ), so tan φ = cot(Θ/2) and **φ = (π − Θ)/2, independent of the masses**, between 0 (Θ = π) and 90° (Θ = 0). PH-010 module 8 gives the full derivation, the three mass cases and the worked examples; they are not repeated here.

## The sine-rule form (new route)

Write tan θ (γ + cos Θ) = sin Θ and multiply through by cos θ: γ sin θ + cos Θ sin θ = sin Θ cos θ, so γ sin θ = sin Θ cos θ − cos Θ sin θ, that is

$$ sin(Θ − θ) = γ sin θ $$

This is the sine rule in the triangle O C P: the side CP = w faces the angle θ at O; the side OC = V faces the angle at P, which is (Θ − θ) (the exterior angle at C is Θ = θ + angle at P). So w/sin θ = V/sin(Θ − θ). It is the key to module 6.

## The tangent construction (new)

For γ > 1, O lies outside the circle. The largest θ occurs when OP is tangent to the circle, so CP ⟂ OP and the angle at P is 90°, i.e. Θ − θ = 90°. Then sin θ_max = w/V = 1/γ, tan θ_max = w/√(V² − w²) = 1/√(γ² − 1), and cos Θ = −1/γ at the tangent point (Θ = θ_max + 90°).

## Numbers (new examples)

A neutron-like projectile of 1 u on a carbon-12 nucleus (12 u), γ = 1/12, Θ = 60°: tan θ = 0.8660/(0.0833 + 0.5) = 1.485, θ = 56.04° and φ = 60°; the target is much heavier than the projectile, so θ is only 4° below Θ. A projectile of 5 u on a target of 2 u (γ = 2.5), Θ = 60°: tan θ = 0.8660/(2.5 + 0.5) = 0.2887, θ = 16.10°, φ = 60°. For that same pair the tangent construction gives θ_max = arcsin(0.4) = 23.58° at Θ = 113.58° (cos Θ = −0.4), and indeed Θ − θ_max = 90°.

## Checks

m₂ → ∞: γ → 0, θ → Θ (lab and CM coincide). Equal masses: γ = 1, tan θ = tan(Θ/2), so θ = Θ/2 and θ + φ = π/2. Dimensionless on both sides.


### 6. Core: the maximum lab angle sin θ_max = 1/γ and the two-valued mapping

*Objective:* Prove that the lab angle of the projectile is limited by sin θ_max = m₂/m₁ when m₁ > m₂, and describe the mapping Θ → θ for all three mass cases.

## Proof of the maximum angle (2022 Q2(b))

Use the relation of module 5: **sin(Θ − θ) = γ sin θ**, with Θ in [0, π] and γ = m₁/m₂. For a given lab angle θ to be reachable, the equation must have a solution, and the sine of any angle is at most 1:

$$ γ sin θ = sin(Θ − θ) ≤ 1 $$

**Case m₁ > m₂ (γ > 1).** Then sin θ ≤ 1/γ, so

$$ sin θ_max = 1/γ = m₂/m₁ $$

and this maximum is attained when Θ − θ = 90°. Check with the geometry: the lab velocity OP is largest in angle when the line OP is tangent to the circle, where CP ⟂ OP, so sin θ = CP/OC = w/V = 1/γ. Every θ below θ_max is reachable, and for γ ≥ 1 we always have θ < 90° (γ + cos Θ > 0 while the projectile moves). Interpretation: a heavy projectile cannot be turned through a large angle by a light target, just as a lorry cannot be turned far off course by a football.

**Case m₁ = m₂ (γ = 1).** sin(Θ − θ) = sin θ gives Θ = 2θ, so θ = Θ/2 < 90°. The value 90° is approached as Θ → π but never reached: at Θ = π the projectile is at rest, so for every scattering that leaves it moving θ < 90°.

**Case m₁ < m₂ (γ < 1).** γ sin θ < 1 always, and Θ = θ + arcsin(γ sin θ) lies in [0, π] for every θ in [0, π] (for θ near π the correction γ(π − θ) is smaller than π − θ). **Any lab angle, up to 180°, is possible**; the projectile can bounce back. This agrees with Mathur's three cases (section 6.11).

## The mapping and its two values

For γ > 1 a given θ < θ_max arises from **two** CM angles, because the sine equation has two solutions:

$$ Θ = θ + arcsin(γ sin θ)   or   Θ = θ + π − arcsin(γ sin θ) $$

They merge at θ = θ_max. For γ ≤ 1 only the first branch is physical.

## Alternative route: lab frame only (2022 Q2(b) is posed in lab angles)

Without the CM frame, eliminate φ and v₂ from the three conservation equations: m₁u = m₁v₁ cos θ + m₂v₂ cos φ, 0 = m₁v₁ sin θ − m₂v₂ sin φ, m₁u² = m₁v₁² + m₂v₂². Squaring and adding the first two gives m₂²v₂² = m₁²(u² − 2u v₁ cos θ + v₁²); the energy equation gives m₂v₂² = m₁(u² − v₁²). Eliminating v₂:

$$ (m₁ + m₂) v₁² − 2 m₁ u v₁ cos θ + (m₁ − m₂) u² = 0 $$

so v₁ = u [m₁ cos θ ± √(m₂² − m₁² sin²θ)]/(m₁ + m₂). A real v₁ needs the discriminant ≥ 0: m₂² ≥ m₁² sin²θ, that is **sin θ_max = m₂/m₁** (possible only when m₁ ≥ m₂; for m₁ < m₂ the square root is real for every θ). The two roots for θ < θ_max are the two speeds belonging to the **two CM angles** of the two-valued mapping below. (The quadratic was verified symbolically.)

## Numbers

m₁ = 4 u, m₂ = 1 u (γ = 4): θ_max = arcsin(0.25) = 14.48°. For θ = 10°: arcsin(4 sin 10°) = arcsin(0.6946) = 43.99°, so Θ = 53.99° or Θ = 10° + 180° − 43.99° = 146.01°. ### 7. Core: energy given to the target and the right-angle result for equal masses

*Objective:* Derive the fraction of the kinetic energy transferred to the target, the projectile's remaining energy, and the right-angle rule for equal masses.

## Energy transferred (recap of PH-010 module 8)

From module 4 the target's lab speed is v₂ = 2V sin(Θ/2), so T₂ = ½ m₂ v₂² = 2 m₂ V² sin²(Θ/2) and, dividing by T = ½ m₁u²,

$$ T₂/T = 4 m₁ m₂ sin²(Θ/2)/(m₁ + m₂)² $$

PH-010 module 8 derives this with the same example set. The maximum is 4m₁m₂/M² at Θ = π (the PH-001 module 8 moderator result): 1 for equal masses and small for very unequal masses; it is zero at Θ = 0.

## Energy left with the projectile

|v₁|² = w²(1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ) and u = w(1 + γ) (since u = V + w and V = γ w), so

$$ T₁/T = (1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ)/(1 + γ)² $$

Check that T₁ + T₂ = T: with 4γ/(1 + γ)² = 4m₁m₂/M² (because γ = m₁/m₂), T₂/T = 2γ(1 − cos Θ)/(1 + γ)², and (1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ + 2γ − 2γ cos Θ)/(1 + γ)² = 1. ✓.

## Equal masses: the right angle (a past-question staple)

m₁ = m₂: γ = 1. From module 5, θ = Θ/2 and φ = (π − Θ)/2, so

$$ θ + φ = Θ/2 + (π − Θ)/2 = π/2 $$

The two particles leave **at right angles** to each other. A second proof using only lab conservation laws: momentum gives u = v₁ + v₂ (as vectors, equal masses, divide by m), so u² = v₁² + v₂² + 2 v₁ · v₂; energy gives u² = v₁² + v₂²; hence v₁ · v₂ = 0, so the velocities are perpendicular (or one is zero). Mathur states this result (section 6.10 and 6.11).

The energies are T₁ = T cos²(Θ/2) and T₂ = T sin²(Θ/2) (put γ = 1 above), consistent with 1 + 1 + 2cos Θ = 4 cos²(Θ/2).

## Numbers (new examples)

An alpha particle (4.0026 u) of 6.0 MeV on a nitrogen-14 nucleus (14.0031 u), Θ = 90°: T₂/T = 4 × 4.0026 × 14.0031 × sin² 45°/(18.0057)² = 0.3458, so T₂ = 2.075 MeV and T₁ = 3.925 MeV. Maximum transfer (Θ = π): 0.6915, 4.149 MeV. Equal masses, billiard ball at 2.0 m/s with Θ = 60°: v₁ = 2 cos 30° = 1.732 m/s at 30°, v₂ = 2 sin 30° = 1.000 m/s at 60° (other side), v₁·v₂ = 0.

## Moderation of neutrons (recap of PH-001 module 8)

The largest fractional energy a neutron can lose in one collision with a nucleus of mass number A is 4A/(A + 1)²: 1.000 for hydrogen (A = 1), 0.889 for deuterium (A = 2), 0.284 for carbon-12, 0.0167 for uranium-238. This is the quantitative basis of Mathur's remark that hydrogen is a good moderator (section 6.10), using mass numbers as an approximation to the masses.

### 8. Core: inelastic collisions, the Q value and the threshold energy

*Objective:* Define Q for a reaction, derive the lab threshold energy T_th = −Q (m₁ + m₂)/m₂, and relate the available energy to the CM frame.

## Inelastic processes

In an **inelastic** collision the particles' kinetic energy changes (Mathur, section 6.9): an atom may be raised to an excited state, or a nuclear reaction may rearrange the particles. Momentum is still conserved. Define the **Q value** as the gain in total kinetic energy, Q = T_final − T_initial; it equals the decrease in rest-mass energy, Q = (Σ m_initial − Σ m_final)c². Q > 0 is exothermic (energy released); Q < 0 is endothermic (energy must be supplied). For an excitation energy x, Q = −x.

## Threshold energy (derivation)

Consider A + B → C + D with A the projectile (m₁, lab kinetic energy T) and B the target (m₂) at rest. Assumptions: non-relativistic; the masses of the products differ from the initial masses by a tiny fraction (so the CM motion can be treated with M = m₁ + m₂).

1. Momentum conservation fixes the CM velocity V = m₁u/M, so the energy ½ M V² = T m₁/M is locked in the motion of the products.
2. In the CM frame, T′_i = T m₂/M (module 3). After the reaction T′_f = T′_i + Q, by energy conservation in the CM frame (Q is a change in total kinetic energy, and the CM-frame kinetic energy changes by the same amount since ½ M V² is unchanged).
3. T′_f cannot be negative. **At threshold the products are at rest in the CM frame** (T′_f = 0, the products move together at V in the lab), so T′_i = −Q.
4. Hence T_th m₂/M = −Q, that is

$$ T_th = −Q (m₁ + m₂)/m₂ $$

The lab threshold exceeds |Q| by the factor (m₁ + m₂)/m₂ > 1: **not all the projectile's energy is usable** because the CM must keep moving.

## Number: nitrogen-14 plus alpha

The reaction ¹⁴N(α, p)¹⁷O has Q = (4.002603 + 14.003074 − 1.007825 − 16.999132) u × 931.494 MeV/u = −1.192 MeV (atomic masses, standard values outside the extracts; verify). T_th = 1.192 × (4.0026 + 14.0031)/14.0031 = 1.533 MeV; with mass numbers (18/14) it is 1.532 MeV.

## Perfectly inelastic as a special case

If the particles stick, the final CM-frame energy is 0 and the loss is T′: Q = −T′ = −T m₂/M; for an excitation, the minimum kinetic energy to excite the target atom of excitation energy x by a particle m₁ is T_min = x (m₁ + m₂)/m₂, the same formula with Q = −x.

## Checks

m₂ ≫ m₁: T_th ≈ |Q|. m₂ ≪ m₁: T_th is much larger than |Q| (a beam on a light target is wasteful). Dimensions are those of energy. Relative to colliders: in the CM frame the available energy is T′, so for an energetic process a collider (V = 0) uses all of the beam energy.

## Relativistic remark (flagged forward link)

At high energies the rest masses and the kinetic energies are comparable and the exact threshold has the standard textbook form T_th = −Q (m₁ + m₂ + m₃ + m₄)/(2 m₂) (rest masses of the two initial and two final particles, Q = (m₁ + m₂ − m₃ − m₄)c²); this is not derived here (verify), and the formula above is the non-relativistic limit valid when |Q| is much less than the rest-mass energies.

### 9. Master: the complete toolkit, the cross-section Jacobian (result only) and checks

*Objective:* Assemble the procedure for any two-body collision problem, quote the cross-section conversion from PH-010, and recognise the usual failures.

## The eight-step procedure

1. Name the frame of every given quantity (lab or CM) and write the masses, γ = m₁/m₂ and u.
2. Compute V = m₁u/M and w = u₁′ = m₂u/M (target at rest).
3. Decide whether the collision is elastic (speeds in the CM frame unchanged) or inelastic (use Q and T′).
4. Choose the unknown that is easiest in the CM frame (usually Θ) and solve there.
5. Return to the lab frame with v = v′ + V, or use tan θ = sin Θ/(γ + cos Θ), φ = (π − Θ)/2.
6. For the energy transferred use T₂/T = 4m₁m₂ sin²(Θ/2)/M².
7. Test the answer: momentum and kinetic energy before and after in the lab frame; limits m₂ → ∞ (θ = Θ) and Θ = 0.
8. For γ > 1 remember the limit sin θ_max = 1/γ and the two Θ values for each θ.

## Cross-section conversion (result only; derivation in PH-010 module 9)

Particles scattered into the CM solid angle dΩ_C are the same particles counted in dΩ_L, so dσ/dΩ_L = (dσ/dΩ_C) |d cos Θ/d cos θ|. PH-010 gives

$$ dσ/dΩ_L = (dσ/dΩ_C) (1 + γ² + 2γ cos Θ)^(3/2)/|1 + γ cos Θ| $$

evaluated at the CM angle Θ that corresponds to the lab angle θ; for γ > 1 sum the two branches; the Jacobian diverges at θ_max. For an alpha particle on nitrogen-14 (γ = 0.2858) at Θ = 90° the factor is (1 + γ²)^(3/2) = 1.125, so the lab cross-section is 12.5% larger than the CM one; for gold (γ = 0.0203) it is 1.0006, practically equal; for alpha on hydrogen the two are very different.

## Alternative methods

For a single problem one can solve the lab-frame momentum and energy equations directly (Mathur section 6.10): three equations, four unknowns (v₁, v₂, θ, φ), so one of them must be given. Eliminating φ and v₂ gives the quadratic (m₁ + m₂)v₁² − 2m₁u v₁ cos θ + (m₁ − m₂)u² = 0 of module 6, whose discriminant yields θ_max directly. The CM method shows why the angle relations are universal.

## Failure checklist

Mixing lab and CM angles (a frequent slip: the lab angle θ is never larger than Θ, since sin(Θ − θ) = γ sin θ ≥ 0); using E_lab when E_cm is meant; taking φ = Θ; forgetting that for γ ≥ 1 the lab angle cannot exceed θ_max; using the elastic formulae for a reaction; treating the CM frame as inertial when external forces act.

### Notes: Definitions

- Lab frame: the frame fixed to the laboratory, taken as inertial; target at rest before the collision.
- CM (C) frame: the frame moving with the centre of mass; total momentum zero; inertial for an isolated system.
- V = (m1 v1 + m2 v2)/(m1 + m2): CM velocity; for a target at rest V = m1 u/M with M = m1 + m2.
- Reduced mass mu = m1 m2/M; gamma = m1/m2; Theta = CM scattering angle; theta = lab angle of m1; phi = lab recoil angle of m2.
- Q value: gain in total kinetic energy, equal to the decrease of rest-mass energy; Q < 0 endothermic.
- Available energy: the CM-frame kinetic energy T' = (1/2) mu v_rel^2.

### Notes: Governing equations

- r_i' = r_i - R, v_i' = v_i - V; m1 v1' + m2 v2' = 0; r1' - r2' = r1 - r2.
- T = (1/2) M V^2 + T'; T' = (1/2) mu v_rel^2 = T m2/M for a target at rest.
- Elastic CM: speeds unchanged, u1' = m2 u/M, u2' = -m1 u/M, directions rotate through Theta.
- Lab: v1 = (V + u1' cos Theta, u1' sin Theta); v2 = V (1 - cos Theta, - sin Theta); |v2| = 2V sin(Theta/2).
- tan theta = sin Theta/(gamma + cos Theta); phi = (pi - Theta)/2; sin(Theta - theta) = gamma sin theta.
- sin theta_max = 1/gamma = m2/m1 for m1 > m2; theta_max = 90 degrees for m1 = m2; any angle up to 180 degrees for m1 < m2.
- T2/T = 4 m1 m2 sin^2(Theta/2)/M^2; T1/T = (1 + gamma^2 + 2 gamma cos Theta)/(1 + gamma)^2.
- T_th = -Q (m1 + m2)/m2 (non-relativistic).

### Notes: Derivation chain to be able to write closed-book

- 1. r_i' = r_i - R so r1' - r2' = r1 - r2 (separation invariant); v' relative velocity likewise.
- 2. T = (1/2) sum m_i (V + v_i')^2; cross term vanishes since sum m_i v_i' = 0; T' = (1/2) mu v_rel^2.
- 3. Zero momentum and energy conservation in CM: |p| unchanged; rotate through Theta; add V.
- 4. v1x = V + w cos Theta, v1y = w sin Theta: tan theta = sin Theta/(gamma + cos Theta); recoil (pi - Theta)/2.
- 5. Multiply by cos theta: sin(Theta - theta) = gamma sin theta <= 1: sin theta_max = 1/gamma (gamma > 1).
- 6. T2 = (1/2) m2 (2V sin(Theta/2))^2: T2/T = 4 m1 m2 sin^2(Theta/2)/M^2.
- 7. Threshold: T'_f = T'_i + Q >= 0 with T'_i = T m2/M gives T_th = -Q M/m2.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Lab angle theta versus CM angle Theta: they coincide only for an infinitely heavy target.
- CM energy versus lab energy: E_cm = E_lab m2/(m1 + m2) is what is available; the rest is locked in the CM motion.
- Recoil angle phi = (pi - Theta)/2 is mass independent; the projectile angle theta is not.
- Elastic (kinetic energy conserved) versus inelastic (Q not zero) versus perfectly inelastic (stick; loss = T').
- Separation invariance (Galilean shift of origin) versus invariance of velocities (they change by V).

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- All formulae are non-relativistic; at high energy the threshold and kinematics change (flagged forward link).
- The CM frame is inertial only for an isolated system (no external force).
- For gamma > 1 the single-valued formulae for the cross-section fail: sum both Theta branches; the Jacobian diverges at theta_max (PH-010).
- Identical particles need a quantum treatment; classical lab-CM relations count the particles as distinguishable.
- The threshold formula assumes product masses close to the initial masses in the CM motion.

    - **task:** Prove that the separation of two colliding particles is the same when observed in the centre-of-mass and laboratory systems. Timed written solution target: 12 minutes for a 10-mark answer (practice target, not an official allotment).

      - Diagram: origin O of the lab frame, particles at r1 and r2, CM at R, CM-frame positions r1' and r2' measured from R.
      - Definitions: M = m1 + m2; R = (m1 r1 + m2 r2)/M; the CM frame has its origin at R and no rotation relative to the lab; times are the same in both frames (Galilean).

      - Subtract: r1' - r2' = (r1 - R) - (r2 - R) = r1 - r2, because R cancels.

      - Meaning: the force between the particles depends only on their separation, so the interaction is described identically in both frames.

### Memory skeleton

- Two frames: V = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/M; vᵢ′ = vᵢ − V; CM frame has zero momentum; separation invariant
- T = ½MV² + T′, T′ = ½μv_rel²: only T′ is available
- Elastic in CM: speeds unchanged, directions rotate through Θ; add V for lab
- tan θ = sin Θ/(γ + cos Θ), γ = m₁/m₂; recoil φ = (π − Θ)/2
- sin(Θ − θ) = γ sin θ gives sin θ_max = 1/γ for m₁ > m₂
- Energy to target: T₂/T = 4m₁m₂ sin²(Θ/2)/M²
- Inelastic: Q value; threshold T_th = −Q(m₁ + m₂)/m₂

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, go from the CM frame to tan θ = sin Θ/(γ + cos Θ), then derive sin θ_max = m₂/m₁ and the threshold energy, stating every condition.

### Last-week summary

- CM frame: Σmᵢvᵢ′ = 0; r₁′ − r₂′ = r₁ − r₂; T = ½MV² + ½μv_rel², only T′ = T m₂/M available (target at rest).
- Elastic CM: u₁′ = m₂u/M, u₂′ = −m₁u/M, rotate through Θ; tan θ = sin Θ/(γ + cos Θ), φ = (π − Θ)/2.
- sin(Θ − θ) = γ sin θ: sin θ_max = m₂/m₁ only if m₁ > m₂; equal masses θ + φ = 90°; m₁ < m₂ any angle.
- T₂/T = 4m₁m₂ sin²(Θ/2)/M², maximum 4m₁m₂/M²; T_th = −Q(m₁ + m₂)/m₂, non-relativistic.
