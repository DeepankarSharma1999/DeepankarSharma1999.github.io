### 1. Orientation: minerals and energy on one page

*Objective:* State what a mineral is, the NCERT classification of minerals and energy resources, and the one rule that tells you where most Indian minerals lie.

## The one-page picture

A **mineral** is a natural substance, of organic or inorganic origin, with definite chemical and physical properties (NCERT Class XII, *India: People and Economy*, chapter 5). Leong adds that minerals make up the rocks of the earth's crust and that well over 2,000 are known, scattered very unevenly. An **ore** is a mineral from which a metal can be extracted at a profit (standard definition, not stated in the extracts). **Energy resources** here means the mineral fuels and the other sources that give power.

NCERT sorts them like this:

- **Metallic** minerals give metals. They split into **ferrous** (containing iron: iron ore, manganese, chromite) and **non-ferrous** (no iron: copper, bauxite).
- **Non-metallic** minerals are either **organic** in origin (the fossil or mineral fuels, coal and petroleum, made from buried plants and animals) or **inorganic** (mica, limestone, graphite).
- **Energy resources** are **conventional** (coal, petroleum, natural gas, nuclear minerals; all exhaustible) or **non-conventional** (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, bio-energy and the like; NCERT calls these the sustainable ones).

## Where are they?

NCERT gives three facts. First, most valuable minerals are very old (pre-Palaeozoic, meaning older than the Palaeozoic era) and sit in the **metamorphic and igneous rocks of peninsular India**. Second, the vast **alluvial plain of north India has no minerals of economic use**. Third, most major mineral resources lie **east of a line joining Mangaluru and Kanpur**. This is a rule of thumb: the north-western belt in Rajasthan and Gujarat, Gujarat's petroleum and the offshore Mumbai High field all lie west of it.

## What UPSC has asked

Twenty-three official questions are verified: fifteen in Prelims (2018-2026) and eight in Mains (2017-2025). The Prelims ones ask for the law on minor minerals and coal, what monazite and ilmenite are, uranium, rare earths and critical minerals, coal ash, furnace oil and coal-fired plants. The Mains ones ask about the environmental hazard of mining, coal, offshore oil, solar and wind, the Deccan Trap and why mining adds so little to GDP. Module 10 maps all of them. Only two or three need a place name; most need a clear idea of how the resource is classified and governed.

### 2. Foundation: characteristics, enrichment and the belts, with a recall method

*Objective:* State the three characteristics of minerals, explain how weathering enriches ores, name the three mineral belts and use the anchor method to recall minerals by state.

## Three characteristics (NCERT)

1. Minerals are **unevenly distributed** over space.
2. There is an **inverse relation between quality and quantity**: good-quality ore is scarce, low-quality ore is plentiful.
3. All minerals are **exhaustible**. They take geological time to form and cannot be replaced when needed, so they have "no second crop". Leong calls mining the "robber industry" for the same reason.

## How an ore is made workable

Weathering, the breakdown of rock where it lies, can help. NCERT *Fundamentals of Physical Geography* (chapter 5) explains **enrichment**: when rock weathers, groundwater leaches (washes out) some materials, so the valuable material left behind becomes more concentrated and worth mining. It names iron, manganese, aluminium and copper ores as examples. Bauxite, tied to laterite (NCERT legy205), is the classic Indian case.

## The three belts and the Himalayan belt (NCERT)

- **North-Eastern Plateau Region**: Chhotanagpur (Jharkhand), the Odisha plateau, West Bengal and parts of Chhattisgarh. It has iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite and mica, which explains why the major iron and steel industry is here.
- **South-Western Plateau Region**: Karnataka, Goa and the neighbouring uplands of Tamil Nadu and Kerala. Rich in ferrous metals and bauxite, with high-grade iron ore, manganese and limestone, but **no coal except Neyveli lignite**. Kerala has monazite and thorium and bauxite clay; Goa has iron ore.
- **North-Western Region**: along the Aravali in Rajasthan and part of Gujarat, linked to the Dharwar system of rocks. Copper and zinc are the main minerals, plus building stones (sandstone, granite, marble), gypsum, Fuller's earth, dolomite and limestone for cement. Gujarat has petroleum; Gujarat and Rajasthan both have rich salt sources.
- **Himalayan belt**: copper, lead, zinc, cobalt and tungsten on both the eastern and western parts, plus oil in Assam (NCERT gives Assam oil as a separate sentence from the Himalayan metals).

## The recall method: line, belt, anchor, chain

Prelims map recall by state fails when you try to memorise whole lists. Use four layers.

1. **Line**: east of Mangaluru-Kanpur, so peninsular states first.
2. **Belt**: put each mineral in a belt. Coal is Gondwana (the Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari-Wardha valleys, from Raniganj to Singareni), tertiary in the north-east, plus lignite (Neyveli and the coastal belts). Mica is Jharkhand, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana and Rajasthan, with minor pockets in Karnataka (Mysuru, Hasan) and Tamil Nadu. Neither is confined to one belt.
3. **Anchor**: learn one state anchor per mineral from the extract's own order of naming. Iron ore: Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka. Manganese: Madhya Pradesh and Odisha. Bauxite: Odisha. Copper: Singhbhum, Balaghat, Jhunjhunu-Alwar. Mica: Jharkhand, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Rajasthan. Coal: the Damodar valley. Oil: Assam, Gujarat, Mumbai High.
4. **Chain**: add districts only after anchors are firm, by chaining to a name you already know (Kendujhar, then Kiruburu; Hazaribagh, then the 150 km mica belt).

Check each new fact against the three-belt map. A claim that puts coal in the Aravali belt or monazite in Chhotanagpur breaks the pattern and deserves a second look.

Module 3 onward teaches each mineral. The master notes hold the state-by-state tables.

### 3. Core: iron ore and manganese

*Objective:* Name the main iron ore types and states, the mines NCERT lists, and the manganese states; explain why iron and steel plants sit near coal.

## Iron ore

NCERT says India has fairly abundant iron ore and the largest reserve in Asia (the book's claim; reserve rank is dynamic, verify). The two main types are **haematite** and **magnetite**. Leong describes them: magnetite is black, from igneous rocks, with the highest iron content (55 to 72 per cent); haematite is reddish, mostly sedimentary, 50 to 70 per cent. Limonite (brown, up to 60 per cent but impure) and siderite (not more than 45 per cent) are poorer. Iron ore mines lie close to the coalfields of the north-eastern plateau, which is an advantage.

About **95 per cent of reserves** lie in eight states (NCERT): Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka, Goa, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu.

- **Odisha**: hill ranges in Sundergarh, Mayurbhanj and Kendujhar. Mines: Gurumahisani, Sulaipet, Badampahar (Mayurbhanj), Kiruburu (Kendujhar), Bonai (Sundergarh).
- **Jharkhand**: some of the oldest iron ore mines, with most iron and steel plants around them. Noamundi and Gua, in Poorbi and Pashchimi Singhbhum.
- **Chhattisgarh**: the belt continues to Durg, Dantewara and **Bailadila**. Dalli and Rajhara in Durg are important mines.
- **Karnataka**: Sandur-Hospet in Ballari district; Baba Budan hills and **Kudremukh** in Chikkamagaluru; parts of Shivamogga, Chitradurg and Tumakuru.
- **Others**: Chandrapur, Bhandara and Ratnagiri (Maharashtra); Karimnagar and Warangal (Telangana); Kurnool, Cuddapah and Anantapur (Andhra Pradesh); Salem and Nilgiris (Tamil Nadu). Goa has also emerged as an important producer.

Standard knowledge, flagged verify: Rajasthan also has iron ore mines (a 2018 Prelims statement turned on this). NCERT's extract does not list it, but not being listed is not proof of absence.

## Manganese

Manganese is a raw material for smelting iron ore and for making ferro-alloys (alloys of iron). It occurs in almost all geological formations but is mainly tied to the **Dharwar system**. Leong adds that traces are always present in steel and that it acts as a "cleanser" removing gases.

- **Madhya Pradesh and Odisha** are the leading producers (NCERT).
- **Odisha**: central part of the iron ore belt, in Bonai, Kendujhar, Sundergarh, Gangpur, Koraput, Kalahandi and Bolangir.
- **Madhya Pradesh**: a belt through Balaghat, Chhindwara, Nimar, Mandla and Jhabua.
- **Karnataka**: Dharwar, Ballari, Belagavi, North Canara, Chikkmagaluru, Shivamogga, Chitradurg and Tumakuru.
- **Maharashtra**: Nagpur, Bhandara and Ratnagiri; these mines are far from the steel plants, which is a disadvantage.
- Minor: Telangana, Goa and Jharkhand.

**Chromite** is named by NCERT as a ferrous mineral but with no map; standard knowledge says most Indian chromite is in Odisha's Sukinda valley (verify).

## Why steel plants sit where they do

A blast furnace needs iron ore, coke (hard fuel made from coking coal) and limestone (Leong). In the early industrial age it took eight to ten times as much coal as iron to make steel, so steel works clustered on coalfields. Better technology later cut the coal needed and pulled plants to the coast, as in Wales or the east coast of the USA (Leong). Leong lists India's large works at Jamshedpur, Durgapur, Rourkela and Bhilai in the Bihar-Odisha outcrops. These data are dated; the logic is not.

Draw a figure now: a triangle of ore, coal and limestone with a blast furnace in the middle, and mark Odisha and Jharkhand as the corner where all three are close.

### 4. Core: bauxite, copper, mica, limestone and the minerals of local use

*Objective:* Name the leading states and districts for bauxite, copper and mica, and the non-metallic minerals NCERT says are mined mainly for local use.

## Non-ferrous metals: India is poor except for bauxite

NCERT states that India is poorly endowed with non-ferrous metallic minerals except **bauxite**. This is the first fact to hold: copper, lead, zinc and tin are scarce at home, which is why NCERT's conservation paragraph urges recycling of scrap copper, lead and zinc.

## Bauxite

Bauxite is the ore of **aluminium**. It is found mainly in tertiary deposits tied to **laterite** rocks, on the plateau or hill ranges of peninsular India and in coastal tracts. (Laterite is a red, iron-and-aluminium-rich weathering product of hot, wet climates; standard definition, not in the extract.) Leong adds that aluminium smelting needs cheap hydro-electric power, which is why smelters sit near power.

- **Odisha is the largest producer.** Kalahandi and Sambalpur lead; Bolangir and Koraput are rising.
- **Jharkhand**: the patlands (flat-topped plateau surfaces) of Lohardaga.
- **Gujarat**: Bhavanagar and Jamnagar (the extract's spelling).
- **Chhattisgarh**: the Amarkantak plateau.
- **Madhya Pradesh**: Katni-Jabalpur and Balaghat.
- **Maharashtra**: Kolaba, Thane, Ratnagiri, Satara, Pune and Kolhapur.
- **Minor**: Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Goa.

## Copper

Copper is indispensable in the electrical industry (wires, motors, transformers, generators) and is alloyable, malleable and ductile. NCERT locates the main deposits in **Singhbhum (Jharkhand), Balaghat (Madhya Pradesh), and Jhunjhunu and Alwar (Rajasthan)**. Minor producers: Agnigundala in Guntur (Andhra Pradesh), Chitradurg and Hasan (Karnataka), South Arcot (Tamil Nadu). The Rajasthan districts lie in the Aravali (north-western) belt; Singhbhum lies in the north-eastern belt, which is also where NCERT places uranium along the "Singbhum Copper belt".

## Mica

Mica splits into very thin, tough, flexible sheets and is used in the electrical and electronic industries. NCERT names Jharkhand, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Rajasthan as producers, followed by Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Madhya Pradesh.

- **Jharkhand**: best quality in a belt about 150 km long and 22 km wide in the lower Hazaribagh plateau (an area of roughly 3,300 sq km if the belt were a full rectangle; that product is arithmetic, not an NCERT figure).
- **Andhra Pradesh**: Nellore district yields the best quality.
- **Rajasthan**: a belt about 320 km long from Jaipur to Bhilwara and around Udaipur.
- Other: Mysuru and Hasan (Karnataka); Coimbatore, Tiruchirapalli, Madurai and Kanniyakumari (Tamil Nadu); Alleppey (Kerala); Ratnagiri (Maharashtra); Purulia and Bankura (West Bengal).

## Other non-metallic minerals

Among non-metallic minerals NCERT says mica is the important one in production terms; limestone, dolomite and phosphate are extracted mostly for **local consumption**. Dolomite and limestone supply the cement industry in the north-western belt. The same belt has gypsum and Fuller's earth and rich salt sources in Gujarat and Rajasthan. NCERT asks why the Dandi March was organised: the answer is the salt law, and salt is a mineral of the western coast.

> Common confusion: mica is **non-metallic and inorganic**; bauxite is **metallic and non-ferrous**; limestone is **non-metallic and inorganic**. The ore that gives aluminium is bauxite, never mica.

### 5. Core: coal and lignite

*Objective:* Rank the types of coal, state the two geological ages and the valleys where Indian coal occurs, and name the main coalfields and mining centres from NCERT.

## Why coal matters

NCERT: coal is used mainly in thermal power (electricity made by burning fuel) and in smelting iron ore. Leong adds that coal started the Industrial Revolution and decided where industry was built, but that oil, electricity, bulk and smoke have since reduced its share.

## Types of coal (Leong), from highest rank to lowest

The ranking runs by carbon content and heat value.

1. **Anthracite**: hardest, smokeless, little ash; not more than about 5 per cent of world output (Leong).
2. **Bituminous**: the most abundant. Leong divides it into steam coal, **coking coal** (which softens, fuses and yields coke for blast furnaces) and gas coal.
3. **Lignite or brown coal**: high moisture and ash, smoky, low heat value.
4. **Peat**: the first stage of coal formation, local importance only.

NCERT: about **80 per cent of India's coal is bituminous and non-coking**, a grade poor for steel-making. This is why coking coal is an input worth watching in questions on steel.

## Two ages, four valleys

Indian coal occurs in rock sequences of two ages: **Gondwana** (older; it includes the Damodar fields) and **tertiary** (younger; the north-east). More than **97 per cent of coal reserves** lie in the valleys of the **Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari** (NCERT). Gondwana, the ancient southern supercontinent, links to PYQ 2021 on why India, a Gondwana country, still has a small mining share of GDP (see module 10).

**Damodar Valley (Jharkhand-Bengal belt)**: Raniganj, Jharia, Bokaro, Giridih, Karanpura. Jharia is the largest coalfield, followed by Raniganj.

**Other coal-mining centres**: Singrauli (Madhya Pradesh; part lies in Uttar Pradesh), Korba (Chhattisgarh), Talcher and Rampur (Odisha), Chanda-Wardha, Kamptee and Bander (Maharashtra), Singareni (Telangana), Pandur (Andhra Pradesh).

**Tertiary coals**: Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya and Nagaland. Meghalaya: Darangiri, Cherrapunji, Mewlong, Langrin. Upper Assam: Makum, Jaipur, Nazira. Arunachal Pradesh: Namchik-Namphuk. Jammu and Kashmir: Kalakot.

**Lignite or brown coal** is found in the coastal areas of **Tamil Nadu, Puducherry, Gujarat and Jammu and Kashmir**; Neyveli in Tamil Nadu is the lignite field the earlier belt passage names.

## Mining methods (Leong)

Coal near the surface is mined by **opencast (strip) mining**: stripping off the overburden (the cover of soil and rock) with giant shovels. Deep seams need **underground mining** by vertical shafts and tunnels, which is costlier and more dangerous (poor ventilation, gas, explosion, roof falls). Seams under the sea need offshore marine mining. These methods explain the hazard types discussed in module 9.

## Source note

Leong says India's "only sizeable coal deposit" is the Damodar Basin centred at Raniganj, and gives India about 5 per cent of world output in a bar figure. Both are far too narrow and dated beside NCERT's four-valley picture. Teach NCERT; use Leong for ranks, methods and world context.

### 6. Core: petroleum and natural gas, onshore and offshore

*Objective:* Describe India's oil and gas regions, how oil is trapped and refined, and the geography of onshore versus offshore oil.

## India's oil: NCERT facts

**Crude petroleum** is a mix of liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons. NCERT calls it "liquid gold" for its scarcity and diverse uses. It occurs in **sedimentary rocks of the tertiary period** (Leong: in the pore spaces of sedimentary rocks, derived from marine or vegetative matter). Systematic exploration followed the **Oil and Natural Gas Commission, set up in 1956**; until then **Digboi in Assam** was the only producing region.

- **Assam**: Digboi, Naharkatiya, Moran. (NCERT states Assam oil separately from the Himalayan-belt metals.)
- **Gujarat**: Ankaleshwar, Kalol, Mehsana, Nawagam, Kosamba, Lunej.
- **Mumbai High**: lies **160 km off Mumbai** in the Arabian Sea; NCERT says it was discovered in 1973 with production from 1976. Other sources date discovery to 1974 (verify).
- **East coast**: oil and natural gas found in exploratory wells in the **Krishna-Godavari and Kaveri basins**.

Crude oil is full of impurities and must be refined. NCERT gives two types of refinery: **field-based** (Digboi) and **market-based** (Barauni).

## Natural gas

Natural gas is found with petroleum and is released at the surface when crude is brought up. It is used for power, industry heating and as raw material for chemicals and fertiliser. The expansion of city gas distribution lets it serve as vehicle fuel (**CNG**) and household cooking fuel (**PNG**). India's main reserves are in Mumbai High and allied fields on the west coast, supplemented by the **Cambay basin** and, on the east, the Krishna-Godavari basin. NCERT's activity asks about GAIL's pipelines under "One Nation One Grid".

## How oil is trapped (Leong)

Oil, gas and water settle by weight in porous rock. A non-porous cap rock stops the oil rising. Leong shows three traps: an **anticline** (upfold, oil at the crest with gas above and water below), a **fault** that brings porous and non-porous rock face to face, and a **dyke** (a sheet of resistant igneous rock) that seals a pool. Modern prospecting begins with a geological survey, not random drilling ("wild-catting").

## Refining and uses (Leong)

Crude is split by **fractional distillation**, heating so each group boils off at its own temperature: light fractions (petrol, kerosene, naphtha) first; heavier diesel, lubricating and fuel oils later; the residue gives bitumen, wax and vaseline. Furnace oil, the subject of PYQ 2021, belongs to the heavy fuel-oil end (standard; verify). Cracking breaks heavy fractions into petrol.

## Onshore versus offshore (PYQ 2025 theme)

**Onshore** wells are drilled on land. **Offshore** wells are drilled from platforms in the sea over the continental shelf. Leong names the North Sea (British and Norwegian oil, found in the early 1970s) and the offshore areas of Nigeria and Gabon; standard knowledge adds the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Mexico (verify). Why offshore oil sits where it does follows from the rule that oil forms in **thick sedimentary basins**; where such basins extend under shallow sea, as under the Gulf or the North Sea, the oil is offshore.

The differences (the first two follow from Leong; the rest are standard knowledge, verify): offshore needs costly platforms and divers or remote vehicles; spill risk threatens marine life; the sea floor beyond a country's territorial limit and exclusive zone raises legal questions. Leong adds that over two-thirds of world reserves were estimated to be in the Middle East in the book's time (dated, verify).

### 7. Core: nuclear minerals, hydel and thermal power, and what 'conventional' means

*Objective:* Locate uranium, thorium and monazite in India, list the nuclear power projects NCERT names, and separate conventional from non-conventional energy without falling for the terminology trap.

## Nuclear minerals

NCERT names **uranium** and **thorium** as the important minerals for nuclear energy.

- **Uranium** occurs in the **Dharwar rocks**: along the **Singbhum copper belt** (Jharkhand); in Udaipur, Alwar and Jhunjhunu (Rajasthan); Durg (Chhattisgarh); Bhandara (Maharashtra); Kullu (Himachal Pradesh). Leong calls uranium the "metal of hope", radioactive, found by Geiger counters, and gives its world sources as 1980s data (dated).
- **Thorium** is obtained mainly from **monazite and ilmenite in the beach sands** along the coast of **Kerala and Tamil Nadu**. NCERT says the world's richest monazite deposits occur in **Palakkad and Kollam** (Kerala), near **Vishakhapatnam** (Andhra Pradesh) and in the **Mahanadi river delta** (Odisha).

Two more facts from standard knowledge, flagged verify: monazite also carries **rare earth elements**; ilmenite and rutile are the main ores of **titanium**. Both come from these same beach-sand placers (deposits washed and sorted by waves). NCERT's list of places does not say monazite occurs along the *whole* coast; it names beaches and a delta. Source slips: NCERT's 'thorium from monazite and ilmenite' is loose, because monazite is the thorium mineral and ilmenite is mainly a titanium ore; and Palakkad is an inland district, so read the placer sands as coastal Kerala (Chavara in Kollam is the usual example; verify).

**Institutions and plants (NCERT).** The Atomic Energy Commission was set up in **1948**; real progress followed the Atomic Energy Institute at **Trombay in 1954**, renamed the **Bhabha Atomic Research Centre in 1967**. Important nuclear power projects: **Tarapur (Maharashtra), Rawatbhata near Kota (Rajasthan), Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu), Narora (Uttar Pradesh), Kaiga (Karnataka) and Kakarapara (Gujarat)** (the extract's spelling). The NCERT exercise treats Tarapur as the first atomic power station.

## Hydel and thermal power (Leong)

Electricity from water-turbines is **hydroelectricity**; from steam-turbines driven by burning coal, oil or gas, **thermal electricity**. Thermal power is found near coalfields and needs large volumes of water for cooling; hydro needs a **steep gradient, a steady supply of water, heavy capital outlay and a large market**. Leong's world figures (thermal over two-thirds, hydro about a third) are dated.

## The terminology trap

NCERT puts coal, petroleum, natural gas and nuclear energy as **conventional**, based on **exhaustible** raw materials. It calls solar, wind, hydro-geothermal and biomass the sustainable or renewable sources. The NCERT exercise names *thermal* as the non-renewable choice among hydel, solar, thermal and wind. In everyday Indian usage large hydro is called conventional **renewable**, and "non-conventional" usually means solar, wind, small hydro, biomass and the like. When a question says conventional, check whether it means *fuel type* (fossil and nuclear) or *old and established technology* (including large hydro).

Why India depends on coal despite having uranium is a PYQ 2023 theme. The simple geography: India has coal in the four valleys of module 5, while uranium ore is of low grade and small in quantity (standard; verify), and reactor fuel must be processed. The refining detail is in module 10.

### 8. Core: non-conventional energy, the five sources NCERT names

*Objective:* Describe the solar, wind, tidal and wave, geothermal and bio-energy sources, where NCERT places them in India, and why potential does not equal use.

## Solar energy

Sunlight is turned into energy in **photovoltaic cells** (direct conversion to electricity) or by **solar thermal technology** (heating a fluid; heaters, crop dryers, cookers). NCERT says solar thermal is cost competitive, environment friendly and easy to construct. It also claims solar is "7 per cent more effective than coal or oil based plants and 10 per cent more effective than nuclear plants". That sentence gives no basis, so do not learn it as a fact. NCERT places the greater potential in the **western part of India: Gujarat and Rajasthan**.

## Wind energy

Wind energy is pollution free and inexhaustible. The kinetic energy of wind turns **turbines** to produce electricity. NCERT names the winds that can be used: permanent systems (trade winds, westerlies), seasonal winds (the monsoon), and local winds (land and sea breezes). Favourable conditions exist in **Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Karnataka**. Standard knowledge adds that Tamil Nadu is among the leaders (verify).

## Tidal and wave energy

Ocean currents and tidal waves are a store of energy. NCERT says large tidal waves occur along the **west coast of India**, giving great potential, **not yet utilised**. Tides themselves are explained in Day 17 lesson GS-109, and coastal landforms in Day 17 lesson GS-130.

## Geothermal energy

Heat from the earth's interior can be tapped. Hot water from geyser wells and hot springs is used for heat and electricity. NCERT notes that a geothermal plant has been commissioned at **Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh**. Its historical example is Boise, Idaho (USA), where in **1890** a hot-water pipe network first successfully heated buildings and still works.

## Bio-energy

Bio-energy is energy from biological products: agricultural residues and municipal, industrial and other wastes. It can become electricity, heat or cooking gas, treats waste, helps rural economies, reduces pollution and takes pressure off fuel wood. NCERT's example is **Okhla in Delhi**, which converts municipal waste to energy.

## Potential is not use

NCERT's own wording ("great potential", "not yet utilised") separates what *can* be produced from what *is*. For Mains, remember four brakes that apply across sources (the first set is reasoning from the extract, the second is standard knowledge to verify):

- **Resource variation**: wind and sun vary by region, season and time of day.
- **Storage and grid**: power must be moved and stored; not stated in the extract.
- **Land and cost**: large installations need land; the initial cost is high but, NCERT says, falls over time.
- **Policy**: targets, subsidies and tariffs change often; always check the latest figure before quoting.

> Mains hook: a regional answer on solar or wind should be arranged by region (west, south, north, east, north-east, islands and coasts) and give the physical reason for the potential (clear skies and long sunshine in the dry west; steady wind along coasts and gaps), then limits.

### 9. Master: conservation, mining hazards and the law that governs mines

*Objective:* Explain why minerals must be conserved, list the environmental hazards of mining and the remedies, and state the legal points on major, minor and critical minerals that Prelims has tested, with verify flags.

## Conservation (NCERT)

Because minerals are exhaustible, sustainable development requires protecting resources for future generations. NCERT's measures: develop inexhaustible alternatives (solar, wind, wave, geothermal); **recycle scrap** metals, especially copper, lead and zinc where Indian reserves are meagre; use **substitutes** for scarce metals; and **reduce exports of strategic and scarce minerals** so reserves last longer.

## Why mining is an environmental hazard (Leong and NCERT)

Group the points rather than listing them.

- **Land**: mining digs up land, leaving hectares derelict; **quarrying**, being widespread, is the most damaging because large-scale mining is localised (Leong).
- **Water**: hollows fill with stagnant water, breeding mosquitoes (malaria); waste dumps breed flies and germs (cholera, dysentery); silt from dumps and runoff pollutes streams and lakes, killing fish and making water unsafe.
- **Soil**: vegetation clearance leads to accelerated erosion and **gully erosion** (Leong cites Nigeria's Jos Plateau tin mines).
- **Air**: dust coats leaves and blocks photosynthesis, respiration and transpiration; smoke and gases in coal areas; lung disease.
- **Oil**: blow-outs, pipeline and terminal leaks, tanker leaks and collisions pollute land and sea (Leong cites the Niger delta).
- **Climate and people** (standard, verify): coal-fired power emits sulphur and nitrogen oxides, and coal ash holds toxic traces (module 10); displacement and loss of forest affect livelihoods and elephant corridors (Day 18 lesson GS-131 on protected areas).

## Remedies (reasoned answer; the first line is NCERT's)

Recycling, substitution and reduced strategic exports; **reclamation** of mined land (backfilling, planting, converting pits to water bodies, which Leong notes some British pools are managed for recreation); dust and water-treatment controls; scientific, mechanised and underground methods where safe; enforcing environmental clearance and monitoring; and shifting the energy mix to renewables. State a limit: reclamation costs money and takes years.

## The legal frame (standard knowledge: verify each point)

The **Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957** (MMDR Act) is the main law. It separates **major minerals** (regulated by the Centre through the Act and its rules, while State Governments grant leases and, since the 2015 amendment, largely auction them; the exceptions are coal, which has its own laws, and the critical minerals listed in Part D of the First Schedule by the 2023 amendment, which the Centre auctions) from **minor minerals**. Minor minerals include building stones, gravel, ordinary clay and ordinary sand, and *any other mineral the Central Government notifies* as minor. The **State Governments** grant leases of minor minerals and make rules for them, including rules to stop illegal mining. Coal is treated under its own laws: coal mines were **nationalised in the early 1970s** (coking coal first, then non-coking) and blocks are now **auctioned**, not given by lottery.

**Critical minerals** are those whose supply is at risk and vital to the economy or defence. The Mines and Minerals Amendment of 2023 let the Central Government auction mining leases and composite licences for certain critical minerals exclusively. India identified **30 critical minerals** and launched the **National Critical Mineral Mission** in 2025; it joined the **Minerals Security Partnership** in 2023. All of this is newer than the textbooks and carries a verify flag.

- **distractorAnalysis:**
  - The option that adds the true statement and the one false statement is the classic distractor.

- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is the north Indian alluvial plain devoid of minerals of economic use while the peninsula is rich?
    - **a:** Valuable minerals are mostly pre-Palaeozoic products of old metamorphic and igneous rocks of the peninsula; the plain is a young deposit of river alluvium (NCERT).

    - **a:** NCERT calls fossil and nuclear sources conventional and exhaustible; everyday usage treats large hydro as conventional renewable. Use the question's own definition.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-NCERT-MIN-CLASS (verified):** NCERT: minerals are grouped as metallic and non-metallic. Metallic minerals are ferrous (with iron content, such as iron ore) or non-ferrous (such as copper and bauxite); non-metallic minerals are organic in origin (fossil fuels such as coal and petroleum) or inorganic (such as mica, limestone and graphite).
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-MIN-CHARS (verified):** NCERT: minerals are unevenly distributed over space; there is an inverse relationship between quality and quantity (good quality minerals are less in quantity than low quality ones); and all minerals are exhaustible over time and cannot be replenished at the time of need, as they do not have the second crop.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-MIN-LINE (verified):** NCERT: most metallic minerals in India occur in the peninsular plateau region in the old crystalline rocks; the vast alluvial plain tract of north India is devoid of minerals of economic use; and most of the major mineral resources occur to the east of a line linking Mangaluru and Kanpur.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-BELT-NE (verified):** NCERT: the North-Eastern Plateau Region covers Chhotanagpur (Jharkhand), the Odisha Plateau, West Bengal and parts of Chhattisgarh, and has a variety of minerals: iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite and mica; NCERT asks the reader why the major iron and steel industry is located there.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-BELT-SW (verified):** NCERT: the South-Western Plateau Region extends over Karnataka, Goa and the contiguous Tamil Nadu uplands and Kerala; it is rich in ferrous metals and bauxite, has high grade iron ore, manganese and limestone, and lacks coal deposits except Neyveli lignite.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-BELT-NW-HIM (verified):** NCERT: the North-Western Region extends along the Aravali in Rajasthan and part of Gujarat, with copper and zinc as major minerals and petroleum in Gujarat; in the Himalayan belt copper, lead, zinc, cobalt and tungsten occur on the eastern and western parts, and the Assam valley has mineral oil.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-IRON-STATES (verified):** NCERT: the iron ore mines occur in close proximity to the coal fields in the north-eastern plateau region, which adds to their advantage; about 95 per cent of total iron ore reserves is located in Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka, Goa, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-COAL-VALLEYS (verified):** NCERT: coal is mainly used in the generation of thermal power and the smelting of iron ore; it occurs mainly in Gondwana and tertiary rock sequences, about 80 per cent of India's coal is bituminous and of non-coking grade, and over 97 per cent of coal reserves occur in the valleys of the Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CONSERVE (verified):** NCERT: minerals need conservation for future generations; measures include developing inexhaustible alternative energy (solar, wind, wave, geothermal), recycling scrap metals (especially copper, lead and zinc, in which India's reserves are meagre), using substitutes for scarce metals, and reducing exports of strategic and scarce minerals.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-SOLAR (verified):** NCERT: solar energy is tapped through photovoltaic cells and solar thermal technology (used in heaters, crop dryers and cookers), and the western part of India has greater potential for the development of solar energy, in Gujarat and Rajasthan.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WIND (verified):** NCERT: the kinetic energy of wind, through turbines, is converted into electrical energy; trade winds, westerlies, the monsoon and local land and sea breezes can be used, and favourable conditions for wind energy exist in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Karnataka.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-NONCONV (verified):** NCERT: fossil fuels (coal, petroleum, natural gas) and nuclear energy use exhaustible raw materials, while the sustainable sources are the renewable ones such as solar, wind, hydro-geothermal and biomass, which are more equitably distributed and environment-friendly and will give more sustained, eco-friendly, cheaper energy after the initial cost is taken care of.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TIDAL (verified):** NCERT: large tidal waves are known to occur along the west coast of India, so India has great potential for the development of tidal energy along the coasts, but so far it has not been utilised.
  Source: [NCERT Class XII, India: People and Economy, ch. 5 Mineral and Energy Resources (legy205, 2026-27 reprint)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/legy205.pdf)
