### Bridge: Tone vocabulary: the words and what they precisely mean


Tone words are precise tools. Learn them in groups by direction.

**Neutral or measured**

- **Objective**: rests on evidence and avoids judgement.
- **Informative / expository**: explains or reports.
- **Analytical**: breaks a question into parts and weighs them.
- **Reflective**: thinks aloud, often conditionally ('if ... then').
- **Ambivalent**: holds two opposed feelings and settles on neither.

**Positive**

- **Appreciative**: values something, mildly. **Admiring** or **laudatory** is stronger.
- **Optimistic**: expects things to turn out well. **Sympathetic**: feels for someone's difficulty.

**Negative, in rising intensity**

- **Critical**: judges with reasons. **Sceptical**: doubts and asks for proof. **Cautionary**: warns in proportion to the evidence.
- **Pessimistic**: expects things to go badly. **Cynical**: assumes bad motives. **Alarmist**: overstates the danger.
- **Dismissive**: waves something aside as unimportant. **Indignant**: angry at something unjust. **Hostile / scathing**: attacks.

**Special manner**

- **Ironic**: says one thing, means another, with restraint. **Sarcastic**: bitter, mocking irony aimed at someone. **Satirical**: uses ridicule to expose folly.
- **Didactic**: teaches or moralises. **Persuasive**: argues to win agreement.
- **Nostalgic**: looks back fondly. **Apologetic**: admits fault or regret. **Defensive**: protects against criticism. **Resigned**: accepts a bad thing with little hope of change.

**One check**: a word is only right if the passage's own words show it, not because the subject is sad, angry or hopeful.


### Bridge: Assumption, inference and implication: a one-page recap


CT-002 module 2 and bridge CT-002-B1 explain these in full; this is a recap to keep the three apart.

| Term | Position | Test | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assumption | Unstated, *before* the argument (the plank) | Denial test: deny it, and the argument collapses | What must the author take for granted? |
| Inference | Unstated, *after* the passage (a step along the bridge) | Contradiction test: deny it, and the passage is contradicted | What must be true if the passage is true? |
| Implication | Unstated, a consequence or suggestion | Does it follow from the passage's own reasoning? | What does the passage commit the author to? |

Use a tiny example (the same argument form as in CT-002's bridge): 'The match was cancelled because of rain. So the pitch must have been unplayable.' Premise: the match was cancelled because of rain. Conclusion: the pitch was unplayable. Assumption: the rain made the pitch unplayable rather than stopping the match in some other way (deny it and the conclusion no longer follows). Inference from the passage: the match did not go ahead as planned. Implication: the match may be rescheduled. The same two tests then separate every option: an assumption statement survives only if its denial breaks the argument; an inference survives only if its denial breaks the passage.

In the official statement-validity items (module 5) the word 'assumption' is used loosely, and the working test is whether the passage supports the statement. In strict stems ('the author's assumption', 'the most logical assumption') use the denial test.


### 1. Three question families: tone, assumption and context

*Objective:* Name the three question families this topic covers and recognise each from its stem.

## What this lesson adds

CT-001 taught you to find what a passage says (main idea, theme, message). CT-002 taught you what follows from it (inference, implication, corollary), with the **contradiction test**, and introduced the **denial test** for assumptions. This lesson adds three families that sit beside those two and are often confused with them:

- **Tone and attitude**: how does the author feel about the subject, and what in the words shows it?
- **Assumption**: what must the author take for granted for the argument to work? In many CSAT stems the question is looser: which numbered statement is valid on the basis of the passage?
- **Context**: what does a word or phrase mean *here*, what does the passage imply, and why is a given sentence or example there?

### 2. Tone, mood and attitude: the three axes

*Objective:* Distinguish tone, attitude and mood, and place any tone word by direction, intensity and target.

## Three words, three jobs

- **Tone** is the manner in which the author speaks about the subject, shown through chosen words: serious, mocking, hopeful, restrained, urgent.
- **Attitude** is the author's underlying stance towards a subject, person or view: approval, doubt, concern, indifference. A stem asking for 'the author's attitude towards X' wants the stance. In CSAT stems tone and attitude are used almost interchangeably; tone is how it sounds, attitude is what the author thinks.
- **Mood** is the feeling a passage creates in the *reader*, or the atmosphere of a scene. It belongs mainly to literary description. A passage can sound calm (tone) and leave the reader uneasy (mood). If a stem says 'mood', answer from the atmosphere; otherwise treat 'tone' and 'attitude' as one question.

## Three axes

Every tone word can be placed on three axes.

1. **Direction**: positive (approving), negative (disapproving) or neutral (reporting without judging).
2. **Intensity**: mild, moderate or strong. 'Critical' is moderate; 'hostile' and 'scathing' are strong.
3. **Target**: whom or what the tone is about. An author can be sympathetic to farmers and critical of a policy in one passage. Read the stem for the target.

Most wrong tone options err in **intensity** (the author is critical; the option says contemptuous) or in **target**. A distressing subject does not make the tone sad: an author reporting famine figures in plain language has an *objective* tone about a *tragic* subject.

## Pairs that are easy to confuse

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| Objective / neutral | Objective rests on evidence and avoids judgement; neutral only means no opinion is shown. Neither uses loaded words. |
| Critical / hostile | Critical judges with reasons; hostile attacks the person or idea. |
| Sceptical / cynical | Sceptical asks for proof; cynical assumes bad motives. |
| Ironic / sarcastic | Irony says one thing and means another, usually with restraint; sarcasm is sharper and mocks someone. |
| Cautionary / alarmist | Cautionary warns in proportion to the evidence; alarmist overstates the danger. |
| Didactic / persuasive | Didactic teaches or moralises; persuasive argues to win agreement. |

The full vocabulary with precise meanings is in bridge module CT-003-B1 and the master notes.

### 3. Reading tone from evidence

*Objective:* Use word choice, qualifiers, examples and structure to decide tone, and apply the method to official 'tone' and 'author's thinking' stems.

## Four kinds of evidence

Tone is never guessed from the topic. It is read from four kinds of evidence.

1. **Word choice.** Loaded words carry judgement: 'glowing', 'supposedly', 'merely', 'bland', 'obsession', against plain ones such as 'noted', 'reported', 'measured'. 'Claims' is cooler than 'states'; 'admits' suggests reluctance.
2. **Qualifiers and hedges.** 'It may be', 'probably', 'perhaps' mark a careful, measured author. 'Undeniably', 'obviously', 'nothing could be clearer' mark an emphatic one. Hedging is the signature of an objective or sceptical voice.
3. **Examples.** What the author chooses to illustrate with shows the stance: a tragic example invites sympathy, an absurd one ridicule, a successful one admiration.
4. **Structure.** Concession followed by 'but' (admittedly ... however ...) shows a balanced or critical author whose real view comes after the turn. A closing verdict sentence gives the tone in one line.

## The three-step method

1. Underline loaded words and qualifiers; ignore plain facts.
2. Decide direction (positive, negative, neutral), then intensity (mild, moderate, strong).
3. Choose the option whose direction *and* intensity match. Reject options one notch too strong. If two are close, prefer the milder, because comprehension passages tend to be measured (a Mission 90 working heuristic, not a rule; check the passage's actual words).

Beware a quoted voice: if the author *reports* someone's anger, the passage is not angry unless the author's own words are.

## Worked: 'underlying tone' in 2022 Q51

The passage argues that machines can bring leisure, that leisure is 'apt to be tedious' without 'intelligent activities and interests', and that a leisured population must therefore be educated for enjoyment as well as usefulness. The stem asks for the 'underlying tone', yet the four options are *statements*. When that happens, read the stem as 'which statement best captures the author's stance'. The stance is reflective and conditional: leisure is good *if* education prepares people for it.

Analysis (no key asserted): the option that only an educated population can best use economic progress mirrors that conditional; its 'Only' is supported by the passage's own 'must' ('it must be an educated population'), so it is not an extreme here. The option that education raises happiness turns a necessary condition into a guarantee. The option that all development should aim at leisure is an absolute the passage never states. The option urging machines to create a large leisured population states a policy the passage does not recommend.

## Worked: 'thinking of the author' in 2024 Q23

The passage says science's *social* responsibility is a modern idea beginning with the industrial revolution, whereas Newton and Galileo acknowledged only a duty to tell the truth. The attitude is historical and corrective: do not assume the sense of social duty was always there. Analysis: options about the 'commitment of scientists', about science as a product of 'civilized society', or about science making the industrial revolution possible add content the passage does not contain. The option joining the pursuit of truth with responsibility for social welfare covers both halves of the passage's contrast, but note the verb 'must': the author describes a history rather than issuing an order. Decide whether you accept that small step; module 7 gives the rule.

### 4. Assumptions and the denial test

*Objective:* Define an assumption as an unstated premise and apply the denial test correctly to seven examples.

## What an assumption is

An argument has **premises** (reasons) and a **conclusion** (the claim). Some premises are *stated*. Others are **unstated premises**, which the author needs but does not write because they seem obvious. In the strict sense an **assumption** is an unstated premise that the argument needs. CT-002 module 2 placed it before the argument (the plank under the bridge) and the inference after it (a step you take). Here the task is to find the plank.

## The denial test

1. State the argument in one line: reason, therefore claim.
2. Take the candidate assumption and **deny it** with a careful, minimal negation.
3. Ask: with the denial true, does the reason still support the claim? If the support collapses, the candidate is **necessary**, so it is an assumption. If the argument survives, it is not.

Deny carefully. 'All' becomes 'not all' (not 'none'); 'will' becomes 'may not' (not 'will never'); 'most' becomes 'not most'. Over-strong denials make everything look necessary. This is a different test from the contradiction test of CT-002, which denies an option to see whether the *passage* is contradicted.

## Seven worked examples (original)

1. *The school's pass rate rose after the new teacher joined, so the teacher improved results.* Candidate: no other factor (say, an easier paper) fully accounts for the rise. Deny: another factor does fully account for it. The reason no longer supports the claim: **necessary**.
2. Same argument; candidate: the new teacher is popular. Deny: she is not popular. She may still teach well; the argument survives: **not needed**.
3. *Since the lake dried up, fewer migratory birds visit; restoring the lake will bring them back.* Candidate: the birds left because the lake dried, not for another reason. Deny: they left for another reason. Restoring the lake would not help: **necessary**.
4. *Ramesh scored well in mock tests, so he will clear the exam.* Candidate: mock scores predict real performance. Deny: they do not. The reason says nothing about the claim: **necessary**.
5. *Cheap loans will let small shopkeepers expand, so the scheme will create jobs.* Candidate: expanded shops will hire more people. Deny: they will not. No jobs follow: **necessary**, a *bridging* assumption linking the reason to the claim.
6. *Helmets reduce head injuries, so helmets should be compulsory for two-wheeler riders.* Candidate A: a rule will raise helmet use. Deny: it will not; the recommendation is pointless: **necessary**. Candidate B: helmets cost money. Deny: they are free. The argument survives: **not needed**, though true.
7. *A new vaccine cut cases in the trial, so the state should buy it.* Candidate: no other vaccine can ever work. Deny: other vaccines can work. The argument survives, because it claims only that this vaccine is worth buying: **not needed**, and extreme.

Examples 2, 6B and 7 are the two traps taught in module 5.

## A quick self-check

If you cannot state the argument as 'reason, therefore claim', there is no strict assumption to find: the passage is descriptive, and the question is the looser statement-validity kind described next.

### 5. Necessary versus consistent, the two traps, and statement-validity items

*Objective:* Separate necessary assumptions from true-but-not-needed and extreme ones, relate strengthen and weaken to denial, and handle the looser 'assumptions valid' stems.

## Four relations between a statement and an argument

Only the first is an assumption.

| Relation | Test | Example (helmet argument, module 4) |
|---|---|---|
| Necessary (assumption) | Denial collapses the argument | A rule will raise helmet use |
| Consistent, not needed | Denial leaves the argument standing | Helmets cost money |
| Strengthens | Makes the conclusion more likely; denying it need not collapse the argument | Hospitals report fewer deaths among helmeted riders |
| Weakens | Makes the conclusion less likely | Forced to wear helmets, riders ride more recklessly |

Denial is the limiting case of weakening: a weakener *damages* the argument, a denied assumption *destroys* it. A strengthener helps but is optional. Do not confuse 'helps the argument' with 'is needed by the argument'.

## Two traps

**True but not needed.** The option may be a fact and may even come from the passage's subject, but deny it and the argument stands. The examiner expects you to reward truth; the test rewards need. Ask 'is the argument dead without it?', not 'is it true?'

**The extreme assumption.** Options with 'only', 'always', 'never', 'all', 'every' or 'cannot' ask for far more than the argument needs. Deny an extreme and you usually get a mild statement the argument tolerates (example 7). Arguments rarely depend on 'no other way exists'. Do not reject a strong word automatically, as CT-001 and CT-002 also say; check whether the passage supports exactly that strength.

## The examiner's looser use of 'assumption'

In the 23 'assumptions ... valid' items the passage is often a descriptive paragraph with no premise-and-conclusion structure to deny. Each numbered statement is simply a claim to judge against the passage. The working test is the CT-002 one: is the statement supported, either stated or one safe step away? Where the stem says 'the author's assumption' or 'the most logical assumption' (2022 Q71, 2021 Q31), use the strict denial test. (In 2022 Q71 the analysis in module 8 matches a *stated* position, 'probably never completely valueless', so the answer is read off the passage with the quantifier ladder and the denial test is only a check.)

The statement-level traps seen in the verified set (analysis, no key asserted):

| Trap | What the statement does | Seen in |
|---|---|---|
| New cause or mechanism | Adds a reason the passage never gives | 2019 Q8, 2025 Q62 |
| Degree upgrade | 'could' or 'may' becomes 'will', 'guarantees', 'huge' | 2019 Q26, 2021 Q24 |
| Reversal or converse | Swaps cause and effect | 2019 Q5 |
| Subject shift | Keeps the words, changes the agent | 2021 Q23 |
| Prescription from description | The passage describes; the statement says 'should' or 'will have to' | 2019 Q25 |
| Outside knowledge | True in the world, absent from the passage | 2021 Q2, 2025 Q52, 2025 Q44 ('circular economy' is an outside term; the growth claim is unstated) |
| Extreme | 'only', 'guarantees', 'cannot' | 2019 Q5, 2019 Q27, 2025 Q54 |

These are CT-001 module 4's wrong-option families and CT-002 module 5's trap types as they appear in statement-validity items; module 8 works through examples.

### 6. Context: word meaning, implication, the closed-world rule and structure

*Objective:* Work out meaning from context, apply the closed-world rule with its two exceptions, and label a passage's structure as claim, support and contrast.

## Meaning from context

A word question asks what X means *here*. Common words have several senses ('bland' can describe food, a manner or a reply). Use three clues in order:

1. **Restatement**: a later phrase explains it ('studiously bland: a few figures, a promise to look into it, no mention of ...').
2. **Contrast**: 'but', 'yet', 'unlike' set it against its opposite.
3. **Example or consequence**: what follows from it shows its meaning.

Then substitute your choice into the sentence. It must read smoothly and keep the passage's direction (positive or negative). Beware an option that is a real meaning of the word but the wrong sense for this sentence.

## 'What does the passage imply about X?'

This is an inference stem with a named target (CT-002 module 4). Find every sentence about X, state what they together commit the author to, and prefer the option that follows with the least added. An implication is a little wider than a bare inference but must still follow from the passage's own reasoning: 'implies that India will have to strengthen X' works only if the chain forces it.

## The closed-world rule

**Use only the passage.** Treat it as the whole world. If the passage neither says nor supports a statement, it cannot be a reason for choosing an option, however true it is. This is the commonest way well-read candidates lose marks. There are two exceptions; both are Mission 90 teaching conventions, not an official rule.

1. **Scientific or definitional stems.** If the stem itself asks you to apply a fact or definition, that fact comes from your knowledge, and the passage must still be consistent with your choice.
2. **Vocabulary.** The meaning of ordinary English words is yours to supply. A one-step paraphrase of the passage is not outside knowledge either (CT-001 module 4).

In the analyses of module 8, options that need outside facts (cereals in 2021 Q2, wild relatives of crops in 2025 Q52) are the bait.

## Purpose and structure

A purpose stem asks *why* a sentence, example or word is there. Label each sentence's job: **claim** (what the author argues), **support** (reason, evidence, example), **contrast** (the opposing view or a concession), **conclusion**. Signal words: 'but', 'however', 'yet' (the turn towards the author's real point); 'admittedly', 'of course', 'it is true that' (concession); 'for instance' (example); 'therefore', 'thus' (conclusion). An example exists to support a claim, so the answer to 'why this example?' is almost always 'to illustrate that ...', not the example's own topic. Practice passage 9 uses this.

### 7. The combined method: one reading, three tags, scope, degree, direction

*Objective:* Run one reading with tags, choose the test from the stem, and eliminate options by scope, degree and direction.

## One read, three tags

Read the passage once at ordinary speed and mark as you go:

- **S**: the sentence that states the claim or stance.
- **L**: loaded words and qualifiers (tone evidence).
- **B**: a 'but' or 'however' turn (structure).

After one read you should be able to say, in your own words, *what the author claims*, *how the author feels about it* (direction and intensity) and *what the author takes for granted*. If you cannot, reread the first and last sentences; the structure usually sits there.

## Decide the family from the stem, before the options

| Stem says | Family | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 'tone', 'attitude', 'mood', 'author feels' | Tone | direction, then intensity |
| 'assumption' on an argument | Strict assumption | denial test |
| 'assumptions ... valid' on a descriptive passage | Statement validity | supported, or one safe step away |
| 'implies', 'infer', 'corollary' | Inference | contradiction test (CT-002) |
| 'means', 'most nearly' | Context | restatement, contrast, example |
| 'in order to', 'mentions ... because' | Purpose | claim, support, contrast |

## Eliminate by scope, degree and direction

Three questions remove most options without a long argument.

- **Scope**: is the option about what the passage is about? Another subject, group or time is out (outside knowledge, subject shift).
- **Degree**: is the option as strong as the passage? Weaker is safer than stronger. Upgrades ('guarantees', 'only', 'huge') and tone options one notch too intense are out.
- **Direction**: does it keep the passage's direction (cause to effect, positive to negative, 'if' to 'then')? Reversals and converses are out.

An option that passes all three is a candidate. If two pass, choose the one that claims less and follows the passage's own words more closely (rung-matching comes first: in 2022 Q71 an option that claims less can sit on the wrong rung). This is CT-001's wrong-option ordering shortened so that it can be run in the head.

## The small-step question

In the 2024 Q23 analysis the verb 'must' raised a doubt. Use this rule: accept a step if a careful reader of the passage alone would take it without hesitation; reject it if you need the world's help, a motive the author never gives, or a stronger word. If neither option passes cleanly, take the one that claims less.

## Statement-validity items: avoid pattern choices

Options such as 'Both 1 and 2' or 'Neither 1 nor 2' are structurally ordinary. Do not choose by pattern. Judge each statement separately, then find the option that matches your verdicts. If your verdicts match no option, one verdict is wrong: re-test the statement you are least sure of, looking for a degree upgrade or a hidden converse.

### 8. Worked analyses of eight official questions

*Objective:* Apply the method to eight verified official questions and name the trap in each. Analysis only: no official key is asserted.

Each analysis names the passage's own words briefly and tests statements against them. None of this is an answer key; compare your own verdicts with it after trying the question from the official paper.

**1. 2019 Q3 (education and culture).** The statement that education is incomplete without culture matches the passage's demand for 'both culture and expert knowledge'. The statement that a society without well educated people cannot become modern adds 'modern society' and 'cannot': the passage praises education's transformatory role but never says it is the *only* route. Trap: degree upgrade.

**2. 2019 Q5 (inequality and power).** 'The only reason' is extreme: power is called the 'organising principle', not the sole cause. 'Inequality reinforces power' reverses the direction, since the passage has power driving inequality. The statement that power is better analysed through effects than direct measurement echoes 'invisible and not measurable' against visible effects. Traps: extreme; reversal.

**3. 2021 Q23 (food prices).** The passage lists high fuel prices; one statement turns this into 'oil producing countries' (subject shift). The other places the crisis *in* emerging economies, whereas the passage says their demand could *create* a crisis (cause versus location).

**4. 2021 Q31 (denominations).** A strict 'most logical assumption' stem. The argument: if each person judges their own religion none is wrong, if they judge each other's none is right. Deny each option. Claims that no one can live without a denomination, that propagation is a duty, or that people misunderstand their own denomination bring in things the argument does not use. The option about denominations ignoring human unity is nearest in subject but not obviously needed. No option passes the denial test cleanly, so this is a hard item: choose by closest fit and respect the guess rule of module 9.

**5. 2022 Q71 (research).** The options form a quantity ladder: never very valuable, sometimes very valuable, never without some value, always very valuable. The passage says research is 'probably never completely valueless' and always leaves 'a chance' of value. Match the quantifier *and* the degree word; 'always very valuable' is an upgrade and 'never very valuable' a reversal. Option (b), 'sometimes very valuable', is the subtle one: 'sometimes' fits the passage's 'a chance', but 'very valuable' upgrades 'may be of value' to a degree the passage never states. Option (c) matches the rung 'never completely valueless' but drops the hedge 'probably'; no option keeps 'probably', so rung-matching decides, and it beats the tie-break 'choose the one that claims less' (which would point to (b)).

**6. 2025 Q54 (the statesman).** 'Only if the Head of Government is indifferent' makes a condition exclusive, while the passage says even a wise statesman should want opponents. 'The more aggressive the opposition, the better' pushes degree into a monotonic rule, against the warning about opposition that is 'revolutionary and irreconcilable'. Here both statements conflict with the passage in nameable ways, which shows that 'Neither' can be the outcome of your verdicts but must never be a pattern.

**7. 2025 Q42 (net zero).** The statement that supply-side investment can produce low-carbon behaviour follows from the passage's need for incentives through such investment. The statement that people are *not capable* without government and companies turns a need for incentives into incapacity: degree upgrade. Treat this as a close call, however: the passage says even demand-side change 'would require companies and governments to provide more incentives', so statement II is not far from its need for company and government involvement; 'not capable' is only stronger than 'would require'.

**8. 2023 Q64 (data and privacy).** The passage argues that no one innovates where personal information is compromised, so privacy and innovation are linked, and privacy has economic value. Both numbered statements follow this chain. Not every pair of statements contains a wrong one.

### 9. Time, risk and the qualifying line

*Objective:* Compute net marks and expected value of a guess, and set a Mission 90 practice time for a single-question passage.

## The arithmetic you must own

Each CSAT question carries 2.5 marks (as recorded for all 27 verified questions here), and a wrong answer costs one-third of that: 2.5 divided by 3 is about 0.833 marks. The qualifying requirement used in this project is 33 per cent of the paper's 200 marks, which is **66 marks**: a candidate needs 66 marks or more as raw net marks. The paper-format facts (80 questions, 200 marks, 33 per cent) come from the project assignment and should be confirmed against the official notification.

Net marks = 2.5 x (correct) minus (5/6) x (wrong), so net scores move in steps of 5/6, about 0.833. Because 66 is not a multiple of 5/6, the first reachable net score at or above 66 marks is 66.67 (80 steps), for example 27 correct and 1 wrong. 26 correct and none wrong gives only 65. Three wrong answers cancel one right answer (3 x 0.833 = 2.5).

## When is a guess worth it?

The expected value of an attempt is 2.5 x p minus 0.833 x (1 - p), where p is your honest chance of being right. It is zero at p = 0.25.

- Blind guess among four options: p = 1/4, EV = 0.625 - 0.625 = **0**.
- One option safely eliminated: p = 1/3, EV = 0.833 - 0.556 = **+0.278**.
- Two options safely eliminated: p = 1/2, EV = 1.25 - 0.417 = **+0.833**.

'Safely' is the catch. Eliminate only by a named trap (degree, scope, direction, reversal, subject shift), not by a feeling.

## Time

The whole paper averages 90 seconds a question (two hours for 80 questions; verify the format against the official notification). A passage item takes longer than a short one. As a **Mission 90 practice target, not an official allotment**, aim for about 2 to 3 minutes per single-question passage: roughly 15 seconds on the stem, 60 on one tagged reading, 60 on the options and 15 to decide. If you cannot name a surviving option by 3 minutes, mark it and move on.

## Passes through the paper

- **First pass**: settle every item you can by the method. Guess only if at least one option is eliminated by a named trap and your honest chance is above 25 per cent.
- **Second pass**: return to marked items and decide by the 25 per cent rule.

CSAT is qualifying: its marks do not count towards the rank, so the aim is a safe margin. This lesson does not predict how many items you will answer correctly.

*Objective:* Attempt the nine original passages under a timer and log each error by trap name.

## What the practice set contains

The nine passages are original. They mix the three families: three tone items (sceptical, admiring, ironic), three assumption items (a strict denial test, a statement-validity set and a revenue argument), and three context items (a word in context, an implication and a purpose question). ## Protocol

1. Attempt each passage at a Mission 90 practice target of 2 to 3 minutes. Read the stem, read the passage once with S, L and B marks, predict, then read the options.
2. Write your answer before opening the explanation. The explanations are there for after the attempt.
3. Open the explanation and compare every option, not just the key.
4. Log each error in four columns: passage number, family, trap name (degree, scope, direction, extreme, true-but-not-needed, outside knowledge, intensity), and the test you skipped.

## What to look for in the log

- Errors on tone items are usually *intensity* errors. If you choose 'hostile' for 'critical', practise ranking words.
- Errors on assumption items are usually *true-but-not-needed* or *extreme* errors. Practise denying the option before reading the next.
- Errors on context items are usually *outside knowledge* or *purpose* errors (answering with the example's topic instead of the claim it supports).

### Notes: Tone vocabulary with precise meanings

- Direction and intensity are the two quickest filters: decide positive, negative or neutral, then mild, moderate or strong.

### Notes: Tone, attitude and mood

- Tone is the manner of speaking about the subject, shown by chosen words.
- Attitude is the author's underlying stance towards a subject, person or view.
- Mood is the feeling created in the reader or the atmosphere of a scene.
- In CSAT stems, treat 'tone' and 'attitude' as one question: direction, intensity and target.
- A distressing subject does not make a passage sad in tone; an objective voice can describe a tragedy.

### Notes: Assumption, inference, implication

- Full explanation in CT-002 module 2; vocabulary shared across CT-001, CT-002 and CT-003.

### Notes: The denial test procedure

- 1. State the argument in one line: reason, therefore claim.
- 2. Choose a candidate unstated statement.
- 3. Deny it with a minimal negation: 'all' becomes 'not all'; 'will' becomes 'may not'; 'most' becomes 'not most'.
- 4. With the denial true, does the reason still support the claim? If not, the candidate is necessary.
- 5. A true statement whose denial leaves the argument standing is not an assumption (true but not needed).
- 6. An extreme statement ('only', 'never', 'cannot') is usually not needed: its denial is a mild statement the argument tolerates.

### Notes: Statement-validity traps seen in the verified set

- Analysis of the verified items; no answer key is asserted.

### Notes: Context clues and structure signals

- Word meaning: restatement, then contrast, then example or consequence; substitute and check direction.
- Claim signals: 'the point is', 'in short', the closing sentence.
- Turn signals: 'but', 'however', 'yet' (towards the author's real view).
- Concession signals: 'admittedly', 'of course', 'it is true that'.
- Example signals: 'for instance', 'for example', 'consider'; an example supports a claim.
- Conclusion signals: 'therefore', 'thus', 'so', 'hence'.

### Notes: The closed-world rule and its two exceptions

- Rule: use only the passage; a true outside fact is not a reason to choose an option.
- Exception 1 (Mission 90 convention): a stem that itself asks you to apply a scientific fact or definition; the passage must still be consistent with your choice.
- Exception 2 (Mission 90 convention): the meaning of ordinary English words.
- A one-step paraphrase of the passage is not outside knowledge (CT-001 module 4).
- No official statement of these two exceptions is cited; they are teaching conventions.

### Notes: Negative marking and the qualifying line

- CSAT carries 2.5 marks per question; a wrong answer costs one-third, which is 0.833 marks.
- 33 per cent of 200 is 66 marks; a candidate needs 66 marks or more as raw net marks. Confirm the format against the official notification.
- Net marks = 2.5 x correct minus 0.8333 x wrong; net scores move in steps of 5/6, so the first reachable net score at or above 66 marks is 66.67 (for example 27 correct, 1 wrong).
- Blind guess among four options: expected value 0. One option eliminated: +0.278. Two eliminated: +0.833. Break-even honest chance: 25 per cent.
- Mission 90 practice target (not an official allotment): about 2 to 3 minutes per single-question passage.

### Notes: Quantifier ladder (2022 Q71 pattern)

- Never < sometimes < usually < always: match the passage's own position on the ladder.
- 'Never completely valueless' is not 'always very valuable' and not 'never very valuable'.
- A hedge in the passage ('probably', 'a chance') should be kept in the option where an option keeps it; in 2022 Q71 no option keeps 'probably', so match the rung ('never without some value') and treat 'very valuable' as an upgrade of 'a chance ... may be of value'.
- 'Only if' makes a condition necessary and exclusive; it is stronger than 'if'.

- **foundationChecks:**

    - **a:** Tone is the author's manner of speaking about the subject; mood is the feeling created in the reader or the atmosphere of a scene.
  - - **q:** Name the three axes on which a tone word can be placed.
    - **a:** Direction (positive, negative, neutral), intensity (mild to strong) and target.

    - **a:** State the opposite in a minimal form; if the assumption is necessary, the argument no longer works.
  - - **q:** In the closed-world rule, what are the two exceptions?
    - **a:** Scientific or definitional stems, and ordinary vocabulary.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is a true statement not necessarily an assumption?
    - **a:** An assumption must be needed by the argument: its denial must collapse the argument. A true statement whose denial leaves the argument standing is true but not needed.
  - - **q:** Why do extreme statements ('only', 'never') rarely pass the denial test?
    - **a:** Their denial is a mild statement ('other ways exist') that the argument usually tolerates, so the extreme is more than the argument needs.

### Memory skeleton

- Tone is manner, attitude is stance, mood is the reader's feeling
- Tone word: direction, then intensity, then target
- Strict assumption: denial test with a minimal negation
- Statement validity: supported, or one safe step away
- Context: restatement, contrast, example; structure signals
- One read with three tags: S claim, L loaded words, B but-turn
- Eliminate by scope, degree, direction; if two pass, the lesser claim

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, write the stem-to-test table (tone, strict assumption, statement validity, inference, context, purpose), the denial-test steps and the three elimination questions.

### Last-week summary

- Tone: direction (positive, negative, neutral), intensity, target; a sad subject does not make a sad tone; reject one notch too intense.
- Assumption: deny minimally ('all' to 'not all'); if the argument collapses it is needed; true but not needed is wrong; extremes are rarely needed.
- Statement-validity traps: new cause, degree upgrade, reversal, subject shift, prescription from description, outside knowledge, extreme.
- One read with S, L, B tags; then scope, degree, direction; judge coded statements one by one, never by pattern.
- Confusables: sceptical versus cynical, ironic versus sarcastic, critical versus hostile, cautionary versus alarmist.
