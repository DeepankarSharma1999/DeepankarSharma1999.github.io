### 1. Why options are close: one best answer or several defensible

*Objective:* State why CSAT comprehension options are close, and tell elimination (a named defect) from guessing.

## What is new in this lesson

CT-001 to CT-005 taught you what a passage says and what follows from it. This lesson covers the last step: choosing among options when two or three of them look reasonable. It uses the earlier tools without re-teaching them: naming wrong options (CT-001 module 4, seven families), structure (CT-001 module 3), degrees of strength and the 'true but not supported' trap (CT-002 modules 3 and 5), tone (CT-003 modules 2 and 3), over-generalisation and causal slips (CT-004 modules 3 and 4), and voice and type (CT-005 module 5). What is new is the seven-step order, the six defect codes, the two elimination styles and the arithmetic of guessing.

## Why options are close

A comprehension item has one **key** (the answer the setter intends) and three **distractors** (the wrong options). A distractor is usually made from the key by changing one thing, so wrong options share words with the passage and sound sensible.

Two situations produce close options.

1. **One right option and three near copies with a flaw.** You eliminate by finding the flaw. The right option survives every test.
2. **Two options defensible and a stem asking for the 'best' or 'most logical'.** No option may be perfect. You rank the options by how much of the passage supports each and how little each adds (module 4).

## Elimination is not guessing

Guessing is choosing without a reason. **Elimination** is removing an option for a *named defect* that you can point to in the option's wording and in the passage's wording. 'It feels like too much' is not a named defect. 'It says *always*; the passage says *often*' is one.

## A first look

> Passage: The canteen cut the sugar in its tea this term. Complaints about the tea fell.

Which is the most careful reading? (a) Cutting sugar made the complaints fall. (b) Complaints about the tea fell after the sugar was cut. (c) Nobody likes sweet tea. Option (a) adds a cause the passage does not state; (c) adds a claim about everyone; (b) says only what the passage says.

### 2. The seven-step procedure and the six defects

*Objective:* Run the seven steps in order and name each option's defect with the codes S, W, O, R, T, U.

## The seven-step procedure

1. **Read the stem first.** Note the family: 'can be inferred', 'best supports', 'most logical', 'central idea', 'NOT', 'EXCEPT', or a numbered-statement code. The stem sets the elimination style (module 4).
2. **Read the passage once** for structure (CT-001 module 3).
3. **Write a claim line.** Before touching the options, paraphrase what the passage claims in about ten words, in your own words. This anchors you to the passage, not to an attractive option.
4. **Test each option** against the six defects below. Mark each rejected option with its code.
5. **One survivor:** find the supporting passage sentence, then choose.
6. **Two survivors:** rank them (module 4).
7. **Three or four survivors, or the time cap reached:** use the decision rule in module 5.

## The six defects

| Code | Defect | The option... | Signal to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | Too strong | claims more than the passage allows | all, always, only, never, cannot, 'proves', 'will' |
| W | Too weak | claims so little that it does not answer the stem | 'some', 'may', a bare detail, when a main point is asked |
| O | Out of scope | brings in a person, place, time, group or topic the passage does not cover | a new noun; 'every town' for one town |
| R | Reversed | flips a direction, comparison, negation or cause and effect | 'more' for 'less'; swapping the thing that causes with the thing caused |
| T | True but irrelevant | is correct in the passage or in life but does not answer this stem | a detail offered as the main point; a fact outside the passage |
| U | Unsupported inference | leaps beyond the passage by adding a cause, a recommendation, a forecast or a motive | 'because', 'should', 'therefore', 'in order to' |

These regroup the eight traps and the PASS check of CT-002 module 5 for elimination: S and W are strength, O and T scope and support, R direction, U the cause, recommendation and forecast leaps.

CT-001 module 4's seven families map onto these codes: too strong = S; too narrow = W (or T when a detail is offered as the point); too broad and outside knowledge = O or T; reversal = R; distortion = R or U; partially true = the borrowed-ending trap (module 3).

## Applying them

Passage: 'The ferry now runs hourly instead of every two hours. Ticket sales are up by a fifth.' (a) The ferry will soon run every half hour. *(U: a forecast.)* (b) Ticket sales rose after the ferry became more frequent. *(Survives.)* (c) Every ferry in the district runs hourly. *(O and S.)* (d) Ticket sales have fallen. *(R.)* Write the code beside each option in practice; the codes become the error-log vocabulary in module 7.

### 3. The six defects in detail and the keyword traps

*Objective:* Apply each defect as a comparison with the passage, with three edge cases, and recognise the four keyword traps.

## Each defect is a comparison, not a mood

Every test compares the option with the passage, never with your own taste. Edge cases are given so the rule does not become a reflex.

**S, too strong.** Compare strength words (CT-002 module 3, CT-004 module 2).
- Passage says 'all' and the option says 'all': no defect. Strength words are only a defect when the passage does not back them.
- Passage says 'may'; option says 'can': same strength. Option says 'will': defect.
- Passage says 'most'; option says 'the majority': same meaning. Option says 'all': defect.

**W, too weak.** A weak option is *safe* in an inference stem ('some' is hard to falsify) but fails a main-idea stem, which asks for the point. Do not reject 'some' for being small.

**O, out of scope.** Look for new nouns. 'Hill towns', 'next decade', 'the government', 'students' appearing for the first time in an option is a flag. A harmless paraphrase ('residents' for 'villagers') is not a new group.

**R, reversed.** Check three things: direction (rose or fell), comparison (more or less) and who does what to whom. A double negative ('not unlikely') can restore the passage's meaning, so decode first.

**T, true but irrelevant.** The option may be a faithful detail. Ask: does it answer *this* stem? A true detail answers 'which is mentioned', not 'main point'.

**U, unsupported inference.** The leap words are 'because' (CT-004 module 4), 'should' (the recommendation leap), 'will' (a forecast) and 'in order to' (a motive).

## Keyword traps

Learners match words, and setters know it.

1. **The echo.** The option copies the passage's most striking word ('serious', 'alarming') but attaches it to a different claim, or to a voice the passage only reports (CT-005 module 5).
2. **The shifted quantifier.** The right words, but 'some' became 'all', or 'often' became 'always'.
3. **The borrowed ending.** The option begins with something the passage says and ends with something it does not. Read to the *last* word.
4. **The missing hedge.** The passage says 'is likely to'; the option drops the hedge. Keep the forecast, its source and its hedge.

A matching word is a reason to read more carefully, not to choose; a missing word is not a reason to reject, since keys often paraphrase.

For the order in which to apply the tests, use CT-001 module 4 (reversal and distortion, then outside knowledge, then too strong, then narrow versus broad). What is new here is only the code for each rejection (S, W, O, R, T, U), so that it can be logged in module 7.

### 4. Absolute versus rank-order elimination; NOT, most logical and code stems

*Objective:* Choose the elimination style from the stem, and handle NOT, most logical and numbered-statement stems.

## Absolute versus rank-order elimination

**Absolute elimination** judges each option alone, pass or fail, and suits 'which can be inferred', 'NOT supported' and codes. If two options pass, the stem probably wants the best one.

**Rank-order elimination** suits 'best', 'most logical', 'most rational', 'central idea'. Here several options may be defensible and the task is to choose the one the passage supports best. Mark each option ✓ (supported by a named sentence), ~ (partly supported or needs one extra assumption) or ✗ (defective). Choose the option with the most ✓ and the fewest additions. If two options both read ✓ throughout, you have probably missed a defect; check the *last* clause of each.

A practical tie-break (a heuristic, not an official rule): prefer the option that needs the fewest assumptions beyond the passage, and whose strength matches the passage's hedges.

## NOT and EXCEPT stems

The task flips: three options pass the absolute test and one fails. Write ✓ beside every option that the passage supports and look for the one that cannot be matched. Two errors are common. One is choosing the first option that is *true*, because you read the stem as 'which is supported'. The other is rejecting the too-strong option and moving on; here it is the answer. After choosing, re-read the stem and check that it still says NOT.

Same family: 'EXCEPT', 'least likely', 'cannot be concluded', 'false', 'incorrect'. Read the negative before the options.

## 'Most logical' and 'most rational'

These mean 'follows from the passage with the fewest extra assumptions', not 'most sensible in life'. An option that goes beyond the records (a certainty, an extreme step) is weaker than a measured option that follows them.

## Numbered statements and codes

For 'which of the statements is/are correct', judge each statement alone, then map to the code. Start with the statement you are surest about. One firm statement can remove two codes at once: if statement 3 is clearly supported, 'only 1' and 'only 2' vanish. Then look at the statement that separates the remaining codes. Do not choose a favourite code and bend the statements to fit; judge statements first, the code last.

### 5. Negative marking, when to guess, and timing

*Objective:* Compute the expected value of a guess exactly and apply the guess-or-skip rule.

## The marking arithmetic, stated exactly

Figures from CT-001-B2 and CT-002 module 9 (one-third penalty, 33 per cent bar and two-hour duration: registry provisions cited in CT-001-B2; 80 questions and 2.5 marks: not in the registry, verify): a right answer earns 2.5 marks, a wrong answer costs one-third of that, 2.5 / 3 = 0.833 marks, and an unattempted question costs nothing. Three wrong answers cancel one right answer (3 x 2.5/3 = 2.5).

Expected value (EV) of an attempt, with p the honest chance of being right:

$$ EV = 2.5 p − (2.5/3)(1 − p) $$

This is zero at p = 0.25, so the break-even is a **25 per cent honest chance**.

| Options left (equally likely) | p | EV in marks |
|---|---|---|
| 4 (blind guess) | 1/4 | 0.000 (exactly 0) |
| 3 (one eliminated) | 1/3 | +0.278 |
| 2 (two eliminated) | 1/2 | +0.833 |
| 1 | 1 | +2.500 |

## The catch: how reliable are your eliminations?

Let r be the chance that an elimination did not discard the key (with two removals, r is the chance that neither removed option was the key). If you remove one option and guess among three, p = r/3, positive only when r is above 75 per cent. If you remove two and guess between two, p = r/2, positive when r is above 50 per cent.

Example: one option removed with r = 0.6 gives p = 0.2 and EV = -0.167, negative. Two removed with r = 0.8 gives p = 0.4 and EV = +0.500. A *named* defect (module 3) is worth far more than a hunch.

## Variance

A positive average is not a guaranteed gain. Ten guesses with two options left each average 10 x 0.833 = +8.33 marks, but 2 right out of 10 gives 5.00 - 6.67 = -1.67; the chance of two or fewer right is 56/1024, about 5.5 per cent. Thin margins over the qualifying line (CT-002 module 9) make this matter.

## Decision rule

- **Guess** when you removed at least one option for a named defect and honestly rate your chance above 25 per cent; the more named removals, the safer.
- **Skip** when you cannot name a defect for any option, or when all four still look equally good after a fair read.

## Timing

Two hours for 80 questions averages 90 seconds each (CT-001-B2). A practice target for a close-option passage item is about two minutes (10 seconds on the stem, 45 on the passage, 10 on the claim line, 55 on the options). Cap at two and a half minutes (CT-002 module 9); if three or more options still stand, mark and move on. These are practice targets, not official allotments.

### 6. The same method for logic, decision-making and quantitative items

*Objective:* Carry the six defects and the guess rule to logic, decision-making and quantitative items.

## The same method outside comprehension

The six defects keep their meaning; only the vocabulary changes.

**Logic (statement and conclusion).** *Too strong*: 'all' where the statements give 'some'. *Reversed*: the converse ('All A are B' does not give 'All B are A'). *Out of scope*: a term in no statement. Judge each conclusion alone. See P9.

**Decision-making.** Decision-making appears in the official CSAT syllabus wording; how often current papers ask it is not verified here, so treat this as optional practice. Eliminate options that break a rule or ignore safety (rule-breaking and extreme options do not fit the six codes neatly; log them as 'rule' or 'extreme', a Mission 90 label, not one of S, W, O, R, T, U), and options that are extreme, such as doing nothing or doing everything. Prefer the option that meets the urgent need *and* stays within the rules and records. See P10.

**Quantitative.** Elimination tests the numbers, not the wording. Four tools:

1. **Bounds.** Estimate a range first and delete options outside it.
2. **Units and powers of ten.** Delete the number with the wrong unit or an extra zero.
3. **Slip answers.** Setters build distractors from common slips: adding percentages, forgetting to convert units, using the wrong operation. When the slip is recognised, the option goes.
4. **Direction.** Should the result be larger or smaller than the starting value?

Worked example. A train 150 m long passes a pole in 10 s. Speed in km/h? Options: 15; 4.17; 54; 540.
- The speed is 150 / 10 = 15 m/s. Option 15 stops at m/s (unit slip).
- Multiply by 3.6 to get km/h: 15 x 3.6 = 54. Option 4.17 divides instead (15 / 3.6 = 4.17).
- 540 has an extra zero. The answer is 54.

See P11 for a second case.

The module 5 guess rule applies to every item type. Full quantitative lessons are not yet written; this module covers only elimination. Timed mixed practice is CT-007, to come on Day 14, not yet written.

### 7. Practice protocol, three timed mini-sets and the error log

*Objective:* Follow the protocol, time three mini-sets and log each error by defect code.

## Protocol

1. **Untimed pass.** Do P1 to P4 untimed. For every option write its defect code (S, W, O, R, T, U) before you choose.
2. **Read every explanation**, including for options you rejected correctly.
3. **Timed mini-sets** (practice targets, not official allotments):
   - **Set A: P1, P2, P3**, 2 minutes each, total 6 minutes. Focus: S, W, R and O.
   - **Set B: P4, P5, P6**, 2 minutes for P4 and P5 and 2.5 for P6, total 6.5 minutes. Focus: T, NOT stems, rank-order.
   - **Set C: P7, P8, P9, P10, P11**, 2 minutes each for P7 and P8, 1 each for P9, P10 and P11, total 7 minutes. Focus: codes, echo traps, logic, decision, quantitative.
4. **Decide before you mark.** Write the guess-or-skip decision for each item using module 5, then compare with the key. One set proves nothing.
5. **Log every error** in one row: item, the defect code you missed, the word that fooled you, the cure. Use the table in the master notes.
6. **Retry after seven days** the items you got wrong, answering before reading the explanation.

## Common wrong turns in practice

- Choosing the option that 'sounds academic'.
- Rejecting a long, careful option for being long; hedged options are often keys.
- Missing a NOT stem (re-read the stem after choosing).
- Treating a skip as a failure: it costs nothing; a wrong answer costs 0.833.

Completing the items is not mastery. The evidence is correct, explained eliminations on fresh passages after a delay, and a log that shows which defect still catches you.

### Notes: Definitions

- Key: the option the setter intends as the answer; distractor: a wrong option, usually built from the key or from a passage sentence.
- Elimination: removing an option for a named defect that can be shown in the option's wording and the passage's wording.
- Absolute elimination: judging each option alone as pass or fail.
- Rank-order elimination: ranking defensible options by how fully the passage supports them and how little each adds.
- Claim line: your own ten-word paraphrase of the passage's claim, written before reading the options.
- Named defect: one of the six codes S, W, O, R, T, U (Mission 90 teaching construct, not an official classification).
- Reliability r of an elimination: the chance that the option you removed was not the key (with two removals, the chance that neither removed option was the key).

### Notes: Keyword traps

- Echo: the option borrows the passage's striking word but changes the claim or voice.
- Shifted quantifier: some becomes all; often becomes always.

### Notes: Edge cases tested against the rules

- 'All' in the option is fine when the passage says 'all'; strength is judged against the passage.
- A modest option ('some') is safe for inference but fails a main-idea stem.

- In a NOT stem, a defective option is the answer, not a rejected one.
- Two ✓ options after a fair read mean a defect was missed; check the last clause of each option.
- Quantitative items: an option can be eliminated by units or bounds without full computation, but check by computing when time allows.
- A decision option that breaks no rule but ignores the urgent need can still be weaker than one that meets both.

### Memory skeleton

- Stem first: its family sets the elimination style
- Read the passage once for structure
- Write a ten-word claim line before the options
- Test each option for the six defects; write the code beside it
- Watch keyword traps: echo, shifted quantifier, borrowed ending, missing hedge
- One survivor: find its support; two: rank; more: decision rule
- Log every error: code, fooling word, cure

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, write the seven-step procedure, the six defect codes with one signal word each, and the four keyword traps.

### Last-week summary

- Elimination removes an option for a named defect shown in its wording against the passage's wording; a feeling is not a defect.
- S too strong, W too weak, O out of scope, R reversed, T true but irrelevant, U unsupported cause, recommendation, forecast or motive.
- Absolute: each option pass or fail. Rank-order: most support, fewest additions. NOT stem: the defective option is the answer.
- Keyword traps: an echoed word changing the claim, some to all, a borrowed ending, 'likely' to 'will'. Length is not a defect.
- Two survivors after a fair read mean a missed defect: check the last clause of each.
