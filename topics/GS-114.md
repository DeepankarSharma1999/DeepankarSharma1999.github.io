### Bridge: Mountain and river words you need first

A **range** is a long line of connected mountains; a **ridge** is the narrow crest along the top of one. A **peak** is a single high summit. A **pass** is a low gap or saddle in a range that lets people, animals and roads cross; it is usually the easiest place to cross, not the lowest place in the whole range. A **valley** is low land between hills or mountains. A **gorge** is a very deep, narrow valley with steep walls, often cut by a river. A **glacier** is a slow-moving river of ice formed from compacted snow; a **snowline** is the height above which snow lies all year. The **source** of a river is where it begins; a **tributary** is a smaller river that joins a larger one; a **confluence** is the place where two rivers meet; a **prayag** is the Indian word for a sacred confluence. Banks are named as you face **downstream** (the direction the water flows): the **left bank** is on your left hand, the **right bank** on your right. Keep this rule in mind, because NCERT uses it when it lists tributaries.

### Bridge: Reading direction words on the Himalayan arc

When NCERT says the ranges run from north-west to south-east it is describing their **orientation**: the direction in which the long axis of the range lies. Read the pair as 'from here to there'. A range that runs north-west to south-east starts in the upper left of a standard map (north is up, west is left) and ends in the lower right. East-west means the range lies along a line from left to right; north-south means it lies along a line from top to bottom. The Himalayas are not a straight wall but an **arc**: they start in the north-west, run broadly west to east across the middle, and then turn towards the south in the north-east, so their orientation changes as you move along them. Practise by tracing an arc on paper with your finger from the top-left corner, along the top, and then down the right side. Every orientation NCERT gives in module 3 fits on that arc.

### 1. Orientation: the Himalayas on one page

*Objective:* See the Himalayas as one arc with three jobs (wall, water tower, divide) and know the order of this lesson.

## The Himalayas in one sentence

The Himalayas are a young, folded mountain system that curves along India's northern edge from the north-west to the north-east. They are built of a series of parallel ranges, they hold the snow and glaciers that feed the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems, and they cut the subcontinent off from Central and East Asia except through a few passes.

## The three jobs (kegy102 p. 5, kegy103 p. 3, kegy104 p. 3)

- **A wall.** NCERT describes the Himalayas as standing almost like a strong, long wall between the Indian subcontinent and the Central and East Asian countries (kegy102 p. 5).
- **A water tower.** The Himalayan rivers are perennial (they flow all year) because they are fed by melting snow as well as rain (kegy103 p. 3).
- **A divide.** NCERT calls the range a physical barrier and also a climatic, drainage and cultural divide (kegy102 p. 5). It shields India from cold northern winds and traps the monsoon winds (kegy104 p. 3).

### 2. Foundation: how the range was built, and why it is young, weak and flexible

*Objective:* Explain, in your own words, how the northward movement of the Indian plate built the Himalayas and what young, weak and flexible mean.

## Words first

- **Endogenic forces** act from inside the earth (they lift, fold and break the crust); **exogenic forces** act at the surface (rivers, ice, wind and rain wear the land down).
- A **plate** is a huge slab of the earth's outer shell. A **fold** is a bend in layers of rock; a **fault** is a crack along which blocks of rock have slipped; a **thrust** is a fault along which one block is pushed up and over another.
- A **gorge** is a deep narrow valley, a **V-shaped valley** is cut by a fast river digging down, a **rapid** is a steep, fast stretch of river, and a **waterfall** is a vertical drop.

## The story in NCERT (kegy102 pp. 2-3)

NCERT says the Indian plate once lay south of the equator, was much larger and had the Australian plate as part of it. Over millions of years it broke up; the Australian plate moved towards the south-east and the Indian plate moved north. That northward movement is **still continuing**, and NCERT says it has significant consequences for the physical environment of the subcontinent. Day 19 lesson GS-097 (Plate tectonics and continental drift) explains why such a collision of two continents crumples and thickens the crust instead of sinking it; Day 20 lesson GS-098 (Earthquakes and volcanoes) explains the earthquakes that follow. Do not re-learn them here.

## Young, weak and flexible

NCERT contrasts two kinds of land (kegy102 p. 3):

| | Peninsular Block | The Himalayas |
|---|---|---|
| Age and strength | Very old, rigid, stable | Young, weak, flexible |
| Origin | Ancient gneisses and granites | Tectonic (made by plate forces) |
| Activity | Vertical movements, block faulting | Faults, folds and thrust plains still forming |
| Rivers | Shallow valleys, low gradients | Fast-flowing, youthful rivers |
| Landforms | Relict and residual hills | Gorges, V-shaped valleys, rapids, waterfalls |

**Flexible** means the rocks are still being bent and broken, not locked in place. **Weak** means they are easily cut and loosened. **Young** here is geological youth: the mountains are recent enough that both endogenic and exogenic forces are still strongly at work.

## Why this matters for the exam

Because the range is young and active, you expect **earthquakes**, **landslides**, **fast erosion**, **huge sediment loads** and **gorges** (Day 21 lesson GS-099, Weathering, erosion and mass wasting, links the landslides). The same sediment fills the plain to the south; NCERT says the Indo-Ganga-Brahmaputra Plain began as a depression that has been filled by sediments from Himalayan and Peninsular rivers, with alluvium 1,000-2,000 m deep on average (kegy102 p. 3; p. 5 says maximum, the GS-113 slip).

## One NCERT wording to watch

NCERT p. 3 groups 'the Himalayas and other Peninsular mountains' as young, weak and flexible, yet p. 2 describes the Peninsular hills (Aravali, Nallamala and others) as relict and residual. The description fits the Himalayas and the north-eastern hills; for the Peninsular hills keep p. 2 (check).

### 3. Core: the ranges - the Trans-Himalaya, three parallel ranges and the eastern hills

*Objective:* Place the Trans-Himalaya, Himadri, Himachal, Shiwalik and the eastern hills on a north-to-south sketch and state what NCERT supports and what is standard knowledge.

## What NCERT says (kegy102 p. 5, kegy101 p. 5)

The Himalayas are a series of **parallel mountain ranges**. NCERT's sentence lists the Greater Himalayan range as including the Great Himalayas and the Shiwalik, which is loose wording (the Shiwalik is the outer range, not part of the Great Himalayas). Their **orientation** changes along the arc:

- **North-western India:** north-west to south-east.
- **Darjiling and Sikkim:** east-west.
- **Arunachal Pradesh:** NCERT prints 'south-west to north-west'; maps show a south-west to north-east trend (a slip; verify).
- **Nagaland, Manipur and Mizoram:** north-south.

NCERT gives about **2,500 km** east to west and **160-400 km** north to south for the Great Himalayan (central axial) range. These figures describe the system as a whole, so quote them as 'the Himalayan system' (Day 21 lesson GS-113 flagged the same point; check).

## The standard division (verify with an atlas)

The extracts do not name the three parallel ranges. The standard textbook picture, from north to south, is:

| Range | Also called | Position and character |
|---|---|---|
| Greater Himalaya | Himadri | Northernmost of the three; the highest crest; snow-covered; source region of the big glaciers |
| Lesser Himalaya | Himachal | South of the Greater Himalaya; middle hill ranges; contains valleys such as the Kashmir, Kullu and Chamba valleys |
| Outer Himalaya | Shiwalik | Southernmost and lowest; the foothills; loose river deposits |

NCERT does support some of this: the **Pir Panjal** and the **Dhauladhar** are named as ranges drained by the Jhelum, Ravi and Beas, and the **Shiwalik** is described in the drainage chapter as made of sands, silt, clay, boulders and conglomerates of lacustrine and river origin (kegy103 p. 4). The **Bhabar** (8-10 km wide) lies at the foot of the Shiwalik (kegy102 p. 5), then the Tarai (10-20 km), then the alluvial plains (Day 21 lesson GS-113; Day 23 lesson GS-115 takes up the Northern plains).

## North of the Great Himalaya: the Trans-Himalaya

Beyond the main crest, on the Tibetan side, lie ranges that NCERT names only in passing: the **Ladakh** and **Zaskar** ranges (the Indus flows north-west between them), and the **Kailash** range in Tibet, where the Indus and the Brahmaputra rise (the Satluj rises at Raksas tal near Mansarovar) (kegy103 pp. 4, 5, 6). The Karakoram is a standard member of this northern belt (verify). The **Hindukush** and **Sulaiman** ranges form the north-western edge of the subcontinent (kegy101 p. 5).

## The eastern hills

NCERT calls the hills of Nagaland, Manipur and Mizoram a north-south trending set, and names the **Purvachal** hills as the north-eastern wall of the subcontinent (kegy101 p. 5). Standard sources list the Patkai, Naga, Manipur and Mizo hills here (verify). Spelling in the later exam: 'Purvachal' or 'Purvanchal' are both used.

### 4. Core: regional divisions, named peaks and the ranges the rivers cross

*Objective:* Divide the arc into four regions between rivers and attach the peaks, hills and glaciers NCERT names to the right region.

## Why use regions

A map question rarely asks about a whole range; it asks about a **place** (a peak, a glacier, a valley). The standard way to organise places is by **regional divisions** between great rivers. The four-part scheme below is a standard one (it is attributed to Burrard's survey; verify); every NCERT place name fits it.

| Region | Lies between (west to east) | Places the NCERT extracts name |
|---|---|---|
| Punjab (Kashmir) Himalaya | Indus and Satluj | Ladakh, Zaskar, Pir Panjal, Dhauladhar; Kullu, Chamba and Kashmir valleys; Verinag, Wular lake, Rohtang pass |
| Kumaon Himalaya (includes Garhwal) | Satluj and Kali | Garhwal hills (Ramganga near Gairsain), Gangotri and Gaumukh, Yamunotri and the Banderpunch range, Satopanth glacier above Badrinath, Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve (Uttarakhand), Milam glacier |
| Nepal Himalaya | Kali and Tista | Dhaulagiri and Mount Everest (the Gandak rises between them), the Arun and the Kosi; Kanchenjunga (Khangchendzonga Biosphere Reserve) and the Darjiling hills (Mahananda), which lie west of the Tista (verify) |
| Assam Himalaya | Tista and the Dihang (Brahmaputra) bend | Sikkim east of the Tista, Bhutan, Arunachal Pradesh, Namcha Barwa |

The Kali (also called Sarda, Chauk or Goriganga on different stretches; standard maps show the Goriganga joining the Kali at Jauljibi; verify) forms part of the Indo-Nepal boundary (kegy103 p. 6), which is why the Kumaon region ends there. Some books treat Sikkim-Darjiling separately (verify).

## Heights and peaks: only what NCERT gives

NCERT gives only two Himalayan heights: **Namcha Barwa 7,755 m**, near which the Brahmaputra carves a deep gorge in the 'Central Himalayas' (kegy103 pp. 6-7; the central axial range at the arc's eastern end, not the Nepal region), and the **Banderpunch range 6,316 m**, on whose western slopes the Yamunotri glacier lies (kegy103 p. 6). Standard peak-to-region links (verify): Mount Everest and Dhaulagiri in Nepal; Kanchenjunga (Khangchendzonga) on the Sikkim-Nepal border; Nanda Devi in Uttarakhand (the Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve is in Uttarakhand per NCERT kegy105 p. 7).

| Bokhar Chu glacier (Tibet) | Indus |
| Gangotri, near Gaumukh (3,900 m) | Bhagirathi, the headstream of the Ganga |
| Satopanth, above Badrinath | Alaknanda |
| Yamunotri | Yamuna |

| Milam | Sarda (Goriganga); NCERT adds 'in the Nepal Himalayas', but the glacier is in Uttarakhand (slip; verify) |

Standard additions asked in Prelims (not in these extracts; verify): **Siachen** feeds the Nubra (NCERT lists the Nubra as an Indus tributary; on maps it joins the Shyok, which joins the Indus; verify); **Bara Shigri** (Lahaul, Himachal Pradesh) feeds the Chandra, one of the two streams that form the Chenab; **Zemu** (Sikkim) feeds the Teesta; **Milam** is in Kumaon.

### 5. Core: the passes - which ones NCERT names and how to place the rest

*Objective:* Name the passes NCERT mentions, place the standard Himalayan passes from west to east and use a three-step method for pass questions.

## Why passes matter

A **pass** is a gap through a mountain barrier. NCERT says that except for a few mountain passes such as the **Khyber, the Bolan, the Shipkila, the Nathula and the Bomdila** it was difficult to cross the Himalayas and the other bounding ranges (kegy101 p. 5). They concentrate movement at a few points, which is why the examiner asks about them.

Two other passes appear in the drainage chapter: the **Shipki La**, through which the Satluj enters India from Tibet (NCERT then jumps to the Punjab plains near Rupar; kegy103 p. 5), and the **Rohtang Pass**, near which the Beas and Ravi rise (kegy103 p. 5). The name is written 'Shipkila' in kegy101 and 'Shipki La' in kegy103; it is the same pass.

## West to east: the standard list (verify with an atlas)

The extracts do not locate the passes (map practice: Day 19 lesson GS-132). The standard positions, from the north-west to the north-east, are:

| Pass | Where (standard; verify) | Faces |
|---|---|---|
| Khyber | Pakistan-Afghanistan border region, north-west | Afghanistan (not in India) |
| Bolan | Balochistan, Pakistan | Afghanistan side (not in India) |
| Banihal | Pir Panjal range; links Jammu with the Kashmir valley | Within Jammu and Kashmir |
| Zoji La | Links Srinagar with Kargil and Leh | Ladakh |
| Rohtang | Links Kullu valley with Lahaul and Spiti, Himachal Pradesh | Lahaul and Spiti |
| Baralacha La | Lahaul (Himachal Pradesh) to Ladakh | Ladakh |
| Shipki La | Himachal Pradesh (Kinnaur); the Satluj enters India here | Tibet |
| Lipulekh, Niti, Mana | Uttarakhand | Tibet |
| Nathu La, Jelep La | Sikkim | Tibet |
| Bomdila | Arunachal Pradesh | Tibet side (verify) |

Khyber and Bolan lie outside India's territory; they belong to the Hindukush-Sulaiman edge of the subcontinent that NCERT names in the same sentence.

## A three-step method

1. **Side and region.** Is the pass in the north-west (Jammu, Kashmir, Ladakh), the north-central part (Himachal, Uttarakhand) or the north-east (Sikkim, Arunachal)?
2. **State or Union Territory.** Attach a State: Banihal in Jammu and Kashmir, Rohtang and Shipki La in Himachal Pradesh, Lipulekh in Uttarakhand, Nathu La in Sikkim, Bomdila in Arunachal Pradesh (verify each).
3. **Neighbour or river.** Say which country lies beyond and which river runs near: Satluj at Shipki La; Beas and Ravi at Rohtang.

## Cautions

- The Beas rises **near** the Rohtang Pass; the pass is not the river.
- The Shipki La is where the Satluj **enters** India; NCERT also mentions a gorge at Rupar, which comes later, in the Punjab plain, so read NCERT's sentence order loosely.
- Bomdila is also a town; in the NCERT sentence it names the pass.

### 6. Core: how Himalayan rivers behave, antecedent drainage and the Indo-Brahma story

*Objective:* Describe the mountain-stretch and plain-stretch behaviour of Himalayan rivers, define antecedent drainage and retell how one big river was split into three systems.

## Behaviour in the mountains and on the plain

NCERT (kegy103 pp. 3-4) says the Himalayan drainage mainly includes the **Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra** basins. They are **perennial** because they are fed both by snowmelt and by rain. In the mountains they pass through **giant gorges** carved by erosion that went on at the same time as the uplift of the Himalayas, and they also form V-shaped valleys, rapids and waterfalls. On entering the plain they build **depositional** features: flat valleys, ox-bow lakes, flood plains, braided channels and **deltas** near the mouth. NCERT adds a neat contrast: in the Himalayan reaches the course is highly **tortuous** (twisting through the gorges), but over the plains the rivers show a strong **meandering** tendency and shift their courses often.

The **Kosi**, the 'sorrow of Bihar', is the example. It brings a huge quantity of sediment from its upper reaches, deposits it in the plain, the channel gets blocked, and the river jumps to a new course (kegy103 p. 4). The **Brahmaputra** is known for floods, channel shifting and bank erosion because most of its tributaries are large and carry heavy sediment from a high-rainfall catchment (kegy103 p. 7). Both follow from module 2: young mountains, steep slopes, fast erosion.

## Antecedent drainage

An **antecedent river** is one that existed before the mountain rose across its path and kept its course by cutting its valley down as fast as the land was lifted. NCERT labels three rivers 'antecedent': the **Satluj** (kegy103 p. 5), the **Kosi** (p. 6) and the **Subansiri**, which has its origin in Tibet (p. 7). The usual textbook list also includes the Indus and the Brahmaputra (standard; verify). A clue to antecedence (not a proof) is a river that begins **north of the main crest** (in Tibet) and cuts **through** the Himalayas in a gorge instead of turning round the mountains. Note the contrast with the Ganga, whose headstreams start in the Himalayan glaciers themselves.

## How the drainage evolved (kegy103 p. 4)

NCERT says geologists differ, but many believe that a mighty river called **Shiwalik** or **Indo-Brahma** once ran the whole length of the Himalaya, from Assam to Punjab and on to Sind, and reached the sea in the Gulf of Sind near lower Punjab in the **Miocene** (5-24 million years ago as NCERT prints it). The evidence is the remarkable continuity of the Shiwalik and its sand, silt, clay, boulder and conglomerate deposits of lake and river origin.

In time it was **dismembered** into three drainage systems:

1. the **Indus** and its five tributaries in the west,
2. the **Ganga** and its Himalayan tributaries in the centre,
3. the **Brahmaputra** in Assam and its Himalayan tributaries in the east.

Two events are named: a **Pleistocene upheaval** in the western Himalaya, including the uplift of the Potwar Plateau (NCERT writes 'Delhi Ridge'), which became the water divide between the Indus and Ganga systems; and the **down-thrusting of the Malda gap** between the Rajmahal hills and the Meghalaya plateau in the mid-Pleistocene, which turned the Ganga and Brahmaputra towards the Bay of Bengal. NCERT's equation of Potwar with the Delhi Ridge is a slip: the Potwar Plateau is in northern Pakistan (Day 18 lesson GS-117 recorded this; verify). The modern water divide inside India is the area of **Haryana and Delhi** (kegy102 p. 6).

The older, shallower Peninsular drainage is in Day 18 lesson GS-117.

### 7. Core: the three systems inside the mountains - sources, ranges crossed and gorges

*Objective:* Follow the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems from glacier source through the mountain stretch, naming the range, gorge or headstream NCERT gives.

The full basin account, left- and right-bank tributaries and the plains are in Day 18 lesson GS-117 (Major river basins and tributaries). Here only the **mountain stretch** matters, because that is what Himalayan questions ask.

## The Indus system (kegy103 pp. 4-5)

The Indus rises from a glacier near **Bokhar Chu** (31 degrees 15 minutes N, 81 degrees 40 minutes E) in Tibet at **4,164 m** in the **Kailash** range; in Tibet it is called Singi Khamban, 'Lion's mouth'. It flows north-west between the **Ladakh and Zaskar** ranges, passes through Ladakh and Baltistan, cuts across the Ladakh range in a spectacular gorge and enters Pakistan near **Chilas** in Dardistan. Its Himalayan tributaries include the Shyok, Gilgit, Zaskar, Hunza, Nubra, Shigar, Gasting and Dras.

The Indus receives the **Panjnad** a little above Mithankot (NCERT words it loosely as the name of the five Punjab rivers; strictly it is their combined stream). The five rivers are:

| River | Mountain-stretch fact (NCERT) |
|---|---|
| Jhelum | Rises from a spring at **Verinag**, foot of the **Pir Panjal**; Srinagar, Wular lake; leaves through a deep narrow gorge |
| Chenab | Chandra + Bhaga meet at **Tandi** near Keylong (Himachal Pradesh); 1,180 km before Pakistan |
| Ravi | Rises west of the **Rohtang** pass, Kullu hills; Chamba valley; between Pir Panjal and **Dhauladhar** |
| Beas | Beas Kund near the Rohtang Pass (4,000 m); Kullu valley; gorges at **Kati and Largi** in the Dhauladhar |
| Satluj | Raksas tal near Mansarovar, **4,555 m**, called Langchen Khambab in Tibet; antecedent; flows parallel to the Indus for about 400 km; enters through the **Shipki La**; feeds the Bhakra Nangal canals |

## The Ganga system (kegy103 pp. 5-6)

The **Bhagirathi** rises in the **Gangotri glacier near Gaumukh (3,900 m)** in Uttarkashi district and cuts through the Central and Lesser Himalayas in narrow gorges. The **Alaknanda** rises in the **Satopanth glacier** above Badrinath. NCERT lists its confluences: Dhauli + Vishnu Ganga at **Joshimath (Vishnu Prayag)**, the Pindar at **Karna Prayag**, the Mandakini at **Rudra Prayag**, and finally Bhagirathi + Alaknanda at **Devprayag**, from where the river is the Ganga. It enters the plains at **Haridwar**. (Standard lists give five prayags, adding Nand Prayag; verify.) The **Yamuna** rises in the Yamunotri glacier on the western slopes of the **Banderpunch** range (6,316 m). Other mountain facts: the **Ghaghara** cuts a deep gorge at **Shishapani**; the **Gandak** rises in the Nepal Himalayas between **Dhaulagiri and Mount Everest**; the **Kosi** (antecedent) rises north of Mount Everest in Tibet where its main stream, the **Arun**, rises.

## The Brahmaputra system (kegy103 pp. 6-7)

The Brahmaputra rises in the **Chemayungdung** glacier of the Kailash range near Mansarovar and runs east for nearly **1,200 km** across the dry, flat southern Tibet as the **Tsangpo**, receiving the **Rango Tsangpo** from the right. It carves a deep gorge in the Central Himalayas near **Namcha Barwa (7,755 m)**, emerges at the foothills as the **Siang or Dihang**, enters India west of **Sadiya** in Arunachal Pradesh, and after the **Dibang** and **Lohit** join from the left it is the Brahmaputra. It flows about 750 km in the Assam valley, receiving the Subansiri (antecedent), Kameng, Manas and Sankosh from the right and the Burhi Dihing and Dhansari (South) from the left. It enters Bangladesh near **Dhubri**; there the Tista joins and it is called the **Jamuna**, which meets the Padma.

### 8. Master: the Himalayas as climate divide, forest gradient and fragile system

*Objective:* Use the extracts to explain the Himalayas as a climatic divide, an altitude gradient of forests, a hazard zone and a glacier-fed water source, and write answers on the PYQ themes.

This is a deferred deep dive: do it after modules 1-7.

## Climate (kegy104)

NCERT says the Himalayas along with their extensions act as an effective **climatic divide** (p. 3). They shield the subcontinent from cold winds that originate near the Arctic circle and blow across central and eastern Asia, and they **trap the monsoon winds**, forcing them to shed moisture within the subcontinent. Snowfall is restricted to the Himalayan region (pp. 3, 12), the north-west Himalayas receive less than 10 cm of annual precipitation, and some sub-Himalayan areas exceed 200 cm (pp. 3, 12). In winter, **temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean** bring meagre rain to the north-western plains and **snowfall to the lower Himalayas**; that snow sustains the flow of the Himalayan rivers in the summer months (pp. 5-6). Day 20 lesson GS-105 (Jet streams and western disturbances) explains the mechanism. In the monsoon, the Bay of Bengal branch splits under the influence of the Himalayas and the north-west thermal low: one arm moves west along the Ganga plains to Punjab, the other up the Brahmaputra valley (p. 11; Day 20 lesson GS-119).

## Forest gradient (kegy105 pp. 3-4)

NCERT: the Himalayan ranges show a succession of vegetation **from tropical to tundra** with altitude.

| Altitude (NCERT) | Vegetation |
|---|---|
| Foothills | Deciduous forests |
| 1,000-2,000 m | Wet temperate forest; evergreen broad-leaf oak and chestnut in the north-east, West Bengal and Uttaranchal; chir pine between 1,500 and 1,750 m; deodar mainly in the west, with chinar and walnut (Kashmir) |
| 2,225-3,048 m | Blue pine and spruce; temperate grasslands in places |
| 3,000-4,000 m | Silver fir, juniper, pine, birch, rhododendron; high pastures |
| Above | Mosses and lichens of the tundra |

Southern slopes carry thicker cover than the drier north-facing slopes. The high pastures are used for **transhumance** (seasonal movement of herds) by the Gujjars, Bakarwals, Bhotiyas and Gaddis. The British replaced oak forests in Garhwal and Kumaon with chir pine for railway needs (kegy105 p. 3).

## Hazards and change

Because the range is young (module 2): earthquakes (Day 20 lesson GS-098), landslides (Day 21 lesson GS-099; Day 21 lesson GS-120 lists the Himalayan landslide zones), floods on the Kosi and the Brahmaputra (module 6). NCERT also says global warming would melt mountain glaciers and ice caps and raise the sea level; it quotes a prediction of 48 cm by the end of the twenty-first century (kegy104 p. 14). Day 19 lesson GS-111 (Critical changes to glaciers and ice caps) holds the glacier detail.

## The method

1. Write the clue or pair down. 2. Place it on the arc (west, centre, east; State or country). 3. Test each option against the tables. 4. If two remain, test the stricter clue. A wrong Prelims answer costs one-third of the marks (0.67 of a 2-mark question). A blind guess among four has an expected value of 0; once one option is removed, a guess among three gives (1/3 x 2) - (2/3 x 0.67) = 0.667 - 0.444 = +0.22 marks on average, so guess only after eliminating an option on a reason.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Range: a long line of connected mountains; ridge: the narrow crest along it; pass: a low gap that lets people and roads cross a range.
- Gorge: a very deep narrow valley, often cut by a river; V-shaped valley: cut by a fast river digging down; rapid: a steep fast stretch of river.
- Orientation: the direction in which the long axis of a range lies (for example north-west to south-east).
- Perennial river: flows all year; fed by snowmelt as well as rain (Himalayan rivers) as against non-perennial rivers that depend on rain.
- Antecedent river: one that existed before the mountain rose and kept its course by cutting down as fast as the land rose (NCERT examples: Satluj, Kosi, Subansiri).
- Indo-Brahma (Shiwalik) river: the long river that, in one theory NCERT reports, once ran from Assam through Punjab to Sind and was later split into three systems.
- Transhumance: seasonal movement of herds between low and high pastures.
- Prayag: a sacred confluence; Devprayag is where the Bhagirathi and the Alaknanda meet to form the Ganga (NCERT).

### Notes: Antecedent drainage and evolution of the Himalayan rivers (process)

- Miocene (5-24 million years ago as NCERT prints): a mighty Indo-Brahma (Shiwalik) river runs from Assam through Punjab to Sind and the Gulf of Sind.
- Pleistocene upheaval in the western Himalaya, with the uplift of the Potwar Plateau (NCERT writes Delhi Ridge), makes a water divide between the Indus and the Ganga systems.
- Mid-Pleistocene down-thrusting of the Malda gap between the Rajmahal hills and the Meghalaya plateau turns the Ganga and Brahmaputra systems towards the Bay of Bengal.
- Result: three systems, the Indus with five tributaries, the Ganga with its Himalayan tributaries and the Brahmaputra in Assam with its Himalayan tributaries.
- Rivers that began north of the crest and cut gorges through it as the mountains rose are antecedent (Satluj, Kosi, Subansiri in NCERT).

    - **approach:** Himalaya: tectonically active young mountains, weak rocks, steep slopes (module 2; Day 21 lesson GS-099). Western Ghats: steep scarps, heavy rain, slope cutting (Day 17 lesson GS-116). Present as a two-column difference. Analysis only.
- **conceptsTested:**
  - Glacier-to-river and peak-to-region matching
  - Antecedent drainage and clue-based river identification
  - Himalayan landforms, ranges and passes as map items
  - Himalayan climate, glacier, landslide and ecosystem themes in Mains
- **reasoningRequired:**
  - Place every named item on the west-to-east arc before judging an option.
  - Test clues in order of strictness and eliminate the options that fail the strictest one.
  - Separate NCERT-supported facts from standard knowledge flagged verify, and check the latter in an atlas.
- **examinerTraps:**
  - Glacier and river pairs that share a State but not a valley.
  - Peak and region pairs that swap Garhwal, Kumaon and Sikkim.
  - A river clue set where several options satisfy the first two clues.
  - Reversing tortuous and meandering, or Himalayan and Peninsular drainage features.
- **distractorAnalysis:**
  - In pair questions the distractor is usually a second real glacier or peak from the same region.
  - In clue questions the distractor satisfies the loose clues (antecedent, Tibetan source) but fails one strict clue.
  - In position questions the distractor is a famous place name that is near but in the wrong direction.
- **prelimsElimination:**
  - Pairs: find one definitely wrong pair, then discard every option containing it.
  - Clues: use the clue that most options fail (for example, no distributaries) first.
  - Direction: link the glacier to its river first, then read the river's valley direction.
- **mainsDirectives:**
  - Examine: give causes, effects and a judgement.
  - Discuss: give both sides of the issue and a conclusion.
  - Differentiate: use a two-column difference with a reason in each row.
  - Critically examine: weigh costs and benefits and state a conclusion.
- **mainsDimensions:**
  - Physical: tectonic setting, relief, glaciers, drainage
  - Human: transhumance, settlements, tourism, pilgrimage routes
  - Economic: hydropower, irrigation, horticulture, border trade through passes
  - Environmental: landslides, glacial melt, forest loss, fragile ecosystem
  - India case: Himalayan States, the Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra basins, the north-eastern hills
- **commonMistakes:**
  - Writing the Arunachal orientation exactly as NCERT misprints it.
  - Treating Garhwal and Kumaon as one region in every question: they are distinct sub-regions of Uttarakhand, but both lie inside Burrard's Kumaon Himalaya region, so a region-level question places both there; Nanda Devi sits near their junction (verify).
  - Giving a height or length for a peak or range that no extract states.
  - Confusing the Bhagirathi/Alaknanda headstreams with the Ganga itself.
