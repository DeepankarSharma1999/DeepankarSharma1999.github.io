### Bridge: Money words you need before this topic

A **tax** is money the government compels people and businesses to pay (income tax on salaries, GST on things you buy, excise on petrol). **Revenue** is the total money a government receives in a year; most of it is tax revenue, the rest is **non-tax revenue** (railway fares, dividends from public companies, fees). **Expenditure** is what the government spends on salaries, schools, roads, defence and so on. When expenditure is more than revenue, the government **borrows**.

A **grant** is money one government gives to another without expecting repayment. A loan must be repaid; a grant need not be. A **Consolidated Fund** is the main account into which all revenue and borrowings of a government flow and out of which all its spending is drawn; the Union has the Consolidated Fund of India (CFI) and each State has its own Consolidated Fund. 'Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India' means the payment is automatic and does not need a yearly vote of Parliament.

The Constitution divides law-making subjects into three lists in the Seventh Schedule: the **Union List** (Parliament alone: defence, railways, banking, currency), the **State List** (State legislatures normally: police, agriculture, land, public health) and the **Concurrent List** (both). Taxes are also divided between the first two lists.

Because the Union holds the richest taxes but the States must pay for schools, police and hospitals, the Union collects more than it needs and the States less than they need. This gap between levels of government is **vertical imbalance**. Because some States are rich (large industry, high incomes) and others poor, equally sized States have unequal capacity to raise money. This gap between States at the same level is **horizontal imbalance**. The Finance Commission exists to correct both gaps.

### Bridge: Reading an Article number and an Amendment number

The Constitution of India is written as numbered **Articles** (Article 1, Article 2 ... Article 395 originally). Articles are grouped into **Parts** by theme: Part XII is 'Finance, Property, Contracts and Suits' and contains Articles 264 to 300A, of which Articles 268 to 293 govern Centre-State financial relations. **Schedules** are lists attached at the end (the Seventh Schedule holds the Union, State and Concurrent Lists).

When Parliament changes the Constitution it passes a **Constitutional Amendment Act**, numbered in order: the 80th Amendment Act of 2000, the 88th of 2003, the 101st of 2016. New Articles inserted between existing ones get a letter suffix so that numbering is not disturbed: Article 269A (added in 2016) sits between 269 and 270, Article 279A created the GST Council. A repealed Article keeps its number but is marked 'Repealed' (Article 268A, Article 272).

For this topic, memorise the sequence 268, 269, 269A, 270, 271 (tax distribution), 273 and 275 (grants), 279 (net proceeds), 279A (GST Council), 280-281 (Finance Commission), 282 (discretionary spending). When a question says 'Article 275', translate it in your head to 'statutory grants on Finance Commission recommendation'.

### 1. Why fiscal federalism needs a referee

*Objective:* Understand, from zero, the problem the Finance Commission exists to solve.

## The one-sentence meaning

The **Finance Commission** is a constitutional body, set up by the President every five years under **Article 280**, that recommends how the Union should share its tax money with the States and how much extra help (grants) weaker States should receive.

## The problem it solves

NCERT's federalism chapter makes a blunt observation: the Constitution centralises economic and financial power, so the States carry 'immense responsibilities but very meagre revenue sources'. Think of it as a household in which one person holds the salary while another has to run the kitchen. That is **vertical imbalance** (bridge module B1): the Union collects the big, elastic taxes (income tax, corporation tax, customs) while the States must fund police, schools, hospitals and roads from narrower sources (land revenue, excise on liquor, stamp duty, taxes on vehicles).

Add a second problem. Maharashtra and Bihar both run schools, but their ability to raise money per person differs enormously. If each State kept only what it collected, poorer States could not provide even basic services. That is **horizontal imbalance**.

## Why a referee and not a negotiation

If the Union decided alone how much to give, States would call it favouritism (NCERT notes exactly this charge: that discretionary grants and loans were seen as 'lopsided' and discriminatory towards opposition-ruled States). If 28 States bargained every year, nothing would be stable. The Constitution's answer is a neutral, expert, time-bound body whose recommendations go to the President and are placed before Parliament. Laxmikanth records that the Constitution envisages the Finance Commission as the **'balancing wheel of fiscal federalism'**.

## Two kinds of transfer you will meet constantly

1. **Tax devolution**: a fixed share of the Union's divisible tax pool automatically flows to the States. The Finance Commission decides what percentage goes to States as a group (**vertical devolution**) and how that amount is split among individual States (**horizontal distribution**).
2. **Grants-in-aid**: additional money for States that still fall short after devolution, or for specific purposes (tribal welfare, local bodies, disaster relief).

## A picture to hold in mind

Union collects 100 rupees of shareable taxes -> Finance Commission says 'give X to the States as a whole' (vertical) -> 'of that X, give Bihar more per head than Maharashtra because its capacity is lower' (horizontal) -> 'and Bihar still cannot balance its budget, so add a grant' (Article 275). Keep this chain; every later module fills in one link.

### 2. The money map: Part XII and who may tax what

*Objective:* Locate the Finance Commission inside the Constitution's scheme of taxing powers.

## Where the rules live

Laxmikanth places Centre-State financial relations in **Articles 268 to 293 of Part XII**. Read them as four blocks: distribution of tax revenues (268-271), grants and related provisions (273-275, 282), the referee and its tools (279, 279A, 280, 281), and borrowing and tax immunities (285-293).

## Who may levy which tax

- Parliament alone taxes subjects in the **Union List** (Laxmikanth counts 13 tax entries).
- State legislatures alone tax subjects in the **State List** (18 tax entries, listed in module 5).
- The **Concurrent List has no tax entries**; the only exception is the **Goods and Services Tax**, where the **101st Amendment Act, 2016** gave Parliament and State legislatures concurrent power.
- **Residuary** taxing power (taxes not named in any list) belongs to Parliament; gift tax, wealth tax and expenditure tax were imposed under it.

## Levy, collect, appropriate: three different verbs

The Constitution separates the power to **levy** (impose) a tax, to **collect** it, and to **appropriate** (keep and spend) its proceeds. Income tax is levied and collected by the Union, but its proceeds are shared with the States. This separation is what makes a Finance Commission necessary: someone must say how shared proceeds are split.

## Limits on State taxing power (worth one Prelims statement each)

- Tax on professions, trades, callings and employments cannot exceed Rs 2,500 per person per year (Article 276).
- A State cannot tax supply of goods or services that takes place outside the State or in the course of import or export; Parliament lays down the principles for deciding where a supply takes place.
- A State can tax consumption or sale of electricity, but not electricity consumed by or sold to the Union, or used for railways.
- A State tax on water or electricity of an inter-State river authority set up by Parliament needs the President's assent.

## Protecting States' interests (Article 274)

Certain bills can be introduced in Parliament only on the President's recommendation: a bill imposing or varying a tax in which States are interested; a bill changing the meaning of 'agricultural income'; a bill affecting the principles on which money is distributable to States; and a bill imposing a surcharge for the Union's purposes. This is a procedural shield for the States.

## 'Net proceeds' (Article 279)

Net proceeds means the proceeds of a tax **minus the cost of collection**. The **Comptroller and Auditor-General** certifies net proceeds and the certificate is final. Every Finance Commission percentage is a percentage of net proceeds, not gross collections.

### 3. Article 280: composition, appointment and qualifications

*Objective:* State precisely how the Finance Commission is constituted and who may sit on it.

## Constitution and timing

Article 280 provides for a Finance Commission as a **quasi-judicial body**. 'Quasi-judicial' means it works like a court in method (hears parties, weighs evidence, gives reasoned recommendations) without being a court. The **President** constitutes it **every fifth year, or earlier if he considers it necessary**. Note the phrase 'or earlier': the five-year interval is a maximum gap, not a fixed calendar.

## Composition

A **chairman and four other members**, all appointed by the President. They hold office for the period specified in the President's order and are **eligible for reappointment**.

## Qualifications: Constitution delegates, Parliament specifies

The Constitution itself does not list qualifications. It authorises **Parliament** to determine the qualifications of members and the manner of their selection. Parliament did so through the **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951** (Laxmikanth's extract cites it as 'the Finance Commission Act, 1951'; the longer short title is standard usage and should be confirmed). Under it:

- The **chairman** must be a person with experience in **public affairs**.
- The **four members** are chosen from among: (1) a High Court judge or a person qualified to be one; (2) a person with specialised knowledge of government finance and accounts; (3) a person with wide experience in financial matters and administration; (4) a person with special knowledge of economics.

Memory hook: **J-A-F-E**: Judge, Accounts, Finance-administration, Economics.

## Reporting: Article 281

The Commission submits its report to the **President**, who lays it before **both Houses of Parliament** together with an **explanatory memorandum** stating the action taken on its recommendations. This is why you often read 'Action Taken Report' alongside a Finance Commission report.

## The chain of commissions

Laxmikanth's table lists fifteen commissions: the First under **K.C. Neogy** (appointed 1951, report 1952, period 1952-57) through the Fourteenth under **Y.V. Reddy** (appointed 2013, period 2015-2020) and the Fifteenth under **N.K. Singh** (appointed 2017, period 2020-2026). Other chairmen often asked: Santhanam (2nd), Chanda (3rd), **P.V. Rajamannar** (4th), Mahavir Tyagi (5th), Brahmananda Reddy (6th), Shelat (7th), Y.B. Chavan (8th), N.K.P. Salve (9th), K.C. Pant (10th), A.M. Khusro (11th), C. Rangarajan (12th), Vijay Kelkar (13th). The Sixteenth Commission is not covered by the sources read here. Standard reporting (verify against the official notification) is that it was constituted on 31 December 2023 under Arvind Panagariya, and that its award period is 2026-27 to 2030-31. As of October 2026 it, not the Fifteenth, is the current award period: do not treat the 15th Commission's shares or grants as the present arrangement. Its headline devolution share and terms of reference are unverified here.

## Common slip

Students write 'the Finance Commission is appointed every five years by Parliament'. Wrong on two counts: it is constituted by the **President**, and 'every fifth year **or earlier**'.

### 4. The four functions: devolution, grants, local bodies, anything else

*Objective:* Reproduce the Article 280 functions and connect each to vertical/horizontal devolution and grants.

## The text, in plain words

Article 280 asks the Commission to recommend to the President on:

1. **Distribution of net proceeds of shareable taxes** between the Union and the States, **and** the allocation among the States of their respective shares. This single clause contains both halves of module 1's chain: the Union-versus-States split (**vertical devolution**) and the State-by-State split (**horizontal distribution**).
2. **Principles governing grants-in-aid** to the States out of the Consolidated Fund of India (these become the Article 275 statutory grants of module 6).
3. **Measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State** so that the State can supplement the resources of its **panchayats and municipalities**, on the basis of the recommendations of the **State Finance Commission**. This function was **added by the 73rd and 74th Amendment Acts of 1992**, which gave constitutional status to panchayats and municipalities. Note the layering: a State Finance Commission advises the State on local bodies; the Union Finance Commission then advises how the State's own fund should be topped up for that purpose.
4. **Any other matter** referred by the President in the interests of sound finance. This is the open door through which terms of reference such as disaster-relief financing, debt consolidation or performance incentives enter.

## A historical fifth task

Till 1960 the Commission also suggested the grants to **Assam, Bihar, Odisha and West Bengal in lieu of export duty on jute and jute products** (Article 273). These were temporary (ten years from the commencement of the Constitution) and are now only of historical and Prelims interest.

## How horizontal distribution is actually decided

Article 280 gives no formula; each Commission designs one. The idea is simple even if the arithmetic is not: pick indicators of **need** (population, area), **capacity gap** (how far a State's income is from the richest, often called 'income distance') and sometimes **performance** (tax effort, forest cover, demographic management), weight them, and compute each State's percentage. Specific criteria and weights of the 14th and 15th Commissions are **not in the sources read for this draft**; a Prelims question in 2023 asked which criteria the 15th Commission used, so you will later need the official report (completeness row flags this).

**15th Commission criteria and weights (flagged: uncertain, verify against the official report before relying on them).** From standard reporting, the Fifteenth Commission's horizontal formula weighed: population 15 per cent; area 15 per cent; forest and ecology 10 per cent; income distance 45 per cent; demographic performance 12.5 per cent; tax and fiscal effort 2.5 per cent (total 100). The quantitative, measurable nature of these indicators is the key to the 2023 Prelims question (PYQ 2023 Q29): items that are not measurable indicators, such as 'governance reforms', are not criteria. Do not quote the weights in an answer until you have confirmed them in the report.

## A guided numerical illustration

Suppose net proceeds of shareable Union taxes are Rs 100. The Commission recommends 40% to States (vertical). Of the Rs 40, its formula gives State A (large, poor) 15% and State B (small, rich) 2%: A receives Rs 6, B receives Rs 0.80 (horizontal). After this, A's budget still shows a gap of Rs 2 for essential services, so the Commission recommends a Rs 2 **revenue-deficit grant** under Article 275. Three instruments, one chain.

## What the Commission does not do

It does not fix GST rates (GST Council), does not approve State plans (the former Planning Commission), does not audit (CAG) and does not itself transfer money (the Union government does, through the budget).

### 5. Distribution of tax revenues: Articles 268-271 after the 80th, 88th and 101st Amendments

*Objective:* Classify any Union tax into the correct constitutional category and explain what each amendment changed.

## Two amendments that rewrote the scheme

**80th Amendment Act, 2000.** It gave effect to the **Tenth Finance Commission**'s 'Alternative Scheme of Devolution': 29% of the total income from certain central taxes should go to the States, effective retrospectively from **1 April 1996**. Its structural effect was to bring Corporation Tax and Customs Duties 'at par' with income tax for constitutionally mandated sharing. Before this, only a few taxes were shareable; after it, nearly the whole Union tax basket became one **divisible pool**.

**88th Amendment Act, 2003** inserted Article 268A and Entry 92C (service tax levied by the Union, collected and appropriated by both). Both were **deleted by the 101st Amendment**, so Article 268A now appears as 'Repealed'.

**101st Amendment Act, 2016** introduced **GST**, gave concurrent GST powers, subsumed a long list of central levies (Central Excise, Additional Excise Duties, Service Tax, Countervailing Duty, Special Additional Duty of Customs, central surcharges and cesses on supplies) and State levies (State VAT/Sales Tax, Entertainment Tax other than local bodies', Central Sales Tax, Octroi and Entry Tax, Purchase Tax, Luxury Tax, taxes on lottery, betting and gambling, State surcharges and cesses on supplies). It created **Article 269A** and the **GST Council (Article 279A)**. Laxmikanth's Appendix IV (PDF p. 1407) records that the 101st Amendment 'made the provision of compensation to the states for loss of revenue arising on account of introduction of GST for a period of five years'. That sentence is the whole source-backed position; the detailed GST Council and compensation-cess mechanics are **not described in the sources read here** and are unverified.

*Dated, flagged note (verify against GST Council decisions and notifications).* In September 2025 the GST Council was reported to have approved a rationalisation of the rate structure towards two main slabs (5 and 18 per cent) with a higher rate for a short list of demerit goods, effective from late September 2025, and the compensation cess has been reported as phased out for most goods once the pandemic-era borrowing was repaid. Rates and cess details change frequently; use them only with an 'as of' date and an official check.

## The five categories today

| Article | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 268 | Levied by Union, **collected and appropriated by States** | Stamp duties on bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes, insurance policies, transfer of shares |
| 269 | Levied and collected by Union, **assigned to States** | Taxes on inter-State sale or purchase of goods (other than newspapers); taxes on inter-State consignment of goods |
| 269A | **GST on inter-State supply**: levied and collected by Union, **divided** between Union and States as Parliament provides on GST Council recommendation | Integrated GST |
| 270 | Levied and collected by Union, **distributed between Union and States** on Finance Commission recommendation (President prescribes) | All Union List taxes except those in 268, 269, 269A, surcharges under 271 and cesses for specific purposes |
| 271 | **Surcharge** for Union purposes on taxes in 269 and 270; **States get no share**; cannot be imposed on GST | Surcharges on income tax |

Proceeds under 268 and 269 do **not** enter the Consolidated Fund of India; they go straight to the States. Article 270 is the heart of the divisible pool and is where the Finance Commission's vertical and horizontal percentages operate.

## The exclusion that drives today's debates

Because **surcharges and cesses are outside the divisible pool**, a Union that raises revenue through them rather than basic rates shrinks what States receive even when the Finance Commission percentage is unchanged. Hold this point for Mains.

## Taxes the States keep entirely

The 18 State List taxes: land revenue; agricultural income tax; succession and estate duties on agricultural land; taxes on lands and buildings; mineral rights; excise on alcoholic liquor, opium and narcotics; electricity consumption or sale; sales tax on petroleum crude, diesel, petrol, natural gas, aviation turbine fuel and liquor (within the State); goods and passengers by road or inland waterways; vehicles; animals and boats; tolls; professions; capitation taxes; entertainment taxes levied by local bodies; stamp duty on documents not in the Union List; fees on State List matters except court fees.

## Non-tax revenue (one line each)

Union: posts, railways, banking, broadcasting, coinage and currency, central PSUs, escheat and lapse. States: irrigation, forests, fisheries, State PSUs, escheat and lapse.

### 6. Grants-in-aid: statutory (Article 275) versus discretionary (Article 282)

*Objective:* Distinguish the two constitutional grant routes and explain why the distinction matters for federalism.

## Why grants exist at all

Tax devolution is formula-driven and uniform in logic; it cannot guarantee that every State can balance its budget or that a specific purpose (tribal welfare, a cyclone) is funded. Grants fill these gaps. Laxmikanth classifies them as **statutory** and **discretionary**, with a third, now historical, category.

## Statutory grants: Article 275

- Parliament may make grants to States **'in need of financial assistance'**, **not to every State**, and **different sums for different States**.
- They are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** every year (no annual vote needed).
- Besides this general grant, Article 275 provides **specific grants** for promoting the welfare of **Scheduled Tribes** or raising the level of administration of **Scheduled Areas** in a State, **including Assam**.
- Both general and specific statutory grants are given **on the recommendation of the Finance Commission**. This is the link to function 2 of Article 280.

## Discretionary grants: Article 282

- Article 282 lets **both the Union and the States** make grants **for any public purpose, even outside their legislative competence**. Under it the Union makes grants to States.
- They are called **discretionary** because the Union is under **no obligation** to give them; the matter lies in its discretion.
- Laxmikanth quotes their two-fold purpose: to help States financially to fulfil plan targets, and to give the Centre **leverage to influence and coordinate State action** on the national plan. Historically this was the Planning Commission's channel, and it is the channel for centrally sponsored schemes.

## The third, temporary category: Article 273

Grants in lieu of export duty on jute and jute products to Assam, Bihar, Orissa and West Bengal for ten years from the commencement of the Constitution, charged on the CFI and given on Finance Commission recommendation. Prelims occasionally lists this as a distractor.

## The comparison you must be able to write

| Feature | Article 275 (statutory) | Article 282 (discretionary) |
|---|---|---|
| Who recommends | Finance Commission | No recommendation required |
| Who decides | Parliament, by law | Union executive, by its discretion |
| To whom | States in need; may differ by State | Any State, any public purpose |
| Legal character | Charged on CFI; an entitlement once recommended and enacted | No obligation; political leverage |
| Federal effect | Rule-based, predictable | Flexible but open to charges of favouritism |

## Why this matters for Mains

NCERT's complaint that the distribution of resources 'is considered lopsided' and invites charges of discrimination is really a complaint about Article 282 channels. The more transfers flow through Article 282 and tied schemes rather than Article 270 devolution and Article 275 grants, the weaker the Finance Commission's 'balancing wheel' role. Borrowing adds a further Union lever: under Articles 292-293 a State may borrow only within India, and needs the Union's consent to borrow while any Union loan or guarantee to it is outstanding.

### 7. Master view: advisory nature, NITI Aayog, GST Council and headline shares

*Objective:* Integrate the institution into its competitive landscape and evaluate how binding its advice is.

## Advisory, not binding

Laxmikanth is emphatic: the recommendations are **only advisory** and not binding; it is for the Union government to implement them. Quoting D.D. Basu, nothing in the Constitution says the recommendations bind the Government of India or create a legal right in favour of States. Yet Dr P.V. Rajamannar (Chairman, Fourth Commission) argued that, being a constitutional and quasi-judicial body, its recommendations should not be rejected without 'very compelling reasons'. In practice tax-devolution recommendations have been accepted; the contest is over grants and conditions. This tension (legally advisory, politically near-binding) is a ready-made Mains point.

## The Planning Commission problem, 1950-2014

Laxmikanth records that till 2014 the Finance Commission's role was **undermined by the Planning Commission**, a body that was **neither constitutional nor statutory**, which controlled plan transfers while the Finance Commission was confined to non-plan revenue needs. Rajamannar highlighted this **overlap of functions** in federal fiscal transfers. NCERT adds the political reading: planning 'led to considerable centralisation', and discretionary grants invited charges of discrimination against opposition-ruled States.

In **2015** the Planning Commission was replaced by **NITI Aayog** (National Institution for Transforming India), a think-tank without the power to allocate funds. Consequence: the plan/non-plan divide dissolved and the Finance Commission became, in effect, the single constitutional channel for formula-based transfers, strengthening its 'balancing wheel' status.

## The GST Council: a second constitutional forum

The **101st Amendment** created the **GST Council under Article 279A**, constituted by the President, a **joint forum of Union and States**. It recommends which taxes merge into GST, what is taxed or exempted, model laws and place-of-supply principles, the turnover threshold, rates including floor rates with bands, and special rates during natural calamities. Under Article 269A, inter-State GST is divided as Parliament provides **on the Council's recommendation**.

So two constitutional bodies now shape State revenue: the Council decides **how big the GST pie is and how it is apportioned between Union and States**; the Finance Commission decides **how the Union's divisible pool (including the Union's GST share) is devolved and which States get grants**. Coordination between them is an open institutional question that Mains examiners like.

## Headline devolution shares (flagged)

The Laxmikanth extract gives the **Tenth Commission's 29%** (via the 80th Amendment) but does **not** state the 14th or 15th Commission percentages. Standard reporting puts the **14th Commission (Y.V. Reddy) at 42%** of the divisible pool, a large jump justified as giving States untied money, and the **15th Commission (N.K. Singh) at 41%**, the one-point reduction reflecting Jammu and Kashmir's change from a State to Union Territories. These figures are **not verified against the sources read here**; use them only with that caveat until you read the official reports. Currency warning: the 15th Commission's award period ended with 2025-26. As of October 2026 the 16th Commission (award period 2026-27 to 2030-31) is the current one, and its headline share is not given in the sources read here; do not present the 41 per cent figure as today's devolution percentage, and do not quote any 16th Commission share until you have checked the official report.

## Emergencies

During a **National Emergency (Article 352)** the President may modify the constitutional distribution of revenues, reducing or cancelling transfers till the end of the financial year in which the emergency ends. During a **Financial Emergency (Article 360)** the Union may direct States to observe canons of financial propriety, cut salaries and reserve money bills for the President.

### 8. Examination application: how UPSC frames this topic

*Objective:* Recognise the recurring question patterns and apply elimination and structuring techniques.

## Prelims patterns (from the verified PYQs)

1. **Recommendation-fact questions.** 2023 asked how many of five listed items the Fifteenth Commission used as horizontal criteria beyond population, area and income distance; 2025 gave four statements about Fifteenth Commission recommendations including a percentage share of net proceeds. Technique: anchor on the one fact you know hardest. If you are confident the 15th Commission's share is 41% (flagged as unverified here), then a statement claiming '45%' cannot stand and every option containing it falls. Such questions reward knowing **one solid anchor**, not all four statements.
2. **Institutional confusion.** Statements that give Finance Commission work to the GST Council or NITI Aayog, or vice versa (module 7).
3. **Article-category questions.** 'Which of the following taxes is levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States?' (module 5 table).
4. **Binding/advisory and composition traps.** 'Constituted by Parliament', 'recommendations binding', 'every five years' without 'or earlier'.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Finance Commission: a quasi-judicial constitutional body under Article 280, constituted by the President every fifth year or earlier, to recommend distribution of shareable taxes, principles of grants-in-aid and measures for local bodies.
- Vertical devolution: the share of the Union's divisible tax pool that goes to the States as a group.
- Horizontal distribution: the formula that splits the States' share among individual States.
- Net proceeds: proceeds of a tax minus the cost of collection, certified by the CAG (Article 279).
- Divisible pool: the Article 270 taxes whose net proceeds are shared; excludes Articles 268, 269, 269A, surcharges (271) and cesses for specific purposes.
- Grants-in-aid: non-repayable transfers from the Union to States; statutory under Article 275, discretionary under Article 282.

### Notes: Articles at a glance

- Article 268: duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States (stamp duties on bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes, insurance policies, share transfers).
- Article 269: taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to States (inter-State sale/purchase and consignment of goods).
- Article 269A: GST on inter-State supply, divided between Union and States on GST Council recommendation.
- Article 270: taxes levied and collected by the Union and distributed between Union and States on Finance Commission recommendation.
- Article 271: surcharge for Union purposes; States get no share; cannot be levied on GST.
- Article 273: grants in lieu of jute export duty (temporary, ten years).
- Article 274: President's prior recommendation for bills affecting taxation in which States are interested.
- Article 275: statutory grants to States in need, charged on the CFI, on Finance Commission recommendation; specific grants for Scheduled Tribes and Scheduled Areas including Assam.
- Article 276: taxes on professions, trades, callings and employments (ceiling Rs 2,500 per annum).
- Article 279: calculation of net proceeds; Article 279A: GST Council.
- Article 280: Finance Commission; Article 281: recommendations laid before Parliament with explanatory memorandum.
- Article 282: expenditure defrayable by Union or State for any public purpose (discretionary grants).
- Articles 292-293: borrowing by the Union and by States.

### Notes: Amendments that changed the scheme

- 73rd and 74th Amendment Acts, 1992: added the local-bodies function to Article 280 (augmenting a State's Consolidated Fund for panchayats and municipalities on State Finance Commission recommendations).
- 80th Amendment Act, 2000: gave effect to the Tenth Finance Commission's Alternative Scheme of Devolution (29% of certain central taxes to States, retrospective from 1 April 1996); brought corporation tax and customs at par with income tax for sharing.
- 88th Amendment Act, 2003: inserted Article 268A and Entry 92C (service tax); both later deleted.
- 101st Amendment Act, 2016: introduced GST, concurrent GST powers, Article 269A, GST Council under Article 279A; deleted Article 268A and Entry 92C.

### Notes: Statutory versus discretionary grants

- Article 275 grants are rule-based and recommended by the Finance Commission; Article 282 grants are at the Union's discretion for any public purpose.

### Notes: Finance Commission versus GST Council versus NITI Aayog

- Three bodies, three different legal bases and jobs; Prelims swaps them deliberately.

### Notes: Cause and effect chains

- Union holds elastic taxes while States hold spending duties -> vertical imbalance -> need for devolution.
- States differ in income and population -> horizontal imbalance -> need for a distribution formula and gap grants.
- Planning Commission controlled plan transfers -> Finance Commission confined to non-plan needs -> overlap criticised by Rajamannar -> 2015 NITI Aayog ends allocative overlap.
- Surcharges and cesses excluded from divisible pool -> Union can raise revenue without sharing -> States' effective share falls even at an unchanged percentage.

### Notes: Exceptions and fine print

- 'Every fifth year or earlier': the President may constitute a Commission before five years.
- Article 275 grants are for States in need, not for every State.
- Surcharge under Article 271 cannot be levied on GST.
- Proceeds under Articles 268 and 269 never enter the Consolidated Fund of India.
- State property and income are immune from Union taxation, but the Supreme Court (advisory opinion, 1963) held this does not extend to customs and excise duties.
- Union corporations and companies are not immune from State taxation because they are separate legal entities.

### Notes: Commonly confused pairs

- Finance Commission (Article 280) versus State Finance Commission (set up by States under the 73rd/74th Amendments for local bodies).
- Assigned to States (Article 269) versus distributed between Union and States (Article 270).
- Statutory grant (Article 275) versus discretionary grant (Article 282).
- Advisory recommendation (Finance Commission) versus binding law (Parliament's Finance Act).
- Gross proceeds versus net proceeds (minus cost of collection, certified by CAG).

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** What is a Consolidated Fund?
    - **a:** The main account of a government into which all its revenues and borrowings flow and from which all its expenditure is drawn; the Union has the Consolidated Fund of India and each State has its own.
  - - **q:** What is the difference between a grant and a loan?
    - **a:** A grant need not be repaid; a loan must be repaid, usually with interest.
  - - **q:** Which list contains the taxes that States keep entirely?
    - **a:** The State List of the Seventh Schedule (18 tax entries such as land revenue, excise on liquor, taxes on vehicles).
  - - **q:** What does 'quasi-judicial' mean?
    - **a:** Working like a court in method (hearing parties, weighing evidence, reasoned decisions) without being a court.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why does the Constitution separate the power to levy a tax from the power to appropriate its proceeds?
    - **a:** So that taxes best collected nationally (for uniformity and efficiency) can still fund States; the separation creates shared proceeds, which in turn requires a neutral body to decide the shares.
  - - **q:** Explain how excluding cesses and surcharges from the divisible pool can reduce States' effective share even if the Finance Commission percentage is unchanged.
    - **a:** The percentage applies only to the divisible pool; if the Union raises more revenue through cesses and surcharges (outside the pool) instead of basic tax rates, the pool grows more slowly than total Union revenue, so States receive a smaller fraction of total collections.
  - - **q:** Why does Laxmikanth say the Planning Commission 'undermined' the Finance Commission?
    - **a:** Because a non-constitutional, non-statutory body controlled a large part of transfers (plan grants) at its discretion, leaving the constitutional Finance Commission a narrower, non-plan role and creating overlapping jurisdictions.
  - - **q:** In what sense are Finance Commission recommendations 'advisory yet nearly binding'?
    - **a:** Legally nothing compels the Union to accept them (D.D. Basu's view), but as a constitutional quasi-judicial body its advice is conventionally accepted, especially on tax devolution (Rajamannar's view).
