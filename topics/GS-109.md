## GS-109: Salinity, tides and marine ecosystems

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Per cent, per mille and density

A **ratio** compares two amounts. **Per cent** means parts in each hundred, so 3.5 per cent is 3.5 parts in 100. **Per mille** (written with the symbol ‰) means parts in each thousand, so 35 per mille is 35 parts in 1,000. To convert per mille to per cent divide by ten: 35 / 10 = 3.5. Salinity uses per mille because seawater holds a small amount of salt. 35 per mille means 35 grams of salt in every 1,000 grams of seawater. **Density** is mass divided by volume: how much matter is packed into a given space. A litre of salty water has more mass than a litre of fresh water, so it is denser, and denser liquid sinks beneath lighter liquid. **Evaporation** is liquid water turning to vapour; **precipitation** is rain, snow or hail falling to the surface; **insolation** is incoming sunlight. Keep these words in view whenever the lesson says 'evaporation exceeds precipitation'.

### Bridge: Gravity, orbits and the Moon's phases

**Gravity** is the pull that any two masses exert on each other; it is stronger when the masses are bigger or closer. The Earth goes around the Sun in a path called an **orbit**, and the Moon orbits the Earth. An orbit is not a perfect circle, so the distance changes. The Moon's closest point to the Earth is **perigee** and its farthest is **apogee**. The Earth's closest point to the Sun (around 3 January) is **perihelion** and its farthest (around 4 July) is **aphelion**. The **new moon** is when the Moon lies between the Earth and the Sun, so we see its dark side. The **full moon** is when the Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon. At both of these the three bodies are in a nearly straight line. At the **quarter moons** the Sun and Moon are at right angles as seen from the Earth. **Centrifugal force** is the apparent outward push felt by a body moving around a centre. Tides are explained by these ideas in module 6.

### Bridge: Producers, consumers, plankton and symbiosis

**Photosynthesis** is the process in which green plants, algae and some bacteria use sunlight, water and carbon dioxide to make food and release oxygen. An organism that makes its own food this way is a **producer**. An organism that eats other organisms is a **consumer**. A **food chain** is the sequence 'who eats whom', starting from a producer. **Plankton** means tiny organisms that drift with the water; the plant-like ones are **phytoplankton** and the animal-like ones are **zooplankton**. **Algae** are simple plant-like organisms, many of them microscopic. **Symbiosis** is a close partnership in which two different organisms live together and both benefit; coral polyps and the algae inside them are the standard example. **Biodiversity** means the variety of species in a place. **Oxygen** dissolved in water is what fish breathe, and its absence is called hypoxia. These words reappear in modules 7 to 9.

## The one-page map

### 2. Foundation: what salinity is and the reference numbers

*Objective:* Define salinity, convert per mille to per cent, and recall the reference salinities of the main seas and salt lakes with their book sources.

## Salinity in one sentence

**Salinity** is the total amount of dissolved salts in seawater. NCERT defines it as the grams of salt dissolved in 1,000 grams (1 kg) of seawater and writes it in **parts per thousand**, symbol ‰, read "per mille". Open-ocean water at 35‰ therefore holds 35 g of salt in every 1,000 g of seawater. As a percentage that is 35 / 1,000 x 100 = 3.5 per cent. Because salinity is a ratio of two masses, a bigger sample gives the same number.

## What the salt is

Every natural water, rain and river water included, carries some dissolved salt. In seawater, common salt (sodium chloride) alone makes up more than 77 per cent of the salts according to Leong, so in 35‰ water about 27 g of each 1,000 g is sodium chloride (0.77 x 35 = 26.95). Magnesium, calcium and potassium come next, and the rest are traces. Leong adds an important point: ocean water mixes freely, so the proportions of the different salts stay almost constant everywhere, even in deep water. What changes from place to place is the **concentration**, not the recipe.

## Reference numbers, book by book

NCERT puts normal open-ocean water at 33 to 37‰ (Leong: average 35.2‰), the Indian Ocean at 35‰ and the Atlantic at about 36‰. The Red Sea is as high as 41‰ in NCERT and about 39‰ in Leong; the Baltic Sea is about 7‰ (Leong); the Black Sea is very low because of huge river inflow (NCERT). Salt lakes are far higher: the Dead Sea 238‰ (Leong records 250‰), the Great Salt Lake 220‰ and Lake Van (Turkey) 330‰. The full table is in the master notes.

NCERT also says that in hot, dry regions with strong evaporation salinity can reach 70‰, and that estuaries and the Arctic swing seasonally between 0 and 35‰. Remember the ordering, not the decimals: Baltic and Black Sea low, normal ocean about 35, Red Sea and Mediterranean higher, salt lakes extreme.

## A flagged slip

Leong prints 180‰ for the Caspian Sea. Standard references put the Caspian at roughly 12‰ (verify), so do not memorise the Leong figure. It is a reminder that a single book can slip; the lesson marks such cases.

## Three words to own

- **Brackish water**: water saltier than fresh water but less salty than normal sea. NCERT treats 24.7‰ as the upper limit.
- **Isohaline**: a line on a salinity map joining places with equal salinity (Leong), like an isotherm for temperature.
- **Salt lake**: an inland water body with no outlet to the ocean. Lake Van, the Dead Sea and the Great Salt Lake are salt lakes, not oceans, which is why their values are so far above 35‰.

### 3. Core: what makes salinity rise and fall

*Objective:* Explain the controls on surface salinity as a balance between water removed and fresh water added, and apply them to a new sea.

## The balance idea

The salt in the sea is almost fixed. What changes surface salinity is the **water**. Remove water and the same salt is packed into less water, so salinity rises. Add fresh water and it falls. NCERT states the rule: surface salinity depends mainly on evaporation and precipitation, and the surface rises with the loss of water to ice or evaporation and falls with fresh water such as river inflow.

A computed example. Take 1,000 g of seawater at 35‰: it holds 35 g of salt and 965 g of water.

- Evaporate 50 g of pure water: total mass 950 g, salt still 35 g, salinity = 35 / 950 x 1,000 = 36.8‰.
- Add 50 g of river water: total 1,050 g, salinity = 35 / 1,050 x 1,000 = 33.3‰.
- Evaporate 100 g: 38.9‰. Add 100 g: 31.8‰.

The salt never changed; only the water did.

## The controls

1. **Evaporation.** The waters beside the high-pressure trade-wind desert belts, between 20 and 30 degrees north and south, are saltier because of high temperature and low humidity (Leong). Cooler temperate oceans evaporate less.
2. **Precipitation and rivers.** Equatorial waters are below the average because of heavy daily rain. Big rivers such as the Amazon, Congo, Ganges, Irrawaddy and Mekong dilute the sea where they enter (Leong); NCERT adds the same for coastal regions.
3. **Ice.** In polar regions freezing removes water from the liquid (raising salinity locally) and thawing adds it. Leong notes that Baltic, Arctic and Antarctic waters are below 32‰ because of little evaporation and fresh water from melting ice and from large poleward-flowing rivers.
4. **Wind.** NCERT says winds change salinity by moving water to other areas.
5. **Currents and mixing.** The North Sea, though at high latitude, is saline because the North Atlantic Drift brings saline water (NCERT). In enclosed or partly enclosed seas such as the Caspian, Mediterranean, Red Sea and Persian Gulf, water does not mix freely with the ocean, so salinity is often over 37‰; in the open ocean where currents mix freely the range is small, near 35‰ (Leong).
6. **Temperature and density** interact with salinity; module 5 treats them.

## A common error

The equator is not the saltiest place. Heat alone does not decide salinity; the balance of evaporation against rain and rivers does.

### 4. Core: where the ocean is salty and where it is fresh

*Objective:* Describe the horizontal pattern of salinity by latitude, ocean and enclosed sea, explain the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea contrast, and describe the vertical profile.

## The pattern by latitude

Take the world's open oceans. Salinity is lowest near the poles (little evaporation, melting ice), rises to a maximum in the subtropical belts around 20 to 30 degrees on each side of the equator (strong evaporation), dips again at the equator (heavy rain) and falls toward higher latitudes. Leong's world map note says the highest salinity is found in enclosed seas and in those adjoining the hot deserts along the tropics. NCERT's sentences on the Atlantic are slightly uneven: it gives the highest zone as 15 to 20 degrees and then a maximum of 37‰ between 20 and 30 degrees north and between 20 and 60 degrees west. The safe summary is "subtropical belts, roughly 15 to 30 degrees".

## The three oceans (NCERT)

- **Pacific.** Variation comes mainly from its shape and large area. In the western part of the northern hemisphere salinity falls from 35 to 31‰ because of melted water from the Arctic region; after about 15 to 20 degrees south it falls to 33‰.
- **Atlantic.** Average about 36‰, with the maximum described above; it decreases gradually toward the north.
- **Indian.** Average 35‰. The Bay of Bengal is low because of the influx of river water, while the Arabian Sea is higher because of high evaporation and low fresh-water influx.

## Seas that break the pattern

The **Red Sea** is very saline (41‰ NCERT; 39‰ Leong) because it is land-locked with high evaporation and few rivers. The **Mediterranean** is high because of high evaporation (NCERT) and poor mixing (Leong). The **North Sea** is saltier than its latitude suggests because the saline North Atlantic Drift flows in (NCERT). The **Baltic Sea** (about 7‰, Leong) and the **Black Sea** (very low, NCERT) are fresh because of heavy river inflow, melting ice and low evaporation. The sea-by-sea table is in the master notes.

For the Red Sea question in Prelims 2024, the first statement (very little rain) matches the evaporation story. For the second statement (no water enters from rivers) the extracts only say "few rivers"; the standard geographical position is that no permanent river flows into the Red Sea (verify). Leong's wording "fewer rivers" is therefore consistent but weaker.

## The vertical profile (NCERT)

Salinity changes with depth, but the pattern depends on the sea. At the surface, evaporation or loss of water to ice raises salinity and river water lowers it. At depth salinity is nearly fixed, because no water is lost and no salt added there. Normally lower-salinity water rests above the higher-salinity, denser water, so salinity generally increases with depth. The zone where salinity rises sharply with depth is the **halocline**. Increasing salinity raises density when other factors are constant, so salty water sinks below fresher water and the water becomes layered, which is called stratification by salinity.

### 5. Core: temperature, salinity and density together

*Objective:* Explain how temperature and salinity set density, describe the thermocline and the three-layer ocean, and show how density differences drive currents and bring life to mixing zones.

## Density in plain words

**Density** is mass per unit volume. Two things raise the density of seawater: more salt and lower temperature. NCERT says salinity, temperature and density are interrelated, that high-salinity water is denser than low-salinity water, that cold water is denser than warm water, and that denser water tends to sink while lighter water rises. So: cold and salty is densest and sinks; warm and fresh is lightest and floats. Leong's Dead Sea and Lake Van remark follows: the water is so dense that a swimmer floats easily.

## Surface temperature: what controls it

NCERT lists four factors. **Latitude**: insolation (incoming sunlight) falls toward the poles. **Land and water distribution**: northern-hemisphere oceans touch more land and are warmer; NCERT gives average annual temperatures of about 19 and 16 degrees Celsius for the northern and southern hemispheres. **Prevailing winds**: winds from the land push warm surface water away from the coast, so cold water rises from below (upwelling), while onshore winds pile up warm water. **Currents**: warm currents raise and cold currents lower temperature, for example the Gulf Stream and the North Atlantic Drift keep the Norwegian coast ice-free at 60 to 70 degrees north (Leong), whereas the Labrador Current chills north-eastern North America.

The books use different numbers (NCERT: about 27 degrees average, 22 at 20 degrees latitude, 14 at 40 and about 0 near the poles; Leong: about 21 at the equator and 13 at 45 degrees). Treat the pattern (warm tropics, cold poles, water changing temperature more slowly than land) as the exam knowledge; Leong adds that the annual range is under 5.5 degrees for most open surface water.

## Temperature with depth

Heat enters at the surface and moves down slowly by convection, so temperature decreases with depth, rapidly down to about 200 m and then more slowly (NCERT). In low and middle latitudes NCERT describes three layers: a warm surface layer about 500 m thick at 20 to 25 degrees, a thermocline layer 500 to 1,000 m thick, and a very cold layer to the floor. NCERT also says the thermocline boundary begins around 100 to 400 m below the surface; the two statements sit uneasily together, so quote each in its own context (the 500 m figure for the three-layer description, 100 to 400 m for where the thermocline starts) and do not try to reconcile them (verify against the NCERT text). About 90 per cent of ocean water lies below the thermocline. In the Arctic and Antarctic circles the surface is near 0 degrees, so only one cold layer exists. Leong adds that more than 80 per cent of ocean water lies between 2 and 4 degrees. A related term, **pycnocline**, is the layer where density changes sharply; it is standard vocabulary but not in these extracts (verify).

## Density at work: currents

Density differences move water vertically. Cold polar water sinks and creeps toward the equator while warm water flows poleward at the surface (NCERT; Leong). The Mediterranean is the textbook case in Leong: the Atlantic's less saline surface water flows in, balanced by an outflow of denser bottom water. NCERT classifies currents by depth: surface currents (the upper 400 m, about 10 per cent of ocean water) and deep currents (about 90 per cent).

## Link to life

The mixing of warm and cold currents replenishes oxygen and favours plankton, so the best fishing grounds are in such mixing zones (NCERT; module 7). A strong thermocline or halocline also stops surface and deep water from mixing, which matters for dead zones (module 9).

### 6. Core: tides, their causes, types and uses

*Objective:* Define a tide, explain the two bulges, distinguish spring and neap tides and the frequency types, and list the importance of tides including the Indian example in the Prelims 2017 question.

## What a tide is

A **tide** is the periodical rise and fall of the sea level, once or twice a day, mainly due to the attraction of the Sun and the Moon (NCERT). Do not confuse it with other movements: **waves** move energy across the surface, **currents** move water along a path, and **surges** are movements of water caused by weather (wind and pressure changes), which are not regular like tides. Tides are the vertical movement; currents and waves are horizontal.

## Cause: two bulges

The Moon's gravitational pull is the main cause and the Sun's a lesser one. A second force, the **centrifugal force** that counterbalances gravity in the Earth-Moon system, matters too. On the side of the Earth facing the Moon, the Moon's pull exceeds the centrifugal force, producing a bulge toward the Moon. On the far side the Moon's pull is weaker because it is farther away, so the centrifugal force dominates and a second bulge forms away from the Moon. The "tide-generating force" is the difference between the two forces. NCERT adds that the horizontal components matter more than the vertical ones in building the bulges.

## Types of tides

- **By frequency**: semi-diurnal (two highs and two lows a day of about equal height, the commonest), diurnal (one high and one low a day) and mixed (successive tides of unequal height, along the west coast of North America and many Pacific islands).
- **By position of Sun and Moon**: spring tides (new and full moon, higher highs) and neap tides (about a week after, when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles). NCERT notes that the Moon's pull is more than twice the Sun's, but at neap the Sun's pull partly cancels it.
- **Perigee and apogee**: when the Moon is nearest the Earth (perigee) tidal ranges are unusually large; when farthest (apogee) they are less than average.
- **Perihelion and aphelion**: when the Earth is nearest the Sun, around 3 January, tidal ranges are greater; around 4 July, when farthest, they are smaller.

**Ebb** is the falling tide from high to low; **flood** (or flow) is the rising tide.

## Local shape matters

Tidal bulges are higher on wide continental shelves, lower when they hit mid-ocean islands, and magnified by funnel-shaped bays and estuaries. The Bay of Fundy in Canada has the highest tides, with a range of 15 to 16 m. NCERT's rough rate: 1,440 cm of rise over about 6 hours is 240 cm per hour (1,440 / 6 = 240). A tide channelled between islands or into bays is a **tidal current**.

## Why tides matter

How tides act on coasts as an agent of erosion and deposition is in Day 17 lesson GS-102 module 2. Tide times can be predicted because Earth-Moon-Sun positions are known, helping navigators and fishermen. Tidal height matters for harbours near rivers or estuaries with shallow bars; tides help desilt sediments and flush polluted water out of estuaries; and they generate power in Canada, France, Russia and China. NCERT mentions a 3 MW tidal power project at Durgaduani in the Sunderbans of West Bengal as "under way"; this is a dated statement (verify the present status).

## An Indian example

Prelims 2017 describes a place where the sea recedes a few kilometres twice a day and one can walk on the sea floor. The standard answer is Chandipur in Odisha, which has a wide tidal flat (standard knowledge, not in these extracts; verify). The cause is a very gently sloping shore, so a small vertical fall of the tide exposes a very wide strip. Day 17 lesson GS-130 places Chandipur on the Odisha coast.

### 7. Core: life in the sea, productivity and the ocean as a carbon sink

*Objective:* Explain who makes food in the ocean, where marine productivity is highest and why, and describe the ocean's role as a carbon sink.

## Who makes food in the sea

An **ecosystem** is a community of living things and their physical surroundings. In the sea the food chain starts with **plankton**, tiny drifting organisms. The plant-like ones, **phytoplankton**, photosynthesise (use sunlight to make food) and are the **primary producers**. The animal-like ones, **zooplankton**, eat them. Diatoms and cyanobacteria (photosynthetic bacteria) are producers. Copepods (tiny crustaceans) and foraminifera (single-celled shelled animals) are consumers; this classification is standard knowledge, not in these extracts (verify). Prelims 2021 asked exactly this selection.

Sunlight reaches only the upper layer of the sea, so life depends on shallow, lit water and on nutrients brought up from below.

## Where productivity is highest

1. **Continental shelves.** Leong gives these reasons: shallow water lets sunlight penetrate, encouraging minute plants and microscopic animals; so shelves are rich in plankton and are the richest fishing grounds, such as the Grand Banks off Newfoundland, the North Sea and the Sunda Shelf. Shelves form about 7 per cent of the area of the oceans (Leong), with a common width of 32 to 160 km; NCERT gives an average width of about 80 km.
2. **Mixing zones of currents.** The mixing of warm and cold currents replenishes oxygen and favours plankton (NCERT). Fog, fish and rain patterns follow the same current boundaries.
3. **Upwelling.** Where winds blow off the land, they push warm surface water away and cold water rises from below (NCERT). Leong's example is the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current: the region is rich in microscopic marine plants and animals, which attract shoals of fish and millions of sea birds whose droppings form guano, a valuable fertiliser.

## Zones of the sea (standard vocabulary, verify)

The **intertidal** (littoral) zone lies between the high-tide and low-tide marks; the **neritic** zone is the sunlit water over the continental shelf; the **oceanic** zone is the open sea beyond the shelf; the **benthic** zone is the sea floor and deep water. The names are not in the extracts and are used only as labels for stated ideas (the shelf is rich, deep water is cold); the table is in the master notes.

## Oxygen and the carbon sink

A claim often repeated, and tested in Prelims 2025, is that marine phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria produce about half of the world's oxygen (standard knowledge, verify the exact share). The sea is also the planet's largest active **carbon sink**: PMF IAS gives about 25 to 30 per cent of human-caused carbon dioxide emissions absorbed by the ocean each year. **Blue carbon** is carbon captured by ocean and coastal ecosystems such as seagrasses, mangroves and salt marshes. PMF adds that these coastal systems, though far smaller than forests, store carbon faster and for millions of years, and release a great deal when damaged. The same absorption **acidifies** the water, which harms corals (module 8).

## Currents, water masses and life (Mains 2019 prompt)

The definitions of a current and a water mass, and the contrast between them, are taught in Day 17 lesson GS-102 module 8. The marine-life angle belongs here: currents carry heat, oxygen and nutrients to coasts; plankton and fish thrive where warm and cold currents mix and where cold water upwells, as off Peru; and water masses such as the deep cold layer of module 5 are the stores of oxygen, heat and nutrients that upwelling returns to the surface (standard; verify).

### 8. Core: coral reefs, zooxanthellae, bleaching and acidification

*Objective:* Describe what coral reefs are, the conditions they need, how bleaching and acidification damage them, and the Indian reef areas.

## What a coral reef is

A **coral polyp** is a tiny soft-bodied animal of the phylum Cnidaria, a relative of sea anemones and jellyfish. It extracts calcium salts from seawater to build a hard calcium carbonate skeleton. Polyps live in colonies; when they die, their skeleton remains and new polyps grow on it, and over very long periods the layers form a **coral reef** (PMF IAS). Leong adds that when polyps die their limy skeletons cement into coralline limestone. The reef types (fringing, barrier, atoll), Darwin's and Daly's theories, and the Great Barrier Reef's length (Leong 1,930 km; PMF IAS about 2,300 km; verify) are treated as landforms in Day 17 lesson GS-102 module 6.

## Conditions needed (six)

- **Warmth**: water not below about 20 degrees Celsius (Leong; PMF IAS "around 20"), which confines reefs to the tropics, roughly 30 degrees north to 30 degrees south (PMF IAS).
- **Cold currents absent**: reefs are generally missing on west coasts of continents because cold upwelling cools the water; a warm current such as the Gulf Stream allows corals farther north (Leong).
- **Light and shallow depth**: sunlight must be enough for photosynthesis. Leong says reef-building coral lives in water shallower than about 55 m (30 fathoms), ideally under 30 m; PMF IAS gives 45 to 55 m as ideal. The books differ; remember "shallow, sunlit, within about 55 m".
- **Clear, salty water**: free from sediment. PMF IAS says freshwater and highly saline water are both harmful, and Leong says reefs do best away from muddy river mouths.
- **Plankton and oxygen**: moving seaward water brings food and oxygen, so growth is strongest seaward.
- **Low pollution**: reefs are fragile.

## Why reefs are called rainforests of the sea

Reefs carry very high productivity and biodiversity because of tight resource recycling, so PMF IAS calls them the "Tropical Rainforests of the Oceans". The key is a partnership. **Zooxanthellae** are single-celled photosynthetic algae (dinoflagellates) living inside the polyp's tissue. They give the coral fixed carbon compounds for energy and its colour; the polyp gives them shelter and carbon dioxide. Corals feed by day through the algae and by night by catching plankton.

## Bleaching

When corals are stressed they expel the zooxanthellae. Without the algae the tissue becomes transparent and the white skeleton shows. Corals commonly lose 60 to 90 per cent of their algae when they bleach. If stress is mild and short, the coral regains algae within weeks or months; if prolonged, the coral dies.

Causes (PMF IAS): small temperature rises over weeks or large changes of 3 to 4 degrees over a few days; high ultraviolet radiation in calm, clear, low-wind seas; sudden cold drops with intense upwelling; **subaerial exposure** (sudden exposure to air at extreme low tides, El Nino sea-level drops or uplift); chemical contaminants such as copper, herbicides and oil; and, less directly, nutrient excess and disease. Coral disease, which leaves a white skeleton after tissue sloughs off, is not the same as bleaching.

## Acidification

The ocean absorbs carbon dioxide, which makes the water more acidic. PMF IAS summarises: rising temperature raises the frequency and intensity of bleaching, while acidification reduces corals' ability to **calcify** (build their skeleton).

## Global events and India

PMF IAS reports repeated global bleaching events (1998, 2010, 2014 to 2017 and a fourth); the percentages are in the master notes. India's major reef areas are the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, the Gulf of Mannar and the Gulf of Kachchh; Lakshadweep is itself a reef-built island group (Day 17 lesson GS-130). The Zoological Survey of India has tried restoring reef in the Gulf of Kachchh with **biorock**, calcium carbonate deposited on steel frames carrying a small electric current (PMF IAS).

### 9. Master: sea surface temperature, dead zones, oil pollution and ocean governance

*Objective:* Link sea surface temperature to cyclones, explain how dead zones form and what they do, outline oil pollution and the IMO, and build cause-effect chains for Mains answers.

## Sea surface temperature and cyclones (Mains 2024)

**Sea surface temperature (SST)** is the temperature of the top layer of the sea. "Sea surface temperature rise" means the long-term warming of that layer, driven by global warming. The extracts give the surface temperature background (module 5) but not the cyclone link, which is standard knowledge to verify: a tropical cyclone draws its energy from warm ocean water, and forms only where the surface is warm (a common threshold is about 26 to 27 degrees Celsius over a deep layer). Warmer seas can therefore intensify cyclones, allow rapid intensification, raise rainfall and storm-surge risk, and widen the season or the area of formation; whether total numbers rise is less certain. NCERT chapter 12 contributes the oceanic side: onshore winds pile up warm water and warm currents raise temperatures, both of which feed heat to a coast. PMF IAS's coral material shows another effect of the same warming: bleaching.

## Dead zones (Mains 2018)

A **dead zone** is an area of the sea with so little dissolved oxygen (hypoxia) that most animals cannot live there. The usual chain (standard knowledge; PMF IAS names nutrient overload and eutrophication as stresses on reefs) is in the diagram: fertiliser and sewage nutrients enter rivers, a surface algal bloom follows, the dead bloom is decomposed by bacteria that consume oxygen, and layered water prevents mixing. Consequences for the marine ecosystem: fish and bottom animals flee or die, fisheries and food chains collapse, bottom habitats are lost, biodiversity falls and the ecosystem shifts toward algae and jellyfish. Large known examples include coastal waters off the Mississippi delta and parts of the Baltic Sea (verify). Remedies: reduce nutrient run-off, treat sewage, restore wetlands and mangroves.

## Oil pollution (Mains 2023)

**Oil pollution** is the release of crude oil or oil products into the sea by tanker accidents, offshore wells, leaking pipelines and ship washing. Impacts: oil films block light and air exchange, harm plankton, coat the feathers and fur of birds and mammals, poison fish and shellfish, smother intertidal habitats, mangroves and mud flats, and raise chemical stress on corals (PMF IAS lists oil among bleaching triggers). For India the harm is sharper because of the 7,517 km total coastline (NCERT Class XI, India: Location), busy tanker routes into Gujarat and Mumbai-area ports, and large coastal fishing and tourism livelihoods (verify the route detail). Response: contingency planning, ship-pollution rules under international conventions and the Coast Guard's role.

## The IMO (Mains 2023 GS II)

The **International Maritime Organization (IMO)** is the United Nations specialised agency for the safety and security of shipping and the prevention of marine pollution by ships (standard knowledge; verify the details). Its best-known conventions: SOLAS (safety of life at sea) and MARPOL (pollution from ships). An answer should link the quoted statement, that the sea is an important component of the cosmos, to ocean climate regulation, food and trade, then to the IMO's safety, security and environmental role and its limits (it makes rules; states enforce them).

## FAO and the sea (Prelims 2026)

## Prelims traps

- **Salinity**: the highest salinity is not at the equator; it follows evaporation minus rain, so deserts and enclosed seas win. A statement of the form "X sea receives no river water" needs a positive reason before you accept or reject it.
- **Primary producers**: the list is cyanobacteria and diatoms among the four; copepods and foraminifera are consumers (verify). The trap is the tiny size, which tempts you to count every plankton as a producer.
- **Oxygen**: water holds far less oxygen than air, so a claim that surface water holds several times more is hard to defend; marine phytoplankton are commonly credited with about half of the world's oxygen (verify; analysis, not the official key).
- **Tide wording**: spring tides come at new and full moon, not at the quarters; neap tides are the weak ones; "twice a day" is the semi-diurnal pattern.
- **Coral**: bleached is not dead; acidification affects calcification, not colour; zooxanthellae are algae, not coral polyps.
- **Option-swapping**: in the FAO question, one word in an otherwise correct set is changed. Memorise the full set.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Salinity: grams of dissolved salt per 1,000 g of seawater, written in per mille (parts per thousand) (NCERT).
- Brackish water: NCERT treats 24.7 per mille as the upper limit.
- Isohaline: a line joining places of equal salinity (Leong).
- Halocline: the zone where salinity increases sharply with depth (NCERT).
- Thermocline: the boundary region where temperature drops rapidly with depth, starting about 100 to 400 m below the surface (NCERT).
- Tide: the periodical rise and fall of the sea level, once or twice a day, mainly due to the pull of the Sun and the Moon (NCERT).
- Surge: movement of water caused by weather (wind and pressure changes); not regular like tides (NCERT).
- Coral bleaching: expulsion of zooxanthellae from coral tissue under stress, exposing the white skeleton (PMF IAS).
- Blue carbon: carbon captured by the ocean and coastal ecosystems such as seagrass, mangroves and salt marsh (PMF IAS).

### Notes: Ocean temperature facts

- Water heats and cools more slowly than land; annual range under 5.5 degrees Celsius for most open surface water (Leong).
- Four factors on surface temperature: latitude, land-water distribution, prevailing winds, ocean currents (NCERT).
- Thermocline begins about 100 to 400 m below the surface; about 90 per cent of ocean volume lies below it, near 0 to 4 degrees (NCERT; Leong).
- Three-layer system in low and middle latitudes: warm layer about 500 m at 20 to 25 degrees, thermocline layer 500 to 1,000 m thick, cold deep layer (NCERT).
- Surface currents are about 10 per cent of ocean water in the upper 400 m; deep currents about 90 per cent (NCERT).
- Books differ on surface figures: NCERT gives about 27 degrees average and 22 degrees at 20 degrees latitude; Leong gives about 21 degrees at the equator and 13 degrees at 45 degrees latitude.

### Notes: Cause and effect chains

- Fertiliser and sewage nutrients to algal bloom to decomposition to oxygen use to hypoxia (dead zone) to fish kill to fishery loss.

### Notes: Coral facts and numbers

- Polyps belong to the phylum Cnidaria with anemones and jellyfish; zooxanthellae are dinoflagellates (PMF IAS).
- Reefs need water not below about 20 degrees Celsius, within roughly 30 degrees north and south (Leong; PMF IAS).
- Depth: Leong says reef-building coral lives shallower than about 55 m, ideally under 30 m; PMF IAS ideal 45 to 55 m.
- Corals commonly lose 60 to 90 per cent of zooxanthellae when they bleach (PMF IAS).
- Global bleaching events in 1998, 2010 and 2014 to 2017 (68.2 per cent of reef areas hit by enough heat stress), and a fourth event confirmed by the US NOAA with the International Coral Reef Initiative, affecting nearly 84 per cent of reefs in at least 83 countries (reported, PMF IAS; figures change).

### Notes: Traps and exceptions

- The equator is not the saltiest belt; rain dilutes it.
- High latitude does not always mean low salinity: the North Sea is saline because of the North Atlantic Drift.
- Leong's Caspian Sea figure of 180 per mille is a slip; standard references give about 12 per mille (verify).
- A bleached coral is not dead; coral disease is a different problem.
- Spring tides have nothing to do with the spring season.
- NCERT's Atlantic salinity statement uses both 15 to 20 and 20 to 30 degrees; use 'subtropical belts'.

### Notes: Four easily confused pairs

- Tide versus current versus wave versus surge: vertical regular rise and fall; moving mass of water; wind energy moving through the surface; weather-driven, irregular.
- Thermocline versus halocline: sharp change of temperature versus sharp change of salinity with depth.
- Producer versus consumer: phytoplankton make food; zooplankton and fish eat.
- Bleaching versus coral disease: loss of algae with tissue present versus tissue sloughing off.

- Chandipur, Odisha: sea recedes a few km twice a day (Prelims 2017; standard knowledge, verify).
- Durgaduani tidal project in the Sunderbans: 3 MW 'under way' in NCERT, status to be verified.
- Reef areas: Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh, Lakshadweep, Andaman and Nicobar (PMF IAS).
- Biorock restoration trial by the Zoological Survey of India in the Gulf of Kachchh (PMF IAS).

### Core concepts

- Salinity is salt per 1,000 g of seawater; it changes because water is added or removed, not because the salt recipe changes.
- Cold and salty water is dense and sinks; warm and fresh water floats; layers form (thermocline, halocline).
- Tides come from the Moon's and Sun's pull plus centrifugal force, giving two bulges; spring and neap depend on alignment.
- Marine life is richest on shallow shelves, at current mixing zones and upwellings.
- Corals depend on algae partners; stress expels them (bleaching) and acidification weakens skeletons.

**Q.** Sketch and label: draw the continental shelf, slope and deep ocean and mark where life is richest.

**A.** Shelf is shallow and sunlit; life is richest over the shelf and at mixing zones; deep ocean is cold and dark.

**Q.** Why does the same amount of salt give different salinity in different seas?

**A.** Because salinity is a ratio; changing the amount of water by evaporation, ice formation, rain, rivers or melt changes the ratio while the salt is almost fixed.

**Q.** How do density differences create ocean layers and currents?

**A.** Cold, salty water is denser and sinks; warm, fresh water floats; this layers the water and drives vertical and some horizontal movement such as the Mediterranean exchange.

**Q.** Why are tidal ranges so large in a funnel-shaped bay such as the Bay of Fundy?

**A.** The narrowing bay squeezes the tidal bulge into a smaller cross-section, magnifying its height (NCERT).

**A.** Its tissue is alive; if the stress is brief the coral regains zooxanthellae within weeks or months.
