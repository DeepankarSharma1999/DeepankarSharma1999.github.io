## GS-129: Industrial belts and location factors

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Bridge: weight, distance and the cost of moving things

Every location argument in this lesson rests on one piece of arithmetic. Moving goods costs money, and the bill grows with how heavy the load is and how far it goes. Write it as: **transport cost = rate per tonne per km x tonnes x km**.

Here is an original, made-up example. Take a rate of Rs 3 per tonne per km, and a mine 200 km from the market. Suppose 3 tonnes of ore must be fed into a furnace to get 1 tonne of metal (the other 2 tonnes are waste, so the process is **weight-losing**).

- Plant at the mine: only the 1 tonne of metal travels. Cost = 3 x 1 x 200 = Rs 600.
- Plant at the market: 3 tonnes of ore travel. Cost = 3 x 3 x 200 = Rs 1,800.

The mine site saves Rs 1,200 per tonne of metal. That gap is the reason ore-based plants sit near ores. Now flip the case: a drink factory needs 1 tonne of syrup but mixes it with local water to make 10 tonnes of product (**weight-gaining**). Plant at the syrup source: 10 tonnes travel, 3 x 10 x 200 = Rs 6,000. Plant at the market: 1 tonne of syrup travels, 3 x 1 x 200 = Rs 600. Now the market wins. (The weight-gaining idea is standard teaching, not in the NCERT extracts; verify before quoting it as NCERT.)

Two cautions. First, real costs also include handling at each end, and a route that is faster or has a cheaper mode (ship rather than truck) lowers the rate; an NCERT exercise treats cheap water transport as helping the jute mills along the Hugli. Second, perishable goods add a time cost: milk and sugarcane lose value while waiting, so they are processed near the farm. Keep this formula in mind in every module.

### 1. Orientation: what a factory adds and why place matters

*Objective:* Define secondary activity, manufacturing, industry and industrial belt, and say why this topic is in the syllabus.

## Start with three words

An **activity** is a way of earning a living. **Primary** activities take things from nature (farming, mining, fishing). **Secondary** activities add value by changing those things into something more useful. NCERT's own example: cotton in the boll has limited use, but as yarn it is worth more and becomes cloth; iron ore cannot be used straight from the mine, but as steel it becomes machines and tools. **Tertiary** activities are services (trade, transport, tourism); Day 20 lesson GS-398 covers them (see its section 'Exam: the 2021 question, the location-factor table and traps').

## Manufacturing and industry

**Manufacturing** once meant 'made by hand'; now it includes goods made by machines. NCERT describes it as turning raw materials into finished goods of higher value for local or distant markets. An **industry**, in NCERT's wording, is a geographically located manufacturing unit that keeps books of accounts and records under a management system. Because 'industry' is used loosely (entertainment industry, tourism industry), the longer 'manufacturing industry' is used for clarity.

## What marks modern large-scale manufacturing

NCERT lists these features: specialisation of skills (each worker repeats one task, so mass production replaces slow made-to-order craft); **mechanisation** (gadgets doing tasks) and its advanced stage **automation**; technological innovation through research and development for quality, less waste and less pollution; a big organisation needing large capital; and an **uneven geographic distribution**. NCERT says the major concentrations cover less than 10 per cent of the world's land area, and that factories take far less land than farms because the process is more intense: its example is that about 2.5 sq km of the American corn belt holds roughly four large farms employing 10 to 20 workers, while the same area could hold several large factories employing thousands.

## Why 'belt'?

Factories do not spread evenly; they clump. This lesson uses **industrial belt** as a working term (the lesson's own wording, not an NCERT definition) for a long strip or cluster of industrial towns that grew along something that pulled them together: a coalfield, an ore field, a river, a port or a transport line. Leong's examples include the Midlands of England, the Ruhr of Germany and the Appalachian-Pittsburgh region of the USA. For India, NCERT describes **mineral belts** that industry grew on.

## The question this lesson answers

The syllabus asks why industries are where they are. The answer is a bundle of costs and pulls, not a single cause. The next module gives the master rule.

### 2. Foundation: the profit rule and weight, perishability, market

*Objective:* State NCERT's profit-and-cost rule and apply it to raw-material, perishability and market pulls.

## The master rule

NCERT (Class XI, Secondary Activities): industries maximise profits by reducing costs, so they should be located where production costs are lowest. Everything else in this lesson is a way that some cost gets lower at some place. Before that, bring in the bridge idea (see the bridge module on weight, distance and cost): transport cost = rate x tonnes x km.

## Market

NCERT calls access to market the most important factor. A **market** is not just people; it is people who want the good and have the **purchasing power** to buy it. Remote areas with few people offer small markets. NCERT names the developed regions of Europe, North America, Japan and Australia as large global markets because incomes are high, and the densely populated regions of South and South-east Asia as large markets too. Some industries, such as aircraft and arms, serve a global market.

## Raw material

Raw materials should be cheap and easy to transport. NCERT says industries using cheap, bulky, **weight-losing** materials (ores) locate close to the source, naming steel, sugar and cement. Weight-losing means the finished product weighs much less than the inputs, so hauling the inputs wastes money. A second reason is **perishability**: agro-processing and dairy products are processed close to farm produce or milk supply, because the material spoils or loses value if it waits.

Note a wrinkle in the book's wording: the bracket '(ores)' fits steel and cement, but sugarcane is a bulky farm crop, not an ore. It sits in the group because it is bulky, weight-losing and perishable.

## Quick contrast table

| Pull | What it means | NCERT example |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Buyers with money | Developed regions, aircraft and arms (global) |
| Bulky raw material | Cheap, heavy, loses weight | Steel, sugar, cement |
| Perishable raw material | Spoils if delayed | Agro-processing, dairy |

## Common beginner error

Students say 'industry goes where raw material is'. That is true only for weight-losing or perishable inputs. A scooter or a computer assembly plant gets parts from many places and may go to the market or to a good road. The correct habit is to ask three questions in order: what are the inputs, how do they change in weight, and who buys the output.

### 3. Core: labour, energy, transport, policy, agglomeration and footloose industries

*Objective:* Explain the other five NCERT factors, define agglomeration and footloose industry, and show how factors combine.

## Labour

NCERT: labour supply matters, and some manufacturing still needs skilled labour. But increasing mechanisation, automation and flexible processes have reduced industry's dependence on labour. So labour is a strong pull for craft-like and labour-intensive work and a weaker one for automated plants.

## Energy

Industries that use a great deal of power locate close to the energy supply; NCERT's example is the aluminium industry. Earlier coal was the main energy source, but hydroelectricity and petroleum are also important today. Leong adds the history: coal was the greatest single factor of industrial location, and most of the world's manufacturing areas grew on or near coalfields (module 5 tells the steel story). Leong also notes that electro-chemical industries, such as smelting aluminium from bauxite, need very cheap hydroelectric power, which explains how Norway, Sweden, Canada and Japan, short of mineral fuels, reached high technical development.

## Transport and communication

NCERT: fast, efficient transport to carry raw materials in and goods out is essential, and the cost of transport plays an important role. Western Europe and eastern North America have highly developed transport systems that induced industrial concentration. Improvements in transport led to integrated development and regional specialisation. Communication matters too, for exchanging and managing information. (Links: Day 20 lesson GS-126 for roads, railways and ports.)

## Government policy

NCERT: governments adopt **regional policies** to promote balanced economic development and so set up industries in particular areas. This is why a factory may exist in a place that costs would not choose.

## Agglomeration economies

Many industries gain by being near a leader industry and other industries. These gains are **agglomeration economies**: savings that come from linkages between industries, for example shared suppliers, shared skilled workers and shared services.

## Footloose industries

NCERT: footloose industries can sit in a wide variety of places. They do not depend on a specific raw material (weight-losing or otherwise), mostly assemble component parts obtainable anywhere, produce in small quantity, employ a small labour force and are generally not polluting. The important locating factor is accessibility by the road network.

NCERT's exercise asks you to spot the wrong statement; the one saying 'sugar, cotton textiles and vegetable oils are footloose' is wrong, because (see module 2) their inputs are bulky or perishable and tie them to farm areas. That is analysis from the text, not an official key.

## How the factors combine

NCERT closes the list: these factors operate together. Practical method for an answer: name the **dominant** factor (the biggest cost saved), name a **supporting** factor, and name a **limiting** factor that explains exceptions.

| Industry | Dominant | Supporting | Limiting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aluminium smelter | Cheap power | Port for imported ore | Distance from market |
| Dairy | Perishable milk | Cold transport | Small local market |
| Footloose assembly | Road access | Skilled labour | Parts supply |

### 4. Core: four ways to classify industries, and high-technology industry

*Objective:* Classify an industry on four separate bases and explain high-technology industry and technopolies.

## Why classify

UPSC options often hide a trap inside a label such as 'basic', 'agro-based' or 'joint sector'. NCERT classifies manufacturing on **four separate bases**: size, inputs (raw materials), output (products) and ownership. One factory sits in all four classes at once; the bases are not rivals.

## Basis 1: size

Size depends on capital invested, workers employed and volume of production.

- **Household or cottage**: the smallest unit; artisans use local raw materials and simple tools, working at home with family or part-time labour; goods may be used at home, sold in the village market or bartered.
- **Small scale**: a workshop outside the home, local raw material, simple power-driven machines and semi-skilled labour. NCERT says India, China, Indonesia and Brazil developed labour-intensive small-scale manufacturing to give employment and raise local purchasing power.
- **Large scale**: a large market, various raw materials, enormous energy, specialised workers, advanced technology, assembly-line mass production and large capital. It developed in the last 200 years in the United Kingdom, north-eastern USA and Europe and has since spread almost everywhere.

NCERT groups the world's large-scale industrial regions into two types: **traditional** regions thickly clustered in a few more developed countries, and **high-technology** regions that have spread to less developed countries.

## Basis 2: inputs

| Class | Raw material | NCERT examples |
|---|---|---|
| Agro-based | Farm produce | Food processing, sugar, tea, coffee, cotton, jute, silk, rubber |
| Mineral-based | Ferrous, non-ferrous, non-metallic minerals | Iron and steel; aluminium, copper; cement, pottery |
| Chemical-based | Natural chemical minerals, wood, coal | Petrochemicals, salts, sulphur, potash, plastics |
| Forest-based | Timber, bamboo, grass, lac | Furniture, paper, lac |
| Animal-based | Leather, wool, ivory | Leather, woollen textiles |

## Basis 3: output

**Basic industries** make products that other industries use as raw material (iron and steel feed machinery). **Consumer goods industries**, also called non-basic, make goods consumers use directly (bread, biscuits, tea, soap, paper, televisions). NCERT's exercise asks which type produces raw materials for other industries; the answer from the text is basic industries.

## Basis 4: ownership

**Public sector**: owned and managed by governments (India had many public sector undertakings; socialist countries have many). **Private sector**: owned by individual investors and managed privately, normal in capitalist countries. **Joint sector**: run by joint stock companies, or by the private and public sectors together. Mixed economies have both public and private.

## High-technology industry

High-tech is the latest generation of manufacturing, built on intensive research and development (R and D) producing advanced scientific and engineering products. Professional white-collar workers form a large share of the workforce and outnumber production blue-collar workers. Examples named: robotics on assembly lines, computer-aided design and manufacturing, electronic controls of smelting and refining, new chemical and pharmaceutical products. The landscape is neat, low, dispersed office-plant-lab buildings in planned business parks. High-tech industries that are regionally concentrated, self-sustained and highly specialised are **technopolies**; NCERT names Silicon Valley near San Francisco and Silicon Forest near Seattle.

### 5. Core: iron and steel, from forests to coalfields to coasts

*Objective:* Tell the three-stage story of iron and steel location and say why plants can now sit far from their raw materials.

## Why this industry anchors the topic

Leong calls iron the commonest, cheapest and most versatile metal, the basis of the modern industrial complex; about 90 per cent of the world's iron ore is used to make steel, and automobiles, aircraft, bridges and machines depend on it. It is also the standard example of a weight-losing, bulky-input industry.

## What goes in

A blast furnace is fed at the top with iron ore, coke and limestone; hot air is blown in; molten iron and slag (the waste) are tapped at the bottom. Water and air are used in huge quantities, and other metals are added to make alloy steels. Leong's furnace figure puts the hottest zone near 1,927 degrees C (3,500 F); note that the text gives 593 C (1,100 F) for smelting, which cannot be the furnace's hot zone (see masterNotes slips). **Coke** is a hard fuel made from coal; **slag** is the waste that floats on molten iron.

## Stage 1: scattered works (charcoal era)

Early iron was made in small scattered works where ore was found and forest supplied timber for charcoal; running water helped to cool the bellows. Output was a few tonnes for local demand, so works were everywhere, especially in Europe near ore and forest.

## Stage 2: the coalfield era

By the end of the eighteenth century coal proved a far better fuel than charcoal, and coalfields also produced coke. Old methods needed eight to ten times as much coal as iron to smelt a given quantity of steel, so it was cheaper to bring ore to the coalfield than to carry coal to the ore. Large works therefore clustered on coalfields. Many persisted by **industrial inertia**, Leong's term for the inherited reservoir of skilled labour, technology, transport network and industrial establishments. Examples: Midlands of England, Central Valley of Scotland, South Wales, the Ruhr, the Franco-Belgian coalfield area, the Appalachian-Pittsburgh region and the Donetz region.

## Stage 3: the coastal shift

By the late nineteenth century better technology let an equal amount of coal and coke, or even less, smelt a given quantity of iron. Coalfields lost their pull. Coastal sites were preferred because raw materials (coal, coke, ores, ferro-alloys, even oil) are easy to assemble by ship, finished products are easy to dispose of, and the consumer market is often near. Leong's examples: newer coastal plants such as Margam and Newport displaced older inland works in South Wales; new plants on the US eastern coast (Sparrows Point) and the St Lawrence-Great Lakes region (Chicago, Hamilton) grew at the expense of interior towns like Pittsburgh, with ore imported from as far as Labrador; Malaysia's works at Prai is coastal, importing coal and drawing iron from inland. Japan produces little ore and imports it, and uses scrap iron.

## India in this frame

Leong lists India's large works at Jamshedpur, Durgapur, Rourkela and Bhilai (he spells the second 'Dugapur'). NCERT explains the advantage: iron-ore mines lie close to the coalfields in the north-eastern plateau region, and Jharkhand has some of the oldest iron-ore mines with most of the iron and steel plants around them. Module 6 builds on this and gives each plant's state, because Leong's 'Bihar and Orissa' wording does not fit Durgapur or Bhilai. NCERT notes that the manganese mines it lists for Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra lie far from steel plants, a disadvantage, while Odisha's lie in the central part of the iron-ore belt.

### 6. Core: India's mineral belts and the industrial belts built on them

*Objective:* Describe NCERT's mineral belts, link each to industries the extracts support, and flag what the extracts leave out.

## Why minerals decide India's industrial map

NCERT (Class XII): most valuable minerals are of pre-Palaeozoic age and associated with metamorphic and igneous rocks of peninsular India; the vast alluvial plain of the north is devoid of minerals of economic use. Most metallic minerals sit in the old crystalline rocks of the peninsula. Over 97 per cent of coal reserves occur in the valleys of the Damodar, Sone, Mahanadi and Godavari. Most major mineral resources lie east of a line joining Mangaluru and Kanpur. Check this on an atlas. The north-western (Aravali-Gujarat) belt, Goa and the Mumbai High/Gujarat oil lie west of the line, but most of Karnataka's iron-ore fields (Ballari-Hospet, Kudremukh, Baba Budan) and the north-eastern belt lie east of it. So 'most' holds by quantity, with the north-western belt as the main exception.

## The belts

| Belt | Where (NCERT) | Minerals named | Industry link |
|---|---|---|---|
| North-Eastern Plateau Region | Chhotanagpur (Jharkhand), Odisha Plateau, West Bengal, parts of Chhattisgarh | Iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite, mica | Major iron and steel industry (NCERT asks the learner to find the reason) |
| South-Western Plateau Region | Karnataka, Goa, contiguous Tamil Nadu uplands, Kerala | Ferrous metals, bauxite, high-grade iron ore, manganese, limestone; Neyveli lignite only | Less diversified; lacks coal |
| North-Western Region | Along Aravali in Rajasthan and part of Gujarat | Copper, zinc, building stones, gypsum, Fuller's earth, dolomite, limestone, salt, petroleum (Gujarat) | Limestone and dolomite are raw materials for cement |
| Himalayan belt | East and west parts | Copper, lead, zinc, cobalt, tungsten; oil in Assam valley | Not developed as an industrial belt in the extract |

## Reading the iron-and-steel link

The answer NCERT hints at is a co-location: in the north-eastern plateau region the iron-ore mines lie close to the coalfields. Jharkhand's coalfields (Jharia, the largest, Bokaro, Giridih, Karanpura, with Raniganj in the Jharkhand-Bengal coal belt) and the ore of Singhbhum (Noamundi, Gua) and Odisha (Sundergarh, Kendujhar, Mayurbhanj) are close together. Add manganese (Odisha's central iron-ore belt) and limestone, and the plants named by Leong make sense: Jamshedpur (Jharkhand), Rourkela (Odisha), Bhilai (Durg district, Chhattisgarh) and Durgapur (West Bengal, on the Raniganj coal side; standard knowledge, verify). Leong puts all four in 'Bihar and Orissa', which does not fit Durgapur or Bhilai (see masterNotes). Durg, Dantewara and Bailadila (NCERT) carry the belt into Chhattisgarh, and Bhilai lies in Durg.

## The other industry links the extracts support

- **Aluminium**: bauxite is mainly in Odisha (largest producer), Gujarat, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra; aluminium needs much power (NCERT, Class XI), so power and ore both matter. Plant names are not in the extracts.
- **Cement**: limestone and dolomite of the north-western belt.
- **Refineries**: NCERT names two types, field-based (Digboi) and market-based (Barauni).
- **Thermal power**: coal is used mainly for thermal power and smelting iron ore.

## What is missing

The Manufacturing Industries chapter of earlier editions of NCERT Class XII (not in the current reprint or the project files; verify), with the named industrial regions (for instance Hugli, Mumbai-Pune) and details of textiles, sugar and petrochemicals, is not in the extracts. Module 8 lists them as standard knowledge with a verify flag.

### 7. Core: power-hungry industries, refineries, agro-processing and cement

*Objective:* Apply the cost rule to power-based, oil-refining, farm-based and limestone-based industries.

## Power-hungry industries

Leong explains that hydroelectricity (HEP) is best developed in mountainous regions with enough rain and good dam sites, while thermal electricity (from burning coal, oil or gas) is scattered wherever fuels are available, best developed in industrial districts and near coalfields, and needs large quantities of water for cooling. Some industries only work with a very cheap supply of HEP: electro-chemical industries such as smelting aluminium from bauxite and making nitrates and calcium carbides. Leong's case is the Kitimat scheme in British Columbia, built to supply HEP to aluminium smelting at Kitimat, with much of the bauxite imported from Jamaica. Lesson to draw (analysis): where power is the main cost, a plant will accept shipping its ore a long way. NCERT's one-line version: industries that use more power are located close to the source of energy, for example aluminium. (Dams and projects: Day 19 lesson GS-125, section 'Core: multipurpose projects and the rivers the extracts name'.)

## Oil refineries

Leong gives three places for a refinery, each with a reason.

| Site | Reason (Leong) | Example named |
|---|---|---|
| In or near the oilfield | Temporary; suits small refineries; fields are often remote so there is little demand for products | None named |
| At the port of export | Serves local needs, protects the producer's interests, gives jobs | Persian Gulf area, Port Harcourt (Nigeria) |
| At the port of import | Cheaper to import crude and refine near consumers than to import refined products; usually owned by large international firms | Southampton, Yokohama, Singapore, Port Dickson |

Refining itself is **fractional distillation**: crude oil is heated and its groups of hydrocarbons, called fractions, are drawn off at different boiling points, with the vapour rising and cooling in a column, so heavier fractions (diesel, lubricating oil) condense lower down, lighter ones (kerosene, petrol) higher up, refinery gas leaves at the top and the heavy residue is drawn off at the bottom; cracking breaks heavier fractions into lighter fuels. India (NCERT Class XII) has two types of refinery: **field-based** (Digboi) and **market-based** (Barauni). Why can a developing country sit far from its oilfields and refine anyway? The lesson's inference from Leong's import-port logic: crude can be shipped in bulk to one port, while many buyers of many products sit near that port, so the refinery goes where the demand and the port are.

## Farm-based industries

Perishability ties agro-processing and dairy to farm and milk supply (module 2). NCERT shows a tea garden with its tea factory in the Nilgiri Hills of Tamil Nadu. NCERT Class XII adds a policy-and-technology link: the Green Revolution package of high-yielding varieties and chemical fertilisers was introduced in irrigated areas of Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat, and this gave a fillip to agro-inputs, agro-processing and small-scale industries; at first it was confined to irrigated areas.

## Cement: a limestone case

NCERT lists cement with steel and sugar as an industry near bulky raw material, and notes that dolomite and limestone of the north-western belt are raw material for it. A simple weight check (standard chemistry, computed from atomic masses; verify): CaCO3 heated gives CaO plus CO2; 100.09 g of limestone leaves 56.08 g of lime and releases 44.01 g of carbon dioxide, so about 56 per cent of the limestone's mass remains. It shows why hauling limestone far is wasteful, and also why cement plants emit CO2. Fuel burnt to heat the kiln is the second CO2 source; clay supplies silica but releases none (standard; verify).

### 8. Master: high-tech siting, jobs, lagging regions and what lies beyond the extracts

*Objective:* Reason about modern trends (high-tech siting, jobs, regional gaps) and know which Indian regions and corridors need outside facts.

## Why high-tech firms go to the edge of big cities

NCERT's exercise asks why high-technology industries in many countries are attracted to the peripheral areas of major metropolitan centres. The extract gives the ingredients; the reasoning below is the lesson's inference. High-tech sites are low, dispersed office-plant-lab buildings in planned business parks, which need space that a crowded centre lacks. They depend on professional workers, who live in the metropolis. They are footloose in the NCERT sense (small output, light parts), so road access matters more than a raw-material source. Silicon Valley and Silicon Forest are the named technopolies.

## Resources are not enough

NCERT's exercise says Africa has immense natural resources yet is industrially the most backward continent. The extracts support three building blocks of an answer. First, industrial location needs a market with purchasing power (module 2). Second, modern large-scale industry needs vast capital, technology and large organisations. Third, Leong writes that the southern continents have rich iron ore but make little steel and that Africa is rich in iron ore but has little coal (South Africa is the main exception; verify), with high-grade deposits being exploited in Liberia and Mauritania. Other causes (history, policy, infrastructure) are outside the extracts; add them only with a verify mark.

## Resource-based manufacturing and jobs

NCERT says small-scale manufacturing is labour-intensive and provides employment and raises local purchasing power, which is why India, China, Indonesia and Brazil developed it. Agro-based and forest-based industries use local inputs; the Green Revolution encouraged agro-processing and small industries. The limit is that mechanisation and automation reduce dependence on labour, so a resource-based industry may create few jobs per rupee invested. A balanced answer names both.

## Industrial inertia and policy

Old belts persist by industrial inertia, yet Leong shows some interior works closing as coastal plants grew. NCERT adds that regional policy places industry in chosen areas. So location is a contest between cost, inheritance and policy.

## Beyond the extracts: Indian industrial regions and corridors

The Manufacturing Industries chapter of earlier editions of NCERT Class XII (Chapter 8 before the rationalised reprint; not in the project files; verify) groups India's major industrial regions into eight, and the Delhi-Mumbai and other industrial corridors are a policy theme; the masterNotes block lists them as standard knowledge, each marked verify. Treat any corridor characteristic here as a framework to check, not a memorised fact.

## Dated numbers

Leong's tables are from the 1980s (Table 30.1 is for 1983). Use them only to show patterns, such as a large iron-ore producer making little steel (Brazil in that table) and the reverse for Japan, never as current figures.

### 9. Exam: the six official questions and answer frames

*Objective:* Read each official question, choose its directive, and build a frame using only supported facts and flagged additions.

## Method for every location question

Use a five-step frame: (1) define the industry and its inputs; (2) give the dominant factor and the mechanism (weight, perishability, power, market); (3) give two or three supporting factors; (4) give an Indian and a world example; (5) give a limit or a change over time. Mark any fact outside the extracts as 'verify'.

## Frame details

**Refineries (2017).** Use Leong's three sites. Implications (analysis, not an official line): import dependence and exposure to crude prices; refineries cluster at ports; products need pipelines, rail or road to inland markets; petrochemical clusters follow; producers keep export-port refineries to protect their interests.

**Corridors (2018).** The extracts do not cover corridors. Teach the idea: a planned belt of industrial nodes along a freight line or highway with shared infrastructure. Named corridors, the National Industrial Corridor Development Programme and the Eastern and Western Dedicated Freight Corridors are taught in Day 20 lesson GS-126, section 'Master: corridors and connectivity projects beyond the extracts' (verify). This lesson adds the location logic: transport spine + agglomeration + policy.

**Jobs (2019).** Argue yes for labour-intensive, local-input industries (agro, forest, small scale), and no-or-limited for automated plants; add pollution and skills.

**Agro-processing, NW India (2019).** Supported pieces: perishability and farm-side processing; irrigated Green Revolution areas of Punjab, Haryana and Western Uttar Pradesh gave agro-inputs and agro-processing a fillip. Market, road and rail links and policy support are standard additions to verify.

**Steel (2020).** Walk through module 5: charcoal, coal, coast. Indian examples: Jamshedpur and the other old plants stay near ore and coal (a counter-example). NCERT says about 80 per cent of India's coal is non-coking (legy205 p. 7), so much coking coal is imported (verify); hence coastal plants such as Visakhapatnam, Hazira and Dolvi (standard; verify). Bhilai draws ore from nearby Dalli-Rajhara but coal from distant fields (verify).

**Cement (2025 Prelims).** Analysis, not the official key: Statement II (clay mixed with limestone) and III (limestone becomes lime during clinker making) are standard cement chemistry; III also explains the CO2 in I, but II does not. The share in I is commonly cited as about 7 to 8 per cent of global CO2 (standard; verify), and kiln fuel is a second source besides calcination. If both are accepted as correct and only III explains I, option (b) fits; confirm against the official key.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Secondary activity: adds value by changing raw materials into more useful products (NCERT Class XI); covers manufacturing, processing and construction.
- Manufacturing industry: a geographically located manufacturing unit keeping books and records under a management system (NCERT wording); the long form 'manufacturing industry' avoids confusion with the 'entertainment industry' or 'tourism industry'.
- Weight-losing material: a bulky input whose product weighs much less, so industry sits near the source (ores; NCERT names steel, sugar and cement).
- Weight-gaining process: output heavier than the carried input, so the plant tends to sit near the market. Standard idea, not in the extracts; verify.
- Market (NCERT): people who demand the good and have the purchasing power to buy it.
- Agglomeration economies: savings from nearness to a leader industry and linked industries.
- Footloose industry: not tied to a specific raw material; small output, small labour force, generally not polluting; road access is the key factor (NCERT).
- Industrial inertia (Leong): inherited skilled labour, technology, transport network and establishments that keep an industrial region going after its original advantage fades.
- Technopolis: a regionally concentrated, self-sustained, highly specialised high-technology industry area (NCERT).
- Basic industry: its products are raw material for other industries; consumer goods industry: its goods are consumed directly (NCERT).
- Industrial belt (this lesson's working term): a long strip or cluster of industrial towns grown along a coalfield, ore field, river, port or transport line.

### Notes: Location factors with mechanism and example

- Rule: industries try to cut cost so as to maximise profit; the factors operate together (NCERT Class XI, pp. 2-3).

### Notes: Four bases for classifying industries (NCERT Class XI)

- One industry belongs to one class under each basis at the same time; the bases are not alternatives.

### Notes: Iron and steel: location chronology (Leong)

- Early period: small scattered works where ore, forest (charcoal) and running water were found, mainly in Europe.
- By the end of the eighteenth century: coal proved better than charcoal; furnaces built on major coalfields; coke from coalfields; old methods used eight to ten times as much coal as iron.
- Industrial regions of the coalfield era still named: Midlands of England, Central Valley of Scotland, South Wales, the Ruhr, Franco-Belgian coalfield area, Appalachian-Pittsburgh, Donetz.
- By the late nineteenth century: equal or less coal and coke per tonne of iron; coalfields lose pull; coastal sites favoured.
- Coastal examples named: Margam, Newport, Sparrows Point, Chicago and Hamilton, Prai; some older inland works closed.
- Leong calls the location of the industry still in a state of transition because smelting and refining techniques keep changing.

### Notes: India's mineral belts and industry links (NCERT Class XII)

- NCERT's own question to the learner: why is the major iron and steel industry in the north-eastern plateau region?

### Notes: Refinery sites and types

- Leong: three sites. NCERT Class XII: two types in India.

### Notes: Cause and effect chains

- Coal replaces charcoal -> works move to coalfields -> clusters of towns -> industrial inertia keeps them going.
- Better smelting technology -> less coal per tonne of iron -> coalfields lose pull -> coastal plants importing ore and coal.
- Cheap hydroelectricity -> electro-chemical industry -> aluminium smelter at the power site, ore shipped in (Kitimat, Leong).
- Green Revolution in irrigated Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh and others -> more marketable surplus and demand for inputs -> fillip to agro-processing and small industries (NCERT Class XII).
- Mechanisation and automation -> less dependence on labour -> labour is a weaker pull and jobs per unit of capital fall.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Weight-losing vs weight-gaining: the first moves the plant toward the raw material, the second toward the market.
- Mineral belt vs industrial belt: minerals occur in a belt; industry grows on it only where coal, power, transport and market also help.
- Field-based vs market-based refinery: Digboi at the field; Barauni near the market (NCERT).
- Basic vs consumer goods: basic industries supply other industries; consumer goods go to final users.
- Footloose vs raw-material-bound: sugar, cement and steel are bound to inputs; footloose industries are not.

### Core concepts

- Industries cut cost to maximise profit; location is where costs are lowest.
- Weight-losing and perishable inputs pull the plant to the raw material.
- Seven NCERT factors operate together: market, raw material, labour, energy, transport and communication, government policy, agglomeration.
- Steel location moved from forests to coalfields to coasts as technology cut coal needs.
- India's industry sits on mineral belts, strongest where ore and coal lie together.

### Practice

**Q.** State NCERT's master rule for industrial location in one line.

**A.** Industries aim to maximise profit by reducing costs, so they sit where production costs are lowest.

**Q.** What does 'weight-losing' mean, and why does it matter?

**A.** The finished product weighs much less than the input, so hauling the input is wasteful and the plant sits near the raw material.

**Q.** Name the four bases of classifying manufacturing industries.

**A.** Size, inputs (raw materials), output (products) and ownership.

**Q.** Explain why coalfields lost their pull on steel plants.

**A.** Better technology cut the coal and coke needed per tonne of iron to equal or less, so carrying coal to ore was no longer dearer; coastal sites then won on ease of assembling inputs and shipping out.

**Q.** Why is an aluminium smelter drawn to cheap power even when ore is imported?

**A.** Power is the largest cost of electro-chemical smelting, so cheap power outweighs ore transport; Leong's Kitimat scheme is the example.

**Q.** Why is a mineral belt not the same as an industrial belt?

**A.** A mineral belt is where minerals occur; industry grows on it only where coal, power, transport and a market also support it.

**Q.** A state wants a fruit-pulp factory. Choose farm-side or city-side siting and justify with two reasons.

**A.** Farm-side: fruit is perishable and loses weight in processing (module 2), so processing near orchards cuts spoilage and haul cost; a limit is the need for roads and cold storage.

**Q.** A country with a long coastline and imported crude plans a refinery. Where and why?

**A.** At the port of import, since importing crude and refining near consumers is cheaper than importing refined products (Leong); add inland product movement as a linked need.
