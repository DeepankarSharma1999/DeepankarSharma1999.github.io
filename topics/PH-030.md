### 1. Orientation: what velocity addition and aberration ask

*Objective:* State the question velocity addition answers, the conventions used throughout, and what this lesson reuses from PH-028 and PH-029 instead of repeating.

## The question

A ship moves past you. It fires a projectile. You know the projectile's speed as the ship's crew measure it. What speed do you measure? The everyday answer is to add the two speeds (the Galilean rule, PH-001 module 2). The Lorentz transformation (PH-028 modules 4 and 5) gives the exact answer, and it never exceeds the speed of light c. The same machinery, applied to a ray of light, gives **aberration**: a change in the direction in which you see light travelling when you move.

## What this lesson reuses

- The Lorentz transformation and its inverse: **not re-derived here**. Use PH-028 module 4 (derivation) and module 5 (inverse and limiting cases).
- A first, compact derivation of velocity addition already exists as a preview in PH-028 module 7 (also derivation PH-028-D3, numerical N2 and MCQ P5). This lesson is its home topic: module 2 gives the along-the-motion rule briefly, and the new material starts at module 4 with directions and angles. Module 3 is a recap with a few extra numbers.
- Time dilation and length contraction (PH-029 modules 3 and 5) are used only as a cross-check.

## Words to know first

- **Velocity component**: the part of a velocity along one axis; uₓ = dx/dt, u_y = dy/dt, u_z = dz/dt.
- **β and γ**: β = v/c and γ = 1/√(1 − β²) (PH-028 module 1).
- **Aberration of light**: the observed direction of a light ray depends on the observer's motion. It is not refraction (bending in a medium).
- **Line of sight**: the direction from the observer towards the source. The light itself travels the opposite way.
- **Relative velocity of A as seen by B**: the velocity of A measured in B's rest frame. It is *not* the difference of two lab velocities when speeds are large.

## Conventions used in every module

Frame S is the laboratory. Frame S′ moves at +v along +x of S, axes parallel and right-handed, origins coinciding at t = t′ = 0 (as in PH-028 module 1). A particle has velocity **u** = (uₓ, u_y, u_z) in S and **u′** = (u′ₓ, u′_y, u′_z) in S′. Angles are measured anticlockwise from +x in the x–y plane.

## Map of this lesson

Modules 2 to 5 are the **core for the first pass**: addition along x, limiting cases, directions, and the three past-paper numbers. Modules 6 to 8 are aberration, stellar aberration with the headlight effect, and Fizeau (the sources extracts do not contain these three; they are standard textbook content flagged for review). Modules 9 and 10 are exam writing and practice. The Doppler effect is **to come on Day 12 (PH-031), not yet written**; it is not used here.

### 2. Foundation: velocity addition along x, derived and used

*Objective:* Derive u′ₓ and uₓ from the Lorentz transformation and use the three-step recipe to set up any two-frame problem.

## Assumptions

S and S′ are inertial; S′ moves at +v along x; the Lorentz transformation of PH-028 module 4 holds: x′ = γ(x − vt), y′ = y, z′ = z, t′ = γ(t − vx/c²). Velocity here means the velocity of one particle, whose position is a function of time in each frame.

## Derivation (see the diagram)

A particle moves a little: in S it covers dx, dy, dz in a time dt. Differentiate the transformation (γ and v are constants):

$$ dx′ = γ(dx − v dt),  dy′ = dy,  dz′ = dz,  dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²) $$

The velocity in S′ is dx′/dt′. Divide numerator and denominator by dt, then replace dx/dt by uₓ:

$$ u′ₓ = dx′/dt′ = γ(dx − v dt)/[γ(dt − v dx/c²)] = (uₓ − v)/(1 − v uₓ/c²) $$

The factor γ cancels. To go the other way, use the inverse transformation (PH-028 module 5), which is the same with v → −v and primes swapped:

$$ uₓ = (u′ₓ + v)/(1 + v u′ₓ/c²) $$

Notation caution: the Mathur extract defines all three components (u′ₓ, u′_y, u′_z) with dt for dt′; each denominator must be dt′, as above. Upadhyaya (equations 8a and 9) states the same results.

## Dimensional check

v u′ₓ/c² is (m s⁻¹)(m s⁻¹)/(m² s⁻²), a pure number, so it can be added to 1; the whole expression has units of velocity.

## Three-step recipe

1. Name the frame in which you want the answer: **S**.
2. Name the frame in which the object's velocity is given: **S′**.
3. Set v = velocity of S′ as measured in S; u′ = object's velocity as measured in S′; use +x as the positive direction and give each a sign. Then uₓ = (u′ₓ + v)/(1 + v u′ₓ/c²).

## First worked case

S′ moves at v = +0.8c relative to S and a particle moves at u′ₓ = +0.8c in S′. Then

$$ uₓ = (0.8c + 0.8c)/(1 + 0.64) = 1.6c/1.64 = 0.9756c $$

The Galilean sum 1.6c is impossible. The relativistic value is below c, as module 3 proves in general.

### 3. Core: limiting cases, the invariance of c and a speed limit

*Objective:* Prove that the rule reduces to the Galilean sum for small speeds, keeps c unchanged, and never produces a speed above c.

## Recap note

Limits 1 to 3 and the identity below were first worked in PH-028 module 7 and D3; this module restates them briefly with a table. The new material begins in module 4.

## Limit 1: small speeds

If v and u′ₓ are both much less than c, then v u′ₓ/c² is tiny and the denominator is nearly 1, so uₓ ≈ u′ₓ + v. This is the Galilean rule of PH-001. Check with 30 m/s and 20 m/s: v u′/c² = 600/(9 × 10¹⁶) = 6.7 × 10⁻¹⁵, so the correction is utterly negligible. This is why Galilean addition worked for centuries.

## Limit 2: light stays light

Let a light signal have u′ₓ = c in S′ and let S′ move at any v < c. Then

$$ uₓ = (c + v)/(1 + v/c) = (c + v)/[(c + v)/c] = c $$

and for u′ₓ = −c, uₓ = (−c + v)/(1 − v/c) = −c. So c is the same in S and S′, which is exactly postulate 2 (PH-028 module 3). The result is a check on the algebra, because the Lorentz transformation was built to keep c fixed.

A caution on the sources: Upadhyaya's check (eq. 10) sets "v = c" for the frame. A frame cannot move at c, since γ would be infinite (PH-028 module 5). The clean statement is u′ₓ = c with any v < c.

## Limit 3: never beyond c

Write a = u′ₓ/c and b = v/c, both with magnitude below 1. Then u/c = (a + b)/(1 + ab). Compute 1 − u²/c²:

$$ 1 − u²/c² = [(1 + ab)² − (a + b)²]/(1 + ab)² $$

Expand the numerator: 1 + 2ab + a²b² − a² − 2ab − b² = 1 − a² − b² + a²b² = (1 − a²)(1 − b²). Hence

$$ 1 − u²/c² = (1 − a²)(1 − b²)/(1 + ab)² $$

The right side is positive for |a| < 1 and |b| < 1, so |u| < c. (Same identity as PH-028 module 7; the steps are repeated here only as a recap.) Also, for a, b > 0 the denominator 1 + ab exceeds 1, so u is less than the Galilean sum u′ + v. Mathur makes both points.

## Numbers (computed in Python)

Table for v = 0.8c, the particle's u′ₓ varying:

| u′ₓ/c | Relativistic uₓ/c | Galilean sum |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.8000 | 0.8 |
| 0.2 | 0.8621 | 1.0 |
| 0.5 | 0.9286 | 1.3 |
| 0.8 | 0.9756 | 1.6 |
| 1.0 | 1.0000 | 1.8 |

Equal speeds a = b: 0.5 gives 0.8, 0.9 gives 0.99448, 0.99 gives 0.99995. However close each speed is to c, the sum stays below c.

### 4. Core: components at right angles and the direction of a velocity

*Objective:* Derive u′_y and u′_z, find the speed and direction of a velocity in both frames, and check that light keeps speed c in two dimensions.

## Why the transverse components change

Along y the transformation gives dy′ = dy: lengths across the motion are unchanged (PH-028 module 4, assumption 3). But dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²) is *not* dt. Dividing:

$$ u′_y = dy′/dt′ = dy/[γ(dt − v dx/c²)] = u_y/[γ(1 − v uₓ/c²)] $$

and in the same way u′_z = u_z/[γ(1 − v uₓ/c²)]. So a velocity component across the motion changes even though the length across the motion does not. Clock rates and the simultaneity effects (PH-028 module 6) both enter through dt′. The inverse (v → −v) is

$$ uₓ = (u′ₓ + v)/(1 + v u′ₓ/c²),  u_y = u′_y/[γ(1 + v u′ₓ/c²)],  u_z = u′_z/[γ(1 + v u′ₓ/c²)] $$

Source check: Upadhyaya eq. (8b) and (8c) and Mathur section 3.7.5 give these results. Upadhyaya's answer to its exercise 29 prints the last component as 7.2 for the input u′ = (3, 4, 2) m/s at 0.8c; the rule gives u_z = 2 × 0.6 = 1.2 (γ = 5/3, 1/γ = 0.6), the same as u_y = 4 × 0.6 = 2.4. Treat 7.2 as a slip and quote 1.2.

## Direction and speed in each frame

Suppose in S′ a particle has speed u′ at angle θ′ to +x′ (u′ₓ = u′ cosθ′, u′_y = u′ sinθ′). Then in S, with D = 1 + v u′ cosθ′/c²,

$$ uₓ = (u′ cosθ′ + v)/D,  u_y = u′ sinθ′/(γD) $$

and the trajectory angle θ satisfies

$$ tanθ = u_y/uₓ = u′ sinθ′/[γ(u′ cosθ′ + v)] $$

D cancels. Note the γ in the denominator: it is the clock factor from dt′.

## A projectile fired across the motion

S′ is a ship at v = 0.6c (γ = 1.25). It fires a projectile along y′ at u′_y = 0.8c, u′ₓ = 0. In S: uₓ = 0.6c, u_y = 0.8c/1.25 = 0.64c. The speed is √(0.36 + 0.4096) c = 0.8773c and the angle is arctan(0.64/0.60) = 46.85° from +x. The Galilean answer (0.6c, 0.8c) has speed c and angle 53.13°. Notice that the relativistic speed is *not* √(v² + u′²).

## Light along y′ (the two-dimensional check, also in PH-028 module 7)

For u′ = (0, c): uₓ = v, u_y = c/γ, so u² = v² + c²(1 − β²) = c². Light keeps speed c in S while travelling at an angle with cosθ = β, which is the seed of the aberration formula in module 6.

## A source check

Mathur Example 33: a particle moves at 0.8c at 60° to x in S; S′ moves at v = 0.4c. Components in S: (0.4c, 0.6928c). Then u′ₓ = (0.4 − 0.4)c/(1 − 0.16) = 0, and u′_y = 0.6928c/(γ × 0.84) with γ = 1/√(1 − 0.16) = 1.0911, giving 0.7559c, along y′ only, in agreement with the source.

### 5. Core: the three verified past-paper tasks, worked

*Objective:* Solve the 2019, 2022 and 2024 velocity-addition questions with the recipe, full algebra and checks.

## 2019 Q4(a), 15 marks: two β-particles, 0.9c each

A source R emits β-particles A and B in opposite directions, each at 0.9c relative to R. Find the velocity of B relative to A.

**Set-up.** Frame S = A's rest frame (the frame where the answer is wanted). Frame S′ = R's frame. Take +x pointing from A towards B. In R's frame A moves at −0.9c, so R moves at v = +0.9c as seen from A. B moves at u′ₓ = +0.9c in R's frame.

$$ uₓ = (0.9c + 0.9c)/(1 + 0.81) = 1.8c/1.81 = 0.99448c $$

With c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s this is 2.983 × 10⁸ m/s. Mathur Example 22 prints 2.982 × 10⁸, a small rounding slip (1.8/1.81 × 3 = 2.9834). Check: 1 − u/c = 0.0055, so u is less than c; the Galilean value 1.8c is impossible.

## 2022 Q4(c), 15 marks: equal speeds, relative speed 0.7c

Two spaceships approach each other with the same speed w (as measured on Earth); their relative speed is 0.7c. Find w.

**Set-up.** Take +x from ship 1 towards ship 2. S = ship 1's frame, S′ = Earth. In Earth's frame ship 1 moves at +w, so Earth moves at v = −w as seen from ship 1; ship 2 moves at u′ₓ = −w in Earth's frame. Then uₓ = (−w − w)/(1 + w²/c²) = −2w/(1 + w²/c²). The minus sign only says that ship 2 approaches ship 1. With β = w/c the relative speed is 2β/(1 + β²) in units of c; set it equal to 0.7:

$$ 2β/(1 + β²) = 0.7 $$

so 0.7β² − 2β + 0.7 = 0, giving β = [2 ± √(4 − 1.96)]/1.4 = (2 ± 1.42829)/1.4. The root above 1 (2.4488) is unphysical; the answer is **β = 0.40837**, so w = 0.408c = 1.225 × 10⁸ m/s. Check: 2(0.40837)/(1 + 0.16677) = 0.81673/1.16677 = 0.7000. The naive Galilean answer w = 0.35c is wrong.

## 2024 Q4(c)(i), 15 marks: three frames

O′ moves at V relative to O, and O″ at V′ relative to O′, both along +x. The composition of two Lorentz transformations is derived in PH-028-D5 (see also PH-028 module 7); the full derivation is not repeated here. The velocity-addition view: the frame O″ moves relative to O at

$$ V″ = (V + V′)/(1 + VV′/c²) $$

since a point at rest in O″ has u′ₓ = V′ in O′, and module 2's rule gives its velocity in O. Check with V = 0.6c, V′ = 0.7c: V″ = 1.3c/1.42 = 0.91549c, and γ″ = 2.4855; the product γ_V γ_V′ (1 + VV′/c²) = 1.25 × 1.4003 × 1.42 = 2.4855, so the direct transformation has the same γ as the composition. The question asks for the transformation equations, so the final answer is x″ = γ″(x − V″t), t″ = γ″(t − V″x/c²), with y″ = y, z″ = z, as PH-028-D5 shows.

### 6. Core: relativistic aberration of light, derived

*Objective:* Derive the relation between the direction of a light ray in S and in S′, with sign conventions, and state the result in terms of the direction to the source.

## Source note

The two source extracts do not contain aberration. This module derives it from module 4, which the sources do support. Treat the aberration statements as standard textbook content (flagged for review).

## Assumptions and conventions

S and S′ as in module 1; S′ (the observer) moves at +v along +x. A photon in S travels at angle θ to +x: uₓ = c cosθ, u_y = c sinθ. Angles run anticlockwise from +x. Here θ is the direction of **propagation**.

## Derivation

Use the S-to-S′ rules of modules 2 and 4 with uₓ = c cosθ, u_y = c sinθ: u′ₓ = (c cosθ − v)/(1 − v cosθ/c) and u′_y = c sinθ/[γ(1 − v cosθ/c)]. Let β = v/c and divide through by c. If the photon's velocity in S′ is c(cosθ′, sinθ′), then

$$ cosθ′ = (cosθ − β)/(1 − β cosθ),  sinθ′ = sinθ/[γ(1 − β cosθ)] $$

**Check that the speed is c.** Compute cos²θ′ + sin²θ′ over the common denominator (1 − β cosθ)²: numerator = (cosθ − β)² + (1 − β²) sin²θ = cos²θ − 2β cosθ + β² + sin²θ − β² sin²θ = 1 − 2β cosθ + β²(1 − sin²θ) = 1 − 2β cosθ + β² cos²θ = (1 − β cosθ)². So cos²θ′ + sin²θ′ = 1: the photon moves at c in S′, as it must.

**Half-angle form.** Since 1 + cosθ′ = (1 − β cosθ + cosθ − β)/(1 − β cosθ) = (1 − β)(1 + cosθ)/(1 − β cosθ),

$$ tan(θ′/2) = sinθ′/(1 + cosθ′) = [sinθ/γ]/[(1 − β)(1 + cosθ)] = [1/(γ(1 − β))] tan(θ/2) $$

and 1/[γ(1 − β)] = √(1 − β²)/(1 − β) = √[(1 + β)/(1 − β)]. So tan(θ′/2) = √[(1 + β)/(1 − β)] tan(θ/2).

## In terms of the source direction

An observer points a telescope along the **line of sight**, opposite to propagation: α = θ + 180°. Then cosα = −cosθ and cosα′ = −cosθ′. Substituting:

$$ cosα′ = (cosα + β)/(1 + β cosα),  tan(α′/2) = √[(1 − β)/(1 + β)] tan(α/2) $$

Here α is the angle between +x (the observer's velocity) and the direction to the source in S, and α′ is the same angle in S′. Since the root is below 1, α′ < α: **the source appears displaced toward the direction of the observer's motion**.

## Limits and numbers

For β ≪ 1: cosα′ ≈ cosα + β sin²α, so α′ − α ≈ −β sinα, a shift of size β sinα radians, greatest at α = 90°. For β → 1: tan(α′/2) → 0 for all α ≠ 180°, so all sources crowd into the forward direction. Worked: β = 0.6, α = 60°: cosα′ = (0.5 + 0.6)/(1 + 0.3) = 0.84615, α′ = 32.20°; check with sinα′ = 0.8660/(1.25 × 1.3) = 0.5329, and sin 32.20° = 0.5329.

### 7. Core: stellar aberration, the classical contrast and the headlight effect

*Objective:* Compute the aberration of a star for the Earth's orbital speed, contrast the classical formula, and derive the headlight cone for a moving source.

## Stellar aberration (flagged: standard knowledge, not in the extracts)

Take S as the frame of the star (the Sun's frame, approximately) and S′ as the Earth, moving at v ≈ 29.8 km/s (the mean orbital speed; verify the value). For a star whose line of sight is at right angles to Earth's velocity, α = 90°, so cosα′ = β and α′ = 90° − δ with sinδ = β. With β = 29.8 × 10³/3.00 × 10⁸ = 9.93 × 10⁻⁵ (dimensionless), so δ ≈ 9.93 × 10⁻⁵ rad, the shift is

$$ δ ≈ β = 9.93 × 10⁻⁵ rad × 206 265 arcsec/rad ≈ 20.5 arcsec $$

This matches the accepted constant of aberration (about 20.5 arcsec, to be verified against an astronomy reference). Over a year the velocity direction rotates, so the apparent position traces a small ellipse of semi-major axis about 20.5 arcsec. Parallax is a different effect (it depends on distance and is under 1 arcsec for any star; verify) and is shifted by 90° in phase.

## The classical formula and why it does not decide

Classically (light as a stream in the star's frame), the light velocity in S is −c(cosα, sinα). In S′ we subtract v from the x part: the vector towards the source becomes (c cosα + v, c sinα), so

$$ tanα′ = sinα/(cosα + β) $$

At α = 90°, tanδ = β. Relativistic: sinδ = β. At α = 90° and β = 9.93 × 10⁻⁵ the two differ by about β³/2 radian (asin β ≈ β + β³/6 against atan β ≈ β − β³/3), utterly invisible. Comparing the two formulas for all α with this β in Python, the largest difference is about 0.0005 arcsec (about 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ rad, of order β²; it occurs at α near 45° and 135°, with opposite signs, since Δα′ ≈ (β²/4) sin 2α′). So the classical contrast does not show up at this speed; it grows as β² (for general α) and becomes large only at speeds approaching c. This is the reason stellar aberration alone cannot test relativity; the derivation here simply shows the relativistic formula includes the classical one.

## Headlight effect

Now let the *source* be at rest in S′ and the lab be S. By the inverse of module 6: cosθ = (cosθ′ + β)/(1 + β cosθ′) and sinθ = sinθ′/[γ(1 + β cosθ′)], with θ the angle in the lab from +x (source velocity), θ′ the angle in the source frame, source moving at +v.

Photons emitted at θ′ = 90° (sideways in the source frame) have cosθ = β and sinθ = 1/γ in the lab. If the source emits isotropically in its own rest frame, every photon in its forward hemisphere (θ′ < 90°), which is half of all photons, therefore lands inside a cone of half-angle θ = arccos β. Numbers: β = 0.6 gives 53.13°; 0.9 gives 25.84°; 0.99 gives 8.11° (close to 1/γ = 8.08° for small angles); 0.999 gives 2.56°. So a source moving close to c squeezes its light into a narrow forward beam: the headlight effect.

### 8. Master: Fizeau's experiment, composition and what is still to come

*Objective:* Show that velocity addition reproduces Fresnel's partial drag for light in moving water, and see composition of velocities in a neater form.

## Fizeau and moving water (flagged: not in the extracts)

Light travels at c/n in water at rest (n = refractive index). Let the water flow with speed w along the light's path, in the lab S. The water's rest frame is S′, moving at +w. In S′ the light has u′ₓ = c/n. Module 2 gives, in the lab,

$$ u = (c/n + w)/(1 + w/(nc)) $$

**Expand to first order in w/c.** Write 1/(1 + w/(nc)) ≈ 1 − w/(nc). Then u ≈ (c/n + w)(1 − w/(nc)) = c/n + w − w/n² − w²/(nc), and dropping the w² term,

$$ u ≈ c/n + w(1 − 1/n²) $$

The lab speed is c/n plus only a *fraction* of the water's speed, f = 1 − 1/n². The classical Galilean sum would give f = 1 (full drag); a stationary ether would give f = 0. For water, n = 4/3 gives f = 1 − 9/16 = 0.4375. With w = 7 m/s, the shift is 7 × 0.4375 = 3.0625 m/s; Python gives the exact result as 3.0624999 m/s. Fizeau's experiment (1851) is described in standard accounts as measuring this fraction by interference of two beams, one with the flow and one against; the numerical value of the flow speed used there is not taken from the extracts and should be verified. Careful treatments of Fizeau-type measurements also include a small dispersion term (it involves how n varies with wavelength; verify before use). The relativity point is that the old Fresnel partial-drag factor comes from the addition rule with no ether assumed.

## Composition in a neater form (extension)

If V″ = (V + V′)/(1 + VV′/c²), then with tanh φ = β (defining the *rapidity* φ), tanh(φ₁ + φ₂) = (tanh φ₁ + tanh φ₂)/(1 + tanh φ₁ tanh φ₂). So velocities compose by adding rapidities. Check: φ(0.6) + φ(0.7) = 0.6931 + 0.8673 = 1.5604, and tanh 1.5604 = 0.91549, the same as module 5. This is an extension beyond the extracts (verify), useful to see why the composition law is associative.

## Doppler: to come

The frequency shift of light between frames is the relativistic Doppler effect. It is to come on Day 12 (PH-031), not yet written. Aberration and Doppler are two faces of how a light wave transforms; Mathur's chapter treats the Doppler effect in its later sections, which are not relied on here.

## Frequently confused

- **Aberration vs refraction**: aberration changes the apparent direction because of the observer's motion; refraction changes direction at a medium boundary.
- **Which frame owns the speed of light**: both; speed c is unchanged, direction and frequency change.
- **Relative velocity vs closing speed**: the speed of A in B's frame is generally not the difference of their lab speeds; the sum of lab speeds (the closing speed in the lab) can exceed c without contradiction, because it is not a velocity measured in any single frame.

### 9. Exam: the three verified questions and the answer format

*Objective:* Recognise the pattern of velocity-addition questions, write a structured answer, and avoid the common sign and frame traps.

## What the three questions ask

- **2019 Q4(a), 15 marks**: two particles emitted in opposite directions at 0.9c from a source; find B's velocity relative to A. A relative-velocity problem: the frame must be A's rest frame (module 5).
- **2022 Q4(c), 15 marks**: two spaceships approach at equal Earth speeds, relative speed 0.7c; find each Earth speed. The *inverse* problem: solve a quadratic and reject β > 1.

## Five-part answer

1. **Set-up**: sketch S and S′ with +x, name which frame measures what.
2. **Law**: write u = (u′ + v)/(1 + vu′/c²) or derive it from the Lorentz differentials (module 2).
3. **Substitute with signs** and keep fractions of c.
4. **Result**: symbols first, then number with units.
5. **Checks**: below c; Galilean limit; dimensions; for angles, speed unchanged for light.

## Traps

- **Signs**: S′ moves at +v, so the rule for S from S′ data has +v; mixing the forward and inverse rules is the commonest error.
- **Wrong frame for 'relative velocity'**: the answer must be measured in the rest frame of the observer named.
- **Closing speed**: 0.9c + 0.9c = 1.8c is the separation rate in the source frame, not a relative velocity of one particle in another's frame.
- **Using Galilean for transverse components**: u′_y ≠ u_y.
- **Putting v = c** in a frame transformation.
- **Aberration direction**: α′ < α (the source shifts forward). Mixing propagation angle θ with line-of-sight angle α flips this.
- **Mixed constants**: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s throughout, or the slip in the printed Mathur Example 22 appears.

### Notes: Definitions

- Velocity addition: the rule converting a velocity measured in one inertial frame to another.
- Aberration of light: the dependence of the observed direction of a light ray on the observer's velocity.
- Line of sight: direction from observer to source; opposite to the direction of propagation.
- Headlight effect: forward beaming of the light of a source moving at relativistic speed.

### Notes: Key equations (S′ moves at +v along x of S; β = v/c, γ = 1/√(1 − β²))

- u′ₓ = (uₓ − v)/(1 − v uₓ/c²), u′_y = u_y/[γ(1 − v uₓ/c²)], u′_z = u_z/[γ(1 − v uₓ/c²)].
- uₓ = (u′ₓ + v)/(1 + v u′ₓ/c²), u_y = u′_y/[γ(1 + v u′ₓ/c²)], u_z = u′_z/[γ(1 + v u′ₓ/c²)].
- tanθ = u′ sinθ′/[γ(u′ cosθ′ + v)] for a particle trajectory direction in S from S′ data.
- Aberration: cosα′ = (cosα + β)/(1 + β cosα); tan(α′/2) = √[(1 − β)/(1 + β)] tan(α/2).
- Fizeau: u ≈ c/n + w(1 − 1/n²) to first order in w/c.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton (module 2 and PH-030-D1)

- Differentiate the Lorentz transformation; divide dx′ by dt′; cancel γ; use the inverse for the other direction.
- Transverse components keep dy′ = dy but dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²), hence the factor 1/[γ(…)].
- Aberration is the same rule applied to c(cosθ, sinθ) (PH-030-D2).

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Relative velocity of B in A's frame versus the closing speed in the lab frame.
- Propagation angle θ versus the line-of-sight angle α = θ + 180°.
- Aberration (observer's motion, direction) versus parallax (distance, 90° out of phase) versus refraction (medium).
- Frame transformation speed v (must be below c) versus particle speed u′ (may equal c for light).

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- u′ = c in any direction gives |u| = c, but the direction changes.
- A frame cannot move at v = c; Upadhyaya's check with v = c is a shorthand for u′ = c with v < c.
- At β → 1 aberration collapses all sources into the forward direction, except 180°.
- The first-order Fizeau expansion drops terms of order w²/c.

      - Apply D1: u′ₓ = (c cosθ − v)/(1 − β cosθ), u′_y = c sinθ/[γ(1 − β cosθ)]. Divide by c and write u′ = c(cosθ′, sinθ′).
      - cosθ′ = (cosθ − β)/(1 − β cosθ); sinθ′ = sinθ/[γ(1 − β cosθ)].
      - Check: (cosθ − β)² + (1 − β²) sin²θ = cos²θ − 2β cosθ + β² + sin²θ − β² sin²θ = 1 − 2β cosθ + β² cos²θ = (1 − β cosθ)², so cos²θ′ + sin²θ′ = 1.
      - Convert to α: cosα = −cosθ, cosα′ = −cosθ′ so cosα′ = (cosα + β)/(1 + β cosα).
      - Half-angle: 1 + cosα′ = (1 + β)(1 + cosα)/(1 + β cosα), 1 − cosα′ = (1 − β)(1 − cosα)/(1 + β cosα), so tan²(α′/2) = [(1 − β)/(1 + β)] tan²(α/2); hence tan(α′/2) = √[(1 − β)/(1 + β)] tan(α/2).
      - Direction: the root is below 1 for β > 0, so α′ < α; sources shift toward the direction of motion.
      - Stellar case: α = 90° gives cosα′ = β. Write α′ = 90° − δ, so sinδ = β. For β = 9.93 × 10⁻⁵, δ = 20.5 arcsec.
    - **checks:**
      - Limit β → 0: α′ → α, the shift ≈ β sinα radians.
      - Limit β → 1: α′ → 0 for every α except 180°.
      - Frames: α is in S (star frame), α′ in S′ (observer frame).
      - Classical comparison: tanα′ = sinα/(cosα + β); at 90°, tanδ = β; at α = 90° they agree through β² (they differ by about β³/2); for general α they differ at order β².
    - **traps:**
      - Using propagation angle θ and line-of-sight angle α interchangeably (flips the sign of the shift).
      - Taking the star to move.

### Last-week summary

- dx′ = γ(dx − v dt), dt′ = γ(dt − v dx/c²): u′ₓ = (uₓ − v)/(1 − vuₓ/c²); inverse has +v.
- Transverse: dy′ = dy but dt′ changes, so u_y = u′_y/[γ(1 + vu′ₓ/c²)].
- 1 − u²/c² = (1 − a²)(1 − b²)/(1 + ab)² > 0, so |u| < c; u′ₓ = c gives u = c.
- 0.8c ⊕ 0.8c = 0.9756c; 0.9c ⊕ 0.9c = 0.99448c; 2β/(1 + β²) = 0.7 gives β = 0.408.
- Composition: V″ = (V + V′)/(1 + VV′/c²), γ″ = γ_V γ_V′ (1 + VV′/c²).
