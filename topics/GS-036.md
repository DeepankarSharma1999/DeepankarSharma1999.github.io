## GS-036: Founding of INC and moderate phase

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words you need: association, petition, memorial, resolution, session, safety valve

The topic has a small vocabulary of political methods. Learn it before the names.

- **Political association**: a club or society formed to speak for a group or a cause, such as the Indian Association. Early associations were local or regional and often led by wealthy people; later ones were led by the educated middle class (Spectrum).
- **Petition and memorial**: a written request or complaint sent to the authorities. A **memorial** is a formal petition. Early associations sent long petitions to the British Parliament.
- **Resolution**: a formal statement of a demand adopted by vote at a meeting. The Congress passed resolutions each year.
- **Session**: one annual meeting of the Congress, held in December in a different place each time.
- **Constitutional agitation**: protest within the law, through speeches, meetings, petitions and the press, and not through force or law-breaking.
- **Public opinion**: the shared views of the informed public, which the Moderates tried to create and then present to the rulers.
- **Safety valve**: a device that lets pressure escape before an explosion. As a theory about the Congress it says the body was meant to let off discontent (module 5).
- **Moderate and Extremist**: names used in the books for the early leaders (1885 to 1905) and the more militant leaders who followed.

**Reading rule.** When a question says 'prayer and petition', it means the Moderate method: ask politely, present facts, expect the rulers to respond.

### 1. Orientation: the Congress and the Moderate phase on one page

*Objective:* Place the foundation of the Congress in 1885 and the Moderate phase (1885 to 1905) in a timeline with the main names, methods and theories.

The Indian National Congress was not the first political organisation in India (Spectrum), but it was the first with an all-India reach. It met first in Bombay in December 1885, with 72 delegates, under the presidency of W.C. Bonnerjee. From 1885 to 1905 the leaders were the **Moderates**: liberal, constitutional and hopeful that the British would reform if shown the facts.

## The three things to remember

1. **Origin.** An all-India body came because the educated middle class, linked by railways, English and the press, met new insults (Lytton's measures, the Ilbert Bill). A retired British official, A.O. Hume, helped.
2. **Theories.** Was it a safety valve for British interests (the popular view), a conspiracy (R.P. Dutt in Spectrum), or a lightning conductor used by Indians (Bipan Chandra's observation as Spectrum records it)? Module 5.
3. **Verdict.** Pioneers who built a national idea and an economic critique, but with a narrow base and little result.

**Link to the previous lessons.** The forces behind this are in GS-035 (press and education) and GS-034 (reform movements); this lesson starts where they stop, with the organisation.

### 2. Foundation: why an all-India body was needed by the 1880s

*Objective:* Give the causes of the growth of nationalism and the 1870s and 1880s provocations that made the Congress possible.

Spectrum, in chapter 10, calls modern Indian nationalism a product of a mix of factors rather than only a reaction to the British: a worldwide upsurge of the idea of nationhood and self-determination after the French Revolution, the Indian Renaissance, an offshoot of British modernisation, and a strong reaction to imperialist policies. It groups the causes as follows.

- **Understanding the contradiction of interests.** People came to see colonial rule as the main cause of India's economic backwardness, and that peasants, artisans, workers and the educated shared a stake against it.
- **Political, administrative and economic unification.** The British created a larger state than that of the Mauryas or the Mughals: a professional civil service, a unified judiciary and common law, and railways, roads and the telegraph. Crop failure in one region affected prices in another, and leaders from different regions could meet and exchange ideas.
- **Western thought and education**, and the **press and literature.** These are taught in GS-035 and are not repeated here. Spectrum records about 169 vernacular newspapers in 1877 with a circulation near 100,000.
- **Rediscovery of India's past** through the work of scholars such as Max Mueller and R.G. Bhandarkar, which gave self-respect; the **socio-religious reform movements** (GS-034), which tried to remove divisions.
- **Rise of the middle-class intelligentsia**, which Spectrum says gave leadership to the Congress at every stage; and the example of movements elsewhere (South America, Greece, Italy, Ireland).

## The provocations of the 1870s and 1880s

Spectrum's heading is 'Reactionary Policies and Racial Arrogance of Rulers'.

- **Lytton's measures:** the Indian Civil Service age limit reduced from 21 to 19 (1876); the grand Delhi Durbar of 1877 during a severe famine; the Vernacular Press Act (1878); the Arms Act (1878).
- **The Ilbert Bill** (1883): Ripon's government wished to remove judicial disqualification based on race and give Indian members of the covenanted civil service the same powers as Europeans. After fierce European opposition Ripon had to modify the bill. NCERT Class VIII says the white opposition forced the government to withdraw it; Spectrum says it was modified so as almost to defeat the purpose. Either way, Indians saw that justice could not be expected where Europeans' interests were involved, and they learned how to agitate.

NCERT Class VIII adds that the need for an all-India organisation of educated Indians had been felt since 1880, and that the Ilbert Bill controversy deepened the desire.

**Why this matters.** Before 1885 there were many associations, but each spoke for a region or a class. The factors above turned local grievances into a national one, and the provocations gave it a sense of urgency.

### 3. Core: the associations before the Congress

*Objective:* List the main pre-Congress associations with founders, places and dates, and avoid the 2017 Prelims traps.

Spectrum says the political associations of the early half of the nineteenth century were dominated by wealthy and aristocratic people, were local or regional, and sent long petitions to Parliament for administrative reform, association of Indians with the administration and spread of education. Those of the later half were led by the educated middle class and had wider aims.

## Why the Indian Association matters most

Younger nationalists under Banerjea and Bose, discontented with the conservative and pro-landlord policies of the British Indian Association, founded it to create strong public opinion and unify Indians in a common programme. It kept its fee low to draw poorer sections, opened branches in Bengal and beyond, campaigned in 1877 against the cut in the ICS age limit announced in 1876, the Arms Act and the Vernacular Press Act, and demanded simultaneous ICS examinations in England and India. Its all-India conference in Calcutta on 28 to 30 December 1883 drew over a hundred delegates, making it, in Spectrum's phrase, a forerunner of the Congress.

## The campaigns

Spectrum's list includes import duty on cotton (1875), Indianisation of the services (1878-79), opposition to Lytton's Afghan adventure, the Arms Act and the Vernacular Press Act, the right to join volunteer corps, protests on plantation labour and the Inland Emigration Act, support for the Ilbert Bill and a campaign in Britain.

### 4. Core: the foundation of the Congress, Bombay 1885

*Objective:* Describe the 1885 session, the role of Hume and the Indian National Conference, the early leaders and the Congress's stated aims.

## The founding

Spectrum: in the later 1870s and early 1880s the ground was ready for an all-India organisation. The final shape was given by **A.O. Hume**, a retired English civil servant, who mobilised leading intellectuals and, with their cooperation, organised the first session of the Indian National Congress at the **Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay, in December 1885**. It was attended by **72 delegates** and presided over by **Womesh Chandra (W.C.) Bonnerjee**. NCERT Class VIII agrees on the 72 delegates and December 1885, names the early leaders as Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji, W.C. Bonnerji, Surendranath Banerji, Romesh Chandra Dutt and S. Subramania Iyer, among others, largely from Bombay and Calcutta, and says Naoroji, a businessman and publicist settled in London and for a time a member of the British Parliament, guided the younger nationalists. Hume, in NCERT's words, 'also played a part' in bringing Indians from the regions together.

Flagged standard details not in the extracts (verify): the session ran from 28 to 31 December 1885, and the venue was first planned at Poona but shifted to Bombay.

## The Indian National Conference

Before this, Spectrum says, two sessions of the **Indian National Conference** had been held in 1883 and 1885, with representatives from all major towns, and Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose were its main architects. The Indian Association's all-India conference of December 1883 (module 3) and the Conference are two names for the same Calcutta-based effort in Spectrum's account; check a standard source for the exact link (verify). Spectrum says the Indian Association merged with the Congress in 1886. Spectrum's summary adds that Hume's earlier body, the **Indian National Union**, became the Indian National Congress (the name appears only there; verify).

## The early sessions and leaders

Hereafter the Congress met every year in December at a different place. Spectrum names as early presidents Dadabhai Naoroji (three times), Badruddin Tyabji, Pherozeshah Mehta, P. Anandacharlu, Surendranath Banerjea, Romesh Chandra Dutt, Ananda Mohan Bose and Gopal Krishna Gokhale. Prominent leaders included M.G. Ranade, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Dinshaw Wacha and others. In 1890 Kadambini Ganguly, one of the first two women graduates of Calcutta University (Spectrum says the first; verify), addressed the Congress, a symbol of its commitment to women's place in public life.

**A claim to represent everyone.** NCERT Class VIII shows the Congress sought from the start to speak for all Indians. A newspaper, the Indian Mirror, called it in 1886 the nucleus of a future Parliament, and Tyabji said in 1887 that it was composed of representatives of all communities.

## Aims (Spectrum)

Condensed from eight aims: to found a democratic nationalist movement; to politicise and politically educate people; to establish a headquarters for the movement; to promote friendship among nationalist workers from different regions; to develop an anti-colonial ideology; to formulate popular demands and unify people around a common economic and political programme; to build national unity beyond religion, caste or province; and to nurture Indian nationhood.

**Memory hook.** Bombay 1885, 72 delegates, Bonnerjee, Hume as the catalyst, December every year.

### 5. Core: the safety-valve theory and its rivals

*Objective:* Explain the safety-valve, conspiracy and lightning-conductor views and give reasons the safety-valve idea is questioned.

Why did a retired British civil servant help form a body that criticised British rule? Spectrum records three answers.

## 1. The safety-valve theory

There is a theory that Hume formed the Congress to act as a **safety valve**, releasing growing Indian discontent before it burst. To that end he persuaded Lord Dufferin, the Viceroy, not to obstruct the formation of the Congress. Spectrum says extremist leaders such as Lala Lajpat Rai believed it. (Spectrum does not name its source; standard accounts trace it to the 1913 biography of Hume by William Wedderburn, verify.)

## 2. The conspiracy theory

Spectrum calls the Marxist historian's 'conspiracy theory' an offspring of the safety-valve notion: R.P. Dutt held that the Congress was born out of a conspiracy to abort a popular uprising in India, and that the bourgeois leaders were party to it.

## 3. The lightning-conductor view

Modern Indian historians dispute the safety-valve idea. In their opinion the Congress represented the urge of politically conscious Indians to set up a national body to express their political and economic demands. If Indians had convened such a body on their own, the officials would have opposed it, and it would not have been allowed to form. As Bipan Chandra observes, in Spectrum's account, the early leaders used Hume as a **lightning conductor**, a catalyst to bring nationalist forces together even under the guise of a safety valve. (Spectrum's summary box lists the lightning-conductor theory against Gokhale's name while the text attributes the observation to Bipan Chandra; flagged verify. The Gokhale attribution likely reflects his 1913 remark that no Indian could have started the Congress without official suppression, verify.)

## Testing the safety-valve idea against the sources

Do not reject the theory only because Spectrum is critical; test it against evidence:

1. **The agenda was Indian.** The Congress's aims and early resolutions were about civil service examinations, councils, land revenue and military spending (NCERT Class VIII, modules 6 and 7).
2. **The British reaction does not fit.** Spectrum says the government was hostile from the beginning and stiffened after 1887; Dufferin later used the phrase 'a factory of sedition' (module 8). A true safety valve would not have been fought.
3. **There were Indian forerunners.** The Indian Association's 1883 conference (module 3) and the Conference of 1883 and 1885 show that Indians had begun to build an all-India body.
4. **There is truth in the cover.** Hume's presence and Dufferin's non-obstruction gave the body legal and political cover in its early years.

## A balanced reading

Hume was a catalyst and a shield, not the author of the Congress's aims. 'Safety valve' fits the cover story, not the content. The theory also tells you something about the later Extremists: they believed it in part because the Moderates' method of petitioning looked like begging (module 6).

**Exam note.** Name all three views with their holders (Lajpat Rai, R.P. Dutt, Bipan Chandra) and give at least two points against the safety valve.

### 6. Core: Moderate ideology and methods

*Objective:* State the Moderates' beliefs and their two-pronged method of prayer and petition and constitutional agitation.

## Who the Moderates were

Spectrum: Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, D.E. Wacha, W.C. Bonnerjee and Surendranath Banerjea, who dominated the Congress from 1885 to 1905, were staunch believers in liberalism and moderate politics, and came to be labelled **Moderates** to distinguish them from the neo-nationalists, the **Extremists**, of the early twentieth century. GS-037 (Extremist politics and Swadeshi movement, Day 15, not yet written) covers the next phase.

## The core belief

The Moderates believed the British basically wanted to be just to Indians but did not know the real conditions. If public opinion could be created in the country and demands presented through resolutions, petitions and meetings, the authorities would concede them gradually (Spectrum). NCERT Class VIII agrees: they thought the British respected ideals of freedom and justice, so the task was to express demands and make the government aware of Indian feeling.

## The two-pronged method

1. **Create public opinion at home**: arouse consciousness and national spirit, educate and unite people on common political questions. NCERT: they published newspapers, wrote articles, gave speeches and sent representatives to different regions to mobilise opinion.
2. **Persuade the British government and British public opinion** to introduce reforms on the lines laid down by the nationalists.

Their method was 'prayer and petition', and if that failed, constitutional agitation within the law. Ranade's remark to Gokhale in 1891, as Spectrum records it, shows the logic: the memorials were nominally addressed to the government but in reality to the people, to teach them how to think about politics, a task to be done for many years without expecting quick results.

## Work in Britain

A British Committee of the Congress was set up in London in 1889, with the journal *India* as its organ. Naoroji spent much of his life and income campaigning for India's case there. In 1890 it was decided to hold a session of the Congress in London in 1892, but, Spectrum says, owing to the British elections of 1891 (the UK general election was actually held in 1892; verify) the plan was postponed and never revived.

## Political position

The Moderates held that political connection with Britain was in India's interest at that stage, and that the time was not ripe for a direct challenge to British rule. They therefore tried to make colonial rule as close to national rule as possible. Their goal widened with time: Naoroji (1904), Gokhale (1905) and Tilak (1906) each asked for self-government on the model of Canada and Australia (Spectrum dates; Naoroji also declared swaraj the goal as Congress president at Calcutta in 1906, verify).

## What critics said

NCERT Class VIII records that by the 1890s leaders in Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab, Bipin Chandra Pal, Tilak and Lajpat Rai, criticised the 'politics of prayers', stressed self-reliance and constructive work, and argued that people must fight for swaraj and not rely on the government's good intentions (Tilak's slogan is in that account). This is the seed of the later split (GS-038 and GS-037).

**Reading rule.** 'Moderate' describes method, not weakness of feeling: their economic critique of British rule was sharp (module 7).

### 7. Core: demands, the economic critique and constitutional work

*Objective:* State the Moderates' economic, constitutional, administrative and civil-rights demands and what they achieved.

Spectrum groups the Moderates' contribution into four heads.

## 1. Economic critique of British rule

Naoroji, R.C. Dutt, Wacha and others analysed the political economy of British rule and put forward the **drain theory** to explain exploitation (the full theory is GS-030, Day 15, not yet written; here it is named only). They opposed turning a self-sufficient economy into a colonial one, a supplier of raw materials and food, an importer of finished goods and a field for British capital. They demanded reduction of land revenue, abolition of the salt tax, better conditions for plantation labour, reduced military spending, and tariff protection and aid for modern industry. NCERT Class VIII adds that the early Congress declared that British rule had produced poverty and famines, that increases in land revenue had impoverished peasants and zamindars (link: GS-029), and that grain exports had caused shortages; it asked for lower revenue, lower military spending and more irrigation, and passed resolutions on the salt tax, Indian labourers abroad and forest dwellers. Naoroji's book *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* is the NCERT example.

## 2. Constitutional reforms and the councils

Spectrum says the legislative councils had no real power till 1920, and that the Imperial Legislative Council of 1861 was an impotent body: only 45 Indians were nominated in 30 years, most wealthy and loyal. From 1885 to 1892 the nationalists demanded **expansion** (more Indian members) and **reform** (more powers, especially over finance).

**The Indian Councils Act 1892** is named here as the British response, with Spectrum's summary flagged for later checking in a standard text and in GS-050 (Constitutional developments 1909-1947, being written the same day; see that lesson):

- Spectrum: non-official members in the Imperial Council raised to between 10 and 16, some recommended by universities, municipalities, zamindars and chambers of commerce; the budget could be discussed and questions asked (standard texts also list district boards and call the 10 to 16 additional members; verify).
- Limits: officials kept their majority; the budget could not be voted or amended; supplementary questions were not allowed.
- The Moderates criticised it and demanded an elected majority and control of the budget, with the slogan 'No taxation without representation'.

The Moderates also turned the councils into platforms for criticising policy, with Mehta and Gokhale prominent in this.

## 3. General administrative reforms

Indianisation of the services (on grounds of cost, of the drain through salaries and pensions remitted home, and of fairness); separation of judicial from executive functions; criticism of a tyrannical bureaucracy and an expensive judiciary; criticism of an aggressive foreign policy (Burma, Afghanistan, tribals in the north-west); more spending on health, sanitation, education, irrigation and agricultural banks; and better treatment of Indian labour abroad. NCERT adds simultaneous civil service examinations in India and in London.

## 4. Defence of civil rights

They campaigned for speech, thought, association and a free press. The arrest of Tilak and others in 1897 and the deportation of the Natu brothers without trial caused public outrage (Spectrum). Press and education as drivers are in GS-035.

**Net result.** They won few immediate concessions but changed what Indians believed about the cause of poverty and about their right to self-government.

### 8. Core: limitations and the British attitude

*Objective:* Give the main limitations of the Moderates and describe how the British government treated the Congress, naming Dufferin and Curzon.

## Limitations (Spectrum)

1. **A narrow social base.** The masses played a passive role. The Moderates lacked political faith in the masses: they saw the many divisions of Indian society and thought the people ignorant and conservative, and believed these elements must first be welded into a nation. Spectrum's reply is that diverse groups come together only through participation in a freedom struggle.
2. **No militant stand.** Because the masses were not involved, the Moderates could not take militant positions against the authorities. The later nationalists differed on exactly this point.
3. **A narrow democratic agenda.** Spectrum says the nationalists failed to widen the democratic base by not including the masses, especially women, and by not demanding the vote for all.
4. **Method and results.** Petitions produced few concessions. The Indian Councils Act of 1892 fell short of the demand for an elected majority and control over the budget (module 7).
5. **Faith in British justice.** The Moderates believed the British wanted to be just, yet the Ilbert Bill episode and Lytton's measures (module 2) had already shown otherwise, and their own economic critique argued that British rule caused poverty. This internal tension is an analysis point from the sources, not a quotation.
6. **Contemporary criticism.** By the 1890s Pal, Tilak and Lajpat Rai called the method the 'politics of prayers' (NCERT Class VIII). Lajpat Rai's later judgement, in Spectrum's collection of views, was harsh (module 9). NCERT also reproduces Wacha's 1887 complaint to Naoroji that busy and well-off leaders were absorbed in making money.

**A counterpoint.** NCERT says that although the Congress was a body of the educated elite, it did not speak only for professionals, zamindars or industrialists: it took up salt tax, plantation labour, forest dwellers and land revenue.

## The British attitude (Spectrum)

- The government was **hostile from the beginning**, despite the Congress's moderate methods and loyalty to the Crown.
- The attitude **stiffened after 1887**, when the government failed to persuade the Congress to confine itself to social issues while it grew increasingly critical.
- Officials openly condemned nationalists as 'seditious brahmins' and 'disloyal babus'. **Lord Dufferin** called the Congress 'a factory of sedition' (Spectrum's wording; standard accounts also quote the 1888 Dufferin jibe that the Congress represented a microscopic minority; verify).
- **Divide and rule.** Officials encouraged Sir Syed Ahmed Khan and Raja Shiv Prasad Singh of Benaras to form the United Indian Patriotic Association to counter Congress propaganda, tried to split nationalists along religious lines, and with a policy of 'carrot and stick' pitted the Moderates against the Extremists.
- **Lord Curzon**, in 1900, said the Congress was tottering to its fall and that he hoped to help it to a peaceful end (Spectrum's 'Views' box).
- Spectrum's verdict: the government failed to check the rising tide of nationalism.

**Link.** The Muslim League and the communal dimension are in GS-038 module 8; the hostility of Curzon is built up in GS-038 module 2; Extremists in GS-037 (Day 15, not yet written).

Note that Dufferin appears twice in this lesson: the safety-valve story (module 5) and the 'factory of sedition' remark. The two do not fit comfortably, which is one of the reasons historians doubt the safety-valve idea.

### 9. Master: evaluation and what historians say

*Objective:* Weigh the Moderates' contribution against their limits and use historians' views in a balanced verdict.

## Spectrum's evaluation

The early nationalists did much to awaken national sentiment even though they could not draw the masses. Spectrum lists that they: represented the most progressive forces of the time; created a wide national awakening and the feeling of belonging to one nation; trained people in political work and popularised modern ideas; exposed the exploitative character of colonial rule and so undermined its moral foundations; based their work on hard realities, not religion or sentiment; established that India should be ruled in Indian interests; and created a solid base for a more militant, mass-based movement. Against this they failed to widen the democratic base and the scope of their demands.

## Four views to quote (as recorded by Spectrum)

| Speaker | The gist |
|---|---|
| M.G. Ranade (1891) | Memorials are really addressed to the people, to teach them politics; the work will take many years |
| Pattabhi Sitaramayya | The Moderates are like the buried foundation of a building: no blame for being pioneers, because swaraj and independence rest on them |
| Bipan Chandra | 1858 to 1905 was the seed time of nationalism, and the early nationalists sowed well |
| Lala Lajpat Rai | At best an opportunist movement with scope for hypocrisy (a critical, Extremist-era view) |

Use them as views, not as facts, and never attach a quotation to the wrong person.

## How far did they prepare a base? (the 2021 question)

Separate **what they built** from **what they could not do**.

| Built | Could not do |
|---|---|
| An all-India body and a yearly meeting | Reach the masses |
| A cadre of leaders and political education | Win real constitutional power |
| An economic critique (poverty from colonial rule) | Create a militant programme |
| Constitutional agitation and civil-rights tradition | Hold the younger generation by 1905 |
| The demand for self-government (Naoroji 1904, Gokhale 1905) | Extend the franchise idea to all and to women |

The later movements used these foundations. Swadeshi and the Extremists (GS-037, GS-038) widened the base; the Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow reunion (GS-040) brought Moderates and Extremists together again; and the Gandhian mass movements (GS-043, Non-Cooperation and Khilafat, Day 14, being written the same day; see that lesson) completed the shift to mass politics. A balanced verdict: **large in ideas and organisation, small in mass mobilisation**.

## Reading the debate fairly

A critic can say 'mendicant politics'; a defender can say 'pioneers in a society where politics was new' (Ranade's own point). Both are partly right. The better question is what each method could achieve in the conditions of the 1880s and 1890s: with a hostile government and a small educated class, organisation and ideas had to come before pressure.

**Link to 1905.** The anger at Curzon and the partition of Bengal ended the Moderate monopoly, as GS-038 shows.

## 2017 Mains Q3 (10 marks): why did the Moderates fail to carry conviction?

**Frame (150 words).** Introduction: the Moderates' ideology was liberal and constitutional, their goal was gradual reform within the empire. Body: (1) the faith that the British were just was shaken by Lytton, the Ilbert Bill and the limits of the 1892 Act; (2) a narrow social base and no faith in the masses; (3) prayer and petition gave small results; (4) the government's hostility and divide-and-rule; (5) younger critics (Pal, Tilak, Lajpat Rai) called the method the 'politics of prayers'; (6) the tension that their economic critique proved British rule harmful while they sought its continuation. Conclusion: they carried conviction among the educated but not the masses by the 1890s, though they laid the base.

## 2021 Mains Q11 (15 marks): to what extent did they prepare a base?

**Frame (250 words).** Introduction with Sitaramayya's foundation image or Bipan Chandra's 'seed time'. Built: organisation, leadership, economic critique, constitutional training, civil-rights tradition, the idea of self-government. Limits: the masses and women, concessions won, internal division. Links: Swadeshi, Home Rule and mass movements (module 9). Verdict: large in ideas and organisation, partial in mobilisation.

- 1875: Indian League; 1876: Indian Association of Calcutta (Banerjea, Bose).
- 1876 to 1878: ICS age limit cut, Delhi Durbar of 1877, Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act (1878).
- 1883: Ilbert Bill controversy; all-India conference of the Indian Association, Calcutta, 28 to 30 December.
- 1884: Madras Mahajan Sabha; 1885: Bombay Presidency Association.
- December 1885: first Congress session, Bombay, 72 delegates, W.C. Bonnerjee president.
- 1886: Indian Association merges with the Congress; 1887: Tyabji president; stiffening of the government attitude.
- 1889: British Committee of the Congress in London, with the journal India; 1890: Kadambini Ganguly addresses the session; 1892: Indian Councils Act, planned London session postponed.
- 1897: Tilak arrested and the Natu brothers deported; 1900: Curzon's remark on the Congress; 1904 to 1906: Naoroji, Gokhale, Tilak ask for self-government on colonial lines.

### Notes: Three theories of the Congress's origin

- Names and attributions are as Spectrum records them; the 'lightning conductor' attribution differs inside Spectrum (flagged).

### Notes: Moderate demands and methods

- Method: public opinion, resolutions, petitions, meetings; prayer and petition, then constitutional agitation; British Committee and the journal India.
- Economic: drain theory named, lower land revenue, abolition of salt tax, reduced military spending, tariff protection and aid for industry, better plantation labour.
- Constitutional: expansion and reform of councils; control over the budget; elected majority; the slogan 'No taxation without representation'; self-government on the model of Canada and Australia (Naoroji 1904, Gokhale 1905, Tilak 1906).
- Administrative: Indianisation, simultaneous ICS examinations, separation of judicial and executive functions, lower cost of administration, more spending on welfare and education.
- Civil rights: free speech and press; protests after the 1897 arrests.

### Notes: Key terms

- Moderates: the Congress leaders of 1885 to 1905 who believed in liberalism and constitutional agitation.
- Prayer and petition: Moderate method of presenting demands politely and expecting gradual concession.
- Constitutional agitation: protest within the law.
- Safety valve: the theory that the Congress was meant to release discontent harmlessly.
- Lightning conductor: the view that Indian leaders used Hume to draw the heat of official opposition.
- Drain theory: the claim that India's wealth was being drawn to Britain; full account in GS-030 (Day 15, not yet written).
- Indianisation: appointing Indians to higher posts in the services.

### Notes: People and what to tie to them

- A.O. Hume: retired British civil servant, helped organise the first session; head of the Indian National Union (Spectrum summary; verify).
- W.C. Bonnerjee: first president (1885).
- Dadabhai Naoroji: East India Association (1866), three-time president, British campaign, Poverty and Un-British Rule in India.
- Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose: Indian Association (1876) and Indian National Conference.
- Pherozeshah Mehta, K.T. Telang, Badruddin Tyabji: Bombay Presidency Association (1885).
- M.G. Ranade: Poona Sarvajanik Sabha; Viraraghavachari, Subramania Aiyer, Anandacharlu: Madras Mahajan Sabha.
- Lord Dufferin: Viceroy tied to the safety-valve story and the 'factory of sedition' remark; Lord Curzon: the 1900 remark.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Congress founded in 1885 does not mean it was the first political body; the Indian Association (1876) was the main forerunner.
- Hume organised the session; Bonnerjee presided.
- Moderate versus Extremist is a difference of method and base, not of love of the country.
- Three theories of origin: safety valve, conspiracy, lightning conductor.
- The Indian Councils Act 1892 gave discussion and questions, not a vote on the budget.

### Core concepts

- The Congress (Bombay, December 1885) was the first all-India political body after many regional associations.
- Moderates believed in British justice and used prayer, petition and constitutional agitation.
- Their contribution was an economic critique, council work and political education; their limit was a narrow social base.

### Practice

**Q.** Who presided at the first session of the Congress, and in which city?

**A.** W.C. Bonnerjee; Bombay, December 1885.

**Q.** What is a safety valve in the theory of the Congress's origin?

**A.** The idea that the Congress was meant to let off discontent harmlessly, so as to prevent an explosion.

**Q.** What did the Moderates mean by prayer and petition?

**A.** Presenting demands politely through petitions and resolutions in the hope that the rulers would concede them gradually.

**Q.** Why was the Indian Association more important than the British Indian Association for the Congress's rise?

**A.** It was led by younger nationalists who rejected the BIA's conservative landlord outlook, aimed at unifying Indians in a common programme, campaigned on all-India issues and held an all-India conference in 1883.

**Q.** Give two reasons modern historians question the safety-valve theory.

**A.** The early Congress had its own Indian agenda, and the government was hostile from the start, which does not fit a body designed to help it; Indian forerunners like the Indian Association already existed.

**Q.** Why did the Moderates not take militant positions?

**A.** They lacked faith in the masses, who were not involved, and believed British connection was in India's interest and the time not ripe for a direct challenge.

**Q.** A student says the Moderates failed because they achieved nothing. Give a balanced answer in three sentences.

**A.** They won few immediate concessions and kept a narrow base. But they built an all-India organisation, exposed the economic cost of colonial rule and trained leaders and public opinion, which the later movements used.

**Q.** Use the Ilbert Bill to show how events weakened the Moderates' belief in British justice.

**A.** After white opposition forced the bill to be diluted or withdrawn, Indians concluded that fairness could not be expected where European interests were involved, which undercut the premise that the British were basically just.

**A.** Large in ideas and organisation, partial in mass mobilisation: they laid the foundation that later movements built on.
