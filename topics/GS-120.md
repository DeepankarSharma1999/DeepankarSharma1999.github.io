## GS-120: Rainfall regions and climatic hazards

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Reading rainfall figures: mm, cm and per cent of area

Hazard and rainfall questions mix units, so settle them before module 1.

**Rainfall is a depth.** One centimetre of rainfall means that if the rain stayed where it fell, the ground would be covered by water one centimetre deep. NCERT uses both **cm** (kegy104) and **mm** (kegy106). The conversion is simple: 1 cm = 10 mm, so 150 mm is 15 cm and 90 mm is 9 cm.

**Annual rainfall** is the total depth over one year. NCERT's all-India average is about 125 cm, which is 1,250 mm, which is 1.25 m. A place with 1,080 cm gets 10.8 m, more than the height of a two-storey house.

**Per cent of an area.** If a book says 19 per cent of the country's area suffers from drought, multiply by the total area. India's area in the NCERT is 3.28 million sq km, so 0.19 x 3.28 = about 0.62 million sq km. For land, 1 hectare = 0.01 sq km, so 40 million hectares = 4,00,000 sq km.

**A caution.** Two statements can use different units for the same fact; always convert before comparing.

### 1. Orientation: where it rains, and what can go wrong

*Objective:* Say in one minute how rainfall varies across India and name the climatic hazards that follow from too much, too little or too violent weather.

## The one-minute picture

India's rainfall is **monsoonal**: most of the country gets its rain in June to September (kegy104 p3). But the amount swings enormously. NCERT's average is about **125 cm** a year, with Cherrapunji and Mawsynram in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya above **1,080 cm** and Jaisalmer in Rajasthan rarely above **9 cm** (kegy104 p3). Tura in the Garo Hills can receive in one day as much as Jaisalmer gets in ten years.

## Four rainfall regions

| Region | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|
| High | Over 200 cm: west coast, Western Ghats, sub-Himalayan north-east, Meghalaya |
| Medium | 100-200 cm: south Gujarat, east Tamil Nadu, north-east Peninsula, northern Ganga plain, Cachar, Manipur |
| Low | 50-100 cm: western UP, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, east Rajasthan, Gujarat, Deccan Plateau |
| Inadequate | Below 50 cm: parts of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra, Ladakh, most of west Rajasthan |

## Why hazards follow

Uneven and unreliable rain means **floods** in some places, **droughts** in others, sometimes in the same year. Violent systems add **tropical cyclones** and **storm surges** along the coast, **landslides** in steep wet hills, and local storms such as **cloudbursts** (a very intense burst of rain over a small area), hailstorms and thunderstorms. NCERT says India has about two-thirds of its area and an equal share of its population vulnerable to disasters (kegy106 p17).

## What this lesson owns

This lesson covers **rainfall regions** and **climatic hazards**: floods, droughts, cyclone hazard in India, landslides and cloudbursts, and how hazards are managed. It does not re-teach the mechanism of the Indian monsoon (Day 20 lesson GS-119), the jet streams and western disturbances (Day 20 lesson GS-105), the interior of the earth and earthquakes (Day 20 lesson GS-098), or the physics of tropical cyclones (Day 22 lesson GS-107).

## The route

Modules 2 to 4 give words, causes and the four rainfall regions. Modules 5 to 8 give the hazards one by one. Module 9 is management and answer frames; module 10 is the exam lens.

### 2. Foundation: the words, and hazard versus disaster

*Objective:* Define the rainfall and hazard words used in this topic and state NCERT's distinction between a hazard and a disaster.

## Rainfall words

- **Precipitation**: all water falling from the air: rain, snow, hail. **Rainfall**: the liquid part.
- **Isohyet**: a line on a map joining places with equal rainfall.
- **Windward side**: the side of a hill that faces the wind. **Leeward side**: the sheltered side.
- **Orographic (relief) rain**: rain caused when moving moist air is forced up a hill and cools. The Western Ghats and the Khasi Hills show it.
- **Rain shadow**: the dry area on the leeward side. NCERT: winds that cross the Western Ghats descend and get heated, lose humidity and give little rain east of the Ghats (kegy104 p9).
- **Convectional rain**: rain from air rising because it is heated, common in the islands (kegy102 p9).

## Hazard words

- **Natural hazard**: elements of circumstances in the natural environment that have the potential to cause harm to people or property or both (kegy106 p3). Examples NCERT gives: ocean currents, steep slopes and unstable structures in the Himalayas, extreme climate in deserts or glaciated areas.
- **Disaster**: a relatively sudden event causing large-scale death, loss of property and disturbance to social systems, over which people have little or no control. NCERT's longer quotation adds that it needs more effort than normal emergency services can give.
- **Vulnerability**: how exposed and unprepared people and places are. NCERT's point: *all hazards need not turn into disasters*, but when people crowd into hazard zones (flood plains, coastal cities such as Mumbai and Chennai), vulnerability rises.
- **Mitigation**: steps taken beforehand to reduce damage. **Preparedness**: readiness to act.
- **Flood**: water spilling over river banks or other bounds onto land. **Flash flood**: a sudden flood after intense rain. **Drought**: a long shortage of water. **Storm surge**: abnormal rise of sea level caused by a cyclone. **Landslide**: rapid sliding of a mass of rock or debris. **Cloudburst**: a sudden, extremely intense burst of rain over a small area (see module 8 for the flagged definition).

## NCERT's four classes of natural disaster (Table 6.1)

| Class | Examples given |
|---|---|
| Atmospheric | Blizzards, thunderstorms, lightning, tornadoes, tropical cyclone, drought, hailstorm, frost, heat wave or loo, cold wave |
| Terrestrial | Earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides, avalanches, subsidence, soil erosion |
| Aquatic | Floods, tidal waves, storm surge, tsunami |
| Biological | Locusts and other colonisers, insect infestation, fungal, bacterial and viral diseases such as bird flu and dengue |

NCERT places drought and tropical cyclone under atmospheric and floods under aquatic. Use that grouping in a Prelims question.

## Human role

NCERT says some disasters are directly human-caused (Bhopal Gas tragedy, Chernobyl, wars) and others are intensified indirectly (landslides and floods from deforestation, unscientific land use and construction in fragile areas). The lesson of the module: a natural event becomes a disaster through human exposure.


### 3. Core: why rainfall differs across India

*Objective:* Explain five causes of India's uneven rainfall using the branches of the monsoon, relief, the retreating monsoon, winter disturbances and local storms.

The mechanism of the south-west monsoon is in Day 20 lesson GS-119. Here we use it as a map of where rain falls.

## 1. The two monsoon branches (kegy104 pp. 9-11)

**Arabian Sea branch** splits in three.
- *First*: climbs the Western Ghats, becomes cool and gives **250-400 cm** to the windward slopes and the Western Coastal Plain; after crossing, the winds descend, warm up, dry out and give little rain east of the Ghats (the **rain-shadow area**).
- *Second*: strikes the coast north of Mumbai and moves along the Narmada and Tapi valleys, causing rain over extensive areas of central India. Then it joins the Bay branch in the Ganga plains. NCERT says the Chotanagpur plateau gets about 15 cm from this branch; that is only this branch's share, because most of its rain comes from the Bay of Bengal branch (it lies in the medium region, 100-200 cm).
- *Third*: strikes Saurashtra and Kachchh, passes over west Rajasthan and along the Aravalis, giving only scanty rain.

**Bay of Bengal branch** hits Myanmar; the Arakan Hills deflect much of it towards India. It enters West Bengal and Bangladesh from the **south and south-east**, then splits: one arm moves **westward along the Ganga plains** as far as Punjab, the other goes **up the Brahmaputra valley**, with a sub-branch striking the Garo and Khasi hills. **Mawsynram**, on the crest of the Khasi hills, receives the highest average annual rainfall in the world (NCERT).

## 2. Relief

The windward side of the Western Ghats and Assam gets heavy rain in June to September; the southern plateau on the leeward side stays dry (kegy104 p4). NCERT asks you to compare the rainfall at Kozhikode, Mangalore, Pune and Bengaluru; the windward pair should be far wetter than the leeward pair (check on the rainfall map).

## 3. The retreating monsoon and the north-east monsoon

The **Tamil Nadu coast is dry in the south-west monsoon** for two reasons: it lies parallel to the Bay branch and is in the rain shadow of the Arabian Sea branch. It gets rain at the start of winter instead: in October and November, north-east monsoon winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal and cause torrential rain over the Tamil Nadu coast, southern Andhra Pradesh, south-east Karnataka and south-east Kerala (kegy104 p8). The widespread rain of that season is linked to cyclonic depressions from the Andaman Sea.

## 4. Winter rain from outside

Weak temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean give small rain to Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and western Uttar Pradesh, and snow in the lower Himalayas. NCERT's figures: Delhi about **53 mm** in winter, Punjab and Bihar **25 mm and 18 mm** respectively. Valuable for rabi crops. See Day 20 lesson GS-105.

## 5. Local storms

Mango showers (Kerala and coastal Karnataka), blossom showers (coffee flowers in Kerala), Nor'westers or Kalbaisakhi in Bengal and Assam (useful for tea, jute, rice) and the dry hot Loo of the northern plains (kegy104 p9).

## Check the logic

The Ganga plain gets less rain towards the west because the Bay branch loses moisture as it travels. That matches the table in module 4: northern plain along the sub-Himalayas in the medium region, western UP and Punjab in the low region.


### 4. Core: the four rainfall regions and how to read them

*Objective:* State the four rainfall regions with their cm ranges, place names and the quirks of the book's lists, and test a rule of thumb on edge cases.

## NCERT's classification (kegy104 p12)

The average annual rainfall of India is about **125 cm**, with great spatial variation.

| Region | Annual rainfall | Places as NCERT lists them |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Over 200 cm | West coast, Western Ghats, sub-Himalayan areas in the north-east, hills of Meghalaya. In parts of the Khasi and Jaintia hills, over 1,000 cm |
| **Medium** | 100-200 cm | Southern Gujarat, east Tamil Nadu, north-eastern Peninsula (Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, eastern Madhya Pradesh), northern Ganga plain along the sub-Himalayas, the Cachar Valley and Manipur |
| **Low** | 50-100 cm | Western Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, eastern Rajasthan, Gujarat and the Deccan Plateau |
| **Inadequate** | Below 50 cm | Parts of the Peninsula (Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra), Ladakh, most of western Rajasthan |

Snowfall is restricted to the Himalayan region.

## Units and cross-checks

- 1 cm = 10 mm, so the desert's **150 mm** (kegy102 p7) is 15 cm; the drought chapter's Jaisalmer and Barmer figure of **less than 90 mm** (kegy106 p13) is under 9 cm, matching kegy104's "rarely more than 9 cm".
- Tura's one-day fall equals ten years at Jaisalmer: 10 x 9 cm gives up to about 90 cm in a day (NCERT's 'rarely more than 9 cm' is an upper bound).
- The north-west Himalayas and western deserts have **under 10 cm** a year, while Meghalaya exceeds **400 cm** (kegy104 p3); the 1,000 cm and 1,080 cm figures are for the wettest hills only.

## Quirks of the book's lists

1. The Brahmaputra valley and adjoining hills appear under *high* rainfall, yet the text says its rain is **less than 200 cm**. Take it as a pocket of lower rain inside the wet north-east.
2. **Gujarat** appears in both medium (southern) and low (the rest). Gujarat is a mixed case; name its part.
3. The exercise asks what share of India gets under 75 cm. The extract gives no share; do not quote one from memory. Estimate it from the rainfall map (Figure 4.4).

## A rule of thumb, tested

*Rule:* "Rain is highest on windward coasts and hills facing the monsoon and falls away to the north-west and into rain shadows."
- Edge case 1: the Western Ghats leeward side (Deccan) is low: fits.
- Edge case 2: east Tamil Nadu is medium though it is the rain shadow in summer: fits only because of north-east monsoon rain; the rule needs the retreating monsoon.
- Edge case 3: the Brahmaputra valley is wet but under 200 cm: partly fits.
- Edge case 4: Jammu and Kashmir sits in the low region although it is a mountain state: it is shielded from the monsoon branches (the lesson's reasoning; verify).
Conclusion: the rule works with two added clauses, "retreating monsoon rain on the south-east" and "mountain shielding".

## Why the 100 cm line matters

The 100 cm isohyet roughly divides the medium and high regions from the low and inadequate ones. NCERT's activity asks you to draw it. Do so on the outline map from module 1.


### 5. Core: floods - causes, regions, consequences and control

*Objective:* Explain why and where floods occur in India, name the four flood situations, and list consequences and control measures as NCERT gives them.

## What causes floods (kegy106 p9)

NCERT: floods are slow in occurrence, happen in well-identified regions at expected times, and their causes are well established. They occur when surface runoff exceeds the carrying capacity of channels and spills onto low-lying flood plains. Natural causes include a **storm surge** on the coast, **long, high-intensity rainfall**, **melting of snow and ice**, and a lower rate of infiltration. **Human causes**: indiscriminate deforestation, unscientific farming, disturbing natural drainage channels, and colonising flood plains and river beds. Eroded material in the water also raises floods (soil erosion makes channels shallower).

Day 17 lesson GS-117 explains why rivers such as the Kosi ('sorrow of Bihar') and the Brahmaputra flood: large sediment loads and heavy catchment rain (kegy103 p4, p7).

## Where (kegy106 p11)

The Rashtriya Barh Ayog (National Flood Commission) identified **40 million hectares** as flood-prone. Convert: 1 hectare = 0.01 sq km, so 40 million ha = 4,00,000 sq km, about **12 per cent** of India's 3.28 million sq km area (kegy101). NCERT names Assam, West Bengal and Bihar among the high flood-prone States; most rivers of Punjab and Uttar Pradesh also flood occasionally.

| Situation | Where NCERT places it |
|---|---|
| Flooding rivers | Assam, West Bengal, Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh |
| Cyclone floods (storm surge) | Coastal Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat |
| Flash floods | Punjab, Rajasthan, northern Gujarat, Haryana; NCERT notes Rajasthan, Gujarat, Haryana and Punjab are getting inundated in recent decades, partly because of the monsoon pattern and partly because human activity blocks streams and channels |
| Retreating-monsoon floods | Tamil Nadu, November to January |

## Consequences

Loss of crops, roads, rails, bridges and settlements; millions homeless; loss of cattle; cholera, gastro-enteritis, hepatitis and other water-borne diseases. A **positive effect**: floods deposit fertile silt; NCERT's example is **Majuli** (Assam), described as the largest riverine island in the world, with good paddy after the annual Brahmaputra floods. NCERT adds that these benefits are insignificant compared with the losses.

## Control and management

Flood-protection **embankments**, **dams**, **afforestation**, discouraging major construction in the upper reaches of flood-creating rivers, **removing encroachment** from channels and **depopulating the flood plains**; in coastal areas hit by a surge, **cyclone centres** help.

## Link to Mains

Floods and droughts occurring in different parts of India in the same year or even the same region in different seasons, because of large variation and unpredictability of the monsoon, is NCERT's central point (kegy106 p13). That is the thought behind the 2020 interlinking question (module 6).

## Check

If a student says 'floods are completely natural', use NCERT: human activities decide the genesis and spread of floods.


### 6. Core: droughts, water stress and the river-linking idea

*Objective:* Define the four types of drought, place India's drought zones, list consequences and measures, and use the extracts for water stress and interlinking answers.

## Definition (kegy106 p13)

**Drought** is an extended period of shortage of water caused by inadequate precipitation, excessive evaporation and over-use of water from reservoirs and other stores, including ground water. It is a complex phenomenon involving rainfall, evaporation, soil moisture, run-off, cropping patterns and ecology.

## Four types

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Meteorological** | Prolonged inadequate rainfall, badly spread over time and space |
| **Agricultural** (soil-moisture) | Low soil moisture, crop failure. An area with more than **30 per cent of gross cropped area irrigated** is excluded from the drought-prone category |
| **Hydrological** | Water in aquifers, lakes and reservoirs falls below what rain can replace |
| **Ecological** | A natural ecosystem's productivity fails because of water shortage |

## How much of India

NCERT (older estimates; dated, verify): nearly **19 per cent** of the area and **12 per cent** of the population suffer from drought every year; about **30 per cent** of the area is drought-prone, affecting about **50 million** people. Check: 19 per cent of 3.28 million sq km is about 6.2 lakh sq km, and 30 per cent is about 9.8 lakh sq km.

## The zones (Figure 6.7)

- **Extreme**: most of Rajasthan, particularly west of the Aravali (Marusthali) and Kachchh in Gujarat; Jaisalmer and Barmer receive under 90 mm a year.
- **Severe**: parts of eastern Rajasthan, most of Madhya Pradesh, eastern Maharashtra, interior Andhra Pradesh and the Karnataka plateau, north interior Tamil Nadu, southern Jharkhand and interior Odisha.
- **Moderate**: northern Rajasthan, Haryana, southern Uttar Pradesh districts, the remaining Gujarat, Maharashtra except Konkan, Jharkhand, Coimbatore plateau and interior Karnataka.
- The rest of India is free or less prone.

## Consequences

NCERT's village words: **akal** (food-grain scarcity), **trinkal** (fodder scarcity), **jalkal** (water scarcity), **trikal** (all three together). Also cattle deaths, migration of people and livestock, and water-borne diseases.

## Measures

Safe drinking water, medicines and fodder; shifting people and livestock; mapping **ground water** potential; **transferring river water** from surplus to deficit areas; **interlinking of rivers**; reservoirs; **remote sensing**; drought-resistant crops; **rainwater harvesting**.

## River linking (kegy103 pp. 8-9): evidence for the 2020 question

NCERT asks whether transferring surplus water from one basin to deficit basins could solve the problem and cites examples: Periyar Diversion Scheme, Indira Gandhi Canal Project, Kurnool-Cuddapah Canal, Beas-Satluj Link Canal, Ganga-Kaveri Link Canal. It raises two difficulties: **uneven terrain** (water must be lifted from plain to plateau) and **whether the north Indian rivers have enough regular surplus**.

## Water stress (2019 Mains): standard knowledge, flagged

Water stress means demand exceeds the water available in a period. A common index, the Falkenmark indicator, calls availability below about 1,700 cubic metres per person per year stress and below about 1,000 scarcity (standard; verify). Regional differences follow the lesson: scanty and erratic rain in the west and Peninsula (modules 3-4, drought zones above), perennial Himalayan rivers in the north, groundwater use in the north-west and dense population on the plains (standard; verify). Do not quote figures from memory.


### 7. Core: tropical cyclones as a hazard in India

*Objective:* State where and when cyclones hit India, the conditions that form them, their size and effects, and how the hazard is managed.

Cyclone physics: Day 22 lesson GS-107; this module gives NCERT's hazard facts.

## What a tropical cyclone is (kegy106 p8, kegy209 p8)

An intense low-pressure area between about **30 degrees N and 30 degrees S**, with high-velocity winds. NCERT sizes: horizontally **500-1,000 km** and vertically from the surface to **12-14 km** (kegy106); the circulating system around the eye **150-250 km** across, and the whole storm over the Bay of Bengal, Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean **600-1,200 km** (kegy209). Winds near the eye wall may reach **250 km per hour**; coastal areas are often struck at an average **180 km per hour** (kegy106 p9); the system moves slowly, **300-500 km a day**.

## Conditions (kegy106 p8; kegy209 p8)

1. A large, continuous supply of warm, moist air to release latent heat (sea surface above **27 degrees C**).
2. A strong Coriolis force, which keeps the central low from being filled (so none forms between **0-5 degrees** latitude).
3. Unstable conditions through the troposphere.
4. No strong vertical wind change (small variation in vertical wind speed).
5. A weak pre-existing low (kegy209).

Energy comes from the condensation in the cumulonimbus clouds around the centre; the storm weakens on land because moisture is cut off (kegy209 p8).

## Where and when in India (kegy106 p9)

India's cyclones form over the **Bay of Bengal** and the **Arabian Sea**. Most originate between **10-15 degrees N** in the monsoon season, but in the Bay the main season is **October and November**. NCERT's printed latitude range for those storms ('16-2 N') is garbled in the book; do not quote a range from it (check another source). NCERT places the origin west of 92 degrees E. By July the origin shifts to about **18 degrees N, west of 90 degrees E**, near the Sunderban delta. Arabian Sea cyclones are less frequent (kegy104 p11).

NCERT's retreating-monsoon passage adds that cyclonic depressions from the **Andaman Sea** cross the **eastern coast of the southern Peninsula**, and that the thickly populated deltas of the **Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri** are preferred targets; a bulk of the Coromandel coast rainfall is from them; a few strike West Bengal, Bangladesh and Myanmar (kegy104 p11).

## Consequences

Force decreases inland because the energy is the warm moist air from the sea. The characteristic danger is the **storm surge**: very high horizontal pressure gradient and very strong winds drive sea water across the coast, flooding settlements and fields and destroying crops and structures (kegy106 pp. 9-10).

## Management

Cyclones are more predictable in time and place than earthquakes, tsunamis or volcanoes, and techniques now track intensity and direction. Steps: **cyclone shelters, embankments, dykes, reservoirs and afforestation** to cut wind speed. NCERT's blame for rising losses in India, Bangladesh and Myanmar is the **high vulnerability of coastal populations**.

## Quick contrast with the western disturbance (Day 20 lesson GS-105)

A western disturbance is an extra-tropical winter system reaching north-west India; extra-tropical cyclones move west to east, tropical cyclones east to west (kegy209 p8).

*Check the arithmetic:* the storm is wide and shallow: 500-1,200 km across against 12-14 km tall, roughly 35 to 100 times wider than it is tall.


### 8. Core: landslides, cloudbursts and local storms

*Objective:* Describe landslide zones and mitigation as NCERT gives them, explain the cloudburst with flagged standard knowledge, and name the severe local storms.

## Landslides (kegy106 pp. 15-16)

A landslide is the rapid sliding of a large mass of bedrock. Unlike most disasters, landslides are controlled by **highly localised** factors (geology, geomorphic agents, slope, land use, vegetation, human activity), so monitoring is difficult and costly. NCERT's zones:

| Zone | Where |
|---|---|
| **Very high** | Young, unstable Himalayas and the Andaman and Nicobar; high-rainfall steep slopes of the Western Ghats and Nilgiris; north-east; areas with frequent earthquakes and heavy construction (roads, dams) |
| **High** | Similar conditions; all Himalayan states and north-eastern states except the plains of Assam |
| **Moderate to low** | Trans-Himalayan Ladakh and Spiti, Aravali, rain-shadow areas of the Ghats and Deccan; mining-linked slides in several Peninsular States |
| **Other areas (safe)** | Rajasthan, Haryana, UP, Bihar, West Bengal except Darjiling, Assam except Karbi Anglong, southern coastal regions |

**Consequences**: small direct area but roadblocks, rail damage, river blocking, diversion of rivers causing floods, costly travel. **Mitigation**: restrict roads, dams and large settlements in high-risk zones; farming limited to valleys and moderate slopes; afforestation; bunds to reduce water flow; terrace farming in north-eastern hill States where jhumming (shifting cultivation) persists. For landslide causes, compare Day 17 lesson GS-116.

## Cloudburst (2022 and 2024 Mains): standard knowledge, flagged verify

The extracts do not define it. A **cloudburst** is an extreme rainfall event over a very small area in a short time, commonly quoted as about 10 cm (100 mm) in an hour over roughly 20-30 sq km (IMD-type definition; definitions vary; verify).

*Mechanism (standard; verify):* very moist air, often monsoon air, is forced up steep mountain slopes (orographic lift) and rises in a tall cumulonimbus cloud; strong updraughts hold large amounts of water aloft; when the updraught fails or the cloud is blocked, the water falls at once in a narrow zone.

*Occurrence:* most reported in the Himalaya, especially in the monsoon, because steep slopes, valleys and moisture meet there; the word is also used loosely for lesser heavy rain (verify). *Effects:* flash floods, landslides and debris flows, washed-out roads and homes. Media-reported examples (verify names and dates): Leh (August 2010), Kedarnath (June 2013, where the cause is debated), the Amarnath cave area (July 2022).

## Severe local storms (kegy209 p9; kegy104 p9)

- **Thunderstorm**: a well-grown cumulonimbus cloud giving thunder and lightning, caused by intense convection on moist hot days; hailstorm if clouds reach sub-zero heights; **dust-storm** if moisture is short.
- **Tornado**: a spiral wind with very low pressure at the centre, mainly in middle latitudes; a **waterspout** over the sea.
- Indian local storms: **Nor'westers** (Kalbaisakhi, Bardoisila), **Loo**, **mango and blossom showers**.

## Earthquake and tsunami: pointer

Earthquake zones, effects and mitigation are in Day 20 lesson GS-098. NCERT's one-line link: the Indian plate moves (kegy106 p4 gives about 1 cm a year; modern estimates are about 4-5 cm a year, verify, and Day 19 lesson GS-097 works out about 5.8 cm a year), hits the Eurasian plate, and the locked plates cause earthquakes along the Himalayan arch (kegy106 p5); the 26 December 2004 tsunami killed more than 300,000 people as NCERT prints it (kegy106 p8; other estimates are lower, about 230,000; verify).


### 9. Master: disaster management, source notes and answer frames

*Objective:* State the three stages of disaster management, the Yokohama Strategy, the source slips, and build answer frames for the Mains hazard questions.

## Three stages (kegy106 p17)

1. **Pre-disaster**: generate data and information, prepare vulnerability zoning maps, spread awareness, plan and prepare, and take preventive steps in vulnerable areas.
2. **During**: rescue and relief: evacuation, shelters and relief camps, water, food, clothing, medical aid.
3. **Post-disaster**: rehabilitation and recovery of victims and capacity-building for future disasters.

NCERT's conclusion: all hazards need not turn into disasters, natural disasters are difficult to eliminate, so **mitigation and preparedness** are the next best option.

## The institutional facts NCERT gives

- **Yokohama Strategy and Plan of Action for a Safer World**: adopted at the World Conference on Natural Disaster Reduction, Yokohama, **23-27 May 1994**, as a mid-decade review of the International Decade for Natural Disaster Reduction (IDNDR, **1990-2000**) (kegy106 p5). The UN General Assembly proclaimed the decade in 1989 (standard; verify); NCERT's box wording reads as if the conference declared it.
- **Disaster Management Bill, 2005**: NCERT quotes its definition of disaster: a catastrophe, mishap, calamity or grave occurrence, from natural or man-made causes, causing substantial loss of life, suffering or damage to the environment, beyond the community's coping capacity. In its text the book calls it a Bill; it was later enacted as the Disaster Management Act, 2005 (standard; verify). The book also names the National Institute of Disaster Management.
- India: about **two-thirds** of its area and a similar share of population vulnerable (NCERT; dated, verify).

## Source slips and cautions

1. The Earth Summit at Rio de Janeiro is printed as **1993** (kegy106 p3); it is generally dated 1992 (verify).
2. 'Bill, 2005' is the later Act (above); the cyclone latitude '16-2 N' is garbled (module 7).
3. The drought and flood statistics are the book's older figures; verify. The Chotanagpur '15 cm' is a branch share, not a slip.
4. NCERT's box reads as if Yokohama declared the IDNDR (above).
5. Call nothing a 'slip' without checking the book page.

### Notes: Definitions

- Natural hazard: elements of circumstances in the natural environment with potential to cause harm to people, property or both (NCERT).
- Disaster: a relatively sudden event causing large-scale death, property loss and disturbance to social systems, with little human control.
- Isohyet: a line joining places of equal rainfall.
- Rain shadow: the dry leeward side where descending air has warmed and dried.
- Storm surge: abnormal rise of sea level from a cyclone's winds and low pressure.
- Akal, trinkal, jalkal, trikal: scarcity of food grains, fodder, water, and all three.
- Cloudburst (standard; verify): an extreme burst of rain over a small area in a short time, commonly about 10 cm in an hour over 20-30 sq km.

### Notes: Cyclone facts and numbers

- Tropical cyclones lie between 30 degrees N and 30 degrees S; none forms within 0-5 degrees of the equator (kegy106).
- Sea surface above 27 degrees C is a favourable condition (kegy209 p8).
- Storm over the Bay of Bengal, Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean: 600-1,200 km across; circulating system around the eye 150-250 km (kegy209).
- Winds up to 250 km per hour near the eye wall (kegy209); average velocity 180 km per hour on the coast (kegy106).
- Movement 300-500 km a day (kegy209); Bay of Bengal main season October-November (kegy106).
- Cyclones crossing 20 degrees N generally recurve and are more destructive (kegy209 p8).
- Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri deltas are preferred targets (kegy104 p11).
- Names: cyclone (Indian Ocean), hurricane (Atlantic), typhoon (western Pacific), willy-willies (western Australia).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Weather versus climate (momentary state versus long-term average).
- Hazard versus disaster; hazard versus vulnerability.
- Windward (wet) versus leeward (rain shadow).
- Tropical cyclone (warm sea, east to west) versus extra-tropical cyclone (fronts, west to east).
- Akal (food) versus trinkal (fodder) versus jalkal (water) versus trikal (all three).
- Meteorological versus agricultural versus hydrological drought.

### Notes: Cloudburst: extra points for a 10-mark answer (standard; verify)

- Occurrence: mainly the Himalaya, but also the Western Ghats and, occasionally, the plains.
- Trigger often cited: monsoon air meeting a western disturbance (Kedarnath, June 2013; cause debated).
- The 100 mm an hour over 20-30 sq km figure is a working IMD threshold; many reported cloudbursts are heavy-rain events that do not meet it.
- Prediction is hard because the affected area is smaller than most radar and model resolution.
- Management: Doppler radar and nowcasting, land-use control in valleys, slope drainage, no building in drainage lines, early warning to villages.

### Core concepts

- Rainfall in India is monsoonal but uneven: four regions from over 200 cm to below 50 cm.
- Relief, the two monsoon branches and the retreating monsoon decide where rain falls.
- A hazard becomes a disaster when exposure and vulnerability are high.
- Floods and droughts can occur in the same year in different regions.
- Cyclone and landslide hazards follow the coast and the young hills.

**A.** 15 cm.

**Q.** Name the four rainfall regions.

**A.** High, medium, low and inadequate.

**Q.** Why does the Tamil Nadu coast get rain in winter and not in the summer monsoon?

**A.** It is parallel to the Bay branch and in the rain shadow of the Arabian Sea branch in summer; in October-November the north-east monsoon picks up moisture over the Bay.

**Q.** Why can India have floods and droughts in the same year?

**A.** Large variation and unpredictability of the monsoon in space and time (NCERT).

**Q.** Why do cyclones lose strength inland?

**A.** Their energy comes from latent heat of warm moist sea air, and the moisture supply is cut off on land.

**Q.** A State has 35 per cent of its gross cropped area irrigated and a poor monsoon. Is it classed as drought-prone for agricultural drought by NCERT's rule?

**A.** No. Areas with more than 30 per cent of gross cropped area under irrigation are excluded from the drought-prone category, although they may still have meteorological or hydrological stress.
