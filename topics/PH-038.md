## PH-038: Beats and superposition

Physics Paper I · Physics · Waves and oscillations

### Bridge: Two trigonometric identities and the counting of events per second

Beats need two identities, both from your earlier lessons' trigonometry.

**Identity 1 (expansion).** cos(a − b) = cos a cos b + sin a sin b, and cos(a + b) = cos a cos b − sin a sin b.

**Identity 2 (sum to product).** Put A = P + Q and B = P − Q. Then cos A + cos B = [cos P cos Q − sin P sin Q] + [cos P cos Q + sin P sin Q] = 2 cos P cos Q. Since P = (A + B)/2 and Q = (A − B)/2:

$$ cos A + cos B = 2 cos[(A − B)/2] cos[(A + B)/2] $$

**Counting.** A quantity that repeats with period T happens 1/T times in each second; that number is its frequency in hertz. If cos(2πf t) is the quantity, its period is 1/f. The magnitude |cos(2πf t)| repeats twice as often, because cos and −cos have the same magnitude: its period is 1/(2f). Keep this in mind for module 6.

**Angular versus ordinary frequency.** ω = 2πf, with ω in rad/s and f in hertz (PH-035 module 2). Beats are always counted in hertz, so Δf = Δω/2π.

**Cycle averages.** The average of cos θ over a whole number of its cycles is zero (PH-037 bridge B2).

### Bridge: Amplitude, intensity and the word 'envelope'

A wave or oscillation that moves like a cos(ωt) has **amplitude** a: the largest displacement from equilibrium. Sound loudness and the brightness of light depend on the **intensity**, the energy delivered per second per unit area, and for a given medium and frequency intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude (Mathur PDF p. 486: I ∝ a²). Doubling the amplitude therefore makes the intensity four times larger.

An **envelope** is the outline traced by the tops of a fast oscillation whose amplitude changes slowly. Picture the vibration of a guitar string after plucking: the string moves back and forth hundreds of times a second (the fast part, called the **carrier**), while the size of the swing dies away over seconds (the envelope). In beats the carrier is a tone near 440 Hz and the envelope is the slow rise and fall of loudness.

A sign convention: an envelope in a formula such as 2a cos(ω_b t) can be negative. Loudness depends on its **magnitude** (or on its square), never on its sign. When a result has the shape (slow factor) × (fast factor), always ask which factor is slow, then ask whether you want the signed factor or its magnitude.

### 2. Foundation: the principle of superposition, linearity and where it fails

*Objective:* State the principle, show why linear equations obey it, and give two examples where it fails.

**The principle.** If two or more waves or oscillations act together, the resultant displacement at any point is the sum of the individual displacements: y = y₁ + y₂ + y₃ + ... (NCERT 14.5, Eq. 14.25 to 14.26; Mathur 10.13, PDF p. 486). Two pulses meeting on a string pass through each other: while they overlap the shape is the sum; afterwards each continues unchanged, 'as if the other were not present' (NCERT PDF p. 10). An orchestra's notes reach the ear as one complex wave yet remain separately audible (Mathur).

**Why it holds: linearity.** Mathur states the only condition: the equation governing the waves must be linear. Here is the reason for the oscillator. Let x₁(t) and x₂(t) each satisfy d²x/dt² = −ω²x. Then

$$ d²(x₁ + x₂)/dt² = d²x₁/dt² + d²x₂/dt² = −ω²x₁ − ω²x₂ = −ω²(x₁ + x₂) $$

so x = x₁ + x₂ also solves it (Upadhyaya 7.8, PDF p. 410). The same two lines work for c₁x₁ + c₂x₂ with constants c₁, c₂, and for the wave equation ∂²y/∂t² = v² ∂²y/∂x². The damped (PH-036) and driven (PH-037) oscillator equations are also linear (Upadhyaya states this), which is why 'transient + steady state' works.

**Where it fails.**
1. *Nonlinear restoring force.* Take d²x/dt² = −k₁x − k₂x² with k₂ ≠ 0 (an anharmonic oscillator, Upadhyaya Eq. 5). If x₁ and x₂ are solutions, then (x₁ + x₂)'' = −k₁(x₁ + x₂) − k₂(x₁² + x₂²). The equation would need −k₂(x₁ + x₂)² = −k₂(x₁² + x₂² + 2x₁x₂). The leftover cross term 2k₂x₁x₂ shows the sum is **not** a solution.
2. *Large pendulum swings.* θ'' = −ω²sin θ is nonlinear; only for small θ (sin θ ≈ θ) is it linear (PH-035 module 7; Upadhyaya PDF p. 410).
3. *Shock waves.* Mathur notes that the waves from a large explosion obey a nonlinear equation and do not superpose.

Other limits of linear behaviour (springs stretched beyond Hooke's law, very intense light in some crystals) are standard physics; they are mentioned here without a source extract and should be verified.

**Displacement adds, intensity does not.** Superposition applies to the **displacement** (the field). Intensity is proportional to its square, so (y₁ + y₂)² = y₁² + y₂² + 2y₁y₂: the cross term is interference. Energy is conserved because the cross term averages to zero over a beat cycle (module 5; Mathur PDF p. 488, interference as redistribution of energy).

**Dimension check.** Every term in y = y₁ + y₂ has the dimension of the same displacement (metres, or pascals for pressure), so only like quantities are added.

### 3. Core: two collinear SHMs of the same frequency (algebra and phasors)

*Objective:* Derive the amplitude and phase of the sum of two SHMs of equal ω, and read its special cases.

**Setting.** One particle moves on the x-axis; x is measured from equilibrium. Two SHMs of the same ω act on it: x₁ = a₁ cos(ωt + φ₁) and x₂ = a₂ cos(ωt + φ₂), a₁, a₂ > 0. Let δ = φ₂ − φ₁ be the phase difference. By superposition x = x₁ + x₂. (Upadhyaya 7.9, PDF p. 411, does this with sin.)

**Algebraic derivation.** Expand each term with cos(ωt + φ) = cos ωt cos φ − sin ωt sin φ:

$$ x = cos ωt (a₁cos φ₁ + a₂cos φ₂) − sin ωt (a₁sin φ₁ + a₂sin φ₂) $$

Name the two brackets C and S, and choose A and φ so that C = A cos φ and S = A sin φ. Then x = A(cos ωt cos φ − sin ωt sin φ) = A cos(ωt + φ): **the sum is again an SHM of the same frequency**. Squaring and adding, A² = C² + S²:

$$ A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ (cos φ₁ cos φ₂ + sin φ₁ sin φ₂) = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos δ $$

and tan φ = S/C = (a₁sin φ₁ + a₂sin φ₂)/(a₁cos φ₁ + a₂cos φ₂). Take φ in the quadrant given by the signs of C and S (use atan2, as in PH-035 module 3).

**Phasor derivation.** Each SHM is the x-projection of an arrow of length aᵢ turning at ω (PH-035 module 4). Both arrows turn at the same rate, so the angle δ between them never changes and the sum arrow is a rigid triangle. Its length follows from the cosine rule with included angle π − δ: A² = a₁² + a₂² − 2a₁a₂ cos(π − δ) = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos δ ✓.

**Special cases.**
- δ = 0 (in phase): A = a₁ + a₂, the maximum.
- δ = π (antiphase): A = |a₁ − a₂|, the minimum; with a₁ = a₂ the motion vanishes (Upadhyaya).
- δ = π/2 (more generally odd multiples of π/2, where cos δ = 0): A = √(a₁² + a₂²), the case where amplitudes add like intensities.
- a₁ = a₂ = a: A = 2a|cos(δ/2)|, the same as NCERT's wave result A(φ) = 2a cos(φ/2) (Eq. 14.32, PDF p. 11).

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* every term in A² is length². *Limit* a₂ → 0: A → a₁ and φ → φ₁ ✓. *Bounds:* −1 ≤ cos δ ≤ 1 gives |a₁ − a₂| ≤ A ≤ a₁ + a₂ (triangle inequality).

**Number (recomputed).** a₁ = 3, a₂ = 4, δ = 90°: A = 5, and the sum leads the first SHM by tan⁻¹(4/3) = 53.13°. With δ = 60°: A² = 37, A = 6.083, phase 34.72°.

**Link forward.** If the two frequencies are not equal, δ is not constant; it grows as Δω·t, and A becomes time dependent: that is beats (modules 4 and 5).

### 4. Core: two collinear SHMs of different frequencies and the product form

*Objective:* Explain why the sum of SHMs of unequal frequency is not an SHM, when it repeats, and how equal amplitudes give a product of a slow and a fast cosine.

**Setting.** x₁ = a₁ cos ω₁t and x₂ = a₂ cos ω₂t on the same line, ω₁ > ω₂. The constant phases are dropped because they only shift the time origin (Upadhyaya makes the same simplification, PDF p. 417 to 418).

**Phasor picture.** The two arrows now turn at different rates, so the angle between them grows steadily: δ(t) = Δω·t. Stand on the turntable that turns with arrow 1: arrow 1 is at rest and arrow 2 slowly swings round it once every 2π/Δω seconds. The length of the sum arrow therefore swells and shrinks: amplitude modulation, the origin of beats (module 5).

**Not an SHM.** Differentiate twice: x'' = −ω₁²x₁ − ω₂²x₂. For this to equal −ω²(x₁ + x₂) with a single ω we would need ω₁² = ω₂² = ω², which is false. So the sum does not satisfy the SHM equation, even though each part does and superposition holds for the *displacement*. Superposition of solutions of the SHM equation gives a solution of a different (higher-order) equation, not of this one.

**Does it repeat?** The sum repeats exactly only if f₁/f₂ is a ratio of whole numbers. For 300 Hz and 200 Hz the pattern repeats every 1/100 s = 10 ms (the period of the greatest common frequency, 100 Hz, recomputed). For f₁/f₂ irrational (for example 1 Hz and √2 Hz) the exact pattern never repeats (standard mathematics). Beats concern the case f₁ and f₂ close, so the ratio is nearly 1 and the slow repeat time is 1/Δf.

**The product form (equal amplitudes).** Put a₁ = a₂ = a and use the sum-to-product identity of bridge B1 with A = ω₁t and B = ω₂t:

$$ x = a cos ω₁t + a cos ω₂t = 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t) $$

with the **mean (carrier) angular frequency** ω̄ = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2 and the **modulation angular frequency** ω_b = (ω₁ − ω₂)/2 = Δω/2. NCERT (PDF p. 16, Eq. 14.47) writes the same result with ω_a and ω_b, and Upadhyaya (PDF p. 418) as a_mod(t) sin ω_av t.

**Condition.** The factor 2a cos(ω_b t) can be called a slowly varying amplitude only if ω_b ≪ ω̄, that is Δf ≪ f₁ + f₂ (NCERT: 'ω_a ≫ ω_b'). Example: 444 Hz and 440 Hz give ω̄/2π = 442 Hz and ω_b/2π = 2 Hz, a ratio of 221.

**Check.** Dimensions: ω_b t is an angle, so the cosines are pure numbers and x has the dimension of a. Limit Δω → 0: ω_b → 0, cos(ω_b t) → 1 and x → 2a cos ω̄t, a single SHM of amplitude 2a (the in-phase case of module 3).

**Warning.** Two different 'frequencies' of the envelope appear in the product form: ω_b/2π = Δf/2 for the signed factor, and Δf for its magnitude. Module 6 explains which one is the beat frequency.

### 5. Core: the beat derivation for unequal amplitudes (the 2018 question)

*Objective:* Derive the resultant amplitude R(t) and phase θ(t), and prove that the loudness repeats Δf times per second.

**Setting.** At one point of the medium the two waves produce x₁ = a₁ cos ω₁t and x₂ = a₂ cos ω₂t, a₁ ≥ a₂ > 0, ω₁ > ω₂, same direction, time t in seconds, with the two waves in phase at t = 0 (Mathur 10.14, PDF p. 489, uses a₁ > a₂ and sines).

**Step 1: write ω₂ in terms of ω₁.** ω₂ = ω₁ − Δω, so x₂ = a₂ cos(ω₁t − Δωt).

**Step 2: expand.** cos(ω₁t − Δωt) = cos ω₁t cos Δωt + sin ω₁t sin Δωt, hence

$$ x = cos ω₁t (a₁ + a₂ cos Δωt) + sin ω₁t (a₂ sin Δωt) $$

**Step 3: collect into one cosine.** Define R(t) and θ(t) by R cos θ = a₁ + a₂ cos Δωt and R sin θ = a₂ sin Δωt. Then x = R(cos ω₁t cos θ + sin ω₁t sin θ), that is

$$ x = R(t) cos[ω₁t − θ(t)] $$

**Step 4: R and θ.** Squaring and adding the defining pair:

$$ R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos(Δωt) ,  tan θ = a₂ sin(Δωt)/[a₁ + a₂ cos(Δωt)] $$

This is Mathur's result with the resultant lagging the first wave by θ.

**Step 5: the beat frequency.** R² depends on time only through cos(Δωt) = cos(2πΔf·t), which repeats with period 1/Δf. R reaches its maximum a₁ + a₂ when Δωt = 2πr (r = 0, 1, 2, ...), that is at t = r/Δf, and its minimum a₁ − a₂ when Δωt = (2r + 1)π, at t = (2r + 1)/(2Δf). Successive maxima are 1/Δf apart (Mathur states exactly this). Intensity ∝ R², so the loudness has Δf maxima per second:

$$ f_beat = f₁ − f₂ $$

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* all terms of R² are amplitude². *Limits:* a₂ → 0 gives R = a₁ (no beats); Δω = 0 gives the constant R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ of module 3 with δ = 0. *Depth:* I_max = (a₁ + a₂)², I_min = (a₁ − a₂)², so the contrast V = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = 2a₁a₂/(a₁² + a₂²). For a₁ = 3, a₂ = 1: R from 4 down to 2, I_max/I_min = 4, V = 0.6. The minimum is **not zero** unless a₁ = a₂ (Mathur: 'minimum but not zero').

**Energy.** Averaged over a beat period the cos term vanishes (PH-037 bridge B2), so ⟨R²⟩ = a₁² + a₂²: the mean intensity equals the sum of the two separate intensities, so beating redistributes energy in time and creates none.

**Carrier rate.** For a₁ > a₂ the angle θ stays within ±sin⁻¹(a₂/a₁) and never winds, so the waveform's average rate is f₁ (the louder wave); for a₂ > a₁ it winds once per beat and the rate is f₂; for a₁ = a₂ the phase is undefined at each silence: in 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t) the carrier factor has f̄, but each silence flips the sign, so a zero-crossing count gives f̄ + Δf/2; either way the difference from f̄ is at most Δf/2, negligible when Δf ≪ f̄. Checked numerically at 50 and 46 Hz (f̄ = 48 Hz): amplitudes 3:1 give 50.0 Hz (f₁), 1:3 give 46.0 Hz (f₂), and 1:1 gives 50.0 Hz = f̄ + Δf/2. This describes the waveform, not what a listener perceives.

### 6. Core: equal amplitudes, the envelope and the 'half-frequency' trap

*Objective:* Obtain the equal-amplitude result, count loud maxima correctly, and state precisely what frequency the signed envelope and its magnitude have.

**The result.** For a₁ = a₂ = a, module 4 gave x = 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t) with ω_b = Δω/2. Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude (bridge B2), so

$$ I ∝ 4a² cos²(ω_b t) = 2a²[1 + cos(2ω_b t)] = 2a²[1 + cos(Δω t)] $$

(Upadhyaya Eq. 9, PDF p. 418). The intensity oscillates at **Δω**, that is at Δf = f₁ − f₂ hertz, between 4a² and 0, and its mean is 2a² (twice a single wave's a², energy preserved).

**Numbers (recomputed).** a = 2 mm, f₁ = 444 Hz, f₂ = 440 Hz. Carrier f̄ = 442 Hz. Signed factor 2a cos(2π·2t) = 4 mm × cos(2π·2t): frequency 2 Hz, period 0.5 s. It is zero at t = 0.125 s, 0.375 s, 0.625 s, ...: silences every 0.25 s. Loud maxima at t = 0, 0.25, 0.5, ... : **4 per second = f₁ − f₂**. A numerical count of intensity peaks over 4 s agrees.

**The careful statement.**
- The **signed** envelope factor cos(ω_b t) has frequency **Δf/2**.
- Its **magnitude** |cos(ω_b t)|, and the intensity cos²(ω_b t), have frequency **Δf**, because cos reaches ±1 twice per cycle (bridge B1).
- The **beat frequency** is the loudness frequency: **Δf**. Upadhyaya writes ν_beat = 2ν_mod = ν₁ − ν₂ with ν_mod = Δf/2; NCERT (PDF p. 16 to 17) says the intensity waxes and wanes at 2ω_b = ω₁ − ω₂.
- Each zero of cos(ω_b t) flips the sign of the carrier: the carrier phase jumps by π there. The ear tracks loudness, not that sign change, which is why the magnitude is what matters.

**A wording trap.** NCERT's Fig. 14.16 caption (11 Hz and 9 Hz, PDF p. 17) says 'the amplitude of the resultant wave shows beats at a frequency of 2 Hz'. Read it as: the magnitude of the amplitude (the loudness) fluctuates at 2 Hz = 11 − 9. The signed factor cos(2π·1·t) has frequency 1 Hz. Answers should say 'loudness' or 'intensity' or 'magnitude of the envelope' and give f₁ − f₂.

**Limits and checks.** f₁ = f₂: ω_b = 0, the envelope is a constant 2a, no beats. *Dimension:* the cosines are dimensionless, so x has the dimension of a. For unequal amplitudes (module 5) the resultant amplitude R(t) has period exactly 1/Δf and never reaches zero; the sudden π jump of the carrier phase belongs to a₁ = a₂ only.

**Graph description.** The curve is a fast 442 Hz oscillation inside the outline ±4 mm|cos(2π·2t)|. The outline touches ±4 mm at t = 0, 0.25 s, ... and pinches to zero at t = 0.125 s, 0.375 s, ...

### 7. Master: waves in space, the beat length, and the limits of the picture

*Objective:* Carry the result from a single point to a travelling wave, derive the beat length, and state the limiting cases and audibility.

**Setting.** Two plane waves of the same direction in a medium where both travel at speed v: y₁ = a₁ cos(k₁x − ω₁t) and y₂ = a₂ cos(k₂x − ω₂t), with wavenumber k = 2π/λ and ω = vk. Mathur 10.14 (PDF p. 488 to 489) describes beats as 'interference in time' and the pattern as travelling onward.

**At a fixed point x₀.** Since cos is even, y₁ = a₁ cos(ω₁t − k₁x₀): an SHM with phase constant −k₁x₀, and likewise y₂. Module 3 and 5 apply, with the relative phase of the two arrows now (ω₁ − ω₂)t − (k₁ − k₂)x₀:

$$ R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos(Δω t − Δk x₀) $$

So the beat frequency is Δf at **every** point; the position x₀ only shifts the instant of the loud maxima.

**At a fixed time: the beat length.** Now R² is periodic in x with period 2π/Δk. With k = 2π/λ, Δk = k₁ − k₂ = 2π(1/λ₁ − 1/λ₂) > 0, so

$$ L_b = 2π/|Δk| = 1/|1/λ₁ − 1/λ₂| = λ₁λ₂/|λ₁ − λ₂| $$

**The pattern moves.** The pattern depends on Δωt − Δkx, so a loud maximum sits at x = (Δω/Δk)t + const and moves at **Δω/Δk**. In a non-dispersive medium ω = vk for both waves, so Δω/Δk = v: the loud and quiet regions travel with the wave. A listener at rest meets them at rate v/L_b = v|1/λ₁ − 1/λ₂| = |f₁ − f₂| ✓. This is the relation used in the 2017 question.

**Number (recomputed).** λ₁ = 0.50 m and λ₂ = 0.52 m at v = 340 m/s: L_b = 0.50 × 0.52/0.02 = 13.0 m; Δf = 340/13 = 26.15 Hz, which equals 680 − 653.85 Hz ✓.

**Not yet covered.** In a medium where ω is not proportional to k (dispersive), the envelope speed Δω/Δk → dω/dk is the *group velocity*, different from the phase velocity ω/k: to come on Day 15 (PH-041), not yet written. Two equal waves travelling in *opposite* directions give standing waves: Day 13 (PH-039), and wave packets: Day 14 (PH-040); none is taught here.

**Limiting cases.** (1) f₁ = f₂: Δf = 0, the beat period is infinite, the amplitude is constant (module 3 with its δ). (2) f₁ and f₂ far apart (say f₁ = 2f₂): the formula for R still holds mathematically but the slow-envelope picture fails because ω_b is not small compared with ω̄ (module 4); the ear hears two pitches. (3) a₂ → 0: no modulation.

**How fast can beats be heard?** The sources differ. Mathur (PDF p. 488) says the ear cannot hear more than 16 beats per second and hardly ever more than 7 or 8; Upadhyaya (PDF p. 418) says beats are audible when |ν₁ − ν₂| is less than about 10 Hz. This is a limit of human hearing, not of the mathematics; treat 'a few to about 10 per second' as the working range and verify. Beyond it the fluctuation is heard as roughness and then as two tones (standard acoustics, flagged).

### 8. Master: tuning, detection and what beats are not

*Objective:* Use beats to compare frequencies, decide the sign of a frequency change, and separate beats from neighbouring phenomena.

**Tuning (NCERT 14.7, PDF p. 16 to 17).** Two musicians playing nearly equal notes hear a waxing and waning at f₁ − f₂ and go on adjusting until the beats are no longer heard. Beats are a sensitive comparison: a 0.1 Hz mismatch is one beat in ten seconds.

**The direction problem.** The beat rate gives |f₁ − f₂|, never the sign. To find the sign, change one frequency in a known direction and watch whether the beats speed up or slow down. NCERT Example 14.6 does this: the tension of string B rises (frequency rises, f ∝ √T), the beat rate falls from 5 to 3 Hz, so B was *below* A = 427 Hz and B = 422 Hz.

**Worked rule (recomputed).** A reference fork has 512 Hz and the unknown fork Y gives 4 beats per second, so Y is 508 or 516 Hz. Loading a fork with a little wax lowers its frequency; filing raises it (standard fact, flagged).
- If wax on Y raises the beats to 6 per second, Y moved away from 512, so Y = 508 Hz (to 506).
- If wax on Y lowers the beats to 2, Y moved towards 512, so Y = 516 Hz (to 514).

**Detection by squaring.** A detector that responds to **intensity** (the ear, a photodiode, a radio mixer) squares the amplitude, and intensity contains the term 2a₁a₂ cos(Δωt) of module 5: a slow signal at Δf even when f₁ and f₂ are far too fast to follow. Brij Lal (PDF p. 266 to 267) describes two uses: a satellite's radio signal is combined with a constant signal in the receiver, giving an audible beat note whose pitch falls as the satellite passes, and a roadside radar measures a car's Doppler shift using beats. For a radar of 10 GHz and a car at 30 m/s the reflected signal is shifted by 2vf/c = 2.0 kHz (factor 2 for reflection from a moving target: standard result, flagged; the relativistic Doppler effect is PH-031, Day 12).

**What beats are not.**

| Phenomenon | Waves | Pattern | Key number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beats | same direction, nearby frequencies | loudness changes in time at a point | Δf = f₁ − f₂ |
| Interference (fringes) | same frequency, fixed phase difference | fixed pattern in space | 2a cos(φ/2) |
| Standing waves | same frequency, opposite directions | nodes fixed in space | Day 13 (PH-039) |

**Nonlinearity.** The ear and some electronic devices also respond nonlinearly and then produce extra tones at combinations such as f₁ − f₂ (standard acoustics; flagged). That is not the beating of module 5, which comes from the linear superposition of the displacements plus the square-law of loudness. Where superposition truly fails (module 2) the whole analysis needs revisiting.

### 9. Exam: the two verified questions solved and presented

*Objective:* Write complete answers to 2017 Q1(e) and 2018 Q1(c) with assumptions, steps and checks.

**2017 Q1(e), 10 marks.** 'Find the velocity of sound in a gas in which two waves of wavelengths 1·00 m and 1·01 m produce 10 beats in 3 seconds.'

*Assumptions.* Both waves travel in the same gas at the same speed v (sound in a gas is non-dispersive), and travel in one direction.

*Solution.* f₁ = v/λ₁ with λ₁ = 1.00 m, and f₂ = v/λ₂ with λ₂ = 1.01 m, so f₁ > f₂. The beat frequency is 10/3 s⁻¹ = 3.333 Hz. Then

$$ f₁ − f₂ = v(1/1.00 − 1/1.01) = v(0.01/1.01) = 10/3 $$

$$ v = (10/3)(1.01/0.01) = (10/3)(101) = 336.67 m/s ≈ 337 m/s $$

*Check.* f₁ = 336.67 Hz, f₂ = 333.33 Hz, difference 3.33 Hz ✓. The value is close to the speed of sound in air near 0 °C that NCERT quotes (331.3 m/s, PDF p. 10); a gas other than air could differ, so only plausibility is claimed. The shortcut v ≈ Δf λ²/Δλ with λ = 1.005 m gives 336.67 m/s (to 0.01 m/s).

*Traps.* Using Δλ without the reciprocal; reading '10 beats in 3 s' as 10 per second (it is 3.33); using f = 1/λ instead of v/λ. This is practice analysis, not an official key.

**2018 Q1(c), 10 marks.** 'When the two waves of nearly equal frequencies interfere, then show that the number of beats produced per second is equal to the difference of their frequencies.'

*Suggested answer structure (practice target about 12 minutes; not an official allotment or key).*
1. State the setting: two waves in the same direction, amplitudes a₁ ≥ a₂, frequencies f₁ > f₂ nearly equal; at a point their displacements are x₁ = a₁ cos ω₁t and x₂ = a₂ cos ω₂t; superposition: x = x₁ + x₂.
2. Do module 5: x = R cos(ω₁t − θ) with R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos(Δωt).
3. Conclude: R² and hence the intensity repeat every 1/Δf, the maxima (a₁ + a₂)² and minima (a₁ − a₂)², so beats per second = Δf = f₁ − f₂.
4. Add the equal-amplitude form x = 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t) with the sentence that |cos| has twice the frequency of cos, and a neat sketch of the envelope.
5. Mention that for a₁ = a₂ the minimum is zero, and that beats are audible only when Δf is a few to about 10 per second.

**Takeaway.** Both questions rest on f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|; the care lies in the derivation (2018), the per-second conversion and the reciprocal wavelengths (2017).

### Notes: Key equations

- Principle: y = y₁ + y₂ + ... holds when the governing equation is linear; displacement adds, intensity does not.
- Same frequency: x = a₁cos(ωt + φ₁) + a₂cos(ωt + φ₂) = A cos(ωt + φ); A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂cos δ, δ = φ₂ − φ₁; tan φ = (a₁sin φ₁ + a₂sin φ₂)/(a₁cos φ₁ + a₂cos φ₂); |a₁ − a₂| ≤ A ≤ a₁ + a₂.
- Equal amplitudes, different frequencies: x = 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t), ω̄ = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2, ω_b = (ω₁ − ω₂)/2; I ∝ 2a²[1 + cos Δωt].
- Unequal amplitudes: x = R cos(ω₁t − θ); R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂cos Δωt; tan θ = a₂sin Δωt/(a₁ + a₂cos Δωt); R from a₁ + a₂ down to |a₁ − a₂|.
- Beat frequency f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|; beat period 1/Δf; contrast V = 2a₁a₂/(a₁² + a₂²); mean intensity ∝ a₁² + a₂².
- Waves: R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂cos(Δωt − Δkx); beat length L_b = λ₁λ₂/|λ₁ − λ₂|; pattern speed Δω/Δk (= v if non-dispersive).

### Notes: Signed envelope versus loudness (the central trap)

- Equal amplitudes: x = 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t). The signed factor cos(ω_b t) has frequency Δf/2 (period 2/Δf).
- Its magnitude |cos(ω_b t)| and the intensity cos²(ω_b t) have frequency Δf (period 1/Δf).
- Beat frequency = loud maxima per second = Δf = 2 × (Δf/2). Upadhyaya: ν_beat = 2ν_mod = ν₁ − ν₂. NCERT: intensity waxes and wanes at 2ω_b = ω₁ − ω₂.
- Loudness depends on magnitude, not sign; the carrier phase jumps by π at each zero of cos(ω_b t) when a₁ = a₂.
- Beat frequency is in hertz: Δf, not Δω (a factor 2π) and not (f₁ + f₂).

### Notes: Beats, interference and standing waves

- Beats are interference in time (Mathur): the relative phase of two waves at a point changes steadily because f₁ ≠ f₂.
- Fixed-phase interference gives a steady spatial pattern; standing waves (opposite directions, same frequency) are taught on Day 13 (PH-039, not yet written).

### Notes: Limits and exceptions

- Superposition fails for nonlinear equations: quadratic or cubic restoring forces, large-swing pendulum, shock waves (Mathur, Upadhyaya).
- The slow-envelope picture needs Δf ≪ f₁ + f₂; for widely different frequencies only the exact sum is valid.
- Unequal amplitudes: the minimum is a₁ − a₂, not zero; the carrier's average frequency is that of the louder wave (waveform zero-crossing rate, not perceived pitch).
- f₁ = f₂: no beats, constant amplitude from module 3 with the given phase difference.
- Audible-beat limit: Mathur gives 16 and 7 to 8 per second, Upadhyaya about 10 Hz; sources differ, verify (human hearing, not mathematics).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton for beats

- x = a₁cos ω₁t + a₂cos(ω₁t − Δωt).
- Expand: = cos ω₁t (a₁ + a₂cos Δωt) + sin ω₁t (a₂ sin Δωt).
- Set R cos θ = a₁ + a₂cos Δωt, R sin θ = a₂ sin Δωt ⇒ x = R cos(ω₁t − θ).
- R² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂cos Δωt: period 1/Δf ⇒ f_beat = f₁ − f₂.
- Check: a₂ → 0 gives R = a₁; mean R² = a₁² + a₂².

### Core concepts

- Superposition: the resultant displacement is the sum; valid for linear equations only.
- Two SHMs of equal ω add to an SHM of the same ω, with A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂cos δ.
- Two SHMs of nearby unequal ω make an amplitude-modulated oscillation: beats at f₁ − f₂.
- Loudness depends on the magnitude (or square) of the envelope.

**Q.** A quantity cos(2π·5 t) has what period and how many times per second does |cos| reach 1?

**A.** Period 0.2 s; |cos| reaches 1 ten times a second (twice per cycle).

**Q.** Two waves of amplitude a each meet in antiphase. What is the resultant amplitude?

**A.** Zero (A = |a − a|).

**Q.** Name the two frequencies in x = 2a cos(ω_b t) cos(ω̄ t).

**A.** ω̄ = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2 (carrier) and ω_b = (ω₁ − ω₂)/2 (modulation).

**Q.** Why does superposition hold for the SHM equation but not for the anharmonic oscillator?

**A.** The SHM equation is linear, so the sum of solutions solves it; the quadratic term gives a cross term 2k₂x₁x₂ that the sum cannot cancel.

**Q.** Explain why unequal-amplitude beats never reach silence.

**A.** The minimum of R is |a₁ − a₂|, which is nonzero unless a₁ = a₂.

**Q.** Give the physical reason that the mean intensity over a beat period is a₁² + a₂².

**A.** The interference term 2a₁a₂cos Δωt averages to zero over a period; energy is redistributed in time, not created.

**Q.** Two forks of 320 Hz and 324 Hz are sounded together. State the beat frequency, the period between loud sounds and the pitch heard (equal amplitude).

### Memory skeleton

- Superposition: displacements add; linear equations only
- Same frequency: A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂cos δ
- Different frequencies: x = a₁cos ω₁t + a₂cos(ω₁t − Δωt) → R cos(ω₁t − θ)

- Limits: nonlinearity, Δf ≪ f₁ + f₂, unequal minimum a₁ − a₂
