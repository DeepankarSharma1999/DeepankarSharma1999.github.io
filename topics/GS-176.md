### Bridge: Collective against individual responsibility

Think of a school cricket team. If the team loses badly, the **whole team** is answerable together: nobody can say 'my part was fine, the loss is the others' business'. That is **collective responsibility**. If one player is dropped for missing practice, that is a matter between that **one player** and the captain. That is **individual responsibility**.

In government, the ministers form the 'team' and the Prime Minister is the 'captain'. Collective responsibility means all ministers answer together to the Lok Sabha: when the House loses confidence, the whole Council goes, and every minister must support the team's decisions in public. Individual responsibility means each minister answers for his own ministry, and the President (acting on the PM's advice) can remove one minister without ending the Council.

Why both exist: collective responsibility makes the government a united team accountable to the elected House; individual responsibility lets the captain remove a weak or disloyal member without a crisis. 'Responsible' here is **political** (answerable to the House, which can remove the government). It is not the same as being **legally liable** in a court; module 8 explains that difference.

### Bridge: Council of Ministers and Cabinet in plain words

Picture a company. The **whole management** includes senior directors, middle managers and assistant managers. A small **board** of the senior directors meets every week and takes the big decisions. The management as a whole does not meet daily, but every manager is bound by the board's decisions.

In the Union government the whole management is the **Council of Ministers**: it contains Cabinet ministers, ministers of state and deputy ministers, with the Prime Minister at the head. The **Cabinet** is the board: only the Cabinet ministers, the senior group that actually meets and decides. Every Cabinet is inside the Council; not every member of the Council is in the Cabinet.

Words you will see again: a **minister of state** is a junior minister (some are given independent charge of a ministry); a **portfolio** is the ministry or department a minister looks after; a **minister of Cabinet rank** is a senior minister who sits in the Cabinet. The Constitution talks about the Council (Articles 74-75) and mentions the Cabinet in Article 352 (added in 1978). Newspapers often say 'the Cabinet approved' when the Cabinet has taken the decision on behalf of the whole Council.

### Bridge: Percentages, 'cannot exceed' and rounding down

Some rules in the Constitution are written as a percentage of a number, such as 'not more than 15 per cent of the members of the Lok Sabha'. To use such a rule, change the percentage into a decimal and multiply: 15 per cent is 15/100 = 0.15, so the cap is 0.15 × (the number of members).

Because ministers are whole people, you cannot have 0.45 of a minister. When the answer is not a whole number, the rule 'shall not exceed' means you must stay at or below the limit, so you **round down**. For example, 15 per cent of 200 is 0.15 × 200 = 30, exactly. 15 per cent of 210 is 31.5; the largest whole number that does not exceed 31.5 is 31 (32 would be 15.2 per cent, which is over the limit).

Quick check method: compute the percentage of your answer. 31 ÷ 210 = 14.76 per cent (allowed); 32 ÷ 210 = 15.24 per cent (not allowed). Always check the number just above your answer. Remember also to read what the rule counts: the Union cap counts the **Prime Minister** as well as the other ministers.

### 1. Orientation: the parliamentary executive and the Articles map

*Objective:* Explain what the executive is, why India chose a parliamentary executive, and which Articles govern the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers.

## What is the executive?

Every government does three jobs: it makes laws, it carries them out, and it settles disputes about them. The organ that carries laws and policies out is called the **executive**. NCERT adds that the executive also helps frame policy and is not only the President, Prime Minister and ministers: it also includes the administrative machinery of civil servants. It then separates two parts. The **political executive** is the heads of government and their ministers, who carry the overall responsibility for policy. The **permanent executive** is the civil servants who handle day-to-day administration and stay in service when governments change.

This lesson is about the political executive at the Union (Centre) level: the **Prime Minister (PM)** and the **Council of Ministers (CoM)**. The President is covered in GS-174; the Vice-President in GS-175; the State version (Governor, Chief Minister) in GS-189. A later lesson on the Cabinet and its committees (GS-177, Day 6) is not yet written, so only an outline appears here.

## Head of state and head of government

Two ideas run through the whole lesson. The **head of state** is the formal, symbolic head of the country. The **head of government** actually leads the day-to-day running of the government. In India the President is the head of state and the PM is the head of government. Laxmikanth uses two Latin-style labels: the President is the **nominal executive** (de jure, meaning 'by law') and the PM is the **real executive** (de facto, meaning 'in fact').

NCERT explains why the framers chose this. India had already worked the parliamentary system under the Acts of 1919 and 1935, which showed that representatives of the people can control the executive. The alternative, a presidential executive, concentrates power in one office and risks what NCERT calls a **personality cult**. The framers wanted a strong executive with safeguards, so the Constitution adopted the parliamentary executive for both the Union and the States. In plain words: a **parliamentary executive** is an executive that depends on the support of the majority in the legislature (NCERT, Exercise 1).

## The Articles map

Laxmikanth says the principles of the parliamentary system are not spelt out in detail; two Articles deal with them in a broad way. Learn the five Articles below as one set, because Prelims keeps asking about them.

- **Article 74**: a CoM with the PM at the head to aid and advise the President.
- **Article 75**: other provisions as to ministers (appointment, number, term, responsibility, oath, six-month rule, salary).
- **Article 77**: conduct of business of the Government of India (executive action is taken in the President's name).
- **Article 78**: duties of the PM towards the President (furnishing information).
- **Article 88**: rights of ministers as respects the Houses (speak, but vote only in their own House).

One more is useful: The word 'Cabinet' was inserted into **Article 352** (clause 3) by the 44th Amendment (1978; module 9; verify the clause).

### 2. Appointing the Prime Minister: convention and discretion in a hung House

*Objective:* Explain how the Prime Minister is appointed, when the President may use his own judgement, and apply the rule to the 1979, 1984, 1989-98 examples.

## What the Constitution says

Article 75(1) says only that the PM **shall be appointed by the President**. The Constitution lays down no procedure for selecting the PM. Laxmikanth stresses that this does not make the President free to pick anyone. Under the conventions of the parliamentary system the President appoints the **leader of the majority party in the Lok Sabha**. NCERT's version, which is the safer form for MCQs, is: the President appoints the leader of the party **or alliance that has the support of the majority** in the Lok Sabha (not simply the leader of the largest party, and not necessarily the largest single party).

## The hung House: where discretion enters

A **hung House** is a Lok Sabha after an election in which no party has a clear majority. Here the President may use personal discretion (GS-174, module 9 sets out this 'situational discretion' for the President and compares it with the Governor's). Laxmikanth's description of usual practice: the President appoints the leader of the **largest party or coalition** and asks him to seek a **vote of confidence** (a vote in which the House shows whether it supports the government) in the Lok Sabha **within a month**.

NCERT gives a concrete case. After the March 1998 election the BJP and its allies had **251 seats, 21 short of a majority**. President K.R. Narayanan did not simply swear Vajpayee in: he asked the alliance leader to furnish documents showing support from the parties concerned and also advised him to win a vote of confidence **within ten days** of being sworn in. 'A month' is Laxmikanth's general description; ten days was the time set in this case.

## Cases to remember

- **1979**: Neelam Sanjiva Reddy appointed Charan Singh, a coalition leader, after the Janata government of Morarji Desai fell. Laxmikanth calls this the first exercise of the President's discretion in choosing a PM.
- **1984**: when Indira Gandhi was assassinated there was no obvious successor. President Zail Singh appointed Rajiv Gandhi, ignoring the earlier practice of a caretaker PM. Laxmikanth's note: after the deaths of Nehru and Shastri the President had named the seniormost minister, Gulzari Lal Nanda, as temporary PM both times (the years 1964 and 1966 are standard knowledge; verify). The Congress parliamentary party later elected him leader unanimously. If the ruling party itself elects a new leader, the President has no choice but to appoint him.
- **The 'appoint first, prove later' rule**: the Delhi High Court held in 1980 that the Constitution does not require a person to prove a majority before appointment; the President may appoint him and then ask him to prove it within a reasonable time. Laxmikanth lists Charan Singh (1979), V.P. Singh (1989), Chandrasekhar (1990), P.V. Narasimha Rao (1991), A.B. Vajpayee (1996), Deve Gowda (1996), I.K. Gujral (1997) and Vajpayee again (1998) as appointed this way. The case name for the 1980 ruling is not in the extract; verify.

## Must the PM be in the Lok Sabha?

No. The PM may belong to **either House** of Parliament. Laxmikanth names three PMs from the Rajya Sabha: Indira Gandhi (1966), Deve Gowda (1996) and Manmohan Singh (2004). In Britain the PM must be in the House of Commons. A person who is in neither House can also be appointed: Laxmikanth reports a **1997 Supreme Court** ruling that such a person can be PM for **six months**, within which he must become a member of either House or cease to be PM (the case name is not in the extract; verify).

## Why the President's discretion is limited

Discretion arises only when the Lok Sabha does not clearly point to one leader. NCERT adds that it grew after 1989: in the four general elections from 1989 to 1998 no single party or coalition won a majority, so the President had to judge who could actually form and run a government.

### 3. Oath, term, salary, vacancy; Chief Ministers who became Prime Ministers

*Objective:* State the PM's oaths, term, salary rules and what happens when the PM resigns or dies, and recall the six Chief Ministers who became PMs.

## Oath

Before the PM enters office the President administers the **oath of office** and the **oath of secrecy**. In the oath of office the PM swears four things: true faith and allegiance to the Constitution; to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India; to discharge the duties of the office faithfully and conscientiously; and to do right to all manner of people in accordance with the Constitution and the law, without fear or favour, affection or ill will. In the oath of secrecy he swears not to reveal, directly or indirectly, any matter brought under his consideration or known to him as a Union minister, except as needed for the due discharge of his duties. Laxmikanth says the forms are the same as for any Union minister (the forms are in the Third Schedule; standard knowledge, verify).

## Term

The PM's term is **not fixed**. He holds office **during the pleasure of the President**. Laxmikanth immediately qualifies this: the President cannot dismiss the PM at will. As long as the PM enjoys majority support in the Lok Sabha he cannot be dismissed; if he loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha he must resign, or the President can dismiss him. Laxmikanth's examples of resignation after defeat in the Lok Sabha are V.P. Singh in 1990 and Deve Gowda in 1997.

Compare with the President, whose five-year term is fixed (GS-174): a head of government dependent on a legislature needs a flexible term; the head of state needs a fixed one. NCERT says exactly this: the Council of Ministers may be removed at any time, so the State needs a head with a fixed term.

## Salary

Parliament determines the PM's salary and allowances from time to time. The PM gets what a Member of Parliament gets and in addition a **sumptuary allowance** (an allowance for the expenses of the office), free accommodation, travelling allowance and medical facilities. Laxmikanth reports that in 2001 the sumptuary allowance was raised from ₹1,500 to ₹3,000 a month; these amounts are dated, so verify the present figures before quoting. The Salaries and Allowances of Ministers Act, 1952 is the law Laxmikanth cites for ministers.

## When the PM goes, the Council goes

The PM stands at the head of the Council. If he **resigns or dies**, the Council of Ministers is **automatically dissolved**, which creates a vacuum. Each minister does not have to resign separately (Laxmikanth, note 3). But the resignation or death of any other minister merely creates a vacancy that the PM may or may not fill. NCERT adds that the Council comes into existence only after the PM has taken his oath.

## Chief Ministers who became Prime Ministers

Laxmikanth names six people who became PM after being Chief Ministers (CM):

- **Morarji Desai**: Bombay State, 1952-56; first non-Congress PM, March 1977.
- **Charan Singh**: undivided Uttar Pradesh, 1967-68 and again 1970; succeeded Desai.
- **V.P. Singh**: Uttar Pradesh; PM in the short-lived National Front government, December 1989 to November 1990.
- **P.V. Narasimha Rao**: Andhra Pradesh, 1971-73; first PM from South India, PM 1991-96.
- **H.D. Deve Gowda**: Karnataka; CM when chosen to lead the United Front government, June 1996.
- **Narendra Modi (BJP)**: Gujarat, four times from 2001 to 2014; he was CM of Gujarat until shortly before being sworn in as PM on 26 May 2014 (Laxmikanth says 'was CM when he became PM'; verify dates).

Memory hook: most of these names also appear in the hung-House cases of module 2, which links the two topics. The extract does not give V.P. Singh's or Deve Gowda's CM years, so they are left out here.

### 4. Powers and functions of the Prime Minister: the three roles

*Objective:* List the Prime Minister's powers in relation to the Council, the President and Parliament, plus his other roles, and tie each to an Article.

## Three roles and a list of others

Laxmikanth studies the PM's powers under three heads: **in relation to the Council of Ministers**, **in relation to the President** and **in relation to Parliament**. A fourth group covers other functions. Think of them as three connections: the PM links the Council with the President above, with Parliament beside it, and with his own team.

## 1. In relation to the Council of Ministers

As head of the Union Council the PM:

1. **Recommends persons** whom the President can appoint as ministers. The President can appoint only those recommended by the PM (Article 75(1)).
2. **Allocates and reshuffles portfolios** (a portfolio is the ministry or department given to a minister).
3. Can ask a minister to **resign**, or advise the President to **dismiss** him, in case of a difference of opinion.
4. **Presides over** meetings of the Council and influences its decisions.
5. **Guides, directs, controls and coordinates** the activities of all ministers.
6. Can bring about the **collapse of the Council** by resigning.

Result: ministers cannot function without the PM. That is why his resignation or death dissolves the Council (module 3).

## 2. In relation to the President

The PM is the **principal channel of communication** between the President and the Council. Article 78 makes it his duty:

- (a) to communicate to the President all decisions of the Council about the administration of the Union and proposals for legislation;
- (b) to furnish whatever information the President calls for about Union administration and legislative proposals; and
- (c) if the President requires, to submit for the Council's consideration any matter on which a **minister** has taken a decision but which the Council has not considered.

The PM also **advises the President on appointments** of important officials: the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chairman and members of the UPSC, election commissioners, and the chairman and members of the Finance Commission. In practice the real choice is thus the PM's.

## 3. In relation to Parliament

The PM is the **leader of the Lower House** (Lok Sabha); if the PM is a Rajya Sabha member, he nominates a Lok Sabha minister as Leader of the House (GS-183 module 8). He therefore:

1. **Advises the President** on summoning and proroguing sessions (to prorogue is to end a session formally without dissolving the House);
2. **Can recommend dissolution** of the Lok Sabha at any time; and
3. **Announces government policies** on the floor of the House.

## Other powers and functions

Laxmikanth adds: he chairs **NITI Aayog** (successor to the Planning Commission), the National Integration Council, the Inter-State Council, the National Water Resources Council and some other bodies; he plays a significant role in shaping **foreign policy**; he is the **chief spokesman** of the Union government; he is the **crisis manager-in-chief** at the political level in emergencies; he meets different sections of people and receives memoranda from them; he is the **leader of the party in power**; and he is the **political head of the services**.

## Quick check on the Council's link with the House

Article 88 gives ministers the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of either House, joint sittings and committees of which they are named members, but **not to vote** except in the House of which they are members (Laxmikanth, p. 469). A Rajya Sabha minister can therefore speak in the Lok Sabha but cannot vote there.

### 5. How powerful is the Prime Minister? Position, Article 78 and the American comparison

*Objective:* Weigh the descriptions 'primus inter pares' and 'keystone', explain the sources and limits of the PM's power, and compare the PM with the President and the US President in outline.

## The labels

Laxmikanth collects British descriptions that he says hold good for India:

- Lord Morley: the PM is **primus inter pares** (Latin for 'first among equals') and the **keystone of the Cabinet arch**.
- Harcourt: **inter stellas luna minores**, 'a moon among lesser stars'.
- Jennings: 'a sun around which planets revolve'; 'all roads in the Constitution lead to the Prime Minister'.
- Laski: central to the formation, life and death of the Cabinet; the pivot of the governmental machinery.
- Munro: 'captain of the ship of State'.
- Crossman and Berkeley (cited by Laxmikanth) go further and describe 'Prime Ministerial government' in Britain, a claim that Laxmikanth says holds in the Indian context too.

Note the sharp tension: 'first among equals' stresses that ministers are colleagues, 'keystone' stresses that the arch falls without him. NCERT's own discussion item (Exercise 6) offers three views: PM is like a king; PM is only first among equals; PM has to consider party members and supporters but still has a greater say in policy and in choosing ministers. NCERT's own text supports the third view.

## Sources and limits of power (NCERT)

NCERT names the **sources** of prime-ministerial power: control over the Council of Ministers, leadership of the Lok Sabha, command over the bureaucratic machine, access to the media, projection of personality during elections, and the role as national leader in international summitry. Nehru described the PM as the **linchpin** of government. Ambedkar, quoted by Laxmikanth, said that if any functionary under our Constitution is to be compared with the US President, it is the PM and not the President of the Union.

The **limits**: the power actually used 'depends on the prevailing political conditions'. It was unassailable when one party held a Lok Sabha majority. In the coalition era (since 1989) NCERT lists four changes: a growing discretionary role for the President in choosing the PM; more consultation between partners, eroding prime-ministerial authority; restrictions on choosing ministers, ranks and portfolios; and policies framed after negotiation, so that the PM acts 'more as a negotiator than as leader of the government'.

## The PM and the President

Three Articles frame the relationship (Laxmikanth): **Article 74** (aid and advice), **Article 75** (the PM is appointed by the President; ministers hold office during pleasure; the Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha), and **Article 78** (the PM's duties of information). The history of binding advice (42nd Amendment, 1976; 44th Amendment, 1978) is kept once, in module 6, and GS-174 module 9 explains the President's side; do not repeat it here, only keep this one line: the President has a right to be informed, and the PM has a duty to inform.

## The PM and the American President, in outline

The comparison table is in GS-202's master notes (NCERT-based) and in this lesson's master notes. The essential point: the US President is both head of state and head of government, elected separately and removable only by impeachment; the Indian PM is only head of government, holds office because the Lok Sabha supports him, and can be removed by losing that support. NCERT calls the US presidential office very powerful in theory and practice. Ambedkar's comparison is therefore about **real executive power**, not about titles.

### 6. Council of Ministers I: Article 74, Article 75 clause by clause

*Objective:* Read Articles 74 and 75 as a checklist and answer questions on advice, appointment, tenure, oath, six-month rule and salary.

## Article 74: aid and advice

**Article 74(1)** says there shall be a CoM with the PM at the head to **aid and advise** the President, who **shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice**. Laxmikanth explains the history in note 1. Originally the Article did not specify that the advice is binding; the **42nd Amendment (1976)** inserted that the President shall act in accordance with the advice, and the **44th Amendment (1978)** added the proviso allowing him to require the Council to reconsider its advice once, with the **reconsidered advice binding** (GS-174 module 9 covers the President's side; do not re-learn it).

**Article 74(2)**: the advice tendered by ministers to the President shall **not be inquired into in any court**. Laxmikanth says this emphasises the intimate and confidential relationship between the President and ministers (module 8 uses this clause on legal responsibility).

Two Supreme Court holdings reported by Laxmikanth: in **1971**, the council does not cease to hold office on dissolution of the Lok Sabha, Article 74 is mandatory and executive power exercised without ministers' aid and advice is unconstitutional; in **1974**, wherever the Constitution requires the President's 'satisfaction', it means the satisfaction of the Council of Ministers. The case names are U.N. Rao v Indira Gandhi (1971) and Shamsher Singh v State of Punjab (1974) (standard knowledge; verify).

## Article 75 in the Constitution's own numbering

Laxmikanth lists Article 75 as eight content points. The actual clauses are as follows (clause numbers are standard knowledge; verify against the official text):

- **75(1)**: the PM is appointed by the President; other ministers are appointed by the President **on the advice of the PM**.
- **75(1A)**: total number of ministers, **including the PM**, shall not exceed **15 per cent of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha** (91st Amendment, 2003; module 7).
- **75(1B)**: a member of either House belonging to a party who is **disqualified on ground of defection** is also disqualified from being appointed a minister (91st Amendment; Laxmikanth states the rule; the period is to be verified).
- **75(2)**: ministers hold office **during the pleasure of the President**.
- **75(3)**: the Council is **collectively responsible to the House of the People** (module 8).
- **75(4)**: the President administers the oaths of office and secrecy.
- **75(5)**: a minister who is **not a member of either House for any period of six consecutive months** ceases to be a minister.
- **75(6)**: salaries and allowances are determined by Parliament.

The six-month rule is satisfied by election **or nomination** to either House (Laxmikanth, p. 469).

## Appointment and rights in Parliament

The President can appoint as ministers **only those recommended by the PM**. Usually MPs are appointed; a non-member may be appointed if he gets into either House within six months. A minister who belongs to one House has the right to speak and take part in the other, but can **vote only in his own** House (Article 88).

## Oath and salary of ministers

Before entering office a minister takes the oaths of office and secrecy, in the same terms as the PM's (module 3). Salary and allowances are what an MP receives, plus a sumptuary allowance by rank and free accommodation. Laxmikanth's 2001 sumptuary figures (PM ₹3,000, Cabinet minister ₹2,000, minister of state ₹1,000, deputy minister ₹600 per month) are dated; verify before use.

## The Deputy Prime Minister question

In **1990** the oath of Devi Lal as 'Deputy Prime Minister' was challenged as unconstitutional, since the Constitution mentions only a PM and ministers. The Supreme Court upheld the oath: describing someone as Deputy PM (or as minister of state or deputy minister, titles the Constitution does not mention) is only **descriptive**, confers no powers of the PM, and does not vitiate the oath as long as the substantive part of the oath is correct.

### 7. Council of Ministers II: categories and the 15 per cent cap with worked arithmetic

*Objective:* Name the categories of ministers, compute the maximum size of the Union Council from a given House strength, and state the State-level floor with a verify flag.

## Categories of ministers

Laxmikanth says the Council consists of three categories: **Cabinet ministers**, **ministers of state** and **deputy ministers**. They differ in rank, emoluments and political importance. At the top stands the PM. The Constitution does **not** list these categories; they follow British conventions (the Salaries and Allowances of Ministers Act, 1952, defines a 'minister' as a member of the Council 'by whatever name called', including a deputy minister).

- **Cabinet ministers** head the key ministries (home, defence, finance, external affairs and so on), are members of the Cabinet and attend its meetings.
- **Ministers of state** may have **independent charge** of a ministry or be attached to a Cabinet minister (given a department or specific items of work). With independent charge they have the same functions and powers as a Cabinet minister in that ministry, but are not Cabinet members and attend Cabinet only if specially invited.
- **Deputy ministers** have no independent charge; they assist Cabinet ministers or ministers of state and do not attend Cabinet.
- **Parliamentary secretaries** are the last category of the 'ministry': they have no department and assist senior ministers in Parliament. Since 1967 none have been appointed except during the first phase of Rajiv Gandhi's government (Laxmikanth).
- A **Deputy Prime Minister** is sometimes included, mostly for political reasons.

**Flag.** Laxmikanth's 'three categories' differ from the popular description of four ranks (Cabinet, Minister of State with Independent Charge, Minister of State, Deputy Minister). The first statement of the 2022 Prelims question asserts that the Constitution classifies ministers into four ranks; Laxmikanth's text says the classification is conventional and absent from the Constitution (module 10). Recent official lists, to be verified, show Cabinet ministers and two grades of ministers of state; deputy ministers are rarely used.

## The 15 per cent cap

Before 2003 there was no limit on the size of the Council; NCERT says that led to very large councils and, when no party had a clear majority, to a temptation to win MPs' support with ministerial posts. The **91st Amendment (2003)** therefore inserted **Article 75(1A)**: total ministers **including the PM** must not exceed **15 per cent** of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha. NCERT applies the same limit to States (percentage of the Assembly).

## Worked example (checked by computation)

Step 1: Laxmikanth prints the Lok Sabha's present strength as **545**: 530 from States, 13 from Union Territories and 2 nominated Anglo-Indians. The nomination of Anglo-Indians ended with the **104th Amendment Act, 2019 (in force January 2020; verify)** (standard knowledge), leaving **543** elected members.

Step 2: 15% of 543 = 0.15 × 543 = **81.45**. Ministers are whole persons, so the largest whole number that does **not exceed** 81.45 is **81**.

Step 3: Using Laxmikanth's 545, 0.15 × 545 = 81.75, again **81**. The answer does not change. With the Constitution's maximum of 550 elected members, 0.15 × 550 = 82.5, giving **82**.

Step 4: Check: 81 ÷ 543 = 14.92 per cent (allowed); 82 ÷ 543 = 15.10 per cent (not allowed). A 'cap' is always rounded **down**.

So a Union Council of 81 including the PM is the legal maximum on a 543-member House, not 82. Whether 'total number of members' means sanctioned strength or sitting members, when seats are vacant, is a point to verify.

## State version (outline)

The same Amendment inserted **Article 164(1A)** for States: 15 per cent of the Assembly's strength. Standard knowledge (flagged **verify**; GS-189 module 9 states the same): a State's Council, including the Chief Minister, must be **at least 12**, so small Assemblies (for example one of 40 members, where 15 per cent is 6) are allowed 12. The Union has **no floor**. GS-189 explains the State position.

### 8. Responsibility of ministers: collective, individual and legal

*Objective:* Explain collective and individual responsibility, trace what a no-confidence defeat forces, and explain why India has no legal responsibility of ministers in the British sense.

## Collective responsibility

Laxmikanth calls collective responsibility the **fundamental principle** of the parliamentary system. **Article 75(3)** says the Council is collectively responsible to the **Lok Sabha** (not to Parliament as a whole, and not to the Rajya Sabha). Meaning:

1. **Joint liability.** All ministers together are answerable to the Lok Sabha for all acts of omission and commission; they 'swim or sink together'.
2. **Loss of confidence.** If the Lok Sabha passes a **no-confidence motion** (GS-181 module 4 explains the motion, the 50-member support and the difference from censure), the whole Council must resign, including ministers from the Rajya Sabha. Alternatively the Council may advise the President to **dissolve the Lok Sabha** and call fresh elections on the ground that the House no longer represents the electorate; Laxmikanth notes the President **need not oblige** a Council that has lost confidence.
3. **Binding decisions.** Cabinet decisions bind **all** ministers, even those who differed in the meeting. Every minister must stand by the decision inside and outside Parliament; a minister who disagrees and cannot defend it must **resign**.

NCERT states that the Council is, in effect, an executive committee of the Parliament and that the principle rests on the **solidarity** of the Cabinet. NCERT also says that a vote of no confidence even against a single minister leads to the resignation of the entire Council. Read this as a statement of the principle of solidarity; formal motions of no confidence under the Rules are directed at the whole Council, while a motion censuring one minister can be moved separately (GS-181 module 4).

Examples in the extract: **B.R. Ambedkar** resigned over differences on the **Hindu Code Bill** (Laxmikanth prints 1953; the date is generally given as 1951, so **verify**); **C.D. Deshmukh** over the policy of reorganising States; **Arif Mohammed** over the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986.

## Individual responsibility

**Article 75(2)** says ministers hold office during the **pleasure of the President**. This means the President can remove a minister even while the Council enjoys the Lok Sabha's confidence. But he does so **only on the advice of the PM**. If a minister disagrees with the PM or performs poorly, the PM can ask him to resign or advise his dismissal. Laxmikanth uses this to explain collective responsibility: the PM's power to nominate and dismiss ministers is what makes joint responsibility achievable. Ambedkar's words, quoted in the extract: collective responsibility can be achieved only through the instrumentality of the PM.

## No legal responsibility

In Britain every order of the Crown is **countersigned** by a minister; if the order violates the law, that minister is liable in court, because 'the King can do no wrong'. In India, Laxmikanth says there is **no provision** in the Constitution for the legal responsibility of a minister. A President's order **need not be countersigned**, and the courts are barred from inquiring into the advice given to the President (**Article 74(2)**). Do not confuse this with political responsibility: ministers are fully answerable to the Lok Sabha; the point is only that a court cannot enquire into the **advice**. Some summaries tie legal responsibility to Article 75(4); in the Constitution's own numbering clause 75(4) concerns the oath, so the legal-responsibility point rests on Article 74(2) and on the absence of a countersignature rule (verify the clause numbers against the official text).


### 9. Cabinet against Council, the Kitchen Cabinet, PMO and Cabinet Secretariat

*Objective:* Distinguish the Council of Ministers from the Cabinet point by point, explain the Kitchen Cabinet with merits and demerits, and place the PMO and Cabinet Secretariat in outline.

## Why the two words are confused

The words 'Council of Ministers' and 'Cabinet' are used interchangeably in newspapers, but the Constitution uses 'Council of Ministers' (Articles 74-75) and, from 1978, 'Cabinet' only in Article 352. Laxmikanth says they differ in composition, functions and role. The full eight-point table is in the master notes. The core of it:

- **Size.** The Council is the wider body: Laxmikanth says 60 to 70 ministers. The Cabinet is a smaller body of 15 to 20. These figures are dated; recent Councils have been larger than 60 (verify), but the legal cap is 15 per cent of the Lok Sabha.
- **Composition.** The Council contains all three categories. The Cabinet contains **Cabinet ministers only**; it is a part of the Council.
- **Meeting.** The Council does not meet as a body to transact business. The Cabinet meets frequently, usually once a week, and takes decisions.
- **Power.** The Council has all powers, but in theory. The Cabinet exercises them in practice and acts for the Council, directing it through policy decisions that bind all ministers. The Council implements; the Cabinet supervises implementation.
- **Constitutional basis.** The Council is dealt with in detail by Articles 74 and 75; its size and classification are not in the Constitution. The word 'Cabinet' was inserted into Article 352 (clause 3) by the **44th Amendment (1978)** (verify the clause); that Article only defines it as the council consisting of the PM and other ministers of cabinet rank appointed under Article 75 and does not describe its powers. Its role rests on **conventions**.
- **Responsibility.** The Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha; the Cabinet enforces that responsibility.

## Role of the Cabinet

Laxmikanth lists nine roles: the highest decision-making authority; the chief policy-formulating body; the supreme executive authority; the chief coordinator of administration; an advisory body to the President whose advice is binding; the chief crisis manager; dealing with major legislative and financial matters; control over higher appointments; and dealing with all foreign policy and affairs. Descriptions quoted in the extract: Ramsay Muir, 'steering wheel of the ship of state' (also used for the PM) and, in theory, a 'Dictatorship of the Cabinet'; Bagehot, 'a hyphen that joins, a buckle that binds' the executive and the legislature.

## The Kitchen Cabinet

The Cabinet's own size has led to an **inner** group. The **Kitchen Cabinet** (also called Inner Cabinet) is an informal body of the PM and **two to four** trusted colleagues who advise him on important political and administrative questions. It can include non-ministers such as friends and family, and it was especially powerful under Indira Gandhi. It is **extra-constitutional**. Laxmikanth gives three merits: a small body decides more efficiently; it can meet more often and deal faster with business; and it keeps secrets better. Two demerits: it lowers the authority and status of the Cabinet as the highest decision-making body; and it lets outsiders play an influential role, **circumventing the legal process**. Laxmikanth adds that the phenomenon exists in Britain and the USA too. Its phrase for the practice: decisions are 'cooked' there and placed before the Cabinet for formal approval.

## PMO and Cabinet Secretariat (outline only)

Laxmikanth's separate chapters on the **Prime Minister's Office (PMO)** and the **Cabinet Secretariat** are not in the extract for this lesson. In outline (standard knowledge; verify): the PMO is the PM's own staff agency headed by the Principal Secretary to the PM; the Cabinet Secretariat is under the PM's direct charge, headed by the **Cabinet Secretary**, the senior-most civil servant, and services the Cabinet and its committees. GS-177 (Cabinet and cabinet committees, Day 6) will deal with them in detail; they are only mentioned here.

### 10. How UPSC tests this topic: four verified questions, traps and a Mains frame

*Objective:* Apply the lesson to the four verified Prelims questions by analysis, recognise recurring traps and plan a Mains answer.

## What the four verified questions test

All four are Prelims GS I questions (2017, 2017, 2020, 2022). They are analysed here from the lesson; no official answer key is asserted.

**2017 Q46: the main advantage of the parliamentary form.** Options mix four ideas: independence of executive and legislature, continuity of policy and efficiency, responsibility to the legislature, and no change of head without election. Apply module 1: in a parliamentary executive the executive **depends on legislative support**. The statement that matches that dependence is 'executive remains responsible to the legislature'. Elimination: independent working describes the presidential system; the head of government **can** be changed without a general election (module 3: loss of confidence); and continuity cannot be a guaranteed advantage of an executive that can fall at any time.

**2017 Q99: the principle underlying the Cabinet form.** Module 8: the principle is **collective responsibility**. Options (a), (b) and (d) describe criticism-minimising, speed or strengthening the head. The one about 'ensuring collective responsibility of the Government to the people' states the principle in democratic language: Cabinet responsibility reaches the people through the elected House.

**2020 Q11: a parliamentary system.** Test the options on the two features: government is responsible to Parliament and can be removed by it. Options that say 'all parties are represented', that the people elect and remove the government directly, or that it cannot be removed before a fixed term fail module 3. Note 'responsible to the Parliament' here is general; the Indian Council is responsible to the Lok Sabha (Article 75(3)), a trap if the option read 'Rajya Sabha'.

**2022 Q14: two statements on ministers.** Statement 1: the Constitution classifies ministers into four ranks. Module 7: the Constitution does not classify; the categories are conventional (Laxmikanth names three), so the statement conflicts with the text. Statement 2: total ministers including the PM do not exceed 15 per cent of the Lok Sabha: matches Article 75(1A) (module 7). On this analysis the pattern points to statement 2 only; this is reasoning, not an official key.

## Recurring traps

- **House**: responsibility is to the **Lok Sabha**, not Parliament or the Rajya Sabha.
- **Cap**: 15 per cent includes the PM and applies to the Lok Sabha's strength; always round down.
- **Categories**: the Constitution does not classify ministers; the Cabinet is defined only in Article 352.
- **Who removes**: the President removes a minister, but only on the PM's advice.
- **Legal responsibility**: no countersignature; courts cannot inquire into advice (Article 74(2)).
- **Article numbers**: Article 78 (information) against Article 88 (rights of ministers in the Houses).

### Notes: Definitions in one place

- Executive: the organ of government that carries out laws and policies; the political executive (heads of government and ministers) and the permanent executive (civil servants) are its two parts (NCERT).
- Head of state: the formal, symbolic head (the President). Head of government: the leader who runs the government (the Prime Minister).
- De jure executive: nominal, by law (President). De facto executive: real, in fact (Prime Minister and Council).
- Parliamentary executive: an executive that depends on the support of the legislature's majority (NCERT, Exercise 1).
- Council of Ministers: the whole body of ministers headed by the PM (Articles 74-75). Cabinet: the smaller body of Cabinet-rank ministers, defined in Article 352 (44th Amendment, 1978).
- Portfolio: the ministry or department given to a minister. Sumptuary allowance: an allowance for the expenses of the office.
- Primus inter pares: first among equals. Keystone: the central stone of an arch that holds the others in place.
- Hung House: a Lok Sabha in which no party has a clear majority. Vote of confidence: a vote by which the House shows it supports the government.

### Notes: Kitchen Cabinet: merits and demerits (Laxmikanth)

- Cause: the Cabinet is large (15-20) and meets formally; a PM wants speed and secrecy.
