## PH-001: Newton laws and conservation laws

Physics Paper I · Physics · Mechanics: particles

### Bridge: Vectors: components, dot product and cross product

A **vector** is a quantity that needs both a size (magnitude) and a direction to be specified: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum. A **scalar** needs only a number: mass, time, energy, work. In print a vector is bold (**F**) or has an arrow; here we write it bold or with a hat for a unit vector.

## Components

Choose three mutually perpendicular axes x, y, z with unit vectors î, ĵ, k̂ (each of length 1). Any vector can be written as a sum of pieces along the axes:

$$ A = Aₓ î + A_y ĵ + A_z k̂ ,  |A| = √(Aₓ² + A_y² + A_z²) $$

Adding vectors means adding components. Newton's second law is really three equations, one per component.

## Dot (scalar) product

$$ A · B = |A| |B| cos θ = Aₓ Bₓ + A_y B_y + A_z B_z $$

θ is the angle between them. The result is a scalar. Meaning: the magnitude of one vector times the projection of the other on it. If the vectors are perpendicular the dot product is zero; if parallel it is the plain product of magnitudes. Work is a dot product: only the part of the force along the displacement counts.

## Cross (vector) product

$$ A × B = |A| |B| sin θ n̂ $$

n̂ is a unit vector perpendicular to both, fixed by the right-hand rule (curl fingers from A to B, thumb gives n̂). Order matters: B × A = −(A × B). A vector crossed with itself (or with any parallel vector) is zero. In components,

$$ A × B = (A_y B_z − A_z B_y) î + (A_z Bₓ − Aₓ B_z) ĵ + (Aₓ B_y − A_y Bₓ) k̂ $$

Torque τ = r × F and angular momentum L = r × p are cross products.

### Bridge: Derivatives as rates, integrals as sums, and the line integral ∫F·dr

## Derivative = instantaneous rate of change

If position x depends on time t, the average velocity over an interval is Δx/Δt (Δ means 'change in'). Let the interval shrink to zero: the ratio settles to a definite value, the **derivative** dx/dt, the velocity at that instant. Likewise a = dv/dt. Rules you need: d(tⁿ)/dt = n tⁿ⁻¹; d(e^{kt})/dt = k e^{kt}; d(ln M)/dM = 1/M; **product rule** d(uv)/dt = u dv/dt + v du/dt; **chain rule** d/dt f(x(t)) = (df/dx)(dx/dt). The product rule is what makes d(mv)/dt = m dv/dt + v dm/dt, the heart of variable-mass problems.

## Integral = limit of a sum

Work by a constant force along a straight line is F × distance. If F varies, chop the path into tiny pieces dx on which F is nearly constant, add F(x) dx for all pieces, and let the pieces shrink: the sum becomes ∫ F dx, the area under the F–x graph. Integration undoes differentiation: ∫ (dK/dx) dx = K_final − K_initial. Standard results: ∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1); ∫ dM/M = ln M; ∫ e^{kt} dt = e^{kt}/k.

## Line integral ∫ F · dr

In three dimensions the path is a curve. Take a tiny step dr along it; the small work is the dot product F · dr = Fₓ dx + F_y dy + F_z dz (only the along-the-path part of F counts). Add over the whole path from A to B:

$$ W = ∫_A^B F · dr $$

For most forces this depends on which route you take. For a special class (conservative forces) it depends only on A and B; that fact is what lets us define potential energy. A closed-loop line integral is written ∮ F · dr.

## Partial derivative

When U depends on x, y, z, the partial ∂U/∂x means 'differentiate with respect to x treating y and z as constants'. The gradient ∇U = (∂U/∂x) î + (∂U/∂y) ĵ + (∂U/∂z) k̂ points uphill in U; force is F = −∇U, downhill.

### Bridge: SI units and dimensional analysis

## Base units used in mechanics

Mass in kilogram (kg), length in metre (m), time in second (s). Everything else in this lesson is built from these. Force: newton, 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻². Energy and work: joule, 1 J = 1 N m = 1 kg m² s⁻². Power: watt, 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹. Momentum: kg m s⁻¹ (equivalently N s). Angular momentum: kg m² s⁻¹ (equivalently J s).

## Dimensions

Write the 'type' of a quantity with square brackets in powers of M, L, T. Velocity [L T⁻¹]; acceleration [L T⁻²]; force [M L T⁻²]; energy, work and torque all [M L² T⁻²] (torque and work share dimensions but torque is a vector, work a scalar); momentum [M L T⁻¹]; angular momentum [M L² T⁻¹]. The argument of a logarithm, exponential or trigonometric function must be **dimensionless**: in V = u ln(M₀/M) the ratio of masses is dimensionless, as it must be.

## How to use it

1. Terms that are added or equated must have identical dimensions. In M dV/dt = −u dM/dt − Mg, each term is [M L T⁻²].
2. A dimensionally wrong equation is certainly wrong; a dimensionally right one may still be wrong by a pure number (e.g. missing ½).
3. Convert units before substituting: 18 km/h = 18 × 1000 / 3600 = 5 m/s; 20 g = 0.020 kg; 10 cm = 0.10 m.
4. Scientific notation: 1.25 × 10⁶ means 1 250 000. Multiply numbers and add exponents separately.

In every derivation in this lesson, the final line is followed by a one-line dimensional check. Write that line in the exam too; it is cheap and it shows control.

### 1. Orientation: what 'Newton laws and conservation laws' asks of you

*Objective:* Know what the topic covers, how it appears in UPSC Physics Paper I, and the order in which this lesson builds it.

## What the topic is

Mechanics asks one question: given the forces, what is the motion? Newton's three laws are the rules; the **conservation laws** (momentum, energy, angular momentum) are consequences of those rules that let you skip the detailed force history and still predict the outcome. This lesson covers both halves and the links between them.

## Where it sits in UPSC

The UPSC Physics Optional Paper I syllabus opens with 'Mechanics of Particles', whose first words are 'Laws of motion; conservation of energy and momentum'. In the paper, Q1 (Section A) and Q5 (Section B) are compulsory, each with several 10-mark short parts drawn from that section, and there are longer questions (15 or 20 marks). The verified past questions attached to this lesson include a conservative-force test (2023), a retarding-force differential equation (2024), an explosion with relative speed (2021), a rocket height under variable gravity (2020) and an energy-conservation charge problem (2026). All five are solved by the ideas in modules 2 to 8. General Science in Prelims GS Paper I occasionally touches these ideas at school level (why a rocket moves in vacuum, why seat belts are needed), so a secondary mapping is noted.

## Vocabulary you will meet

- **Particle**: an object whose size does not matter for the problem; it has mass and position but no shape.
- **System**: the set of bodies you choose to analyse. Everything else is the **environment**.
- **Frame of reference**: the coordinate system and clock an observer uses. Module 2 explains why this matters more than beginners expect.
- **Derivation**: a chain of justified steps from a stated law to a result. UPSC rewards complete chains, not remembered final formulas.
- **Numerical**: a problem with numbers; the method is the derivation with values substituted at the end.

### 2. Frames of reference, inertial frames and the real content of the first law

*Objective:* Explain what an inertial frame is, why the first law is not merely a special case of the second, and show Galilean invariance of Newton's laws.

## Rest and motion are relative

A tree is at rest relative to the ground and moving relative to the Sun. Before you can write any law of motion you must say **relative to what** positions and velocities are measured. That 'what' is a frame of reference: a rigid body (imagined extended everywhere) carrying coordinate axes and a clock. Position is the vector r = x î + y ĵ + z k̂ from its origin; velocity v = dr/dt; acceleration a = dv/dt.

## The first law and what it really says

Galileo's inclined-plane reasoning (a ball rolling down one plane climbs the other to the same height; flatten the second plane and it would roll forever) led to the **law of inertia**: if the net external force on a body is zero, its acceleration is zero; it stays at rest or moves with constant velocity. Newton made this his first law.

A common objection: 'this is just F = ma with F = 0'. The deeper reading is that the first law **defines the frames in which the second law may be used**. In a frame that is itself accelerating, a force-free body appears to accelerate, so the first law fails there and so does F = ma. A frame in which the first law holds is an **inertial frame**. The first law is thus a statement about frames; the second law then gives the quantitative rule inside such frames.

## Which frames are inertial?

Any frame moving with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial (shown below). A frame rotating or accelerating relative to one is **non-inertial**; in it, Newton's laws can be rescued only by adding fictitious (pseudo) forces, treated in the rotating-frames topic. The Earth rotates, so a laboratory on Earth has a centripetal acceleration of about ω²R ≈ (7.3 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹)² × 6.4 × 10⁶ m ≈ 0.034 m s⁻² at the equator; small, so the laboratory is treated as inertial for most problems. A frame fixed relative to the distant stars is the usual idealised inertial frame.

## Galilean transformation

Let frame S′ move with constant velocity v along +x relative to inertial frame S; origins coincide at t = 0 and clocks agree (Newtonian absolute time). An event at (x, y, z, t) in S has in S′:

$$ x′ = x − v t,  y′ = y,  z′ = z,  t′ = t $$

Differentiate once: uₓ′ = uₓ − v (velocity is **not** invariant; this is the Galilean velocity-addition rule). Differentiate again: a′ = a (acceleration **is** invariant, because v is constant). Separations between two simultaneous points are also invariant: x₂′ − x₁′ = x₂ − x₁.

## Galilean invariance of the laws

Mass is taken the same in both frames. Since a′ = a, F′ = m a′ = m a = F: the second law has the same form in S′, so S′ is inertial too. For two colliding bodies, m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ in S; substitute u′ = u − v, and the extra terms −(m₁ + m₂)v appear equally on both sides and cancel, so momentum conservation also holds in S′. Note the distinction: the **value** of momentum or kinetic energy differs between frames, but the **conservation statements** hold in every inertial frame. This is the principle of Galilean (Newtonian) relativity: no mechanical experiment inside a smoothly moving closed vehicle can reveal its uniform velocity.

Limiting case: for v → 0 the transformation becomes the identity. Beyond scope but worth knowing: for speeds near light the Galilean rule fails and is replaced by the Lorentz transformation (special relativity, a later topic).

### 3. Second law as F = dp/dt, impulse and free-body diagrams

*Objective:* State and use the second law in its momentum form, compute impulses, and draw a correct free-body diagram for a connected system.

## Momentum and the law

**Momentum** p = m v (a vector, kg m s⁻¹). Newton's second law: the rate of change of momentum of a body equals the net external force and is in the direction of that force.

$$ F = dp/dt = d(m v)/dt $$

For constant mass the product rule gives d(mv)/dt = m dv/dt + v dm/dt = m a + 0, so F = m a. The proportionality constant is made 1 by defining the newton: 1 N gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻². Points to carry in your head:

- It is a vector law, i.e. three component equations Fₓ = m aₓ, etc. A force perpendicular to the velocity changes direction, not speed (uniform circular motion: F = m v²/R toward the centre).
- F is the **net external** force; internal forces between parts of the system never appear (module 5 proves why).
- It is local in time: acceleration now is fixed by force now; the body carries no memory.
- It holds only in inertial frames (module 2).

## Impulse

When a large force acts briefly (bat on ball, hammer on nail) neither F nor Δt is known separately, but their product is measurable:

$$ J = ∫_{t₁}^{t₂} F dt = p₂ − p₁ = Δp $$

Derivation: integrate dp/dt = F over the interval; the left side is the total change in p. Unit N s = kg m s⁻¹. Worked example: a 0.15 kg ball arrives at 12 m s⁻¹ and is hit straight back at 20 m s⁻¹. Taking the return direction as positive, Δp = 0.15 × [20 − (−12)] = 0.15 × 32 = 4.8 N s. If contact lasts 0.001 s, the average force is 4.8/0.001 = 4800 N. Sign trap: the initial velocity is negative; forgetting the minus gives 1.2 N s, a factor-4 error.

## Free-body diagrams (FBD)

An FBD is a sketch of **one chosen system** with **every external force on it** drawn as an arrow, and nothing else: no forces the system exerts on others, no internal forces, no velocities. Procedure: (1) draw the whole assembly; (2) choose the system; (3) draw it alone with all forces on it from the environment (weight, normal reactions, tensions, friction, applied pushes); (4) choose axes, usually along the acceleration; (5) write Σ Fₓ = m aₓ, Σ F_y = m a_y; (6) repeat for another system if needed, using the third law to relate the forces between them.

## Worked example: Atwood machine

Masses m₁ < m₂ hang from a light string over a frictionless light pulley. Take 'up for m₁, down for m₂' as the positive sense (the string is inextensible, so both have the same acceleration magnitude a, and tension T is the same throughout a massless string).

FBD of m₁: T upward, m₁g downward: T − m₁ g = m₁ a.
FBD of m₂: m₂g downward, T upward: m₂ g − T = m₂ a.

Add: (m₂ − m₁) g = (m₁ + m₂) a, so

$$ a = (m₂ − m₁) g / (m₁ + m₂),  T = 2 m₁ m₂ g / (m₁ + m₂) $$

Checks: m₁ = m₂ gives a = 0 and T = m₁g (balance). m₁ → 0 gives a → g (free fall) and T → 0. Dimensions: T is mass × g, a force. Numbers: m₁ = 1 kg, m₂ = 3 kg, g = 9.8: a = 2 × 9.8/4 = 4.9 m s⁻², T = 6 × 9.8/4 = 14.7 N; note T lies between the two weights (9.8 N and 29.4 N), as it must.

Graph in words: a against m₂ (with m₁ fixed) rises from 0 at m₂ = m₁ and flattens toward g as m₂ grows; T rises from m₁g toward 2m₁g.

### 4. Variable mass: the rocket equation derived

*Objective:* Derive M dV/dt = F_ext + v_rel dM/dt from momentum bookkeeping, integrate it for a rocket with and without gravity, and check it.

## Why F = m a is not enough

When the mass of the moving body changes (rocket burning fuel, rain drop gathering moisture, sand leaking from a trolley), F = m a is wrong unless you also account for the momentum carried in or out by the mass that joins or leaves. The safe route is always: pick a **fixed collection of matter**, write its total momentum before and after a short interval dt, and apply F_ext = dP/dt to that collection.

## Setup, sign convention, frame

Inertial laboratory frame; one dimension; +x is the direction of the rocket's motion (upward for a vertical launch). At time t the rocket (shell plus unburnt fuel) has mass M and velocity V. In dt it ejects gas of mass −dM (dM itself is negative because the rocket loses mass) with speed u relative to the rocket, directed backward, so the gas velocity in the lab frame is V − u.

## Derivation

Momentum of the chosen matter at t:

$$ P(t) = M V $$

At t + dt the same matter is a rocket of mass M + dM moving at V + dV plus gas of mass (−dM) moving at V − u:

$$ P(t + dt) = (M + dM)(V + dV) + (−dM)(V − u) $$

Expand: M V + M dV + V dM + dM dV − V dM + u dM. The V dM terms cancel; dM dV is second-order small and is dropped. So

$$ dP = M dV + u dM $$

Divide by dt and set dP/dt equal to the external force (gravity, drag; **not** the thrust, which is internal to this collection):

$$ M dV/dt = F_ext − u dM/dt $$

In vector form, with v_rel the exhaust velocity relative to the rocket: M dV/dt = F_ext + v_rel dM/dt. The term −u dM/dt is positive (dM/dt < 0) and is the **thrust**: the force the escaping gas exerts on the rocket by the third law. The equation reads 'instantaneous mass × acceleration = external force + thrust'.

## Integration: free space

With F_ext = 0 and u constant: M dV = −u dM, so dV = −u dM/M. Integrate from (M₀, V₀) to (M, V):

$$ V − V₀ = −u [ln M − ln M₀] = u ln(M₀/M) $$

This is the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation. If fuel burns at constant rate m (kg s⁻¹), M = M₀ − m t and V = V₀ − u ln(1 − m t/M₀).

## Integration: vertical launch under constant g

F_ext = −M g (downward). M dV/dt = −u dM/dt − M g, so dV = −u dM/M − g dt. Integrating with the same limits and t from 0:

$$ V = V₀ + u ln(M₀/M) − g t $$

## Checks

- Dimensions: u dM/dt is (m s⁻¹)(kg s⁻¹) = kg m s⁻² = N. In the integrated form every term is a speed.
- Limiting case dM/dt = 0: the equation collapses to M dV/dt = F_ext, Newton's constant-mass law.
- Lift-off condition: thrust must exceed weight at t = 0, i.e. u m > M₀ g, or m > M₀ g/u.
- Physical reading: a high final speed needs a large exhaust speed u and a large mass ratio M₀/M; because of the logarithm, doubling M₀/M adds only u ln 2 ≈ 0.69 u. A single stage with M₀/M = 10 and u = 2 km s⁻¹ reaches about 4.6 km s⁻¹, below orbital speed, which is why multistage rockets are used.
- Interpretation of the gravity term: slow burning (large t) wastes speed as g t; so fuel is burnt fast, but not so fast that drag and acceleration loads become excessive.

## Derivation traps

Writing F = d(MV)/dt = M dV/dt + V dM/dt and calling V dM/dt the thrust is wrong: it uses the gas velocity in the lab frame instead of relative to the rocket, and the result is not Galilean invariant. Also keep dM/dt negative when mass is lost; many sign errors come from writing the burn rate as +m but then also putting a minus on the thrust.

## Graph in words

V against t (free space, constant burn rate) curves upward: the acceleration u m/M grows as M shrinks. With gravity, the curve is lowered by the straight line g t.

### 5. Third law, internal versus external forces, conservation of linear momentum and the centre of mass

*Objective:* State the third law precisely, derive conservation of momentum for a system of particles, and show the centre of mass obeys F_ext = M A.

## The third law, stated carefully

Forces occur in pairs: the force on body A by body B is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force on B by A, F_AB = −F_BA. Four clarifications that examiners test:

1. 'Action' and 'reaction' are just two forces; neither causes the other and they act simultaneously.
2. They act on **different bodies**, so they never cancel in the FBD of one body. Weight and normal reaction on a book are not a third-law pair (both act on the book); the book's weight pairs with the pull the book exerts on the Earth.
3. For the **system** containing both bodies, the pair are internal forces and do sum to zero. This is what makes F = M a usable for extended bodies.
4. Limits: the law in its simple form assumes instantaneous interaction. For bodies at a distance interacting through fields that propagate at finite speed (e.g. moving charges and magnetic forces), the mechanical third law can fail momentarily; momentum is still conserved once the field's momentum is included. Within Newtonian mechanics it holds for contact forces and for bodies at rest.

## Conservation of linear momentum: derivation

Consider n particles. On particle i act an external force F_i^ext and internal forces F_ij from each other particle j. Newton's second law for particle i:

$$ dp_i/dt = F_i^ext + Σ_{j≠i} F_ij $$

Sum over all i:

$$ dP/dt = Σ_i F_i^ext + Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} F_ij,  where P = Σ_i p_i $$

In the double sum every pair appears twice, as F_ij + F_ji, and each such pair is zero by the third law. Hence

$$ dP/dt = F_ext (total external force) $$

If F_ext = 0 (an isolated system), dP/dt = 0 and **P is constant**. This is the law of conservation of linear momentum. Two remarks: it is a vector law, so a single component can be conserved even when others are not (e.g. horizontal momentum of a projectile that explodes, while gravity acts vertically); and it holds during a collision at every instant, because the third law is true at every instant, however complicated the contact force.

Simple illustration: gun and bullet start at rest, P = 0. After firing, p_bullet + p_gun = 0, so the gun recoils with p_gun = −p_bullet: a 4 kg gun firing a 0.02 kg bullet at 400 m s⁻¹ recoils at 0.02 × 400/4 = 2 m s⁻¹.

## Centre of mass

Define the centre of mass (CM) position

$$ R = Σ m_i r_i / M,  M = Σ m_i $$

a mass-weighted average position (for two equal masses, the midpoint). Differentiate with masses constant: M V_cm = Σ m_i v_i = P. So the total momentum of a system equals total mass times CM velocity. Differentiate again and use dP/dt = F_ext:

$$ M A_cm = F_ext $$

The CM moves exactly as a single particle of mass M acted on by the total external force would. Internal forces cannot move the CM: a shell exploding in flight scatters fragments, but their CM continues along the original parabola; a person cannot lift themselves by their own belt. If F_ext = 0 the CM velocity is constant, which is the same statement as momentum conservation.

## Checks and traps

- Dimensions: P is [M L T⁻¹], dP/dt is [M L T⁻²], a force.
- Limiting case n = 1: dp/dt = F, the single-particle law.
- Trap: applying momentum conservation when an external impulsive force acts (a ball bouncing on the ground: the ground's normal force is external and large; the ball's momentum is not conserved, though ball + Earth momentum is).
- Trap: the law requires an inertial frame; module 2 showed it is Galilean invariant across inertial frames.

### 6. Work–energy theorem, conservative forces, potential energy and conservation of mechanical energy

*Objective:* Derive ∫F·dr = ΔK from the second law, define conservative forces three equivalent ways including curl F = 0, and state exactly when mechanical energy is conserved.

## Work and kinetic energy

Work by a force over a small displacement dr is dW = F · dr (only the component of F along the step counts). Over a path, W = ∫ F · dr (bridge module B2). Kinetic energy K = ½ m v² = ½ m v · v. Both are scalars in joules; work can be negative (friction on a sliding block), kinetic energy cannot.

## Derivation of the work–energy theorem (3-D, variable force)

Start from the second law for constant mass, F = m dv/dt. Take the dot product with v:

$$ F · v = m v · dv/dt $$

By the product rule, d(v · v)/dt = 2 v · dv/dt, so the right side is d(½ m v · v)/dt = dK/dt. Multiply by dt and use v dt = dr:

$$ F · dr = dK $$

Integrate along the actual path from A to B:

$$ W_AB = ∫_A^B F · dr = K_B − K_A $$

**Statement**: the work done by the net force on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy. It is an integrated (scalar) form of the second law: time information is lost, direction information is lost, and it holds in any inertial frame. Example: a 1.00 g raindrop falls 1.00 km and lands at 50.0 m s⁻¹; ΔK = ½ × 10⁻³ × 2500 = 1.25 J, work by gravity = m g h = 10⁻³ × 10 × 10³ = 10.0 J, so the work by air resistance is 1.25 − 10 = −8.75 J, found without knowing the drag force.

## Conservative forces: three equivalent definitions

A force field F(r) is **conservative** if any one (hence all) of the following holds:

1. The work ∫_A^B F · dr depends only on the end points A and B, not on the path.
2. The work around every closed path is zero: ∮ F · dr = 0.
3. There exists a scalar **potential energy** function U(r) with F = −∇U, i.e. Fₓ = −∂U/∂x, F_y = −∂U/∂y, F_z = −∂U/∂z.

From (3), F · dr = −(∂U/∂x dx + ∂U/∂y dy + ∂U/∂z dz) = −dU, so W_AB = U_A − U_B, which proves (1); and for a closed path U_A = U_B gives (2).

## The curl-free condition

For F = −∇U to exist, the mixed second partial derivatives of U must agree, e.g. ∂²U/∂x∂y = ∂²U/∂y∂x. Written in terms of F this is ∂F_y/∂x = ∂Fₓ/∂y, and similarly for the other two pairs. The three conditions together say that the vector

$$ ∇ × F = (∂F_z/∂y − ∂F_y/∂z) î + (∂Fₓ/∂z − ∂F_z/∂x) ĵ + (∂F_y/∂x − ∂Fₓ/∂y) k̂ $$

is zero. **Curl F = 0 everywhere (in a region without holes) is necessary and sufficient for F to be conservative.** In the exam, this is the test to apply (2023 Q1(a) asks exactly this). Worked check: gravity F = −m g k̂ has all partials zero, so curl F = 0, U = m g z. Spring F = −k x î: curl zero, U = ½ k x². Friction has magnitude set by the normal force and direction opposite to motion, not a function of position alone, so no U exists; its closed-loop work is negative, never zero.

## Conservation of mechanical energy

If all forces that do work are conservative, W = −ΔU from above and W = ΔK from the theorem, so

$$ ΔK + ΔU = 0,  K_A + U_A = K_B + U_B = E $$

**Condition**: total mechanical energy E = K + U is conserved only when the work is done solely by conservative forces (forces doing no work, like a normal reaction or string tension perpendicular to motion, do not spoil it). With a non-conservative force doing work W_nc, the theorem gives

$$ ΔE = Δ(K + U) = W_nc $$

For friction W_nc < 0 and E falls; the loss appears as heat, so total energy, including non-mechanical forms, is still conserved.

## Checks

- Dimensions: F · dr is [M L T⁻²][L] = [M L² T⁻²], same as K.
- The zero of U is arbitrary (choose ground, or infinity for 1/r forces) but must not be changed mid-problem.
- Limiting case: constant force along a straight line gives W = F s and ½ m v² − ½ m u² = F s, the school result v² = u² + 2 a s multiplied by m/2.
- Graph in words: for a spring, U(x) = ½ k x² is a parabola opening upward; K is an inverted parabola; their sum is a horizontal line E; the particle turns back where the U curve meets E.

### 7. Angular momentum, torque, τ = dL/dt and central forces

*Objective:* Define L and τ as cross products, derive τ = dL/dt for a particle and a system, and show L is conserved under a central force.

## Definitions

For a particle at position r (from a chosen origin O) with momentum p, the **angular momentum about O** is

$$ L = r × p,  |L| = r p sin θ = r_⊥ p $$

where r_⊥ is the perpendicular distance from O to the line of p. The **torque (moment of force) about O** of a force F applied at r is

$$ τ = r × F,  |τ| = r F sin θ = r_⊥ F $$

Both are vectors perpendicular to the plane of the two factors (right-hand rule) and both depend on the choice of origin. Units: L in kg m² s⁻¹, τ in N m (dimensions [M L² T⁻²], same as energy, but torque is a vector).

## Derivation of τ = dL/dt

Differentiate L = r × p using the product rule for cross products (order must be preserved):

$$ dL/dt = (dr/dt) × p + r × (dp/dt) $$

The first term is v × (m v) = m (v × v) = 0, because the cross product of parallel vectors vanishes. The second term is r × F by the second law. Hence

$$ dL/dt = r × F = τ $$

Statement: the rate of change of a particle's angular momentum about O equals the net torque about O. This is the rotational analogue of F = dp/dt.

## System of particles

Sum over particles: dL_total/dt = Σ r_i × F_i^ext + Σ r_i × (internal forces). If internal forces between each pair are equal, opposite **and directed along the line joining the pair** (the strong form of the third law, true for gravity and electrostatics), the internal torques cancel in pairs: for a pair, r_i × F_ij + r_j × F_ji = (r_i − r_j) × F_ij = 0 since r_i − r_j is parallel to F_ij. So

$$ dL/dt = τ_ext $$

If τ_ext = 0, **L is conserved**. This is independent of momentum conservation: a system can have zero net external force but non-zero external torque (a couple), or vice versa.

## Central forces

A central force is directed along the line joining the particle to a fixed centre O, F = f(r) r̂. Then τ = r × F = f(r) (r × r̂) = 0 about O, so L about O is constant in magnitude and direction. Two consequences: the motion stays in the plane perpendicular to L through O, and the area swept by r per unit time, dA/dt = |r × v|/2 = L/(2m), is constant, which is Kepler's second law (developed in the central-force topic). Worked example: a particle on a string moves in a circle of radius 2 m at 3 m s⁻¹; the string is pulled in to 0.5 m. Tension is central, so m r₁ v₁ = m r₂ v₂ and v₂ = 3 × 2/0.5 = 12 m s⁻¹; kinetic energy rises by a factor 16, supplied by the work of pulling the string in.

## Checks and traps

- Dimensions: r × F is [L][M L T⁻²] = [M L² T⁻²], and dL/dt is [M L² T⁻¹]/[T], equal.
- Limiting case: a particle moving in a straight line with no force has constant L = m v r_⊥ even though it is not 'rotating'; angular momentum does not require circular motion.
- Trap: changing the origin mid-problem. L and τ must be about the same point.
- Trap: dropping the dr/dt × p term without stating why it vanishes; examiners look for 'v parallel to p'.

### 8. Collisions in one and two dimensions; laboratory and centre-of-mass frames

*Objective:* Solve elastic and inelastic collisions in 1-D and 2-D in the laboratory frame, re-solve them in the centre-of-mass frame, and derive the explosion relative-speed result.

## What a collision is

Two bodies interact strongly for a short time; before and after, the interaction is negligible. **Total momentum is always conserved** (internal impulses cancel). Kinetic energy is conserved only in an **elastic** collision; in an **inelastic** one some K becomes heat, sound or deformation; in a **perfectly inelastic** collision the bodies stick and move together (maximum K loss consistent with momentum conservation). The quantities 'before' and 'after' are what we solve for; during contact, K is momentarily smaller (bodies deformed).

## 1-D elastic collision (laboratory frame, target at rest)

m₁ at speed u hits m₂ at rest; final velocities v₁, v₂ along the same line (positive = initial direction of m₁).

$$ m₁ u = m₁ v₁ + m₂ v₂  (momentum) $$
$$ ½ m₁ u² = ½ m₁ v₁² + ½ m₂ v₂²  (kinetic energy) $$

Rearrange: m₁(u − v₁) = m₂ v₂ and m₁(u − v₁)(u + v₁) = m₂ v₂². Divide the second by the first (valid when v₂ ≠ 0, i.e. a collision happened): u + v₁ = v₂. This says relative speed of separation equals relative speed of approach. Substitute back:

$$ v₁ = (m₁ − m₂) u / (m₁ + m₂),  v₂ = 2 m₁ u / (m₁ + m₂) $$

Limiting cases: m₁ = m₂ gives v₁ = 0, v₂ = u (exchange of velocities, as in billiards or a neutron hitting a proton). m₂ ≫ m₁ gives v₁ ≈ −u, v₂ ≈ 0 (ball off a wall). m₁ ≫ m₂ gives v₁ ≈ u, v₂ ≈ 2u. The fraction of K transferred to the target is 4 m₁ m₂/(m₁ + m₂)², largest (100 %) for equal masses; this is why light nuclei (deuterium, carbon) are used as neutron moderators.

For a general inelastic head-on collision one uses the **coefficient of restitution** e = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂), with e = 1 elastic and e = 0 perfectly inelastic (standard textbook definition).

## 1-D perfectly inelastic

m₁ u = (m₁ + m₂) V, so V = m₁ u/(m₁ + m₂). Loss of kinetic energy ΔK = ½ m₁ u² − ½ (m₁ + m₂) V² = ½ [m₁ m₂/(m₁ + m₂)] u², always positive.

## The centre-of-mass (C) frame

The C-frame moves with the CM at V_cm = (m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂)/(m₁ + m₂). For an isolated system V_cm is constant, so the C-frame is inertial, and in it the total momentum is zero (the 'zero-momentum frame'). Transform: u₁′ = u₁ − V_cm, u₂′ = u₂ − V_cm, so m₁u₁′ = −m₂u₂′. For an **elastic** collision, momentum zero before and after plus K conserved forces each velocity simply to **reverse**: v₁′ = −u₁′, v₂′ = −u₂′ (in 1-D), or to rotate by a common angle keeping its magnitude (in 2-D). Transform back by adding V_cm. Worked: m₁ = 2 kg at 6 m s⁻¹, m₂ = 4 kg at rest. V_cm = 12/6 = 2 m s⁻¹. C-frame: u₁′ = 4, u₂′ = −2; after: v₁′ = −4, v₂′ = +2; lab: v₁ = −2 m s⁻¹, v₂ = 4 m s⁻¹, agreeing with the formulas above. The C-frame also shows that the kinetic energy available for deformation or reaction is only the C-frame kinetic energy ½ μ u_rel², with reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂); the CM kinetic energy ½ M V_cm² can never be converted.

## 2-D collisions (laboratory frame)

m₁ with velocity u along x hits m₂ at rest; after collision they leave in the x–y plane at angles θ₁ and θ₂ on opposite sides of x:

$$ m₁ u = m₁ v₁ cos θ₁ + m₂ v₂ cos θ₂,  0 = m₁ v₁ sin θ₁ − m₂ v₂ sin θ₂ $$

plus the K equation if elastic: three equations, four unknowns (v₁, v₂, θ₁, θ₂), so one angle must be measured. Special result: for equal masses, elastic, target at rest, momentum conservation gives u = v₁ + v₂ as vectors; squaring, u² = v₁² + v₂² + 2 v₁ · v₂, while energy gives u² = v₁² + v₂², so v₁ · v₂ = 0: the two balls move off at right angles. In the C-frame the lab angle θ₁ relates to the C-frame angle θ′ by tan θ₁ = sin θ′/(cos θ′ + m₁/m₂), so for m₁ = m₂ the lab angle is θ′/2 and the maximum deflection is 90°; for m₁ > m₂ the projectile cannot be deflected beyond sin⁻¹(m₂/m₁).

## Explosion as a reverse collision (2021 Q2(a) pattern)

A body of mass m at rest splits into m₁ and m₂ with total kinetic energy T. Momentum: m₁ v₁ = m₂ v₂ = p (opposite directions). Energy: T = p²/(2m₁) + p²/(2m₂) = p²(m₁ + m₂)/(2 m₁ m₂) = p² m/(2 m₁ m₂), so p = √(2 T m₁ m₂/m). Relative speed v₁ + v₂ = p(1/m₁ + 1/m₂) = p m/(m₁ m₂) = √(2 T m/(m₁ m₂)). Dimensions: T m/(m₁ m₂) is [M L² T⁻²][M]/[M²] = [L² T⁻²], a speed squared. Limiting case m₁ = m₂ = m/2: v_rel = √(8T/m) = 2√(2T/m), each fragment at √(2T/m), consistent with ½(m/2)v² × 2 = T.

## The five-part structure of a derivation answer

1. **Statement**: what is to be shown, in one line, with symbols defined ('Show that for a rocket in free space V = V₀ + u ln(M₀/M), where u is exhaust speed relative to the rocket').
2. **Assumptions and conventions**: frame (inertial lab), coordinate axes and positive direction, what is constant (u, g), what is neglected (drag, variation of g), sign of dM/dt. A labelled sketch or FBD belongs here.
3. **Steps**: start from a named law (F_ext = dP/dt), show each algebraic step, and name the manoeuvre ('product rule', 'third law cancels internal forces', 'dM dV second order, dropped'). Never jump from the law to the result.
4. **Result**: boxed or underlined, with the physical meaning in one sentence.
5. **Check**: dimensions of the final expression and one limiting case. For a numerical, also the units of the answer and a sanity comment ('less than the frictionless value, as expected').

Length: a 10-mark part is usually 1 to 1.5 pages; roughly 7–10 minutes. Every derivation in modules 4 to 8 is already laid out in this order; copy the pattern.

## Mapping the verified past questions

Write all three components; stating that one non-zero component suffices earns the logic, but show the computation. Trap: computing the divergence instead of the curl.
- **2024 Q1(a)**: retarding force proportional to speed, F = −k v. Method: module 3 second law as a differential equation, m dv/dt = −k v. - **2021 Q2(a)**: explosion into two masses with total kinetic energy T; show relative speed √(2Tm/(m₁m₂)). Module 8, last section; cite momentum conservation (body initially at rest) and energy bookkeeping.

## Problem A (FBD and second law)

A 4 kg block on a rough horizontal table (μ_k = 0.25) is pulled by a rope at 30° above horizontal with tension 40 N. Find the acceleration (g = 10).

*Thinking aloud*: System = block. Forces: weight 40 N down; normal N up; tension 40 N at 30°; kinetic friction μ_k N opposite to motion. Axes: x along motion, y up. The rope has an upward component, so N is **not** m g; that is the trap. y: N + 40 sin 30° − 40 = 0 → N = 40 − 20 = 20 N. Friction = 0.25 × 20 = 5 N. x: 40 cos 30° − 5 = 4 a → 34.64 − 5 = 4 a → a = 7.41 m s⁻². Check: dimensions N/kg = m s⁻²; limiting case θ = 0 gives N = 40 N, friction 10 N, a = 7.5 m s⁻²; the result is plausible.

## Problem B (momentum then energy, in that order)

A 10 g bullet at 400 m s⁻¹ embeds in a 2 kg block hanging from a long string. Find the height the block rises.

*Thinking aloud*: Two stages, two different laws. Stage 1 (collision, very short, string force vertical and no horizontal external impulse): momentum conserved, energy not. 0.01 × 400 = 2.01 V → V = 1.99 m s⁻¹. Stage 2 (swing, tension does no work, only gravity works): mechanical energy conserved. ½ V² = g h → h = 1.99²/(2 × 9.8) = 0.202 m. Trap: applying energy conservation across the embedding loses almost all the bullet's energy (½ × 0.01 × 400² = 800 J before, ½ × 2.01 × 1.99² ≈ 4 J after; 796 J became heat). Using energy from the start would predict h ≈ 40 m, absurd.

## Problem C (variable mass with external force)

A 6000 kg rocket is fired vertically; exhaust speed 1000 m s⁻¹. What burn rate just balances the weight, and what rate gives an initial upward acceleration of 9.8 m s⁻²?

*Thinking aloud*: Equation M dV/dt = −u dM/dt − M g (module 4). To hover, dV/dt = 0: −u dM/dt = M g → |dM/dt| = 6000 × 9.8/1000 = 58.8 kg s⁻¹. For a = 9.8: −u dM/dt = M (g + a) = 6000 × 19.6 → |dM/dt| = 117.6 kg s⁻¹. Check: (kg)(m s⁻²)/(m s⁻¹) = kg s⁻¹. Sign: dM/dt is negative; we report the magnitude as the burn rate.

### Notes: Definitions

- Inertial frame: a frame of reference in which a body with zero net external force has zero acceleration (Newton's first law holds); any frame moving uniformly relative to it is also inertial.
- Momentum p = m v; total momentum of a system P = Σ p_i = M V_cm.
- Impulse J = ∫F dt = Δp, the change in momentum produced by a force over an interval.
- Free-body diagram: a drawing of one chosen system showing every external force on it and nothing else.
- Conservative force: one whose work between two points is path-independent; equivalently ∮F·dr = 0; equivalently F = −∇U; equivalently curl F = 0.
- Potential energy U: a function of position such that F = −∇U; defined up to an additive constant.
- Angular momentum L = r × p and torque τ = r × F, both about a stated origin.
- Centre-of-mass frame: the inertial frame moving with the CM of an isolated system; total momentum in it is zero.

### Notes: Governing laws and equations

- Second law: F = dp/dt; for constant mass F = m a (three component equations).
- Galilean transformation: x′ = x − v t, t′ = t; u′ = u − v; a′ = a.
- Variable mass: M dV/dt = F_ext + v_rel dM/dt; rocket thrust = −u dM/dt.
- Rocket: V = V₀ + u ln(M₀/M) (free space); V = V₀ + u ln(M₀/M) − g t (vertical, constant g).
- System: dP/dt = F_ext; M A_cm = F_ext; P constant if F_ext = 0.
- Work–energy: ∫F·dr = K_f − K_i; conservative forces: K + U = E constant; with non-conservative work ΔE = W_nc.
- Rotational: τ = dL/dt; τ_ext = dL/dt for a system (strong third law); L constant for central forces.
- 1-D elastic, target at rest: v₁ = (m₁ − m₂)u/(m₁ + m₂), v₂ = 2m₁u/(m₁ + m₂); energy transferred fraction 4m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)².
- Explosion at rest: v_rel = √(2 T m/(m₁ m₂)).

### Notes: Rocket equation derivation (skeleton)

- Choose the matter: rocket of mass M at velocity V (lab frame, +x forward) at time t.
- At t + dt: rocket (M + dM) at V + dV; ejected gas (−dM) at lab velocity V − u.
- P(t + dt) − P(t) = M dV + u dM after cancelling V dM and dropping dM dV.
- F_ext = dP/dt gives M dV/dt = F_ext − u dM/dt; the second term is the thrust (positive since dM/dt < 0).
- Free space: dV = −u dM/M → V − V₀ = u ln(M₀/M). With gravity: subtract g t.
- Checks: u dM/dt has units N; dM/dt = 0 recovers F = M a; lift-off needs u|dM/dt| > M₀ g.

### Notes: Work–energy theorem derivation (skeleton)

- F = m dv/dt (constant mass, inertial frame).
- Dot with v: F·v = m v·dv/dt = d(½ m v·v)/dt = dK/dt (product rule).
- Multiply by dt, use v dt = dr: F·dr = dK.
- Integrate along the path: ∫_A^B F·dr = K_B − K_A.
- If F = −∇U then F·dr = −dU and K_A + U_A = K_B + U_B.
- Check: both sides [M L² T⁻²]; straight-line constant force gives v² = u² + 2as.

### Notes: Elastic versus inelastic collisions

- Momentum is conserved in every collision; the kinetic-energy row is what differs.

### Notes: Inertial versus non-inertial frames; laboratory versus centre-of-mass frames

- Both pairs are tested in conceptual parts of Question 1.

### Notes: Commonly confused ideas

- Weight and normal reaction on the same body are not an action–reaction pair; the pair of the weight is the body's pull on the Earth.
- Conservation of momentum is a vector statement; one component may be conserved while another is not (horizontal momentum under gravity).
- Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions and only when comparing before and after, not during contact.
- Torque and work share dimensions [M L² T⁻²] but torque is a vector (N m) and work a scalar (J).
- The curl test, not the divergence, decides whether a force is conservative.
- In F = dp/dt for variable mass, the extra term uses the velocity of the ejected mass relative to the body, not its lab velocity.
- Zero net force does not imply zero net torque, and vice versa; momentum and angular momentum conservation are separate conditions.

### Notes: Limits and exceptions

- Newton's first and second laws fail in accelerating or rotating frames unless fictitious forces are introduced.
- The mechanical third law can fail momentarily for charges interacting through fields propagating at finite speed; total momentum including field momentum is still conserved.
- F = m a assumes mass independent of velocity (speeds much less than light) and constant mass; otherwise use F = dp/dt with the full bookkeeping.
- Mechanical energy conservation fails whenever a non-conservative force (friction, drag) does work; total energy is still conserved.
- Curl F = 0 guarantees a potential only in a region without holes (simply connected); this subtlety does not arise in the standard exam fields.

### Notes: Worked numbers to remember

- Atwood machine with 1 kg and 3 kg (g = 9.8): a = 4.9 m s⁻², T = 14.7 N.
- Rocket with M₀/M = 10 and u = 2 km s⁻¹ from rest in free space: V = 2 ln 10 ≈ 4.6 km s⁻¹.
- Hover condition for a 6000 kg rocket with u = 1000 m s⁻¹: burn rate 58.8 kg s⁻¹.
- Neutron on deuteron (m₂ = 2m₁), head-on elastic: 8/9 of the neutron's kinetic energy is transferred.
- Equal masses, elastic, target at rest, glancing: the two leave at 90° to each other.
- 2 kg at 6 m s⁻¹ on 4 kg at rest, elastic: final velocities −2 and +4 m s⁻¹; V_cm = 2 m s⁻¹.

### Core concepts

- Inertial frames are defined by the first law and are the only frames in which the second law holds without pseudo-forces.
- F = dp/dt is the general second law; F = m a is its constant-mass special case.
- Internal forces cancel in pairs by the third law, which is why dP/dt = F_ext and M A_cm = F_ext.
- The work–energy theorem is the second law integrated along the path; mechanical energy is conserved only when all working forces are conservative.
- Curl F = 0 is the test for a conservative force and guarantees a potential energy function.
- τ = dL/dt; central forces exert no torque about the centre, so angular momentum is conserved.
- Collisions conserve momentum always and kinetic energy only if elastic; the C-frame makes elastic collisions a simple reversal.

**Q.** State the three equivalent definitions of a conservative force and apply the curl test to F = x²y î + zy² ĵ + xz² k̂.

**A.** Path independence; zero closed-loop work; F = −∇U with curl F = 0. For the given field the k̂ component of curl is −x² ≠ 0 (others −y², −z²), so it is not conservative by this analysis.

**Q.** Explain why weight and normal reaction on a resting book are not an action–reaction pair, and name the true partners.
