## GS-152: Globalisation and social transformation

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Globalisation and social transformation and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Meaning and economic drivers

Globalisation is the growing interdependence of people, regions and countries as social and economic ties extend worldwide. India's interconnections are old, from the Silk Route to colonial-era indentured migration, but the present phase differs in intensity, speed and scope. Liberalisation, starting in 1991, removed many rules regulating trade, finance and investment on the assumption that integration with world markets would benefit the economy. It involved IMF borrowing tied to structural adjustment, which often meant reduced state spending on health, education and social security, and a larger role for the WTO. Other drivers include transnational corporations producing in many countries, the electronic economy that moves money instantly, the weightless or knowledge economy based on information and services, and globally integrated financial markets trading around the clock.

### Uneven effects on work and livelihoods

The same policy can widen choice for affluent urban consumers while threatening a farmer's livelihood. NCERT lists casualties: women silk workers in Bihar, women fish sorters and net makers displaced by large trawlers, Gujarat's gum collectors and urban rag pickers. A new international division of labour shifts routine manufacturing to cheaper locations; Nike's relocation from Japan to South Korea, then Southeast Asia and India, shows how production follows low wages, leaving workers insecure under flexible post-Fordist systems. For urban middle-class youth, IT, call centres, BPOs, malls and restaurants opened new careers, yet broader job creation lagged: an ILO report found Asian employment grew only 1.6% in 2003–04 despite growth above 7%. Traditional workers such as Sircilla's handloom weavers and Mumbai's mill-area theatre groups suffered badly.

### Culture: homogenisation, glocalisation and consumption

One view predicts cultural homogenisation; another sees glocalisation, the blending of global and local, often as a deliberate marketing strategy. Foreign channels broadcast in Indian languages, McDonald's adapts menus and goes vegetarian during Navaratri, and music has seen Bhangra pop, Indi pop and fusion. NCERT warns that defenders of a fixed tradition sometimes invoke globalisation as a bogey to justify discrimination against women. A culture of consumption now shapes city growth through malls, multiplexes and amusement parks, and advertising treats spending as a virtue. A new class of highly paid, stressed professionals in software, banking and media forms the core of consumer demand. Meanwhile indigenous crafts and knowledge face pressure, illustrated by attempts by multinational firms to patent uses of tulsi, turmeric, rudraksha and basmati.
