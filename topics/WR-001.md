## WR-001: Decoding question and key terms

Essay (Mains 250) · Writing/Language · Essay craft

### Bridge: Words a topic may use: aphorism, metaphor, paradox, oxymoron, irony

Many essay topics are written in figurative language. Before you can decode them you need the names of the commonest forms.

- **Aphorism**: a short sentence that states a general truth, for example 'Truth knows no color.' Treat it as a claim that you may agree with, qualify or reject.
- **Metaphor**: calling one thing another to bring out a likeness. 'A ship in harbour is safe' is about a person or an institution, not about ships. Paraphrase it in plain words first.
- **Paradox**: a statement that sounds self-contradictory but may be true in a deeper way, for example 'There is no path to happiness; Happiness is the path.'
- **Oxymoron**: two opposite words placed together, such as 'deafening silence'. A prompt like 'Oxymorons reflect the ironies of life.' asks what such combinations reveal about real life.
- **Irony**: a gap between what is expected and what happens, or between what is said and what is meant. Life is full of ironies; the prompt asks you to find and explain some.

A useful habit: when a topic uses figurative words, first write the **literal** meaning in one plain sentence, then the **figurative** meaning in another, and check the two together. Reading only the literal sense is the commonest cause of off-subject essays on quotation topics.

### Bridge: Stance, thesis, counter-view and dimension

Four words recur in every module. Define them first.

- **Stance**: your position on the prompt's claim: agree, agree with conditions, or disagree. A stance answers 'what do I think about this claim?'
- **Thesis**: your stance written as one clear sentence that the whole essay will defend. Later lessons teach how to write it; here you only choose the stance.
- **Counter-view**: the strongest argument against your stance. It is not a weak objection set up to be knocked down, but the objection a thoughtful opponent would raise.
- **Dimension**: an angle from which the topic can be examined, such as social, economic or ethical.

Here is a tiny illustration. Prompt: 'Best lessons are learnt through bitter experiences.' Stance: agree with conditions (bitter experience teaches deeply, but learning from others' mistakes is cheaper and safer). Counter-view: in fields such as aviation, systems learn from other people's accidents precisely to avoid bitter experience. Dimensions: psychological, educational, organisational, ethical.

Keep the words apart. A stance is not a dimension: 'social' is an angle, while 'social media harms the young' is a stance. Mixing them is a common beginner's confusion.

### 1. What the Essay paper is, and what 'closely to the subject' means

*Objective:* State the facts of the Essay paper and explain, with one example, what keeping closely to the subject requires.

## The paper in plain facts

The Essay is **Paper I of the Mains examination**. It carries **250 marks** and is one of the papers counted for merit (the Plan of Examination lists it first among the merit papers). Each verified prompt record carries 125 marks, which is consistent with two essays of 125 marks each. The project's syllabus registry summarises the paper as two essays of about 1,000 to 1,200 words each, chosen from the topics given, written in three hours. Treat that summary as a planning figure and check the instructions printed on the paper itself.

In every paper from 2017 to 2026 the verified list holds **eight prompts**. The 2017 labels (A1 to A4 and B1 to B4) show two groups of four, so the paper is arranged in two sections. The printed instruction on the paper, not this lesson, says how many essays you write from each section.

## The official wording

The syllabus clause reads exactly:

> Candidates may be required to write essays on multiple topics. They will be expected to keep closely to the subject of the essay to arrange their ideas in orderly fashion, and to write concisely. Credit will be given for effective and exact expression.

Pull the clause into four demands.

- **Keep closely to the subject**: relevance to the exact topic given.
- **Arrange ideas in orderly fashion**: structure.
- **Write concisely**: no padding.
- **Effective and exact expression**: precise words and clear sentences.

This lesson serves the first and fourth demand, because you cannot stay close to a subject you have not understood, and you cannot be exact about words you have not defined. Structure and conciseness come in later lessons.

## 'Closely to the subject' in practice

The subject is not the general theme of the topic; it is the specific claim or phrase in front of you. Take the prompt 'Forests precede civilizations and deserts follow them.' Its theme is the environment, but its subject is much narrower: a claim about *order of events* (forests come first, deserts come after) and about a link between forests and the rise and fall of civilisations. An essay on pollution, climate summits or tree-planting drives would be about the theme and would drift from the subject.

A simple test applies to every paragraph you will ever write: **point to the words of the topic that this paragraph answers.** If you cannot, the paragraph is drifting.

## What this lesson does and does not do

It teaches you to *decode* a topic: to read it exactly, define its words, see what it asks, and list the angles from which it can be argued. It does not teach essay writing; full essays, thesis statements and outlines come later. UPSC publishes no model essay and this project has found no official marking scheme, so nothing here is an official expectation. The method is a way to be relevant and precise, which are the qualities the syllabus clause names.

### 2. Reading the topic: the types of essay topic

*Objective:* Recognise seven topic types in the verified prompts and state the right first move for each.

## Why type matters

Different topics need different first moves. A quotation must be paraphrased before it can be judged; a policy topic must be bounded before it can be discussed. The 80 verified prompts from 2017 to 2026 show the types below. Only one of the 80 is a direct yes/no question (2021 Q6 is phrased as a question but is rhetorical), and none opens with an instruction word such as 'discuss'; the command is nearly always **implicit** and you must find it (module 4).

| Type | How to recognise it | First move | Verified example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quotation or aphorism | A complete sentence stating a general truth, often a metaphor | Paraphrase in plain words, then decide whether you agree | 'Truth knows no color.' |
| Abstract or philosophical | A big idea word with a short claim | Define the abstract noun and find concrete anchors | 'Wisdom finds truth' |
| Issue or policy | Names a problem, often tied to India | Fix the scope: what, where, whose | 'Management of Indian border disputes—a complex task' |
| Two-sided or dilemma | 'Or', 'versus', or two named outcomes | Map both sides and the conditions that decide between them | 'Rise of Artificial Intelligence : the threat of jobless future or better job opportunities through reskilling and upskilling' |
| Contrast pair | Two clauses joined by a pivot | Find the pivot (not/but, is/is) | 'Culture is what we are, civilization is what we have' |
| Question form | Opens with 'Has' or 'Is' | You must answer it; choose a stance | 'Has the Non-Alignment Movement (NAM) lost its relevance in a multipolar world?' |
| Claim with cause and effect | Says that X produces Y | Test the causal link and its limits | 'Social media is triggering ‘Fear of Missing Out’ amongst the youth, precipitating depression and loneliness.' |

## The first move for each, in detail

**Quotation.** Rewrite it as if explaining it to a friend who has never read it. 'A thorn is a changed bud.' becomes: something harsh may be a thing that changed form or was changed, so hardship may have a history. Only after the paraphrase can you ask whether it is true.

**Abstract.** The shortest verified prompts are three and four words long ('Wisdom finds truth', 'Truth knows no color.'). Short does not mean easy: each word carries a large idea. Define each idea word (wisdom, truth) and then ask what *relationship* the verb claims.

**Issue.** Name the actor, the place and the time in the topic. 'Management of Indian border disputes' is not 'India's neighbours' or 'security': it is about *managing*, not resolving, and about *Indian* borders.

**Two-sided.** Do not choose a side in the first minute. First write the conditions on which each outcome depends; that list usually becomes the essay's argument.

**One-word or short-phrase topics.** None of the 80 verified prompts is a single word, but if one appears, treat it as a claim waiting for a verb: ask what could be said about it that a thoughtful person could dispute.

## A warning about familiarity

A topic that feels familiar is dangerous because you may answer the essay you prepared instead of the one set. Decode the exact words every time.

### 3. Key-term analysis: define, bound and ask

*Objective:* Apply the five-step key-term routine to a prompt and state what the topic does and does not ask.

## Why key terms decide the essay

An essay is only as exact as its definitions. Two candidates can read the same prompt and write different essays because they meant different things by one word. The syllabus clause asks for 'exact expression', so your first job is to be exact about the words you are given.

## The five-step routine

1. **Underline** the words that carry the meaning (usually nouns, the verb that links them, and any comparative or qualifier).
2. **Define** each in a working sentence of your own.
3. **Bound** each: where does it stop applying?
4. **Relate**: what does the verb or comparison claim about them?
5. **Ask / not ask**: write one line on what the topic demands and one on what it does not.

## Worked example 1: a quotation

Prompt: 'Shelving hard decisions is the least ethical course.'

| Key term | Working definition | Bound |
|---|---|---|
| Shelving | Putting a decision aside with no date or intention to decide | Different from *deferring* to gather evidence, which can be responsible |
| Hard decisions | Decisions where every option costs someone something | Not every decision is hard; easy ones are outside the topic |
| Least ethical | The lowest-ranked among the available courses | A **superlative**: the prompt claims shelving ranks below deciding badly and below passing the decision to others |
| Course | A way of acting | The topic is about conduct, not about the law |

**Relate**: the verb 'is' joins shelving to the lowest ethical rank. **Asked**: is postponing a hard decision worse, ethically, than the alternatives? **Not asked**: a general account of ethics or of leadership.

## Worked example 2: a claim about India

Prompt: 'Biased media is a real threat to Indian democracy'

- *Biased*: departing from fair and balanced reporting. Whose bias: owners, advertisers, the state, a political side? Bound: bias is not the same as having an editorial line that is openly declared.
- *Media*: print, television, digital news, and (if you include it) social platforms. Decide, and say, what you include.
- *Real threat*: the word 'real' suggests the prompt expects you to weigh evidence of harm against claims that the danger is exaggerated.
- *Indian democracy*: elections, debate, accountability and federalism, in India specifically.
- **Asked**: does biased media seriously endanger Indian democracy? **Not asked**: a history of the press or a list of media laws.

## The qualifier test

For every prompt, ask whether a **qualifier** or **comparative** is present ('least', 'real', 'only', 'more'). Such words turn a topic from 'discuss X' into 'judge this exact claim about X'. Skipping them is the commonest way to write a good essay on the wrong question.

## Time

The routine takes about two minutes once practised. Write the table in rough notes; the aim is clarity, not neat handwriting.

### 4. Command, scope and qualifier words

*Objective:* Find the implicit command in a prompt and mark its scope in place, time, actor and domain.

## The command is usually hidden

In many examination papers a command word ('discuss', 'critically examine') tells you what to do. In the verified essay prompts none opens with such a word; the command is **implicit** in the wording. You must read it out of the prompt.

## Signals and what they ask you to do

| Signal in the prompt | What it asks you to do | Verified example |
|---|---|---|
| Superlative ('least', 'best', 'most', 'supreme') | Compare against alternatives; justify the rank | 'Shelving hard decisions is the least ethical course.', 'Best lessons are learnt through bitter experiences.' |
| Absolute ('always', 'inherently', 'no ... without') | Test the universal claim; look for the exception | '‘Social media’ is inherently a selfish medium.', 'There can be no social justice without economic prosperity but economic prosperity without social justice is meaningless' |
| 'Not what X is, but what X ought to be' | Separate description from ideal; judge the gap | 'Values are not what humanity is, but what humanity ought to be' |
| 'Not necessarily' | The prompt concedes some cases; your job is to find the boundary | 'Best for an individual is not necessarily best for the society' |
| Evaluative label ('myth', 'real threat') | Weigh evidence for and against the label | 'Fulfilment of ‘new woman’ in India is a myth.' |
| Question form ('Has ... lost its relevance') | Give a direct answer, then the conditions | 'Has the Non-Alignment Movement (NAM) lost its relevance in a multipolar world?' |
| Cause word ('triggering', 'precipitating', 'reasons for') | Test the causal chain and the other causes | 'Social media is triggering ‘Fear of Missing Out’ amongst the youth, precipitating depression and loneliness.' |

## Common reading rules that apply even when the word is absent

These general rules are not tied to a word in the verified list, but they are standard in reading any topic.

- **'Should'** makes a topic about a recommendation: state who should do what, and what the cost is.
- **'Is it ...'** or a question mark demands a stance.
- **'In the age of ...'** fixes the time frame; the essay must be about what is different now, not about the thing in general.

## Scope: place, time, actor, domain

Every topic has four scope handles. Read each and write a one-word answer or 'open'.

- **Place**: India, South Asia, the world, or open? 'Indian democracy' fixes place; 'Forests precede civilizations' leaves it open.
- **Time**: past, present, future or timeless? 'Has now been technologically outsourced' ('The process of self-discovery has now been technologically outsourced.') fixes the present; 'Technology as the silent factor' is open.
- **Actor**: individual, community, state, institution? 'Girls ... boys ...' fixes two groups.
- **Domain**: social, economic, political, moral, scientific?

When the prompt leaves a handle open, choose it and **say what you chose**. A chosen scope is a strength; an unexamined one produces drift.

## Putting it together

Read the prompt once for meaning, once for qualifiers, once for scope. Then write one sentence beginning 'This topic asks me to ...'. If you cannot finish that sentence in plain words, return to the key terms.

### 5. Finding the implicit dimensions and choosing three to five

*Objective:* List the nine lenses with a question for each and choose three to five that fit a given prompt.

## What a dimension is

A **dimension** is an angle from which a topic can be examined. Most strong essays do not make one argument; they look at the topic from several angles. Each angle is a **lens**. Nine lenses cover most prompts. For each, keep one question in mind.

| Lens | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Social | How does this affect groups, relationships, norms and inequality? |
| Economic | Who gains or loses money, jobs, growth, resources? |
| Political | Who holds power, makes rules, or is left out? |
| Ethical | What is right or fair, and who bears the cost? |
| Environmental | What is the effect on land, water, air and species? |
| Historical | How did this arise, and what do earlier cases teach? |
| Technological | What does a tool or invention change, and who controls it? |
| International | How does this look across borders and between states? |
| Gender | Do women, men and other groups experience this differently? |

The list is a starting kit. Others (psychological, legal, cultural, educational, health) are common; add them when a topic calls for them.

## Choosing three to five

Do not use every lens; use the ones that fit. Five rules:

1. **Key-term link.** Choose lenses that your key terms raise ('justice' invites ethical, political and social).
2. **Distinct.** Two lenses that say the same thing (for example, 'economic' and 'financial') count as one.
3. **Example ready.** Keep a lens only if you can name one concrete example for it.
4. **One that complicates.** Include at least one lens that tests your stance, so the essay is not one-sided.
5. **Not by comfort.** Do not choose a lens only because you know facts about it; choose it because the topic needs it.

As a working guideline (not an official rule), fewer than three lenses usually gives a one-dimensional essay; more than five tends to be shallow in an essay of 1,000 to 1,200 words.

## Worked example

Prompt: 'Social media is triggering ‘Fear of Missing Out’ amongst the youth, precipitating depression and loneliness.'

- Key terms: *Fear of Missing Out*, *youth*, *triggering*, *precipitating depression and loneliness*. The prompt is a cause-and-effect claim.
- Lenses that fit: **psychological or health** (the central link), **social** (peer comparison, changing friendships), **technological** (how platform design drives attention), **economic** (the attention business behind the platforms), **ethical** (responsibility of platforms and users).
- A lens that complicates: **psychological (protective effects)**, because online communities can support isolated youth. The counter-view is kept separate, as a statement and not a lens: social media can also reduce loneliness, which tests the word 'triggering'.
- Lenses dropped: *historical* (no strong example ready), *international* (not asked), *environmental* (not relevant).

The result is five lenses (psychological or health, social, technological, economic, ethical); the psychological lens is the one that complicates, and the counter-view is stated separately in one sentence. A candidate who began writing before choosing would probably have produced a single-lens essay about mental health.

## Link to the essay paper

The syllabus clause asks you to keep closely to the subject. Lenses keep you *inside* the subject while widening your argument. Each lens should return to the exact words of the prompt, not wander into the lens's general subject.

### 6. The decoding sheet and the topic triage

*Objective:* Fill the eight-field decoding sheet in ten minutes and use it to choose between topics.

## The eight fields

The sheet joins everything so far. Fill it in rough notes; the aim is to stay on the subject, not to produce a neat page.

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| Topic | The prompt, then a plain-words paraphrase in one sentence |
| Key terms | Each term with a working definition and its bound |
| Demand | 'This topic asks me to ...' in one sentence |
| Scope | Place, time, actor, domain; note what you chose where the prompt is open |
| Stance options | Agree, agree with conditions, disagree; mark the stance you lean to |
| Dimensions | Three to five lenses, each with its question |
| Counter-view | The strongest argument against your stance, in one sentence |
| Examples | One or two example seeds per dimension (mark any that need checking) |

**Stance options** are options, not a commitment. The sheet lets you see all three before you pick. Many strong stances are 'agree with conditions': name the conditions.

**Counter-view** is the single most useful field for avoiding a one-sided essay: the best opposing argument is the paragraph that makes your own argument credible.

**Examples** are seeds, not full examples. Write a few words per dimension and mark any you must verify. The aim is to avoid starting the essay with nothing concrete to say.

## A ten-minute plan

This is a suggested allocation for practice, not an official norm.

| Minutes | Field |
|---|---|
| 1 | Topic and paraphrase |
| 2 | Key terms |
| 2 | Demand and scope |
| 1 | Stance options |
| 2 | Dimensions |
| 1 | Counter-view |
| 1 | Example seeds |

If you reach minute 10 with fields unfinished, stop. A ten-minute sheet with gaps is better than a thirty-minute sheet.

## Topic triage: choosing among four prompts

In each section of the paper you choose from four prompts (check the instruction on the paper). Familiarity is a poor guide. Instead, spend about three minutes on a **triage**:

1. For each of the four prompts write a one-line paraphrase. If you cannot, note 'unclear'.
2. For each, list the lenses you could name and an example for each.
3. Choose the prompt for which the paraphrase is clearest and at least three lenses each have an example.

When two prompts tie, choose the one whose counter-view you can state more fairly. If you find yourself drawn to a prompt only because you have a memorised essay on its theme, check the *subject* again: a ready-made essay is the surest route to drifting.

## Using the sheet later

The sheet is the input to later essay lessons: the stance becomes the thesis, the lenses become the outline's body, the counter-view becomes a concession paragraph. Here you only fill it.

### 7. Worked decodings 1 to 3

*Objective:* Follow three complete decoding sheets for a claim, a comparative quotation and a phrase topic.

These are decodings, not essays and not model answers. They show one reasonable reading; UPSC publishes no official expectation. Example seeds marked *verify* must be checked before you use them.

## Decoding 1

**Topic.** 'Forests precede civilizations and deserts follow them.' Paraphrase: societies rise where forests stand and fall after forests are destroyed.

**Key terms.** *Forests*: resource base (timber, water retention, soil, fuel, food). *Civilizations*: organised societies with agriculture, cities and records. *Precede*: come before and perhaps enable. *Deserts follow*: degraded land after the forest has gone. Bound: some civilisations grew in dry river valleys, and some declines had other causes.

**Demand.** Judge a historical and ecological claim: how true, how general, how relevant today. **Scope.** Place open (choose: world with Indian anchors); time: past to present.

**Stance.** Agree with conditions: forests are a precondition for many societies, but not the only cause of decline. **Dimensions.** Historical, environmental, economic, political (governance of shared resources), ethical (duty to later generations). **Counter-view.** Civilisations also flourished in arid river valleys (a factual seed: verify before use), and decline often had several causes. **Examples (verify).** The Chipko movement for forest protection in Uttarakhand; historians' debates on forest loss and early states.

## Decoding 2

**Topic.** 'A society that has more justice is a society that needs less charity.' Paraphrase: the fairer a society's institutions, the less it must rely on voluntary giving.

**Key terms.** *Justice*: fair treatment in law, in opportunity and in the sharing of resources. *Charity*: voluntary giving to relieve need. *More ... less*: an inverse relationship; the prompt claims that as one rises the other falls. Bound: charity is also an expression of solidarity, not only a response to need.

**Demand.** Evaluate an inverse relationship; ask whether it holds always. **Scope.** Society in general; time open.

**Stance.** Agree with conditions: justice addresses causes, charity treats symptoms, yet no society removes all need. **Dimensions.** Ethical, social, economic, political (a right is different from a favour), historical (the rise of welfare entitlements). **Counter-view.** Even a just society faces disasters and misfortune that charity meets. **Examples (verify).** Rights-based schemes such as food-security and employment-guarantee entitlements; disaster relief by volunteers.

## Decoding 3

**Topic.** 'Technology as the silent factor in international relations' The prompt has no verb, so supply one: technology shapes relations between states quietly.

**Key terms.** *Technology*: tools and systems (digital, military, space, energy). *Silent*: working without announcement, rather than as a stated policy. *Factor*: one cause among several. *International relations*: diplomacy, trade and security between states.

**Demand.** Explain how technology influences states without being named, and judge whether 'silent' is accurate. **Scope.** World; time past to present. **Stance.** Agree with conditions: technology often decides outcomes quietly, but is now loudly debated. **Dimensions.** Technological, political and strategic, economic, international law and ethics, historical. **Counter-view.** Politics, geography and ideology decide how technology is used; 'silent' may be outdated. **Examples (verify).** Control of advanced chip supply; satellite communication; nuclear technology and deterrence.

**Notice** that each sheet ends with a counter-view, and that no sheet drifts into a general essay on its theme.

### 8. Worked decodings 4 and 5, and the common mistakes

*Objective:* Follow a two-sided and a metaphor decoding, then recognise six common decoding mistakes.

## Decoding 4: a two-sided topic

**Topic.** 'Rise of Artificial Intelligence : the threat of jobless future or better job opportunities through reskilling and upskilling' Paraphrase: will AI leave people without work, or open better work for those who learn new skills?

**Key terms.** *Artificial Intelligence*: computer systems that do tasks which need judgement or learning. *Jobless future*: not job loss alone but a long-run shortage of work. *Reskilling*: learning skills for a new role. *Upskilling*: deepening skills for the present role. Bound: the prompt names two outcomes; it does not say they are exclusive.

**Demand.** Weigh two outcomes and the conditions on which each depends. Not demanded: a technical account of AI. **Scope.** Place open (choose India with global comparison and say so); time: future.

**Stance.** Conditional: the result depends on the speed of change and on the strength of training systems. **Dimensions.** Economic, social, educational, ethical, political (policy choices), gender (who gets retrained). **Counter-view.** Both can happen at once, to different groups. **Examples (verify).** Earlier waves of automation; retraining programmes for workers in service industries.

## Decoding 5: a metaphor

**Topic.** 'A ship in harbour is safe, but that is not what ship is for' Paraphrase: avoiding risk keeps you safe but defeats your purpose.

**Key terms.** *Ship*: something built for a purpose. *Harbour*: safety, comfort and the status quo. *Safe*: free of risk. *Is for*: purpose. Bound: ships also need harbours for repair; the prompt does not praise recklessness.

**Demand.** Explore the tension between safety and purpose and decide where risk is justified. **Scope.** Individual, institution and nation. **Stance.** Agree with conditions: calculated risk. **Dimensions.** Psychological (fear of failure), economic (enterprise and innovation), political (reform against inertia), scientific (exploration), ethical (risk to others). **Counter-view.** Some institutions exist to provide safety, such as public health or financial stability. **Examples (verify).** A space mission; a reform that risked public opinion.

## Six common decoding mistakes

1. **Topic hijack.** Writing about the theme (forests, technology) instead of the subject. *Guard*: point to the topic's words for each paragraph.
2. **One dimension.** Choosing a single lens, usually the one you know best. *Guard*: three lenses, one that complicates.
3. **Quote-dumping.** Opening with several quotations that never connect to the prompt's claim. *Guard*: use a quotation only to serve a lens, and only if you can explain how it bears on the exact words.
4. **Skipping the qualifier.** Ignoring 'least', 'real', 'inherently'. *Guard*: run the qualifier test of module 3.
5. **Literal reading of a metaphor.** Treating 'ship' as shipping policy. *Guard*: paraphrase in plain words first.
6. **Over-decoding.** Spending twenty-five minutes on the sheet. *Guard*: stop at ten.

A seventh to watch: **the memorised essay**, forcing a prepared essay onto a prompt it nearly fits.

### 9. Drills, the rubric and delayed retention

*Objective:* Run the ten-minute decoding drill on new prompts, score it with the four-criterion rubric, and plan delayed retention.

## The timed ten-minute decoding drill

Use the nine practice prompts in the practice section. For each, set a timer for **ten minutes** and fill the eight-field sheet on paper.

1. Minute 0 to 1: paraphrase.
2. Minute 1 to 3: key terms.
3. Minute 3 to 5: demand and scope.
4. Minute 5 to 6: stance options.
5. Minute 6 to 8: dimensions.
6. Minute 8 to 9: counter-view.
7. Minute 9 to 10: example seeds.

Stop at ten. Then, and only then, compare your sheet with the self-check points beside the prompt in the practice section. Those points are one reasonable reading, not an official expectation.

## The rubric

Score each criterion from 0 to 2. This is a self-assessment rubric made for this project; it is **not** a UPSC marking scheme.

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Drifts to the theme | Mostly on the subject | Every field returns to the prompt's exact words |
| Completeness of dimensions | One lens | Two lenses, or three that overlap | Three to five distinct lenses, one that complicates |
| Key-term precision | Terms listed without definition | Defined but no bounds | Defined and bounded; qualifiers noted |
| Stance clarity | No stance | Stance stated, conditions vague | Stance with conditions and a stated counter-view |

Total out of 8. Treat 6 or above on three consecutive new prompts as evidence that the method is settling, not as evidence of exam readiness; essay quality also depends on writing, which later lessons train.

## The decoding error log

When a criterion scores 0 or 1, write one line: the prompt, the criterion, the cause (for example, 'ignored the comparative'), the fix. After ten drills, count the causes; the most frequent becomes your next focus.

## Delayed retention

Memory of a method fades if it is not retrieved. Use these gaps.

- After **7 days**, redo one of the nine prompts on a blank sheet without notes, and compare with your first attempt.
- After **14 days**, write the eight fields and the five steps of key-term analysis from memory.
- After **30 days**, decode two unseen verified prompts under the ten-minute limit and score them.

## What this lesson does not claim

Completing the drills is not mastery. The lesson is a draft awaiting independent review. Writing full essays comes later; this lesson trains only the reading and planning step.

### Notes: Decoding sheet (eight fields)

- 1. Topic: prompt and a plain-words paraphrase.
- 2. Key terms: definition and bound for each.
- 3. Demand: 'This topic asks me to ...'.
- 4. Scope: place, time, actor, domain.
- 5. Stance options: agree, agree with conditions, disagree; lean.
- 6. Dimensions: three to five lenses.
- 7. Counter-view: the strongest opposing argument.
- 8. Examples: one or two seeds per dimension; mark those to verify.

### Notes: Self-check rubric (not a UPSC scheme)

- Score 0 to 2 on each criterion; total out of 8.

### Notes: Common decoding mistakes

- Topic hijack: writing about the theme rather than the subject.
- One dimension: a single lens.
- Quote-dumping: quotations that never connect to the prompt's claim.
- Skipping the qualifier.
- Literal reading of a metaphor.
- Over-decoding beyond ten minutes.
- The memorised essay forced onto a nearly matching prompt.
- Stance mixed up with dimension.

### Notes: One-page checklist for the ten-minute decode (ADHD-friendly)

- Set a ten-minute timer. Stop when it rings.
- Box 1: say the topic in plain words, one sentence.
- Box 2: underline key terms; define each in five words; note any comparative or qualifier.
- Box 3: write 'This topic asks me to ...'.
- Box 4: place, time, actor, domain; choose any that are open.
- Box 5: stance options; circle one; write the condition.
- Box 6: pick three to five lenses; one must complicate.
- Box 7: write the counter-view in one line.
- Box 8: two example seeds; mark any to verify.
- Final check: does each box return to the exact words of the prompt?
- If stuck for more than a minute on a box, leave it blank and move on.

### Core concepts

- Keep closely to the subject: the subject is the exact claim, narrower than the theme.
- Decode before you write: paraphrase, key terms, demand, scope, stance, dimensions, counter-view, examples.
- The command in an essay prompt is usually implicit; read it from qualifiers, comparatives and question forms.
- Three to five distinct dimensions, one of which complicates the stance, give a balanced essay.
- The ten-minute limit protects the writing time.

### Practice

**Q.** Write the syllabus's four demands for the Essay paper in your own words.

**A.** Keep closely to the subject (relevance), arrange ideas in orderly fashion (structure), write concisely (no padding) and use effective and exact expression (precise words).

**Q.** What is a dimension, and what is a stance?

**A.** A dimension is an angle of examination such as social or ethical; a stance is your position on the claim, such as agree with conditions.

**Q.** Why does the lesson say the command in an essay prompt is usually implicit?

**A.** None of the 80 verified prompts begins with an instruction word such as 'discuss'; the demand has to be read from qualifiers, comparatives, question forms and cause words.

**Q.** Explain with one example why theme and subject differ.

**A.** For 'Forests precede civilizations and deserts follow them.' the theme is the environment, but the subject is a claim about the order in which forests, civilisations and deserts appear.

**Q.** Why does a superlative such as 'least' require a comparison?

**A.** A superlative ranks one thing against others; to judge the claim you must name the alternatives and weigh them, otherwise you cannot say it is 'least'.

**Q.** Why keep at least one lens that complicates your stance?

**A.** A lens that tests the stance supplies the counter-view and prevents a one-sided essay.

**Q.** Decode the key terms of 'The process of self-discovery has now been technologically outsourced.' in five minutes: define each term and name what it asks and does not ask.

**A.** Indicative points: 'self-discovery' (learning who one is), 'technologically outsourced' (handed over to apps, algorithms and devices), 'now' fixes the present; asked: has technology taken over how people come to know themselves; not asked: a history of technology. One reasonable reading, not an official expectation.

**Q.** Choose between 'Forests are the best case studies for economic excellence' and 'History is a series of victories won by the scientific man over the romantic man' in a three-minute triage; explain your choice.

**A.** Paraphrase both, list lenses with an example for each, and choose the one whose paraphrase is clearest; either choice is acceptable if the reasoning follows the triage steps.
