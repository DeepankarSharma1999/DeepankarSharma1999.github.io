### Bridge: Civil and criminal cases, trial and appeal, bench

A **civil case** is a dispute between private parties (or between a person and the government acting like a private party) about rights, property, contracts, service conditions or family matters. The usual remedy is a declaration, an order to pay, or an order to do or not do something. A **criminal case** is one in which the State prosecutes a person for an **offence** defined in a penal law; the possible outcome is a fine, imprisonment or, in the gravest cases, death. Many Indian courts handle both kinds, which is why you will see 'civil side' and 'criminal side' in this lesson.

A **trial court** is the first court that hears the evidence and decides the facts. An **appeal** asks a higher court to re-examine what the lower court did. A **question of fact** is 'what actually happened?' (was the signature forged?). A **question of law** is 'what does the law say about those facts?' (is a forged signature enough to cancel the contract?). The Constitution and codes often allow a **first appeal** on both fact and law, but a **second appeal** only on a **substantial question of law** (CPC section 100). You will meet exactly this pattern for High Courts in module 5.

A **bench** is the group of judges who sit together to hear a case: a single judge, a **division bench** of two, or a larger bench. An **intra-court appeal** is an appeal from a single judge of a High Court to a division bench of the same High Court.

Finally, **original jurisdiction** means a court may hear a matter at the very first stage; **appellate jurisdiction** means it hears appeals; **supervisory** or **revisional** jurisdiction means it can correct lower bodies without waiting for a formal appeal.

### Bridge: Tribunal, ADR, compoundable offence and pecuniary jurisdiction

A **tribunal** is a specialised adjudicating body, usually created by a statute, to decide a particular category of disputes (for example, government service matters) outside the regular civil courts. It is judicial in function but usually less formal and not bound by every rule of the civil procedure code. **Tribunalisation** is the trend of moving whole families of disputes from the courts to such tribunals.

**ADR** stands for **Alternative Dispute Resolution**: ways of settling disputes without a full court trial, such as negotiation, **conciliation** (a neutral helps the parties reach their own settlement), **mediation** and **arbitration** (a neutral decides). A **Lok Adalat** ('people's court') is an Indian ADR forum you will study in module 7.

A **compoundable offence** is a criminal offence which the complainant is allowed to settle with the accused, so that the case can be closed by agreement; a **non-compoundable offence** cannot be settled that way and must be decided by the court. This matters because Lok Adalats cannot take non-compoundable offences.

**Pecuniary jurisdiction** is a court's money limit: it may hear civil suits only up to or above a certain value. You will meet this for the munsiff (limited), the subordinate judge (unlimited) and Permanent Lok Adalats (a stated limit). **Natural justice** is the basic fairness duty: hear both sides and decide without bias. Many tribunals and Lok Adalats follow natural justice rather than rigid procedure.

### 1. The state's judicial pyramid: High Courts, subordinate courts, tribunals

*Objective:* Place the High Court, the subordinate courts and the tribunals on one picture, and say who controls whom.

## Where this lesson sits

The Day 1 lesson **GS-186 (Supreme Court: jurisdiction and review)** covered the top of the pyramid. If you have not studied it, keep one idea from it: India has a **single integrated judicial system**, not separate federal and state court ladders. NCERT puts it as a pyramid with the Supreme Court at the top, the High Courts below and the district and subordinate courts at the lowest level, with lower courts working under the direct superintendence of higher courts. This lesson is about everything below the Supreme Court.

## Three layers

| Layer | What it is | Main constitutional or statutory base |
|---|---|---|
| High Court | The top court of the state's judicial administration | Part VI, Articles 214 to 231 |
| Subordinate (lower) courts | District court and the courts below it, civil and criminal | Articles 233 to 237; each State's own laws fix the detailed structure |
| Tribunals and alternative forums | Special bodies outside the ordinary court ladder: administrative tribunals, Lok Adalats, Family Courts, Gram Nyayalayas | Articles 323A and 323B (Part XIV-A); statutes such as the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 |

## The High Court in one paragraph

Laxmikanth records that high courts began in India in **1862** at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (under the Indian High Courts Act, 1861), and a fourth was set up at Allahabad in **1866**. After 1950 the high court of each province became the high court of the corresponding State. The Constitution provides for a High Court for each State (Article 214), but the **Seventh Amendment Act, 1956** let Parliament set up a **common** High Court for two or more States, or for States and a Union Territory (Article 231). A High Court's territorial jurisdiction is the same as that of the State, or the combined territory for a common High Court.

## The map, as printed in the source

At the time of the Laxmikanth edition (2019) there were **25** High Courts, and only three of them covered more than one State. **Delhi** alone among the Union Territories has a High Court of its own (since 1966; the count of Union Territories was nine in 2019 and has been eight since the January 2020 merger of Dadra and Nagar Haveli with Daman and Diu); **Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh** shared a common High Court; other Union Territories fell under State High Courts, and Parliament can extend or exclude a High Court's jurisdiction over any Union Territory (Article 230). Four High Courts (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi) have original civil jurisdiction in higher-value cases. *Dated note: the count of 25, the Delhi and J&K and Ladakh arrangements and the list of benches are as printed in 2019; verify the present position on the official site of the Supreme Court or Law Ministry before using a number in an answer.* The comparison table in the master notes lists the combinations given in the source.

## Who controls whom

- The **High Court** controls subordinate courts (Article 235) and superintends all courts and tribunals in its territory except military ones (Article 227).
- The **Supreme Court** hears appeals from High Courts and its law binds all courts (see GS-186).
- Tribunals sit outside the ladder, but since the 1997 *L. Chandra Kumar* decision their orders can be tested in the High Court (module 8).

## Why it matters

Prelims asks precise facts (age 62, 'same manner of removal as a Supreme Court judge', Article 226). Mains asks about judicial independence, women's representation in the higher judiciary, and vacancies and pendency in the lower courts. Eight image-verified PYQs map to this topic; module 10 shows how they are tested.

### 2. Composition and appointment: strength, qualifications, oath and pay

*Objective:* State who appoints High Court judges, who must be consulted, what qualifies a person, and how salary is protected.

## Strength: left to the President

A High Court has a **Chief Justice** and such other judges as the **President** thinks necessary. Unlike the Supreme Court, where Parliament can change the number by law (GS-186), the Constitution does **not** fix the strength of a High Court; the President decides it from time to time according to the workload.

## Who appoints and whom must he consult (Article 217)

Judges are appointed by the **President**, who in practice acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The sequence of consultation is:

- **Chief Justice of the High Court**: appointed after consultation with the **Chief Justice of India (CJI)** and the **Governor** of the State.
- **Other judges**: the same, and the **Chief Justice of that High Court** is also consulted.
- **Common High Court**: the Governors of all the States concerned are consulted.

The Second Judges case (1993) held that no High Court judge can be appointed unless it conforms to the opinion of the CJI. The Third Judges case (1998) held that the CJI's sole opinion is not 'consultation'; he must consult a collegium. *Two rules, both correct and distinct (Third Judges case, 1998):* for **High Court appointments** the CJI acts with a collegium of the **two** senior-most Supreme Court judges; for **transfers of High Court judges** (and for Supreme Court appointments, see GS-186) the CJI acts with the **four** senior-most Supreme Court judges. There is no discrepancy: the number depends on the decision being taken. Verify present practice.

The **99th Amendment and NJAC Act (2014)** tried to replace the collegium for both the Supreme Court and High Courts; the Supreme Court struck both down in **2015** (Fourth Judges case) as affecting judicial independence, and the collegium system revived. The full story is in GS-186 module 2; do not repeat it in an answer unless the question is about appointments.

## Qualifications

A person must be (1) a **citizen of India**, and (2) either have held a **judicial office** in India for **ten years**, or have been an **advocate** of a High Court (or of High Courts in succession) for **ten years**. Two points the examiner likes: the Constitution prescribes **no minimum age**, and, unlike for the Supreme Court, there is **no 'distinguished jurist' category** for High Courts.

## Oath

Before entering office a High Court judge makes an oath or affirmation **before the Governor** of the State (or a person the Governor appoints). It includes faith and allegiance to the Constitution, upholding the sovereignty and integrity of India, performing duties without fear or favour, affection or ill-will, and upholding the Constitution and laws.

## Salary and pension

Salaries, allowances, privileges, leave and pension are fixed by **Parliament** and **cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment, except during a financial emergency**. Laxmikanth notes that in 2018 the salary of the Chief Justice was raised to ₹2.50 lakh and of a judge to ₹2.25 lakh per month (verify the present figures, which Parliament revises). Retired Chief Justices and judges receive 50 per cent of last-drawn salary as monthly pension, per the source. Judges' salaries and allowances, and the staff and administrative expenses of the Court, are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State**; a judge's **pension** is charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**.

### 3. Tenure, removal, transfer and temporary judges, and why they protect independence

*Objective:* Apply age 62, the removal procedure, transfer rules and the four kinds of temporary or retired judge to a short factual scenario.

## Tenure

The Constitution does not fix a term, but four provisions apply. A judge **holds office until 62** (raised from 60 by the **15th Amendment, 1963**); any dispute about age is decided by the President after consulting the CJI, and his decision is final. A judge may **resign** by writing to the President. A judge can be **removed** by the President on Parliament's recommendation. A judge **vacates office** on being appointed to the Supreme Court or transferred to another High Court. (The Supreme Court retirement age is 65.)

## Removal: the same as for a Supreme Court judge

A High Court judge can be removed only by a **Presidential order**, issued after an **address by Parliament has been presented in the same session**, supported by a **special majority** in each House (a majority of the total membership of that House **and** at least two-thirds of the members present and voting). The only grounds are **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**. The procedure is regulated by the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**:

1. A motion signed by **100 members of the Lok Sabha or 50 of the Rajya Sabha** goes to the Speaker or Chairman.
2. The Speaker or Chairman may admit or refuse it.
3. If admitted, a **three-member committee** investigates: a Chief Justice or judge of the Supreme Court, a Chief Justice of a High Court, and a distinguished jurist.
4. If the committee finds misbehaviour or incapacity, the House may take up the motion.
5. After a special-majority vote in each House, an address goes to the President.
6. The President passes the removal order.

Laxmikanth (2019) notes that no High Court judge had been impeached so far. *Verify later developments:* a removal motion against a sitting High Court judge was reported in Parliament in 2025; check the outcome before writing 'none has ever been removed'; an inquiry committee was reportedly formed (verify the outcome). NCERT's Ramaswami example (1991-93) shows why the double-majority rule matters: that motion got two-thirds of those voting but not a majority of the total strength of the House.

## Transfer (Article 222)

The President may transfer a judge from one High Court to another **after consulting the CJI**, with a compensatory allowance. In **1977** the Supreme Court said transfer must be exceptional, in public interest, and **not a punishment**; in **1994** it held that judicial review is needed against arbitrary transfer, but **only the transferred judge** can challenge it. The Third Judges case (1998) says the CJI must consult the collegium **and** the Chief Justices of both High Courts (the one the judge leaves and the one he joins).

## Temporary and retired judges

| Who | Article | When | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acting Chief Justice | 223 | Post vacant, or Chief Justice absent or unable to work | Until the position is filled or he returns |
| Additional judges | 224 | Temporary increase in business or arrears of work | Not more than **two years** |
| Acting judge | 224 | A judge (other than the Chief Justice) is absent or acting as Chief Justice | Until the permanent judge resumes |
| Retired judge | 224A | Chief Justice requests, with the **previous consent of the President** and of the retired judge | Temporary; has all the powers of a judge but is not otherwise deemed one |

An additional or acting judge cannot continue after age **62**.

## Link to independence

Fixed age, a hard removal procedure, protected salary and consultation with the judiciary itself are four of the ten safeguards listed in the master notes. NCERT's argument is that a difficult removal procedure gives security of office, and that judges must work 'without fear or favour'.

### 4. Original and writ jurisdiction: Article 226 compared with Article 32

*Objective:* List the High Court's original jurisdiction heads and compare the writ powers of the High Court (Article 226) and the Supreme Court (Article 32) in a table.

## Where the powers come from

The Constitution says only that a High Court's jurisdiction and powers stay as they were before 1950, with one **addition: revenue matters**, which it did not enjoy earlier. Other provisions add writs, superintendence and the consultative role. Parliament and the State legislature can change the jurisdiction. Laxmikanth lists seven heads: original, writ, appellate, supervisory, control over subordinate courts, court of record, and judicial review. This module takes the first two; module 5 takes the rest.

## Original jurisdiction

**Original jurisdiction** means the power to hear a dispute **in the first instance**, not by way of appeal. A High Court has it in: (a) admiralty and contempt of court; (b) **disputes about the election of members of Parliament and State legislatures**; (c) revenue matters or acts done in revenue collection; (d) **enforcement of fundamental rights**; (e) cases **transferred from a subordinate court** because they involve the interpretation of the Constitution; and (f) the four High Courts of **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi**, which have **original civil jurisdiction** in cases of higher value. Before 1973 the first three also had original criminal jurisdiction; the Criminal Procedure Code, 1973 abolished it fully. *Verify:* the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita has replaced the 1973 Code from July 2024; the abolition of original criminal jurisdiction is the historical point to remember. How election petitions are filed and what grounds void an election are governed by the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (standard knowledge, not in the extracts; see module 10 on the 2022 Mains PYQ).

## Writ jurisdiction: Article 226

Article 226 empowers a High Court to issue writs **including habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto** for the enforcement of **fundamental rights and for any other purpose**; 'any other purpose' means enforcing an **ordinary legal right**. It can issue writs to any person, authority or government within its territory and, since a 1963 amendment, **outside** its territory if the **cause of action arises within** it. In plain words (standard knowledge, flagged in the completeness table): habeas corpus orders a detained person to be produced; mandamus commands a public authority to do its duty; certiorari quashes an order of a lower body; prohibition stops a lower body from exceeding its jurisdiction; quo warranto asks by what authority someone holds a public office.

In **L. Chandra Kumar (1997)** the Supreme Court ruled that the writ jurisdiction of both the High Court and the Supreme Court is part of the **basic structure**; it cannot be removed even by a constitutional amendment.

## Article 226 versus Article 32

| Feature | High Court, Article 226 | Supreme Court, Article 32 |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Fundamental rights **and** any other purpose (ordinary legal rights) | Fundamental rights only |
| Width | Wider | Narrower |
| Relationship | **Concurrent**: the aggrieved person may go to either | Concurrent: may go directly to the Supreme Court |
| Territory | Own territory, plus outside it if the cause of action arises within | Whole of India |
| Basic structure | Part of basic structure (Chandra Kumar) | Part of basic structure (Chandra Kumar) |
| Nature of the remedy (standard knowledge; verify) | A constitutional power of the Court | Itself a Fundamental Right, so the Court is a guarantor of rights |
| Discretion (standard knowledge; verify) | May refuse relief, for example where another remedy exists | Court is generally slower to refuse where a Fundamental Right is shown |

NCERT adds that the High Court, like the Supreme Court, can declare a law unconstitutional, and that people whose rights are violated have the choice between the two.

### 5. Appellate, supervisory and other powers; court of record and judicial review

*Objective:* Explain each remaining head of High Court jurisdiction with its limits, including superintendence under Article 227 and control under Article 235.

## Appellate jurisdiction

A High Court is **primarily a court of appeal**, so its appellate jurisdiction is wider than its original jurisdiction.

- **First appeals** from district courts, additional district courts and other subordinate courts come directly to the High Court on **fact and law**, if the value exceeds the stipulated limit.
- **Second appeals** lie only on **questions of law**.
- Calcutta, Bombay and Madras have **intra-court appeals** from a single judge to a division bench.
- **Appeals from administrative and other tribunals** lie to the **division bench of the High Court**; since 1997 tribunals are subject to the High Court's writ jurisdiction, so a person cannot go straight to the Supreme Court without first going to the High Court.
- **Criminal:** anyone convicted at a **Sessions trial** may appeal to the High Court whatever the sentence (CrPC s.374(2), now BNSS s.415(2)); the seven-year limit applies to trials in other courts (Laxmikanth's wording is narrower: it says appeals from sessions courts lie where the sentence exceeds seven years; verify); a **death sentence** passed by a sessions court must be **confirmed** by the High Court whether or not the convicted person appeals. Other appeals from magistrates lie in specified cases.

## Supervisory jurisdiction (Article 227)

The High Court has the power of **superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory except military courts or tribunals**. It may call for returns, make general rules and prescribe forms, and settle fees. The power is broad: it covers courts **whether or not they are subject to its appellate jurisdiction**, covers **judicial as well as administrative** superintendence, is **revisional**, and can be exercised **suo motu**. It is also **extraordinary** and to be used sparingly, normally for six reasons: excess of jurisdiction, gross violation of natural justice, error of law, disregard of the law of superior courts, perverse findings and manifest injustice.

## Control over subordinate courts (Articles 228 and 235)

The Governor consults the High Court on appointment, posting and promotion of district judges and on appointments to the judicial service below them. The High Court itself handles posting, promotion, leave, transfer and discipline of judicial officers below the district judge. It can **withdraw a case** from a subordinate court if it involves a substantial question of law needing interpretation of the Constitution, and then decide it or answer the question and send the case back. Its law **binds all subordinate courts** in its territory just as the Supreme Court's law binds all courts in India.

## Court of record (Article 215)

As a court of record the High Court (a) keeps its judgments and proceedings for perpetual memory and testimony; they have evidentiary value and are precedents; and (b) can **punish for contempt** with simple imprisonment, fine or both. The Constitution does not define contempt; the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** defines **civil contempt** (wilful disobedience of a court order or breach of an undertaking) and **criminal contempt** (scandalising a court, prejudicing a proceeding or obstructing justice). Innocent publication, fair reports of proceedings, fair criticism of judicial acts and comment on the administrative side of the judiciary are **not** contempt. A High Court also has the power to **review and correct its own judgment**, even though the Constitution gives no specific power of review to it (the Supreme Court has one, in Article 137).

## Judicial review

Judicial review is the power to examine whether central and State laws and executive orders conform to the Constitution and to declare them void if not. The phrase does not appear in the Constitution, but **Articles 13 and 226** give the power to the High Court. Grounds: violation of Part III, absence of competence in the authority that made it, or repugnance to constitutional provisions. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** debarred High Courts from considering the validity of **central** laws (Article 226A); the **43rd Amendment (1977)** restored the original position and Article 226A was repealed.

### 6. Subordinate courts: Articles 233-237 and the civil and criminal hierarchy

*Objective:* Name the civil and criminal courts below the High Court, say who appoints district judges and who controls the lower judiciary.

## The constitutional frame (Articles 233 to 237)

The State judiciary consists of the High Court and a hierarchy of **subordinate courts**, so called because they are subordinate to the High Court. Articles 233-237 regulate their organisation and protect them from the executive.

- **District judges (Article 233):** appointed, posted and promoted by the **Governor in consultation with the High Court**. Laxmikanth (2019) says the person must not already be in the service of the Centre or State, must have been an **advocate or pleader for seven years**, and must be **recommended by the High Court**. *Dated note (verify):* an October 2025 Constitution Bench ruling (Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa) is reported to allow judicial officers with seven years' combined Bar and judicial experience to compete for direct recruitment as district judge, so the 'not already in service' limb may no longer hold; check the judgment before using it.
- **Other judicial service (Article 234):** appointed by the **Governor after consulting the State Public Service Commission and the High Court**; in practice the State PSC holds a competitive examination.
- **Control (Article 235):** control over district courts and other subordinate courts, including posting, promotion and leave of persons below the district judge, is vested in the **High Court**.
- **Interpretation (Article 236):** 'district judge' includes the city civil court judge, additional and assistant district judges, sessions judges and others; 'judicial service' means a service made up of persons meant to fill the post of district judge and posts below it.
- **Magistrates (Article 237):** the Governor may extend these provisions to classes of magistrates. (Article 233A, added in 1966 by the 20th Amendment, validated certain earlier appointments.)

## The hierarchy

The structure, jurisdiction and names are laid down by the States, so they differ slightly; broadly there are **three tiers** of civil and criminal courts below the High Court.

| Tier | Civil side | Criminal side | Key limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | District Judge | Sessions Judge (same person) | District judge is the highest judicial authority in the district; supervises all subordinate courts in the district; the sessions judge may impose **any sentence including death**, but a death sentence needs High Court confirmation |
| Middle | Court of Subordinate Judge | Court of Chief Judicial Magistrate | Subordinate judge: **unlimited pecuniary** jurisdiction; Chief Judicial Magistrate: offences punishable up to **seven years** |
| Lowest | Court of Munsiff | Court of Judicial Magistrate | Munsiff: **limited** (small) value suits; Judicial Magistrate: offences punishable up to **three years** |

In some metropolitan cities there are **city civil courts** and **metropolitan magistrates**. Some States and presidency towns (Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta, Madras) have **small causes courts** that decide civil cases of small value summarily; decisions are final but the High Court has a power of revision. In some States **panchayat courts** (Nyaya Panchayat, Gram Kutchery, Adalati Panchayat and so on) try petty civil and criminal cases. A subordinate judge is also known as civil judge (senior division) and a munsiff as civil judge (junior division); a subordinate judge may combine civil and criminal powers as an assistant sessions judge.

## Separation of judiciary from the executive

Article 50 (a Directive Principle) tells the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services. Laxmikanth notes that the Criminal Procedure Code, 1973 effected this separation. *Verify:* the BNSS (2023) now replaces the 1973 Code; check magistrate sentencing limits before using the seven-year and three-year figures, which are as printed in the 2019 edition.

## Why this layer matters

The subordinate judiciary is where most citizens meet the law. Its quality depends on **recruitment** (State PSC and High Court), **independence** (High Court control rather than the Governor or State government) and **vacancies**; the Mains issues are in module 9.

### 7. Access to justice: legal aid, Lok Adalats, Family Courts and Gram Nyayalayas

*Objective:* Explain why these institutions exist, who sits in them, what they can and cannot decide, and which Act created each.

## Legal aid: Article 39A and NALSA

**Article 39A** (a Directive Principle added by the 42nd Amendment; see GS-165 for the Directive Principles) directs the State to ensure that the legal system promotes justice on the basis of equal opportunity and to provide **free legal aid** so that no citizen is denied justice for economic or other disabilities. Articles 14 and 22(1) support it. The **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (in force from **9 November 1995**) set up a nationwide network: the **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)** lays down policy; each State has a **State Legal Services Authority**; High Courts and the Supreme Court have legal services committees; districts have **District Legal Services Authorities** and taluks have **Taluk Legal Services Committees**. Their duties are free and competent legal services, organising **Lok Adalats**, and legal awareness camps. Eligible persons include women and children, SC/ST members, workmen, disaster victims, disabled persons, persons in custody and trafficking victims, and those under an income limit (the ₹1 lakh printed in the source is dated; verify).

## Lok Adalats (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987)

A **Lok Adalat** ('people's court') is a forum where disputes pending in a court, or at the **pre-litigation** stage, are settled amicably. The first post-Independence camp was held in **Gujarat in 1982**; the 1987 Act gave it **statutory status**. It is organised by the legal services authorities and usually has a judicial officer as chairman with a lawyer and a social worker as members. It can take civil matters and **compoundable** criminal matters (family, bank recovery, labour, pension, consumer and municipal disputes), but has **no jurisdiction over non-compoundable offences**. A case goes to it if the parties agree, one party applies, or the court considers it appropriate. Its award is **deemed a decree of a civil court**, **final and binding**, with **no appeal**. There is no court fee (a fee already paid is refunded on settlement) and procedure is flexible.

## Permanent Lok Adalats (amendment of 2002)

The ordinary Lok Adalat depends on a **compromise**; if none is reached the case goes back to court. The 2002 amendment created **Permanent Lok Adalats** for **public utility services** (transport, postal and telephone, power and water supply, sanitation, hospitals, insurance). It has a chairman who is or has been a district judge or higher and two members with experience in utility services. A party may apply **before going to court**; once the application is made, no party may go to court on the same dispute. If settlement is not reached, it **decides the dispute on merits**; its award is final and binding. The pecuniary limit printed in the source is ₹10 lakh (the Centre may raise it; reported raised to Rs 1 crore in 2015; verify the present limit). 

## Family Courts (Family Courts Act, 1984)

Created to promote conciliation and speedy settlement of disputes on marriage and family affairs: matrimonial relief, spouses' property, legitimacy, guardianship and custody, and maintenance. States set them up in consultation with the High Court, and **must** do so in every city or town with population above **one million**. The court must first try **reconciliation**; parties have no right to a lawyer, though the court may appoint an amicus curiae; there is **one appeal, to the High Court**.

## Gram Nyayalayas (Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008)

Meant to bring justice to the rural poor 'at their doorsteps', following the Law Commission's 114th Report. A Gram Nyayalaya is the court of a **Judicial Magistrate of the first class**, presided by a **Nyayadhikari** appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court; it is a **mobile court** exercising civil and criminal powers, follows **summary procedure** in criminal trials, is not bound by the Indian Evidence Act (replaced by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 from July 2024; verify the Gram Nyayalayas Act's current wording) but by **natural justice**, tries to **conciliate** first, and its judgment is deemed a decree. **Appeals** go to the **Sessions Court (criminal)** or the **District Court (civil)**, each to be decided within six months. Laxmikanth records implementation problems: States already had regular taluka courts, and police, State functionaries and the Bar were reluctant. Do not quote a number of functioning Gram Nyayalayas without a current source.

### 8. Tribunals: Articles 323A and 323B, administrative tribunals and Chandra Kumar

*Objective:* State what Articles 323A and 323B authorise, how they differ, how the Central Administrative Tribunal works, and what L. Chandra Kumar changed.

## Why tribunals

The original Constitution had no provision for tribunals. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** added **Part XIV-A (Tribunals)** with two Articles. **Tribunalisation** is the trend of moving classes of disputes from the ordinary courts to specialised bodies in the hope of faster, cheaper and more expert decisions. GS-188 (Day 3, Tribunals and justice reforms) goes deeper; here the outline is enough.

## Article 323A: administrative tribunals

It empowers **Parliament** to provide for tribunals for disputes about **recruitment and conditions of service** of persons in public services of the Centre, States, local bodies, public corporations and other authorities, taking these matters out of the civil courts and High Courts. Under it Parliament passed the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**.

- **Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)**, set up in 1985 with the principal bench at Delhi and additional benches; Laxmikanth (2019) says 17 regular benches. It has **original jurisdiction** over recruitment and service matters of the all-India services, the Central civil services, civil posts under the Centre and civilian defence employees; **defence forces, Supreme Court staff and Parliament secretariat staff are not covered**.
- Members come from the **judicial and administrative streams**, are appointed by the President, and hold office for **five years** or until age **65 (chairman) or 62 (members)**, whichever is earlier (as printed in 2019; superseded, verify current law: the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 reportedly changed this to four years with ages 70 and 67, and a November 2025 Supreme Court ruling (Madras Bar Association) is reported to have struck down parts of that Act; verify). The Vice-Chairman post was abolished in 2006.
- It is **not bound by the Civil Procedure Code, 1908**, and is guided by **natural justice**; the fee is nominal (₹50 in the source) and the applicant may appear in person.
- **State Administrative Tribunals** can be set up by the Centre at the request of States; **joint administrative tribunals** can serve two or more States.

## Article 323B: tribunals for other matters

Parliament **and State legislatures** may set up tribunals for taxation, foreign exchange, import and export, industrial and labour matters, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to Parliament and State legislatures, foodstuffs, and rent and tenancy rights (the last added in 1993 by the 75th Amendment).

| Point | Article 323A | Article 323B |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Public service matters only | Taxation, labour, land reform, elections, rent and others |
| Who may create | Parliament only | Parliament **and** State legislatures (within their legislative competence) |
| Hierarchy | One for the Centre and one for each State or group; **no hierarchy** | A **hierarchy** of tribunals may be created |

## L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India (1997)

The original text allowed the Acts to exclude the jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court declared those exclusion clauses (Article 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d)) **unconstitutional**, holding that **judicial review is part of the basic structure**. Consequences stated in the source: appeals from the CAT lie to the **division bench of the concerned High Court**, and a public servant cannot go directly to the Supreme Court. Tribunals are therefore subject to the High Court's writ jurisdiction. The 1997 case is the main case to cite in Prelims and Mains (GS-173 has the other landmark judgments).

## Later developments (not in the sources read; verify each)

*Tribunals Reforms legislation (2021) and subsequent Supreme Court challenges* dealing with the qualifications, tenure and service conditions of tribunal members, and the merger or abolition of some tribunals, have followed since Laxmikanth's 2019 text. Treat any statement about the present number or powers of tribunals as 'verify' until you have read the Act and the judgments; do not rely on the 2019 CAT bench count for current answers.

### 9. Mains lens: vacancies, pendency, representation and reform, without borrowed numbers

*Objective:* Build a balanced Mains framework on delays and diversity in the lower and higher judiciary using only claims you can attribute.

## Rule for this module: no statistics without a source

The extracts you have read contain **no** figures for case pendency or judge vacancies, and current figures change every month. In an exam answer, quote numbers only if you have a dated, named source (for example, a Law Ministry or Supreme Court report); otherwise write 'millions of cases are pending' only if you can defend it, and prefer the qualitative argument below. Treat the framework as a skeleton to hang verified data on.

## Why delays arise (structure the answer)

- **Judge strength and vacancies.** The President fixes High Court strength; appointments depend on consultation (module 2) and on the collegium and the Executive moving together. Delay in filling posts leaves workload on fewer judges. The Constitution already provides stop-gaps: **additional judges** for arrears (Article 224) and **retired judges** (Article 224A), both in module 3.
- **Procedure and structure.** Multiple tiers of appeal (first appeal, second appeal, intra-court appeal, revision, then the Supreme Court) lengthen cases. The High Court's supervisory power is itself meant to be used sparingly (module 5).
- **Subordinate court capacity.** Recruitment is by the State PSC and the High Court (module 6); shortages of judicial officers, courtrooms and staff slow trial courts.
- **Specialised forums as a response.** Family Courts, tribunals, Lok Adalats and Gram Nyayalayas were created to divert disputes (modules 7-8). Laxmikanth's text on Gram Nyayalayas records implementation problems; on Lok Adalats it notes benefits of less cost, less time and no technicalities.

## Representation and diversity

The 2021 Mains question asks about **women in the higher judiciary**. The pipeline is: law graduates, bar membership of ten years or judicial service of ten years (Article 217), recommendation by the collegium, appointment by the President. Arguments for greater representation: legitimacy and **diversity, equity and inclusiveness**; experience and perspective in gender-related cases; role-model effect. Barriers (reason them, do not state data): small number of women entering and staying at the Bar, long selection timelines, seniority-based criteria, lack of transparency. Measures: conscious widening of the zone of consideration, transparent criteria, support for women in the lower judiciary, but **without diluting merit**. Do not quote any proportion unless you have a source.

## Reform ideas (flag as standard knowledge, verify)

An **All-India Judicial Service** (Article 312 authorises Parliament to create All-India Services on a Rajya Sabha resolution; the idea has been debated for decades); better **court infrastructure** and technology; **ADR** and **mediation**; and clear timelines for appointment. Any recent scheme or amendment must be dated and verified.

## A skeleton for a 15-mark answer

1. Introduction: the integrated judiciary is only as effective as its lowest courts.
2. Constitutional safeguards that already help (Articles 224, 224A, 235, 39A).
3. Causes of delay (four groups above).
4. Institutions created to relieve pressure: Lok Adalats, Permanent Lok Adalats, Family Courts, Gram Nyayalayas, tribunals; their limits.
5. Way forward.
6. Conclusion tying delay to **access to justice** (Article 39A).

## What not to write

Do not blame a single organ; the Executive, legislature and judiciary all contribute. Do not write 'judges are corrupt' without a documented case. Do not present the collegium-versus-NJAC debate as the only cause of vacancies.

### Notes: Core definitions

- High Court: the top court of a State's judicial administration, below the Supreme Court and above subordinate courts; Part VI, Articles 214-231.
- Common High Court: one High Court for two or more States, or States and a Union Territory, allowed by the Seventh Amendment Act, 1956 (Article 231).
- Original jurisdiction: power to hear a dispute in the first instance, not on appeal.
- Court of record (Article 215): a court whose records are kept as evidence and precedent and which can punish for its own contempt.
- Superintendence (Article 227): the High Court's power over all courts and tribunals in its territory except military ones; administrative and judicial; can be used suo motu; to be used sparingly.
- Subordinate court: a court below the High Court at district or lower levels, under the High Court's control (Article 235).
- Lok Adalat: a statutory ADR forum for amicable settlement of pending or pre-litigation disputes; award has civil-decree status.
- Tribunal: a specialised adjudicating body outside the ordinary courts, such as the Central Administrative Tribunal.
- Tribunalisation: the shifting of categories of disputes from courts to tribunals.

### Notes: Removal of a High Court judge (process)

- Motion signed by 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members.
- Speaker or Chairman admits or refuses it.
- If admitted: three-member committee (SC Chief Justice or judge, HC Chief Justice, distinguished jurist) investigates.
- If the committee finds misbehaviour or incapacity, the House takes up the motion.
- Special majority in each House (majority of total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting).
- Address presented to the President in the same session.
- President passes the removal order.

### Notes: Ten safeguards of High Court independence (sourced list)

- Mode of appointment: President in consultation with the judiciary itself (CJI and the Chief Justice of the High Court).
- Security of tenure: removal only by the constitutional procedure; judges do not hold office at the pleasure of the President.
- Fixed service conditions: Parliament fixes them; no variation to disadvantage except in financial emergency.
- Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State (salaries, allowances, staff, administrative expenses): non-votable though discussable; the judge's pension is charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
- Conduct of judges cannot be discussed in Parliament or a State legislature except during an impeachment motion.
- Ban on practice after retirement: retired permanent judges cannot plead or act in any court or authority in India except the Supreme Court and other High Courts.
- Power to punish for contempt.
- Freedom to appoint staff and fix their service conditions (Chief Justice).
- Jurisdiction specified in the Constitution cannot be curtailed by Parliament or State legislatures.
- Separation of the judiciary from the executive in public services (Article 50).

### Notes: Access-to-justice forums compared

- Lok Adalat and Permanent Lok Adalat come from the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Permanent Lok Adalat by the 2002 amendment).
- Only the Family Court and Gram Nyayalaya allow an appeal; Lok Adalat awards cannot be appealed.

### Notes: Articles 323A versus 323B

- Both were inserted by the 42nd Amendment (1976) in Part XIV-A; clause 323B(2)(h) rent and tenancy was added in 1993.
- L. Chandra Kumar (1997) struck the clauses excluding High Court and Supreme Court jurisdiction.

- 1861 Indian High Courts Act; 1862 Calcutta, Bombay, Madras High Courts; 1866 Allahabad.

- Second Judges case (1993): no High Court appointment contrary to the CJI's opinion.
- Third Judges case (1998): CJI's sole opinion is not consultation; collegium and, for transfers, the two Chief Justices.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Article 226 versus Article 32: 226 is wider; both concurrent.
- Article 227 (superintendence) versus Article 235 (control over subordinate courts): 227 covers courts and tribunals broadly; 235 is about appointments, posting and discipline of the lower judiciary.
- Additional judge (temporary workload, up to two years) versus acting judge (substitute for an absent judge) versus retired judge (Article 224A).
- District judge (civil) versus sessions judge (criminal): the same person.
- Lok Adalat versus Permanent Lok Adalat: only the latter decides on merits when settlement fails.
- Article 323A versus 323B: who may create the tribunal and whether a hierarchy is allowed.

  - Option 'High Court has no power to declare central law invalid' relies on the superseded 42nd Amendment position.
  - 'Governor appoints the High Court judges' mistakes the formal appointing authority (the President).
  - 'Any retired judge' type distractors drop a required condition (consent).
- **prelimsElimination:**
  - Eliminate absolutes ('no', 'only', 'never') unless a sourced rule makes them true.
  - Statements that sound plausible but reverse a charge of expenses (State versus India) can be eliminated if you recall pension is charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
  - Check the Article number: 226 for High Court writs, 32 for Supreme Court writs.
- **mainsDirectives:**
  - Discuss
  - Comment
  - Examine
  - Critically examine
  - Elucidate
- **mainsDimensions:**
  - Constitutional design: Articles 214-237 and 323A-323B.
  - Independence and accountability.
  - Access to justice: legal aid, Lok Adalats, Gram Nyayalayas.
  - Efficiency: vacancies, pendency, procedure.
  - Diversity and representation.
  - Federal dimension: Governor's role in appointment of subordinate judges; High Court as State's apex court.
- **commonMistakes:**
  - Using the Supreme Court's numbers (65 years, 34 judges) for High Courts.
  - Writing pendency or vacancy figures from memory.
  - Stating 'no High Court judge has been removed' as a timeless fact.
  - Confusing Article 235 with Article 227.
  - Treating Lok Adalats as courts of appeal.
