## GS-034: Social and religious reform movements

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Reading dates: centuries, CE and the 1800s

Every date in this lesson is in the **Common Era (CE)**, the ordinary calendar in which 1857 means 1,857 years after year 1. BCE (before the Common Era) is not needed here, because modern Indian history begins about 1757 CE.

**Centuries are counted one ahead of the year digits.** The 19th century is the years 1801 to 1900; the 20th century is 1901 to 2000. So Rammohan Roy's Brahmo Sabha (1828) is early 19th century, the Arya Samaj (1875) is late 19th century, and the Sarda Act (1929) is early 20th century.

**How to remember dates.** Do not memorise every year. Hold the anchors (1828, 1829, 1856, 1875, 1897, 1929) and place everything else before or after them. A UPSC chronology question usually separates events by decades, so knowing the decade is often enough.

**Reading the sources.** Spectrum prints book page numbers, and a few of its dates differ from other books; this lesson teaches the better-supported date and flags the difference (see the master notes).

### Bridge: Vocabulary: reform, revival, rationalism, universalism, obscurantism

- **Reform**: changing a belief or custom by argument and law while keeping the community's core identity.
- **Revival (revivalism)**: restoring what is believed to be an original, purer form of a religion.
- **Renaissance**: literally rebirth, the European awakening of learning. Spectrum says its use for India's 19th-century awakening is not fully justified; treat it as a label used in exam questions.
- **Rationalism**: judging beliefs by reason and evidence, not by authority.
- **Humanism**: giving first importance to human dignity and welfare.
- **Universalism (universal theism)**: the view that all religions share a common core.
- **Monotheism / polytheism**: belief in one God / in many gods. **Idolatry**: worship through images.
- **Obscurantism**: deliberate resistance to enlightenment and new ideas; **orthodoxy**: strict adherence to established belief.
- **Secular**: concerned with worldly life, not with a particular religion.
- **Purdah**: seclusion of women. **Kulinism**: the Bengal practice in which high-ranking Brahmin men took many, often very young, wives.
- **Proselytising**: trying to convert others. **Shuddhi**: the Arya Samaj's rite of purification and reconversion.
- **Fatwa**: a religious ruling issued by an Islamic scholar.

### 1. Orientation: the reform movements on one page

*Objective:* State what the reform movements were, separate reformist from revivalist, and hold six dated anchors.

## What the topic is

In the 19th century, small educated groups in India began to question their own religion and society. Spectrum notes a widely shared awareness that a vast country had been colonised by a handful of foreigners partly because of weaknesses in Indian social structure and culture. The response was a set of **socio-religious reform movements**: bodies and individuals that tried to change belief and custom, from sati and caste to women's education and idol worship.

## Two words to separate from day one

- **Reformist** movements lean mainly on reason and conscience (Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Aligarh).
- **Revivalist** movements lean mainly on the lost purity of an original faith (Arya Samaj, Deoband).

Spectrum adds a caution: both streams relied, to different degrees, on an appeal to lost purity; the difference is the degree of reliance on tradition versus reason and conscience. Treat the labels as ends of a scale, not boxes.

## Six anchors

- 1828: Rammohan Roy founds the Brahmo Sabha (later Brahmo Samaj).
- 1829: Regulation XVII makes sati illegal.
- 1856: Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act.
- 1875: Arya Samaj (Bombay); also the Aligarh school (Spectrum: college; classes 1877, verify).
- 1897: Ramakrishna Mission.
- 1929 (in force 1930): Sarda Act.

## Where it sits in the course

The Revolt of 1857 is taught in GS-032. Its socio-religious causes (GS-032 module 4) are the fear of cultural interference that reformers had to answer, and the dates above fall on both sides of 1857: the Widows' Remarriage Act a year before, the Arya Samaj and the Aligarh institution after. Peasant and tribal resistance (GS-031) had a different social base from these middle-class movements. Women's laws, the Rukhmabai case and Dalit and non-Brahmin movements get fuller treatment in GS-052 (modules 2 and 5 to 7); here they are summarised so that the whole picture sits in one place.

### 2. Foundation: why reform arose in the 19th century

*Objective:* Explain the colonial, social and intellectual reasons for reform and name the social and ideological base of the movements.

## A colonial setting, but not a colonial creation

Spectrum argues that the British presence played a complex but decisive role. Earlier invaders had settled and been absorbed; the British arrived when Europe had been changed by science, and they met an Indian society that, in the book's words, looked stagnant. Yet the book is careful: the regeneration was *occasioned* by the colonial presence but *not created* by it.

## The social ills in view

- **Religion**: priests held a monopoly of scriptural knowledge; idolatry, polytheism, magic and superstition were widespread.
- **Women**: female infanticide, child marriage, polygamy (Kulinism in Bengal), sati for widows (at least among higher castes), purdah, little education, no right for Hindu women to inherit or end a marriage.
- **Caste**: hierarchy by ritual status, with untouchables at the bottom; caste also shaped marriage among Muslims, Christians and Sikhs. Spectrum calls it a major obstacle to unity and democracy.

## The new awareness

Western education and the shock of defeat produced varied reactions. Some English-educated Bengali youth rejected Hindu culture outright (Spectrum's examples are drinking wine and eating beef as deliberate defiance). Others tried to reinvigorate traditional institutions against colonial cultural pressure. The common conviction was that social and religious life needed reform. In the later decades, rising nationalism and democracy, spreading education, new economic forces and wider awareness strengthened the resolve.

## Who made the movements

The social base was the new **middle class** and the educated, both Western-educated and traditionally educated. Spectrum stresses a contrast: the ideals came from the European middle class (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment), but the Indian intelligentsia did not grow from trade or industry, which British agencies controlled. Its roots lay in government service and the professions: law, education, journalism and medicine, often with some link to land through intermediate tenures.

## The ideas that gave unity

Three: **rationalism**, **religious universalism** and **humanism**. Examples from the extract: Roy treated demonstrability as the test of truth; Akshay Kumar Dutt called rationalism 'our only preceptor' and used medical opinion against child marriage; Syed Ahmed Khan said religious tenets were not immutable; Vivekananda held that religion must justify itself by the method of science. Roy saw the Vedas' monotheism and Christian unitarianism as expressions of one universal theism; Syed Ahmed Khan said all prophets brought the same faith.

## Why religion and society moved together

Nearly all social ills, such as untouchability and gender inequity, drew their legitimacy from religion. So reformers had to argue about scripture before they could change custom. Early movements were narrow (upper castes, upper and middle classes); later ones reached lower strata, and in the 20th century the national movement supplied leadership.

### 3. Core: Rammohan Roy and the Brahmo Samaj

*Objective:* Narrate Roy's ideas and work, the Brahmo Samaj's features and its splits under Debendranath Tagore and Keshab Chandra Sen.

## Roy (1772 to 1833)

Often called the father of the Indian Renaissance (a label, see module 8), Roy believed in a modern scientific approach, human dignity and the social equality of all men and women, and he wrote *Gift to Monotheists* (Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin, in Persian; Spectrum 1809, standard about 1803-04, verify) and translated the Vedas and five Upanishads into Bengali to show that ancient texts support monotheism. He set up the **Atmiya Sabha** in Calcutta in 1814 (Spectrum's date; some books give 1815, verify). In *Precepts of Jesus* (1820) he separated Christ's moral message from miracle stories and drew missionary anger. He declared that reason may justify departing from scripture.

## The Brahmo Sabha (August 1828)

Founded to institutionalise his ideas, it was renamed Brahmo Samaj. It worshipped one eternal being through prayer, meditation and readings from the Upanishads, with no images allowed in its buildings. Its twin pillars were **reason** and the **Vedas and Upanishads**. Roy did not want a new religion, only a purified Hinduism. Orthodox Hindus led by Raja Radhakant Deb organised the **Dharma Sabha** (1830) against it.

Features of the Samaj (Spectrum): it rejected polytheism and idol worship, avatars and the final authority of any scripture over reason; it took no fixed stand on karma and rebirth; it criticised caste.

## Roy's wider work

- **Sati**: began his campaign in 1818, quoted texts to show no religion sanctioned it, visited cremation grounds, organised vigilance groups and petitioned; the 1829 Regulation followed.
- **Women**: opposed polygamy, defended widows and demanded inheritance rights.
- **Education and language**: supported David Hare's Hindu College (1817), taught mechanics and Voltaire in his own school, founded a Vedanta college (1825), wrote Bengali grammar and a modern prose style.
- **Press and politics**: pioneered journals in several languages; asked for fixed maximum rents, Indianisation of services, separation of executive from judiciary, trial by jury and judicial equality.

His death in 1833 was a setback.

## After Roy

**Debendranath Tagore** (1817 to 1905) joined in 1842; his Tattvabodhini Sabha (1839) studied India's past rationally. He fought both within Hinduism and against missionary conversion. **Keshab Chandra Sen** (1838 to 84) joined the Samaj (Spectrum 1858; standard 1857) and was soon made acharya (1862, verify); he spread branches beyond Bengal, but Debendranath found his ideas too radical (teachings of all religions in meetings; strong views against caste; support for inter-caste marriage) and dismissed him in 1865. Keshab's followers formed the **Brahmo Samaj of India** (1866); Debendranath's became the **Adi Brahmo Samaj**. In 1878 Keshab married off his 13-year-old daughter to the minor Maharaja of Cooch Behar with orthodox rites, which was against Brahmo teaching and caused a second split. Some followers had begun to treat him as an incarnation and he was accused of authoritarianism (Spectrum). The break-away body was the **Sadharan Brahmo Samaj** (Ananda Mohan Bose and others).

## Assessment

It attacked dogma, the prejudice against going abroad, sati, purdah, child marriage and polygamy; it backed widow remarriage and women's education. Its success against caste and untouchability was limited, and its influence rarely went beyond Calcutta and Bengal.

### 4. Core: Derozio, Vidyasagar, Prarthana Samaj and western India

*Objective:* Describe Young Bengal, Vidyasagar's work and the Maharashtra reformers including the Prarthana Samaj.

## Derozio and Young Bengal

In the late 1820s and early 1830s a radical trend appeared among Bengali youth: the **Young Bengal movement**. Its inspirer, **Henry Vivian Derozio** (1809 to 31), a young Anglo-Indian, taught at the Hindu College from 1826 to 1831. Drawing on the French Revolution, he urged pupils to think freely, question authority, love liberty and equality, and oppose decadent custom; they supported women's rights and education. He was removed in 1831 for radicalism. Spectrum calls him perhaps India's first nationalist poet.

Why the Derozians made limited long-term impact (Spectrum): social conditions were not ripe; no other group backed them; they lacked links with the masses, for example they did not take up the peasants' cause; their radicalism was bookish. Yet they carried forward Roy's public education on social, economic and political questions: Indians in higher services, protection of ryots from oppressive zamindars, freedom of the press, trial by jury, revision of the Company's charter. Surendranath Banerjea called them pioneers of the modern civilisation of Bengal.

## Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820 to 91)

A scholar-reformer who blended Indian and Western thought. As principal of Sanskrit College (1850 in Spectrum; most accounts say 1851, verify) he opened it to non-Brahmins and introduced Western thought to break the priestly monopoly of scriptural knowledge. He cited Vedic texts to show that widow remarriage had religious sanction and led the campaign that produced the **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856**, which legalised remarriage and declared the children of such marriages legitimate. He opposed child marriage and polygamy, helped organise 35 girls' schools as school inspector and, as secretary of the Bethune School (established 1849), was a pioneer of women's higher education. Early girls' schools faced abuse and even boycott of parents.

## Western India

- **Balshastri Jambhekar** (1812 to 46): began the *Darpan* (1832), father of Marathi journalism, promoted scientific outlook and widow remarriage.
- **Paramahansa Mandali** (1849): a secret society that broke caste rules by eating food cooked by lower castes; backed widow remarriage and women's education.
- **Prarthana Samaj** (1867, Bombay): Atmaram Pandurang founded it with Keshab Chandra Sen's help. **M.G. Ranade** joined in 1870 and gave it an all-India character; R.G. Bhandarkar and N.G. Chandavarkar were other leaders. It stressed monotheism but was more concerned with social reform than religion, valued Maharashtra's bhakti tradition and used education and persuasion, not confrontation. Its four-point agenda: disapproval of caste, women's education, widow remarriage, a higher marriage age for both sexes.
- **Karve and Ranade** worked on widow remarriage and widows' homes. **Gopalhari Deshmukh** ('Lokahitawadi', 1823 to 92) argued from rational and humanistic principles and wrote that if religion did not sanction social reform, religion should be changed. **G.G. Agarkar** (1856 to 95) criticised blind reliance on tradition and edited *Sudharak*.

Organisations such as the Indian Social Conference (1887, Ranade) and the Servants of India Society (1905, Gokhale) are in the master notes.

### 5. Core: Arya Samaj, Ramakrishna Mission and Theosophical Society

*Objective:* Compare Dayananda's Vedic revival, Vivekananda's service-based Vedanta and the Theosophical movement.

## Dayananda Saraswati and the Arya Samaj

Dayananda (Mulshankar, 1824 to 83), a Gujarati Brahmin, wandered as an ascetic for 15 years and set up the first Arya Samaj unit in **Bombay in 1875**; headquarters later moved to Lahore. His book is *Satyarth Prakash*, and his slogan was **'Back to the Vedas'**, which Spectrum explains as revival of Vedic learning and purity of religion, not revival of Vedic times; he accepted modernity and was patriotic. He treated the Vedas as infallible, criticised later scriptures such as the Puranas and ignorant priests, and held that every person has direct access to God.

He attacked caste rigidity, untouchability, idolatry, polytheism, magic, animal sacrifice, the taboo on sea voyages and shraddhas. He accepted *varna* by merit and occupation, not birth. The Samaj fixed minimum marriage ages at 25 for boys and 16 for girls and encouraged widow remarriage and inter-caste marriage. It served in famines and earthquakes.

**Education and the 1893 split.** The D.A.V. school was set up in Lahore in 1886 (Spectrum says college; the college followed in 1889, verify). Two groups disagreed: the *College Party* (Lala Hansraj, Lala Lajpat Rai) wanted English education and was relaxed about diet; the *Mahatma (Gurukul) Party* (Guru Datta Vidyarthi, Munshi Ram, later Swami Shraddhanand) wanted Sanskrit, Vedic study and strict vegetarianism. The Samaj split in 1893. Shraddhanand began the Gurukul in 1900 (moved to Kangri near Haridwar in 1902).

**Shuddhi.** In its zeal to protect Hindu society from Christianity and Islam, the Samaj began shuddhi, reconverting converts. Spectrum links an aggressive shuddhi campaign to growing communalisation of social life in the 1920s. The same campaign tried to lift untouchables into caste Hindus.

## Ramakrishna and Vivekananda

**Ramakrishna Paramahamsa** (1836 to 86), a poor priest at the Kali temple at Dakshineshwar, taught through parables; he stressed the oneness of all religions ('as many faiths, so many paths') and service of man as service of God. The **Ramakrishna Math** gave the monastic order; **Swami Vivekananda** (Narendranath Datta, 1862 to 1902) founded the **Ramakrishna Mission in 1897** for philanthropic work. At the Chicago Parliament of Religions (1893) he called for a balance of Western materialism and Eastern spiritualism. He urged pride in India's past, unity of Hinduism's sects, uplift of the downtrodden through practical Vedanta, and said it was an insult to teach religion to a starving man. Unlike the Arya Samaj, the Mission values image worship as an aid; it is not a proselytising body and does not call itself a Hindu sect. Belur (near Calcutta) is the headquarters; the Math was shifted there in 1898. Subhas Bose's comment on the page: Vivekananda was the spiritual father of modern nationalism in Bengal.

## Theosophical Society

Founded in New York in 1875 by Madame H.P. Blavatsky and Colonel M.S. Olcott, it moved to **Adyar (Madras) in 1882**. It accepted reincarnation and karma, drew on the Upanishads and Vedanta, and aimed at universal brotherhood without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour. **Annie Besant** (in India from 1893) became president in 1907, founded the Central Hindu College at Benaras in 1898 (the nucleus of Benaras Hindu University, 1916) and worked for women's education. Spectrum judges that it gave self-respect to Indians but remained vague to the average Indian and also encouraged a false pride in outdated traditions.

### 6. Core: Muslim, Sikh and Parsi reform movements

*Objective:* Contrast the Muslim movements (Wahabi, Faraizi, Deoband, Aligarh, Ahmadiyya) and outline Singh Sabha, Akali and Parsi reform.

## Muslim movements: revivalist and resistance strand

- **Wahabi / Walliullah movement**: inspired by Abdul Wahab and **Shah Walliullah (1702 to 63 in Spectrum; usual dates 1703 to 62, verify)**, a revivalist call to the true spirit of Islam, seeking harmony among the four schools of jurisprudence and recognising individual conscience. Shah Abdul Aziz and Syed Ahmad Barelvi gave it a political turn: India was treated as dar-ul-Harb needing conversion to dar-ul-Islam. First aimed at the Sikhs, after the annexation of Punjab (1849) it faced the British, helped spread anti-British feeling in 1857 and faded in the 1870s before British force.
- **Titu Mir** (died 1831) organised Bengal's Muslim peasants against landlords (mostly Hindu) and indigo planters; Spectrum says British records made it look more militant than it was.
- **Faraizi movement**: Haji Shariatullah, 1819, East Bengal, against un-Islamic practices; under his son **Dudu Mian** it turned revolutionary from 1840, with village-to-province organisation and a refusal to pay rent; weakened by his arrest in 1847 and survived as purely religious after his death in 1862.

## Muslim movements: educational and liberal strand

- **Aligarh Movement and Syed Ahmed Khan (1817 to 98)**: a loyalist judicial officer, knighted in 1888. He wanted Western science reconciled with a Quran interpreted in the light of rationalism, said religion must adapt or fossilise, and opened the **Mohammedan Anglo-Oriental school at Aligarh in 1875** (Spectrum calls it the college; college classes began 1877 and Aligarh Muslim University dates from 1920, verify). He opposed purdah and polygamy, favoured easy divorce, criticised piri-muridi and edited *Tahdhib-ul-Akhlaq*. He asked Muslims to focus first on education and jobs and opposed political activity; Spectrum says he later let himself be used in divide-and-rule and began stressing a divergence of Hindu and Muslim interests.
- **Deoband School**: founded in 1866 at Darul Uloom, Deoband (Saharanpur) by Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi for the moral and religious regeneration of Muslims, teaching original Islamic learning and (per Spectrum) keeping alive the spirit of jihad against foreign rulers. It welcomed the Congress and in 1888 issued a fatwa against Syed Ahmed Khan's organisations. Mahmud-ul-Hasan later fused Islamic principles with nationalist aims; the Jamiat-ul-Ulema followed.
- **Ahmadiyya** (Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, 1889): liberal and universal, rejected jihad against non-Muslims and spread Western education, but Spectrum notes it suffered from mysticism. **Shibli Numani** founded the Nadwatul Ulama at Lucknow (1894 to 96), backing English and sciences and Hindu-Muslim cooperation.

## Sikh reform

The **Singh Sabha** (Amritsar, 1873) had two aims: modern education (Khalsa schools) and countering Christian missionaries and other groups' proselytising. The **Akali movement**, an offshoot, wanted gurudwaras freed from corrupt hereditary Udasi mahants. After the non-violent satyagraha of 1921 the government passed the Sikh Gurudwaras Act (Spectrum: 1922, amended 1925; other books treat the 1925 Act as the main one, verify), placing control with the SGPC.

## Parsi reform

The **Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha** (1851; Naoroji Furdonji, Dadabhai Naoroji, K.R. Cama, S.S. Bengalee) sought to restore Zoroastrianism to its purity and improve women's position by ending purdah, raising marriage age and spreading education; its paper was *Rast Goftar*.

### 7. Core: caste and women, a compact map with links to GS-052

*Objective:* Summarise non-Brahmin and Dalit reform, the early Ambedkar lines and the women's reform laws, and see where GS-052 gives detail.

Spectrum names two big agendas for social reform: the **position of women** and the **disabilities of untouchability**. GS-052 (modules 2 and 5 to 7) teaches them in depth; this module keeps the minimum for a connected picture.

## Caste

Four forces undermined caste (Spectrum): unintended British effects (private property in land, towns, equality before law, secular education), reform movements, the national movement and the lower castes themselves. Many reformers defended *chaturvarna* while criticising untouchability.

- **Jyotirao Phule (1827 to 90)**, a Mali from Maharashtra, wanted social transformation, not just reform. He founded the **Satyashodhak Samaj in 1873**, stressed education for women and lower castes, wrote *Gulamgiri* and *Sarvajanik Satyadharma* and promoted simple weddings without a Brahmin priest. With **Savitribai Phule** he opened a girls' school at Bhidewada, Pune, in 1848. Savitribai is called modern India's first woman teacher.
- **Gopal Baba Walangkar** published the monthly *Vital Vidhvansak* from 1888 (the monthly that Spectrum links to Walangkar; the 2020 Prelims question calls it the first monthly journal for the untouchable audience).
- **Sree Narayana Guru (1856 to 1928)**: in 1888 he installed a Siva-linga at Aruvippuram to show that consecration was not a higher-caste monopoly; the **SNDP Yogam** (1903) worked for Ezhavas. His slogan: one religion, one caste, one God for mankind.
- **Justice movement** (Madras): Spectrum names C.N. Mudaliar, T.M. Nair and P. Tyagaraja; the South Indian Liberal Federation (Justice Party) dates to 1916 (standard, verify). Spectrum adds that a Madras Presidency Association formed in 1917 demanded separate representation for lower castes; teach it as a separate Congress-aligned non-Brahmin body (verify). Also the **Self-Respect Movement** (E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker, mid-1920s; weddings without Brahmin priests).
- **Vaikom Satyagraha (1924)** for temple and road access; the Travancore proclamation of 12 November 1936 opened government temples.
- **Ambedkar, early lines only**: *Mooknayak* (1920), the Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha (1924, 'educate, agitate, organise'), the Mahad water satyagraha (March 1927) and the Manusmriti burning (December 1927). The Poona Pact and the Gandhi-Ambedkar debate belong to the Gandhi era and are in GS-052 modules 6 and 7.

## Women: the legislation line

Sati banned (1829); infanticide regulations (1795, 1804); Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856); Native Marriage Act (1872); Age of Consent Act (1891); Sarda Act (passed 1929, in force 1930; minimum 18 for boys and 14 for girls). Rows with source-slip flags are in the master notes; the full timeline is in GS-052 module 2.

**The 1891 Act.** Spectrum says it 'forbade the marriage of girls below the age of 12'; standard knowledge is that it raised the age of consent for sexual intercourse from 10 to 12 (verify). Treat the Spectrum phrase as a slip.

## Institutions for women

Calcutta Female Juvenile Society (1819); Bethune School (1849); Phule schools (1848); Karve's Indian Women's University (1916); Bharat Stree Mahamandal (1910); Ladies Social Conference (1904); AIWC (1927). Detail is in GS-052 module 3.

### 8. Master: themes, limitations and the link to nationalism

*Objective:* Argue the strengths, weaknesses and nationalist relevance of the reform movements in a balanced judgement.

## Four themes to carry into any answer

1. **Rationalism**: test belief by reason (Roy, Syed Ahmed Khan, Vivekananda).
2. **Revival**: appeal to purity of an original religion (Arya Samaj, Deoband, Wahabi).
3. **Universalism**: shared core of religions (Roy, Ramakrishna, Ahmadiyya, Theosophy).
4. **Caste and gender equality**: humanism applied to society (Vidyasagar, Phule, Narayana Guru).

Spectrum says neither a total revival of the past nor a total break with tradition was envisaged; reference to the past was an aid, not a goal. Ranade's line (on the page): the dead past cannot be revived except by reforming the old materials into new organised forms.

## Positive contributions (Spectrum)

- Freed individuals from conformity born of fear and uncritical submission to priests; translation of texts, the right to interpret and simpler rituals made worship personal.
- Promoted human reason and a modern, secular, this-worldly outlook; shifted ideas of pollution and purity.
- Gave the middle class cultural roots and eased the humiliation of conquest; answered the official taunt that Indian society was decadent.
- Sought modernisation rather than blind westernisation; ended cultural isolation.
- Contributed to national consciousness and to resistance to colonial cultural hegemony.

## Limitations

- **Narrow base**: educated, urban middle classes, with peasants and the urban poor largely outside; compare GS-031.
- **Appeal to the past and scripture** encouraged mysticism in new garb and pseudo-scientific thinking.
- **Compartmentalisation**: Hindu praise of the ancient period and a view of the medieval age as decline versus Muslim turning to West Asian history; this fed a notion of two separate peoples and, Spectrum says, contributed to communal consciousness.
- **Caste limits**: many defended *varna*; the Samaj and other bodies limited success against untouchability; Spectrum concludes the struggle could not succeed under foreign rule, which would not risk orthodox hostility.
- **Individual lapses**: Syed Ahmed Khan's later politics, Keshab's 1878 act, shuddhi.

Orthodox backlash was real: abuse, persecution, fatwas and even assassination attempts.

## Link to nationalism

Reformers did political thinking early: Roy asked for Indianisation and a free press; the Derozians raised public questions; Vivekananda inspired pride. Nehru's remark on the page: the rising middle classes wanted cultural roots to reduce frustration and humiliation. The reformist concern for revival of the native cultural personality fed national consciousness, yet cultural backwardness persisted despite political advance. Political associations (British Indian Association 1851, Indian Association 1876 and others) are listed in the master notes; the Swadeshi-era cultural side is in GS-038 module 6, the press and education link in GS-035 (to come on Day 13, not yet written) and the Congress founding in GS-036 (to come on Day 14, not yet written).

### 9. Exam: PYQ patterns, answer frames and traps

*Objective:* Read the 14 verified PYQs by theme, recognise recurring Prelims forms and build frames for Mains questions.

## What the 14 PYQs test

The verified set (2017 to 2025) groups into four themes: **Reformer** (Roy 2025, Walangkar 2020, Vidyasagar 2021, Phule 2025 Mains); **organisation and chronology** (pre-Congress associations 2017, leader pairs 2019, Self-Respect founder 2025, the 2018 sequence); **women and caste** (Rukhmabai 2020, Gandhi's Harijan programme 2025, women's questions 2017 Mains); and **interpretive Mains** (Young Bengal and Brahmo 2021, Renaissance 2019, customs and reason 2020). The full table is in the master notes. Every note is analysis, not an official key.

## Recurring Prelims forms

- **Pairs**: person-organisation, movement-leader. Trap: one near-miss pair (a famous name attached to a nearby organisation, as in the 2017 Madras question).
- **Earliest or sequence**: sort by decade (2018). Literary events (Neeldarpan, Anandmath) sit among reform dates.
- **Statement checks on a reformer** (2025 Roy): reads like praise; match each statement to a documented stand.
- **Single fact** (Vital Vidhvansak, Hindu Female School secretary, founder of Self-Respect): the distractors are other reformers of the same topic.

## Mains frames (suggested, not model answers)

- **2021 Young Bengal and Brahmo (10 marks)**: context in one line; Derozio 1826 to 31 and limits; Roy 1828, features; Tagore and Keshab splits; achievements and limits.
- **2019 Renaissance and national identity (10 marks)**: rationalism and pride in the past; cultural roots; political demands; limits (compartmentalisation); question the label.
- **2017 women's questions (15 marks)**: issues (sati, widows, child marriage, education, purdah, property) and debates (scripture versus reason; persuasion versus law; Rukhmabai appeal; upper-caste focus). Link GS-052 module 2.
- **2020 customs and reason (15 marks)**: agree where custom blocked reason (sati, caste); qualify with reformers who reinterpreted tradition (Vidyasagar's texts, Syed Ahmed Khan) and the value of some customs.
- **2025 Phule (15 marks)**: group by subaltern class: lower castes, untouchables, women, widows and infants, peasants (his *Shetkaryacha Asud* is not in the extracts, verify), education.

## Traps

Mixing Prarthana Samaj with Arya Samaj; dating the Sarda Act to 1930 only; calling Arya Samaj purely revivalist; forgetting the causes of the two Brahmo splits; saying Aligarh was anti-modern.

### Notes: Chronology of reform, 1772 to 1936

- 1772: birth of Rammohan Roy (died 1833).
- 1795 and 1804: Bengal regulations declare female infanticide illegal.
- 1814: Atmiya Sabha, Calcutta (Spectrum; some books say 1815, verify).
- 1819: Haji Shariatullah founds the Faraizi movement; Calcutta Female Juvenile Society (missionaries).
- 1826 to 1831: Derozio teaches at the Hindu College; removed 1831.
- August 1828: Brahmo Sabha founded; later Brahmo Samaj.
- 1829: Regulation XVII bans sati in Bengal; extended to Madras and Bombay in 1830.
- 1830: Dharma Sabha of Radhakant Deb.
- 1839 to 1842: Tattvabodhini Sabha (1839); Debendranath joins the Samaj (1842).
- 1848: Phule couple's girls' school at Bhidewada, Pune.
- 1849: Bethune School, Calcutta; Paramahansa Mandali.
- 1850 (Spectrum; most accounts 1851, verify): Vidyasagar principal of Sanskrit College.
- 1851: Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (Parsi); British Indian Association.
- 1856: Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act.
- 1865 and 1866: Keshab dismissed as acharya; Brahmo Samaj of India; Adi Brahmo Samaj.
- 1866: Deoband Darul Uloom.
- 1867: Prarthana Samaj.
- 1872: Native Marriage Act.
- 1873: Satyashodhak Samaj; Singh Sabha (Amritsar).
- 1875: Arya Samaj (Bombay); Theosophical Society (New York); Aligarh school (college classes 1877, AMU 1920, verify).
- 1876: Indian Association of Calcutta.
- 1878: Cooch Behar marriage and the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj split.
- 1882: Theosophical headquarters move to Adyar.
- 1884: Dadaji Bhikaji versus Rukhmabai: suit filed 1884; Pinhey dismissed it (1885, verify); reversed on appeal 1887.
- 1886: D.A.V. school, Lahore (college 1889, verify).
- 1887: Indian Social Conference (first session, Madras, with the Congress); Dev Samaj at Lahore.
- 1888: Narayana Guru at Aruvippuram; Vital Vidhvansak begins; Phule given the title Mahatma.
- 1889: Ahmadiyya (Mirza Ghulam Ahmad).
- 1891: Age of Consent Act.
- 1893: Chicago Parliament of Religions (Vivekananda); Arya Samaj split.
- 1897: Ramakrishna Mission.
- 1903: SNDP Yogam registered.
- 1905: Servants of India Society.
- 1916: South Indian Liberal Federation, the Justice Party (standard, verify).
- 1917: Madras Presidency Association (Spectrum; a separate non-Brahmin body, verify).
- 1920: Mooknayak.
- 1921 to 1922 and 1925: Akali satyagraha and the Gurudwara legislation (verify which Act is the main one).
- 1924: Vaikom Satyagraha; Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha.
- 1927: AIWC; Mahad satyagraha (March) and Manusmriti burning (December).
- 1929, in force 1930: Sarda Act.
- 1936: Travancore temple-entry proclamation (12 November).

### Notes: Why reform arose, and how it ended up (cause and effect)

- Colonial culture and Western education led to comparison, shock and self-criticism, so a reform urge arose.
- A new middle class from service and professions provided the leaders, so the base was narrow.
- Social ills drew legitimacy from religion, so reformers argued scripture before custom.
- Reformers' appeal to ancient glory gave cultural roots, yet also encouraged mysticism and compartmentalisation.
- Revival and shuddhi campaigns in the 1920s fed communal consciousness (Spectrum).
- National leadership took over social reform in the 20th century; the struggle against caste could not fully succeed under a foreign government (Spectrum).

### Notes: Source slips and differences to handle

- Age of Consent Act 1891: Spectrum says it 'forbade the marriage of girls below the age of 12'; standard knowledge is that it raised the age of consent for sexual intercourse from 10 to 12 (verify).
- Sarda Act: Spectrum's text says the 1929 Act came into force in 1930, but its summary writes 'Sarda Act, 1930'; teach passed 1929, in force 1930.
- AIWC law list: 'Hindu Marriage and Divorce Act (1954)' should read Hindu Marriage Act 1955; 'Equal Remuneration Act (1958, 1976)' and 'Suppression of Immoral Traffic in Women Act (1958)' differ from the summary (1976, 1956); verify.
- Atmiya Sabha: Spectrum 1814; some books 1815 (verify).
- Sikh Gurudwaras Act: Spectrum 1922 amended 1925; other books give 1925 as the main Act (verify).
- Spectrum names the Dev Samaj 'Dev Sadan' once, and the Ambedkar sabha is spelt two ways (Bahishkrit Hitakarini / Bahushkrit Hitakarani).
- Not in the extract and flagged verify: Radhakanta Deb as first president of the British Indian Association; Gazulu Lakshminarasu Chetty's Madras Native Association (1852) as distinct from the Madras Mahajana Sabha (1884); Neeldarpan 1860; Satyendranath Tagore's ICS success 1863; Anandmath 1882; Hindu Female School as the early name of the Bethune School; Phule's Shetkaryacha Asud; the Gulamgiri date 1873.
- Rukhmabai case: Spectrum names Pinhey's decision and the 1887 reversal but gives no year for the first decision; the usual date is 1885 (GS-052 module 2; verify).
- Justice Party: Spectrum's text does not call the 1917 Madras Presidency Association the Justice body; teach the South Indian Liberal Federation (Justice Party) 1916 and the Madras Presidency Association 1917 (a separate Congress-aligned non-Brahmin body) as different (verify).
- Gift to Monotheists: Spectrum 1809; the standard date is about 1803-04 (Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin, Persian) (verify).

### Notes: Frequently confused

- Prarthana Samaj (Bombay, 1867, Atmaram Pandurang, bhakti-linked) versus Arya Samaj (Bombay 1875, Dayananda, Vedic revival).
- Brahmo Samaj of India (Keshab, 1866) versus Adi Brahmo Samaj (Debendranath) versus Sadharan Brahmo Samaj (after 1878).
- Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884) versus Indian Association (1876) versus British Indian Association (1851).
- Ramakrishna Math (monastic order) versus Ramakrishna Mission (1897, service).
- Aligarh (Syed Ahmed Khan, education and loyalty) versus Deoband (religious regeneration, closer to Congress).
- Bethune School (women's education) versus Phule's Bhidewada school (1848): different schools, different founders.
- Self-Respect Movement (E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker) versus Justice movement (T.M. Nair and others).

### Core concepts

- Reform arose from colonial contact, social ills and a new middle class.
- Reformist movements lean on reason; revivalist on original purity; both appeal to some purity.
- Rationalism, universalism and humanism gave the movements their unity.
- Social reform turned to women and caste, later led by the national movement.

**A.** 1801 to 1900.

**A.** Reformist leans on reason and conscience; revivalist leans on a lost original purity; both appealed to some purity.

**Q.** Which three ideas gave the reform movements ideological unity?

**A.** Rationalism, religious universalism and humanism.

**A.** They arose from the educated middle class in service and professions and largely ignored peasants and the urban poor.
