## PH-003: Central-force dynamics and effective potential

Physics Paper I · Physics · Mechanics: particles

### Bridge: Polar coordinates: velocity and acceleration components (derived)

Orbits are easiest to describe in **polar coordinates** (r, φ): r is the distance of the particle from the centre O and φ is the angle of the line from O to the particle, measured from a fixed axis and increasing anticlockwise. (Bridge B2 of PH-009 and PH-002 gives the rotating unit vector; here the acceleration is added.)

Two **unit vectors** (arrows of length 1) move with the particle: r̂ = (cos φ, sin φ) points outward from O, and φ̂ = (−sin φ, cos φ) is perpendicular to it and points the way φ increases. If the particle moves, φ changes with time, and the chain rule gives

$$ dr̂/dt = φ̇ φ̂,   dφ̂/dt = −φ̇ r̂ $$

(a dot over a symbol means d/dt). The position is r = r r̂. By the product rule the **velocity** is

$$ v = ṙ r̂ + r φ̇ φ̂ $$

so ṙ is the radial speed and r φ̇ is the transverse speed. Differentiate once more, term by term:

$$ a = r̈ r̂ + ṙ φ̇ φ̂ + (ṙ φ̇ + r φ̈) φ̂ + r φ̇ (−φ̇ r̂) $$

Collecting the r̂ terms and the φ̂ terms gives the **acceleration**

$$ a = (r̈ − r φ̇²) r̂ + (r φ̈ + 2 ṙ φ̇) φ̂ $$

The transverse bracket can be written (1/r) d(r² φ̇)/dt, because d(r² φ̇)/dt = 2 r ṙ φ̇ + r² φ̈. Remember this identity: it turns 'transverse force = 0' into 'r² φ̇ is constant' in module 2.

Checks. Units: r φ̇² has units m s⁻², the same as r̈. Uniform circular motion (r = R, φ̇ = ω constants): a = −R ω² r̂, the usual inward centripetal acceleration of size v²/R. The term 2 ṙ φ̇ vanishes if either the radial speed or the angular speed is zero.

### Bridge: Conic sections in polar form: r = l/(1 + e cos φ)

A **conic section** is the set of points whose distance from a fixed point (the **focus**) is a constant e times their distance from a fixed line (the **directrix**). The constant e ≥ 0 is the **eccentricity**.

Place the focus at the origin O and the directrix on the vertical line x = l/e, where l is a length. A point at polar coordinates (r, φ) has x = r cos φ, so its distance from the directrix is l/e − r cos φ (for points to the left of the line). The defining property says r = e (l/e − r cos φ) = l − e r cos φ, hence

$$ r = l / (1 + e cos φ) $$

The length l is the **semi-latus rectum**, the value of r at φ = 90°. The shape depends on e:

- e = 0: r = l, a **circle**.
- 0 < e < 1: an **ellipse** (a closed oval). Closest point (φ = 0): r_min = l/(1 + e). Farthest point (φ = π): r_max = l/(1 − e). The **semi-major axis** is a = (r_min + r_max)/2 = l/(1 − e²) and the **semi-minor axis** is b = a √(1 − e²), so that b² = a l. Area = π a b.
- e = 1: a **parabola** (r → ∞ as φ → π).
- e > 1: a **hyperbola**; r → ∞ when cos φ = −1/e, and only angles with 1 + e cos φ > 0 are allowed.

Words used later: the points of closest and farthest distance are the **apsides** (singular apsis); for an orbit about the Sun they are the **perihelion** and **aphelion**, for an orbit about the Earth the **perigee** and **apogee**.

Numerical check. With l = 1 and e = 0.6: r_min = 0.625, r_max = 2.5, a = 1.5625, b = 1.25 and b² = 1.5625 = a l. For e = 0 all of r_min, r_max, a and b equal l.

### Bridge: Calculus of the substitution u = 1/r

Orbit problems ask for the **shape** r(φ), not r(t). The trick is to use the angle φ as the independent variable and to work with u = 1/r. Suppose h = r² φ̇ is constant (it is, for a central force: module 2). Then φ̇ = h/r² = h u².

Because any quantity f depends on time only through φ, the chain rule gives d f/dt = φ̇ d f/dφ = h u² d f/dφ. Apply it twice.

Radial speed: r = 1/u, so ṙ = d(1/u)/dt = −(1/u²) u̇. Using u̇ = h u² du/dφ:

$$ ṙ = −h du/dφ $$

Radial acceleration: differentiate ṙ again with the same rule,

$$ r̈ = −h d/dt (du/dφ) = −h (h u²) d²u/dφ² = −h² u² d²u/dφ² $$

Also r φ̇² = (1/u)(h u²)² = h² u³, which will be subtracted from r̈ in the radial equation.

Checks. Units: h has units m² s⁻¹ and du/dφ has units m⁻¹ (φ is dimensionless), so h du/dφ is m s⁻¹, a speed, as ṙ should be. Circular orbit: u is constant, so du/dφ = 0 and ṙ = r̈ = 0, as it must. Second check with a known orbit: for u = (1 + e cos φ)/l, ṙ = h (e/l) sin φ, which vanishes at φ = 0 and φ = π, the two apsides, where the distance is a minimum or maximum, as it must.

### 1. Orientation: what 'Motion under a central force; Conservation of angular momentum, Kepler's laws' asks of you

*Objective:* Say in plain words what a central force is, what the topic asks you to find, and how the ten modules are ordered.

## What the syllabus words mean

The Physics Paper I syllabus (section 1(a), Mechanics of particles) contains the phrase 'Motion under a central force; Conservation of angular momentum, Kepler's laws'. In plain words: a **central force** is a push or pull whose line of action always passes through one fixed point, the **centre of force**, and whose size depends only on the distance from that point. Examples: the Sun's gravity on a planet, the electric attraction of a nucleus on an electron, and the pull of an ideal spring anchored at a point. The topic asks what such a force does to the motion: what stays constant, what shape the path has, and why planets obey Kepler's laws.

## Words to know before starting

**Orbit**: the path of the particle. **Bound** orbit: the particle stays at finite distance for ever. **Unbound**: it escapes to infinity. **Energy E**: kinetic plus potential. **Angular momentum L**: a measure of the sideways motion about the centre (PH-001, module 7). **Effective potential**: a one-variable energy function that holds the angular motion inside it (module 3).

### 2. Foundation: conservation of angular momentum, planar motion and equal areas

*Objective:* Prove that for a central force the angular momentum is constant, the path lies in a plane and the radius vector sweeps equal areas in equal times.

## Definition, convention, assumptions

A force F on a particle is **central** about O if F = F(r) r̂, where r̂ = r/r is the unit vector from O to the particle and F(r) depends only on r = |r|. Sign convention used throughout: **F(r) < 0 is attractive** (towards O), F(r) > 0 is repulsive. Examples: gravity F = −G M m/r²; the Coulomb force F = q₁q₂/(4π ε₀ r²) (negative, so attractive, for opposite charges); the ideal spring F = −κ r. A dipole field is not central (it depends on direction too). Assumptions: O is fixed in an inertial frame (for two bodies use the reduced one-body problem of PH-009), the particle has mass m, and no other force acts.

## Theorem 1: L is constant

Let L = r × p = m r × v. Differentiate with the product rule, keeping the order of the cross product:

$$ dL/dt = v × (m v) + r × (dp/dt) = 0 + r × F $$

because v × v = 0. With F = F(r) r/r, r × F = (F(r)/r)(r × r) = 0. So dL/dt = 0: L is constant in **magnitude and direction**.

## Theorem 2: the motion is planar

Take the dot product of r with L = r × p: r · L = r · (r × p) = (r × r) · p = 0, by the cyclic property of the scalar triple product (a parallelepiped built on r, r and p has zero volume). So r is always perpendicular to the fixed vector L: the particle stays in the plane through O perpendicular to L. If L = 0, then r × v = 0 at all times, v is along r and the path is a straight line through O.

Choose the z axis along L, so the motion is in the plane z = 0, and use polar coordinates (r, φ) with φ increasing in the sense of motion, so that L = m r² φ̇ > 0 (B1: the r̂ part of v contributes nothing to r × v).

## Theorem 3: constant areal velocity

In time dt the radius vector sweeps a thin triangle of base r dφ and height r (see the figure), area dA = ½ |r × dr| = ½ r² dφ. Therefore

$$ dA/dt = ½ r² φ̇ = L/(2m) $$

which is constant. This is **Kepler's second law**; notice that it holds for **any** central force, not only gravity. Writing h = r² φ̇ = L/m, we have h = 2 dA/dt and the transverse speed v_φ = r φ̇ = h/r: the particle is fastest where it is nearest the centre.

## Checks

Units: L is kg m² s⁻¹ and dA/dt is m² s⁻¹ = (kg m² s⁻¹)/kg. Limit: uniform circular motion of radius R gives dA/dt = ½ R² ω. For the Earth (ω = 1.991 × 10⁻⁷ rad s⁻¹, R = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m) this is 2.23 × 10¹⁵ m² s⁻¹, or 1.92 × 10²⁰ m² per day.

Conditions matter: the argument needs the centre fixed in an inertial frame and no torque from any other force.

### 3. Core: the radial equation and the effective potential

*Objective:* Derive m r̈ = F + L²/(m r³) and E = ½ m ṙ² + U_eff(r) with U_eff = U + L²/(2 m r²), and read allowed motion from the graph of U_eff.

## Setting and assumptions

A particle of mass m moves under F = F(r) r̂ with the centre O fixed in an inertial frame. By module 2 the motion is in a plane, so polar coordinates (r, φ) suffice. Use the acceleration of bridge B1.

## Equations of motion

Newton's second law, component by component:

$$ m (r̈ − r φ̇²) = F(r) $$
$$ m (r φ̈ + 2 ṙ φ̇) = 0 $$

The second equation is (m/r) d(r² φ̇)/dt = 0, i.e. L = m r² φ̇ is constant: module 2 again, now in polar form. Eliminate φ̇ = L/(m r²) from the first:

$$ m r̈ = F(r) + L²/(m r³) $$

This involves r alone: a **one-dimensional** problem.

## A central force is conservative

For a displacement dr the work is F · dr = F(r) (r̂ · dr) = F(r) dr, because r̂ · dr is the change dr in distance. The work between two points therefore depends only on their distances from O, not on the path. Define U(r) by F = −dU/dr, i.e. U(r) = −∫F dr (an additive constant is a convention; for gravity and Coulomb forces take U → 0 as r → ∞).

## Energy and effective potential

E = ½ m (ṙ² + r² φ̇²) + U(r). With r² φ̇² = L²/(m² r²):

$$ E = ½ m ṙ² + L²/(2 m r²) + U(r) = ½ m ṙ² + U_eff(r) $$

$$ U_eff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2 m r²) $$

The new term is the kinetic energy of the angular motion written through the conserved L; it is called the **centrifugal barrier**. It is large at small r and keeps a particle with L ≠ 0 away from the centre.

## Consistency check

Differentiate E = const: m ṙ r̈ + U_eff'(r) ṙ = 0, so (for ṙ ≠ 0) m r̈ = −dU_eff/dr = −U' + L²/(m r³) = F + L²/(m r³), the same radial equation. Two routes, one result.

## Interpretation

The term L²/(m r³) = m r φ̇² is not a new force in the inertial frame; it is the term −m r φ̇² of the polar acceleration moved to the other side of Newton's equation. In a frame rotating with the particle's φ̇ it appears as the centrifugal force of PH-002.

## Allowed motion

Since ṙ² = (2/m)(E − U_eff) ≥ 0, the particle can only be where U_eff(r) ≤ E. The figure shows U_eff for U = −k/r and four energy levels (module 6 identifies the orbits).

## Checks

Units: L²/(m r²) = (kg m² s⁻¹)²/(kg m²) = kg m² s⁻² = joule. Limit L → 0: the barrier disappears and the motion is radial. Limit r → 0 with L ≠ 0: U_eff → +∞ whenever U is less singular than 1/r², so the centre cannot be reached.

### 4. Core: turning points, circular orbits and the stability condition

*Objective:* Find turning points and circular orbits from U_eff, and show that a circular orbit is stable when d²U_eff/dr² > 0, which for F = −K/rⁿ means n < 3.

## Turning points

At a turning point ṙ = 0, so E = U_eff(r). The particle is confined to the region where U_eff(r) ≤ E. For U = −k/r (module 3 figure): if E < 0 there are two turning points r_min < r_max and the motion is **bounded** between them; if E ≥ 0 there is only an inner turning point and the particle can escape to r → ∞. At a turning point the particle is momentarily at rest *radially* only: its transverse speed L/(m r) is not zero.

## Circular orbits

For r = r₀ constant, ṙ = r̈ = 0, so the radial equation gives

$$ dU_eff/dr |_(r₀) = 0  ⇔  −F(r₀) = L²/(m r₀³) = m r₀ φ̇² = m v²/r₀ $$

The right side is positive, so a circular orbit needs an **attractive** force, and the condition is the centripetal requirement. Each radius with F(r₀) < 0 has one angular momentum, L² = −m r₀³ F(r₀). The energy is E = U_eff(r₀).

## Stability: assumptions

Disturb the orbit slightly without changing L: r = r₀ + x with |x| ≪ r₀. Taylor-expand about r₀ and use U_eff'(r₀) = 0:

$$ m ẍ = −U_eff'(r₀ + x) ≈ −U_eff''(r₀) x $$

If **U_eff''(r₀) > 0** this is simple harmonic motion with ω_r = √(U_eff''(r₀)/m): the circular orbit is **stable**. If U_eff''(r₀) < 0 the displacement grows exponentially: **unstable**. If it is zero, higher derivatives decide (marginal).

## Stability in terms of the force

U_eff'' = U'' + 3L²/(m r⁴). At r₀, L²/(m r₀³) = −F(r₀) gives L²/(m r₀⁴) = −F(r₀)/r₀; also U'' = −F'. Hence

$$ U_eff''(r₀) = −F'(r₀) − 3 F(r₀)/r₀ $$

Write |F| = −F > 0. Then U_eff'' = |F|' + 3|F|/r₀, which is positive exactly when d ln|F|/d ln r > −3.

## Power laws

For |F| = K/rⁿ (K > 0, attractive), d ln|F|/d ln r = −n, so the circular orbit is stable if and only if **n < 3**. Explicitly U_eff''(r₀) = (3 − n) K r₀^−(n+1). Gravity and Coulomb (n = 2): K/r₀³ > 0, stable. The spring F = −κ r is n = −1: U_eff'' = 4κ, stable. Inverse cube (n = 3): marginal. n = 4: unstable.

## Frequencies and checks

The angular speed is ω_φ² = |F|/(m r₀) = K r₀^−(n+1)/m, so ω_r² = (3 − n) ω_φ². Gravity: ω_r = ω_φ, a radial oscillation per revolution, which anticipates the closed ellipse (module 9). Numerical check by integrating the equations of motion (units K = m = r₀ = 1, speed raised 0.1 % above circular): for n = 2.8 the radius stays between 1.000 and 1.020; for n = 3.2 it grows from 1 to about 9 within 40 time units.

### 5. Core: the orbit equation (Binet) in u = 1/r

*Objective:* Derive u'' + u = −(m/(L² u²)) F(1/u) and use it both ways: find the force from a given orbit and the orbit from a given force.

## Derivation

Start from the radial equation of module 3, m r̈ = F(r) + L²/(m r³). Put h = L/m and use bridge B3: r̈ = −h² u² d²u/dφ² and 1/r³ = u³. Then

$$ −m h² u² u'' = F(1/u) + m h² u³ $$

where a prime means d/dφ. Using m h² = L²/m and dividing by −L² u²/m:

$$ u'' + u = −(m/(L² u²)) F(1/u) $$

This is the **orbit equation** (often called Binet's equation). It contains φ and r only, no time. Sign check: for an attractive force F < 0, the right side is positive.

## Second derivation (energy route)

With ṙ = −h u' from bridge B3, E = ½ m h² u'² + L² u²/(2m) + U(1/u), i.e.

$$ (L²/2m)(u'² + u²) + U(1/u) = E $$

Differentiating with respect to φ and dividing by u' reproduces the Binet equation. Because it needs only a first integral it is also the quickest way to get e (module 6).

## Use 1: orbit given, find the force

Rearranging, F(r) = −(L² u²/m)(u'' + u), with u = 1/r. Recipe: write u(φ), differentiate twice, add u, multiply by −L² u²/m, then rewrite in terms of r.

Worked example: a circular orbit that passes through the centre of force, r = 2a cos φ. Then u = sec φ/(2a); u'' = (sec φ tan² φ + sec³ φ)/(2a), so u'' + u = sec φ (tan² φ + sec² φ + 1)/(2a) = sec³ φ/a = 8 a² u³, using sec³ φ = 8 a³ u³. Hence

$$ F = −(L² u²/m) 8 a² u³ = −8 a² L²/(m r⁵) $$

an attractive inverse-fifth-power force. (This orbit has r → 0 at φ = ±90°, so the particle falls into the centre: it is not a bound circular orbit like those of module 4.)

## Use 2: force given, find the orbit

For the inverse-square force F = −k/r² = −k u², the right-hand side is the constant m k/L², and the equation is linear: **u'' + u = m k/L²** (module 6 solves it). For the spring F = −κ r = −κ/u: u'' + u = (m κ/L²) u⁻³, a non-linear equation; its solutions are ellipses centred on O (module 9).

## Checks

Units: m F/(L² u²) has units kg (kg m s⁻²)/(kg² m⁴ s⁻² m⁻²) = m⁻¹, the same as u. Circular orbit: u = const so u'' = 0 and u = −(m/(L² u²)) F, i.e. −F = L² u³/m = L²/(m r³), the circular-orbit condition of module 4. The substitution makes sense only when L ≠ 0.

### 6. Core: the inverse-square orbit is a conic; the eccentricity and the classification by energy

*Objective:* Solve the Binet equation for F = −k/r², show the orbit is a conic r = ℓ/(1 + e cos φ), derive e = √(1 + 2 E L²/(m k²)) and classify the orbits by the sign of E.

## Setting and assumptions

Attractive inverse-square force F = −k/r² with k > 0 (k = G M m for gravity, k = Z q_e²/(4π ε₀) for an electron and a nucleus of charge Z q_e). Then U = −k/r, taking U = 0 at infinity. The constants of the motion are E and L ≠ 0. The centre O is fixed in an inertial frame (or use the reduced mass, PH-009).

## Solving the orbit equation

With F = −k u², the Binet equation of module 5 becomes linear:

$$ u'' + u = m k/L² = 1/ℓ,   ℓ = L²/(m k) $$

The general solution is the constant 1/ℓ plus the general solution of u'' + u = 0:

$$ u = 1/ℓ + A cos(φ − φ₀) $$

Turn the axis so that φ₀ = 0 (it then points to the closest approach) and write A = e/ℓ with e ≥ 0:

$$ r = ℓ/(1 + e cos φ) $$

By bridge B2 this is a **conic** with the centre of force at a focus, semi-latus rectum ℓ and eccentricity e.

## The eccentricity from the energy

At φ = 0, u' = 0 (an apsis), and u₀ = (1 + e)/ℓ. The first integral of module 5 gives E = (L²/2m) u₀² − k u₀. Use L²/(m ℓ) = k:

$$ E = (k/2ℓ)(1 + e)² − (k/ℓ)(1 + e) = (k/2ℓ)(1 + e)(e − 1) = (k/2ℓ)(e² − 1) $$

so e² = 1 + 2 E ℓ/k, that is

$$ e = √(1 + 2 E L²/(m k²)) $$

## Classification by energy

| Energy E | e | Orbit | Motion |
|---|---|---|---|
| −m k²/(2L²) (the minimum allowed) | 0 | circle, r = ℓ | bound |
| between −m k²/(2L²) and 0 | 0 < e < 1 | ellipse | bound |
| 0 | 1 | parabola | just unbound |
| positive | e > 1 | hyperbola | unbound |

Values of E below −m k²/(2L²) would make e² negative: they are impossible, matching the fact that U_eff has the lowest value −k/(2ℓ) = −m k²/(2L²) at r = ℓ (module 3 figure).

## Checks

Dimensionless: [E L²/(m k²)] = (kg m² s⁻²)(kg² m⁴ s⁻²)/(kg (kg m³ s⁻²)²) = 1. Numerical test: a particle starting at radius r₀ with tangential speed 1.2 times the circular speed has e = |v²/v_c² − 1| = 0.44 and r_max/r_min = (1 + e)/(1 − e) = 2.571; numerical integration of the equations of motion returns the farthest distance 2.5714 r₀. For a hyperbola the particle only moves between the asymptote angles where cos φ_∞ = −1/e; the repulsive case (k < 0) gives hyperbolas that bend away from O and is treated in PH-010.

### 7. Core: Kepler's three laws deduced, the energy −k/(2a) and the vis-viva relation

*Objective:* Deduce Kepler's laws from the central inverse-square force, obtain T² = 4π² m a³/k and E = −k/(2a), and state which laws need the inverse-square form.

## Kepler's laws as statements

(1) Each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. (2) The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times. (3) The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. (NCERT Class XI, Gravitation, states the laws of orbits and areas on page 2 and applies the law of periods to satellites on page 11.)

## First law

For F = −k/r² a bound orbit has E < 0, so e < 1 (module 6): an ellipse with the centre of force at a focus. Strictly the focus is the centre of mass of Sun and planet; because the Sun is so much heavier the centre of mass lies very near the Sun's centre (for comparable masses use the reduced-mass treatment of PH-009).

## Second law

Module 2: dA/dt = L/(2m) = constant, for any central force.

## Third law

The period is the ellipse area divided by the areal velocity: T = π a b/(L/2m) = 2π m a b/L. From B2, b² = a ℓ = a L²/(m k), so b = L √(a/(m k)). Substitute:

$$ T = 2π m a (L √a/√(m k))/L = 2π √(m/k) a^(3/2)   ⇒   T² = (4π² m/k) a³ $$

For gravity k = G M m, so **T² = 4π² a³/(G M)**: the mass m of the planet cancels and the constant is the same for every planet of the same Sun. Check with a circle (a = r): m v²/r = G M m/r² and v = 2π r/T give the same result. Numbers (T in years, a in astronomical units, T² = a³): Mars, a = 1.524 AU, T = 1.524^1.5 = 1.881 yr = 687 days; Jupiter, a = 5.20 AU, T = 11.86 yr.

## Energy of a Kepler orbit

From module 6, E = (k/2ℓ)(e² − 1), and ℓ = a(1 − e²) by B2. Therefore

$$ E = −k/(2a) $$

The energy depends only on the semi-major axis, not on the eccentricity: all orbits of the same a have the same energy and the same period. For a circle, E = −k/(2r) = −K, where K = k/(2r) is the kinetic energy; the potential energy −k/r = −2K.

## Vis-viva relation

Put E = ½ m v² − k/r = −k/(2a):

$$ v² = (k/m)(2/r − 1/a) $$

For gravity, v² = G M (2/r − 1/a). Check: at r = a it gives the circular speed squared G M/a.

## Which law needs what

Law 2 follows from any central force. Laws 1 and 3 need the inverse-square form: Newton reversed the argument, since for the ellipse u = (1 + e cos φ)/ℓ the Binet equation gives F = −(L² u²/m)(u'' + u) = −(L²/(m ℓ)) u², an inverse-square force (module 5).

### 8. Core: apsides, speeds, escape speed and two worked examples (satellite, comet)

*Objective:* Compute perihelion and aphelion speeds, escape speed and the orbit type from the speed at an apsis, and apply them to a low-Earth satellite and a comet.

## Apsides and their speeds

At an apsis ṙ = 0, so the velocity is perpendicular to r and L = m r v. Closest and farthest distances are r_p = a(1 − e) and r_a = a(1 + e). Conservation of L gives r_p v_p = r_a v_a, so

$$ v_p/v_a = r_a/r_p = (1 + e)/(1 − e) $$

The vis-viva relation (module 7) at r_p gives v_p² = GM(2/(a(1 − e)) − 1/a) = (GM/a)(1 + e)/(1 − e), and similarly v_a² = (GM/a)(1 − e)/(1 + e). Eccentricity from measured speeds: e = (v_p − v_a)/(v_p + v_a).

## Escape speed

Escape means E ≥ 0. The minimum speed has E = 0 (a parabola): ½ m v² = G M m/r, so

$$ v_esc = √(2GM/r) = √2 v_circ(r) $$

The direction does not matter, only the energy (provided the path does not hit the body). With g = GM/R², v_esc = √(2gR). Earth: g = 9.8 m s⁻², R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m gives 11.2 km s⁻¹; with GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³ s⁻² and R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m the value is 11.19 km s⁻¹. Moon (GM = 4.905 × 10¹² m³ s⁻², R = 1.737 × 10⁶ m): 2.38 km s⁻¹. A body leaving with speed v > v_esc keeps v_∞ = √(v² − v_esc²) far away.

## Orbit type from a tangential speed

If the velocity at distance r is tangential and x = v²/v_circ², then e = |x − 1|, and the orbit is bound if x < 2 (v < v_esc). If x > 1 the point is the perigee/perihelion; if x < 1 it is the apogee/aphelion. (Derivation: e² = 1 + 2EL²/(mk²) with E = ½ m v² − k/r and L = m v r gives e² = (x − 1)².)

## Worked example 1: a satellite

A 500 kg satellite is in a circular orbit 400 km above the Earth: r = 6.371 × 10⁶ + 4.0 × 10⁵ = 6.771 × 10⁶ m, GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³ s⁻² (standard values; verify). Then v = √(GM/r) = 7.673 km s⁻¹; T = 2π r/v = 5545 s = 92.4 min; K = ½ m v² = 1.472 × 10¹⁰ J; U = −2K = −2.943 × 10¹⁰ J; E = −K = −1.472 × 10¹⁰ J; L = m v r = 2.598 × 10¹³ kg m² s⁻¹. Escape speed there is √2 × 7.673 = 10.85 km s⁻¹, so an extra 3.18 km s⁻¹ is needed.

## Worked example 2: a comet (hypothetical data)

Perihelion q = 0.60 AU and aphelion Q = 30 AU. Then a = 15.3 AU, e = (Q − q)/(Q + q) = 0.9608, T = a^1.5 = 59.8 yr. The circular speed at 1 AU is 29.78 km s⁻¹. v_p = 29.78 √(2/0.60 − 1/15.3) = 53.8 km s⁻¹ and v_a = 29.78 √(2/30 − 1/15.3) = 1.08 km s⁻¹; the ratio is 50 = Q/q, as angular-momentum conservation demands. The escape speed at perihelion is 29.78 √(2/0.6) = 54.4 km s⁻¹, so the comet there is only 1 % below escape speed: a very elongated bound ellipse.

### 9. Master: forces F ∝ r⁻ⁿ, the apsidal angle and precession

*Objective:* Say qualitatively which power laws allow bound orbits, derive the apsidal angle π/√(3 − n) for near-circular orbits, and explain precession.

## Do bound orbits exist?

Take an attractive F = −K/rⁿ (K > 0), so U = −K/((n − 1) r^(n−1)) for n ≠ 1 and U_eff = L²/(2 m r²) + U.

- **n < 3**: at small r the barrier 1/r² beats r^−(n−1), so every orbit with L ≠ 0 has an inner turning point and a stable circular orbit exists (module 4). For n ≤ 1 (the spring, n = −1) U grows with r and every orbit is bound; for 1 < n < 3 an orbit is bound if E < 0.
- **n = 3**: U_eff = (L² − m K)/(2 m r²). If L² > m K the barrier wins and the particle is turned back; if L² < m K it falls into the centre; L² = m K is the borderline.
- **n > 3**: −K/r^(n−1) beats the barrier at small r, U_eff → −∞, there is no barrier and circular orbits are unstable: a particle that gets near the centre falls in.

## Apsidal angle for near-circular orbits

From module 4, ω_r² = (3 − n) ω_φ². The radius goes from r_min to r_max in half a radial period, π/ω_r. In that time φ advances ω_φ π/ω_r:

$$ Δφ_apsidal = π/√(3 − n) $$

- n = 2: π. From one apsis to the next the particle turns through half a revolution: a **closed** ellipse with a fixed perihelion.
- n = −1 (spring): π/2: four apsides per revolution; the orbit is an ellipse centred on O.
- n = 1: π/√2 = 127.3°; n = 2.5: π/√0.5 = 254.6°. A numerical orbit with n = 2.5 started 1 % above circular speed gives 254.6° between apsides. The path closes only if ω_r/ω_φ = √(3 − n) is a rational number.

## Bertrand's theorem (stated, not proved here)

A standard result (not in the extracts; verify in a mechanics text): the only attractive power-law central forces for which **every** bound orbit is closed are n = 2 and n = −1.

## Precession

If F is inverse-square plus a small extra term, the effective exponent n = 2 + δ with δ small. The apsidal angle becomes π/√(1 − δ) ≈ π(1 + δ/2), so the line of apsides advances by about π δ per revolution (2π/√(1 − δ) − 2π ≈ π δ). For n = 2.5 the advance per radial period is 2π/√0.5 − 2π = 2.60 rad = 149.1°. A pure inverse-square force from one Sun would give no precession. Standard remark (not in the extracts; verify): Mercury's observed extra perihelion advance of about 43 arcseconds per century is accounted for by general relativity.

## Checks

n = 2 gives π and n = −1 gives π/2, in agreement with the exact solutions (the conic and the ellipse x = A cos ωt, y = B sin ωt). The result holds only for small departures from a circle.

## 2018 and 2019 (planar motion; definition; L constant)

Module 2: Theorem 1 gives dL/dt = r × F = 0, Theorem 2 gives r · L = 0 so the path lies in a plane, and the remark L = 0 gives a straight line. For 2019 part (i) give the definition and two examples (gravitation and electrostatic Coulomb force; the spring force −κ r is a third).

## 2017 (angular momentum of a circular-orbit satellite)

For a circular orbit of radius r under F = −k/r²: m v²/r = k/r², so K = ½ m v² = k/(2r), U = −k/r = −2K and E = K + U = −K. Then L = m v r = r √(2 m K), and since U = −2K and E = −K,

$$ L = r √(2 m K) = r √(−m U) = r √(−2 m E) $$

(all positive roots; U and E are negative). Cross-check: L = √(m k r). The question asks the three forms; give all three and the check.

## 2021 (find the force law of a given path)

The question gives r = k e^(αθ) (rename its constants: r = c e^(αφ) here, so that k stays the force constant). Then u = e^(−αφ)/c, u'' = α² u, u'' + u = (1 + α²) u, and

$$ F = −(L² u²/m)(1 + α²) u = −(1 + α²) L²/(m r³) $$

an attractive inverse-cube force, with the same value for every c.

## 2022 (the electron's orbit is an ellipse)

Assume a classical electron of charge −q_e bound to a fixed nucleus Z q_e, only the Coulomb force F = −k/r² with k = Z q_e²/(4π ε₀). Binet gives r = ℓ/(1 + e cos φ) with e = √(1 + 2 E L²/(m k²)). For a bound electron E < 0, so e < 1: an ellipse (a circle if e = 0), with the nucleus at a focus. (For E ≥ 0 the orbit is a parabola or hyperbola: the electron is not bound.) Mention that for a finite nucleus mass m is replaced by the reduced mass.

## 2026 (two-dimensional force F = −k r)

Here k is the spring constant. J = r × p is conserved since r × F = 0. The polar equations are m(r̈ − r φ̇²) = −k r and m(r φ̈ + 2 ṙ φ̇) = 0; the second gives J = m r² φ̇, and U_eff = ½ k r² + J²/(2 m r²) (full steps in derivation PH-003-D2).

## Traps

Mixing the two k's; forgetting the sign convention; treating the centrifugal term as a real force; dividing by u' when u' = 0 in the energy route; assuming every conic is an ellipse.

### Notes: Definitions

- Central force about O: F = F(r) r̂, directed along the line from a fixed point O and depending only on r = |r|; F(r) < 0 attractive, F(r) > 0 repulsive.
- Angular momentum L = r × p = m r × v; areal velocity dA/dt = ½ |r × v| = L/(2m) = ½ r² φ̇.
- Effective potential U_eff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2 m r²); the second term is the centrifugal barrier, the kinetic energy of the angular motion written through L.
- Turning point: a radius where ṙ = 0, i.e. E = U_eff(r). Apsis: a turning point of the orbit (perihelion/aphelion, perigee/apogee).
- Semi-latus rectum ℓ = L²/(m k); eccentricity e; semi-major axis a = ℓ/(1 − e²); semi-minor axis b = a√(1 − e²).
- Bound orbit: r stays finite (E < 0 for inverse-square); escape speed: minimum speed with E = 0.

### Notes: Governing equations

- Polar acceleration: a = (r̈ − r φ̇²) r̂ + (r φ̈ + 2 ṙ φ̇) φ̂.
- Radial equation: m r̈ = F(r) + L²/(m r³) = −dU_eff/dr; energy E = ½ m ṙ² + U_eff(r).
- Orbit (Binet) equation: u'' + u = −(m/(L² u²)) F(1/u), u = 1/r, prime = d/dφ.
- Force from orbit: F = −(L² u²/m)(u'' + u).
- Inverse-square: r = ℓ/(1 + e cos φ), ℓ = L²/(m k), e = √(1 + 2 E L²/(m k²)).
- Kepler orbit: E = −k/(2a); v² = (k/m)(2/r − 1/a); T² = 4π² m a³/k (gravity: 4π² a³/(G M)).
- Apsides: r_p = a(1 − e), r_a = a(1 + e); v_p/v_a = (1 + e)/(1 − e); v_esc = √(2GM/r) = √2 v_circ.
- Stability: U_eff''(r₀) = −F'(r₀) − 3F(r₀)/r₀; for F = −K/rⁿ stable iff n < 3; ω_r² = (3 − n) ω_φ²; apsidal angle π/√(3 − n).

### Notes: Orbit classification for the inverse-square force

- Energy decides the shape; angular momentum decides how far E can go below zero.

### Notes: What each Kepler law needs

- Second law (equal areas): any central force; it is the statement L = constant.
- First law (ellipse, Sun at a focus): inverse-square force and E < 0.
- Third law (T² ∝ a³): inverse-square force; the constant 4π²/(GM) is the same for all planets of one Sun when the planet's mass is negligible.
- A force F = −κ r (Hooke) also gives closed elliptical orbits, but with the force centre at the centre of the ellipse and with four apsides per revolution.

### Notes: Derivation summary: the chain of results

- F = F(r) r̂ → τ = r × F = 0 → L constant → r · L = 0 → planar motion; dA/dt = L/(2m).
- Newton in polar form → m r̈ = F + L²/(m r³) → E = ½ m ṙ² + U_eff.
- Circular orbit: U_eff' = 0; stable if U_eff'' > 0, i.e. n < 3 for F ∝ r⁻ⁿ.
- Put u = 1/r: Binet equation; for F = −k/r² it is linear: u'' + u = m k/L².
- Solution r = ℓ/(1 + e cos φ); energy at the apsis gives e² = 1 + 2 E L²/(m k²).
- T = area/areal velocity → T² = 4π² m a³/k; E = −k/(2a); vis-viva follows.

### Notes: Worked results to remember

- Satellite 400 km above Earth: v = 7.67 km/s, T = 92.4 min, E = −K = −U/2.
- Escape speed from Earth's surface ≈ 11.2 km/s (11.19 with GM and R); √2 times the near-surface circular speed 7.91 km/s.
- Comet with q = 0.60 AU, Q = 30 AU: a = 15.3 AU, e = 0.961, T = 59.8 yr, v_p/v_a = 50.
- Orbit r = c e^(αφ): F = −(1 + α²) L²/(m r³). Orbit r = 2a cos φ (centre of force on the circle): F = −8 a² L²/(m r⁵).
- Spring F = −k r: U_eff = ½ k r² + J²/(2 m r²), minimum at r₀⁴ = J²/(m k), value J √(k/m).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Centrifugal 'force' in U_eff is a bookkeeping term of the inertial frame (kinetic energy of angular motion); it becomes a real-looking force only in a rotating frame (PH-002).
- A turning point of r is not a point where the particle stops: only ṙ = 0; the transverse speed L/(m r) is not zero.
- A speed above the circular speed does not mean escape: escape needs v ≥ √2 v_circ.
- Conservation of angular momentum does not need an inverse-square force; it needs only that the force is central. The inverse-square form is needed for closed ellipses and for the third law.
- The constant k of F = −k/r² and the constant in r = k e^(αθ) (2021) or the spring constant (2026) are different things; rename before substituting.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- L = 0: the motion is radial (a straight line through O); the orbit equation does not apply.
- n = 3 is marginal for stability; for n > 3 circular orbits are unstable.
- Apsidal angle π/√(3 − n) is valid only for nearly circular orbits.
- Two-body problems: replace m by the reduced mass μ and the orbit of each body is about the centre of mass (PH-009); the Sun-planet case is the limit M ≫ m.
- A real planetary orbit is not exactly closed: other planets and general relativity cause precession.

### Core concepts

- Central force F(r) r̂ about a fixed centre; sign convention F < 0 attractive.
- Conservation of L, planar motion and constant areal velocity all follow from r × F = 0.
- Effective potential U_eff = U + L²/(2 m r²) reduces the problem to motion along r; turning points at E = U_eff.
- Circular orbits at U_eff' = 0 are stable when U_eff'' > 0 (n < 3 for F ∝ r⁻ⁿ).
- Binet equation u'' + u = −m F(1/u)/(L² u²) connects orbit and force.
- Inverse-square force gives conics with e = √(1 + 2 E L²/(m k²)); Kepler's laws are consequences.
