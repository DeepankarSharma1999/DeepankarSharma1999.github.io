## GS-134: Manufacturing industries and clustering

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Geography · Human/economic/world

*Goal:* Explain Manufacturing industries and clustering and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### What modern manufacturing looks like

Manufacturing adds value by transforming raw materials - cotton into yarn, iron ore into steel. Modern large-scale manufacturing combines specialised skills and mass production of standardised parts, mechanisation advancing to automation, R and D-driven innovation, and complex organisation needing large capital and bureaucracies. Its distribution is very uneven: a few regions covering under a tenth of the world's land host the major concentrations, and a factory district packs far more workers into a given area than farmland does. Large-scale manufacturing grew over the last two centuries in the United Kingdom, north-eastern USA and Europe and has since diffused widely. NCERT separates traditional heavy-industry regions in developed countries from high-technology regions spreading into less developed ones.

### Why clusters form and persist

Location factors - market, raw material, labour, energy, transport, policy - work together, but clustering adds an extra force. When a leader industry is established, suppliers and related industries gather to reduce the cost of linkages; these are agglomeration economies. Transport improvements encourage regional specialisation, and western Europe and eastern North America show how dense transport systems have long drawn industry. Indian examples show the same tendency to concentrate: NCERT locates the automobile industry around centres such as Delhi, Gurugram, Pune and Chennai, and places the main IT concentrations in Bengaluru, Noida, Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad and Pune.

### Footloose and high-tech: new locational logic

Footloose industries do not depend on a particular raw material, weight-losing or otherwise; they assemble components, employ few people, usually pollute little and choose sites mainly for road access. High-technology industry goes further: products of advanced scientific and engineering character, robotics and computer-aided design on the line, constant development of chemicals and pharmaceuticals, and a workforce dominated by white-collar specialists. Its landscape is low, dispersed office-plant-lab buildings rather than massive sheds, and planned business parks for start-ups now feature in regional plans. Self-sustaining specialised clusters are technopolies; Silicon Valley and Silicon Forest are NCERT's examples, and the textbook invites readers to ask whether Indian technopolies are emerging.
