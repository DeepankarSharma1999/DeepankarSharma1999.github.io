### Bridge: Path difference, phase difference and when two beams reinforce

**Phase from path.** A wave of vacuum wavelength λ₀ gains a phase of 2π for every λ₀ of optical path (PH-043 bridge B2). Two beams that left a common point in phase and arrive with an optical path difference Δ therefore have a phase difference

$$ δ = 2π Δ / λ₀ $$

**Adding two beams.** For amplitudes a₁ and a₂ the resultant amplitude obeys A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos δ (PH-038 module 3), and the intensity is proportional to A². With equal amplitudes a: A² = 2a²(1 + cos δ) = 4a² cos²(δ/2). Hence

- **Maxima** (constructive, bright) when δ = 2mπ, that is Δ = mλ₀, with m = 0, 1, 2, ...
- **Minima** (destructive, dark) when δ = (2m + 1)π, that is Δ = (m + ½)λ₀.

**One extra rule.** These conditions assume the two beams start in phase. If one of them suffers an extra phase jump of π on reflection (equivalent to an extra path of λ₀/2), maxima and minima exchange places. Bridge B2 says when that happens. Adding or removing a whole number of wavelengths never changes the interference, so (m + 1)λ may be renamed mλ.

**Coherence.** Two beams interfere steadily only if they come from the same wave train and the path difference is smaller than the coherence length of the light. In a thin film the two reflected beams are two parts of one incident ray, which is why the film is a source of coherent beams (module 2).

**Unit drill.** 1 µm = 1000 nm = 10 000 Å. A path difference of 1.5 µm = 15 000 Å = 1500 nm.

### Bridge: Phase change on reflection: when does a half-wave appear?

**The rule.** When a wave is reflected at the boundary of a rarer medium with a denser medium (lower to higher refractive index), the reflected wave is shifted by π in phase, equivalent to a path change of λ/2 (Brij Lal PDF p. 359, where it is called a path change of λ/2 for the ray reflected at the top of the film). When it is reflected at a boundary leading to a lower index there is no jump. A **transmitted** wave has no jump at either boundary (PDF p. 362).

**A familiar parallel.** A pulse on a string reflected at a fixed end returns inverted, a phase change of π (PH-040 module 3); at a free end it returns unchanged. A heavy medium behaves like a fixed end, a light medium like a free end.

**Amplitude form (standard result at normal incidence; verify).** The amplitude reflection coefficient from medium 1 into medium 2 is

$$ r = (n₁ − n₂) / (n₁ + n₂) $$

When n₂ > n₁, r is negative: the reflected wave is inverted, which is the π jump. When n₂ < n₁, r is positive. The reflected **intensity** fraction is R = r². For air to glass of index 1.52, R = (0.52/2.52)² = 0.0426, the 4% figure Brij Lal quotes on PDF p. 357.

**Four cases for a film of index n_f between a medium above (n₁) and below (n₃).**

| Case | Top surface | Bottom surface | Net extra path in reflection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soap film or glass plate in air | air to film: π | film to air: none | λ/2 |
| Oil (n = 1.45) on water (n = 1.33) | air to oil: π | oil to water, a lower index: none | λ/2 |
| Anti-reflection coating: air, n_f = 1.38, glass 1.5 | air to coating: π | coating to glass, a higher index: π | none |
| Air film between two glass plates (wedge, Newton's rings) | glass to air: none | air to glass: π | λ/2 |

In each reflected-light formula of this lesson, "−λ/2" (or "+λ/2", the same by a whole wave) appears exactly when the net extra path is λ/2; it is dropped when the net is zero.

**Transmitted beams.** For a film in air, the transmitted beam that has been reflected twice inside (once at each face, both times from film to air) suffers no phase jump (Brij Lal PDF p. 362), so bright transmitted fringes satisfy 2μt cos r = mλ, the complement of the reflected case (module 4).

### 1. Orientation: what thin-film interference asks, vocabulary and the plan

*Objective:* Say what a thin film is, name the four verified questions and the type of answer each needs, and fix the vocabulary and units used in the lesson.

## What this topic asks

Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam (PDF p. 356) call an optical medium a **thin film** when its thickness is about the order of one wavelength of visible light, roughly 0.5 µm to 10 µm: a sheet of glass or mica, an air film between two plates, a soap bubble. When light falls on such a film a small part is reflected from the top surface and the rest enters; part of that is reflected from the bottom surface, and so on. The reflected beams overlap and **interfere**. Because each reflection divides one wave into a reflected and a transmitted part, this is **interference by division of amplitude** (PDF p. 357).

### 2. Foundation: two beams from one ray, division of amplitude and the limits on the film

*Objective:* Explain why only the first two reflected beams matter for a transparent film, contrast division of amplitude with division of wavefront, and state why an extended source and a thin film are needed.

## Why two beams are enough

Light striking a transparent film is mostly transmitted and only weakly reflected. Brij Lal (PDF p. 357) works the case of a glass plate of index 1.52, whose top surface reflects (0.52/2.52)² = 4.3% of the intensity. Recomputed without the book's rounding: the first reflected beam carries 4.26% of the incident intensity; the second beam (transmitted in, reflected at the bottom surface, transmitted out) carries (1 − 0.0426)² × 0.0426 = 3.90%; the third beam carries (1 − 0.0426)² × 0.0426³ = 0.0071%. (The book rounds R to 4%: 3.8% is reflected at the bottom face, of which 0.15% is re-reflected inside and 3.65% emerges; its 0.15% is internal re-reflection, not the third emerging beam, so it is not compared with 0.0071%.) So the first two beams have nearly equal amplitudes, in the ratio 0.957, and every further beam is negligible. Equal amplitudes give nearly perfect darkness at minima. The treatment of many beams, needed when the reflectivity is large, is in module 8.

## Division of amplitude

At each reflection the amplitude of the incident wave is divided into a reflected part and a transmitted part; the parts travel different paths and overlap again. Brij Lal (PDF p. 357) therefore calls thin-film interference **interference by division of amplitude**. The two reflected beams are coherent because they derive from the same incident ray.

**Contrast with division of wavefront** (standard knowledge, not defined in this extract; verify): in Young's double slit, Fresnel's biprism or Lloyd's mirror two different parts of the same wavefront are made to overlap, and the fringes need a narrow source. Brij Lal (PDF p. 360) notes that those experiments use a narrow initial source, unlike the thin film.

## Narrow versus extended source

With a narrow source only one portion of the film gives rays that reach the eye, so only a small area is seen. An **extended (broad) source** sends rays at many angles, so rays from different source points, reflected from the upper and lower surfaces, reach the eye from every part of the film and the whole film is seen (Brij Lal PDF pp. 360 to 361).

## Why the film must be thin

Light consists of finite wave trains. Interference occurs only when the two reflected parts belong to the same wave train, i.e. when the optical path difference is smaller than the coherence length l_coh = λ²/Δλ (Brij Lal Eq. 15.11 and the relation cited from his §16.3). For normal incidence this gives, in the book's rounding, a thickness limit of order t < λ²/(2μΔλ) (PDF p. 362, Eq. 15.14).

*Numbers (recomputed):* a spectral line with λ = 589 nm and Δλ = 1 nm has l_coh = 589²/1 nm = 0.35 mm, so for μ = 1.5 the limit is t < l_coh/(2μ) ≈ 0.12 mm. For white light, with Δλ of order 300 nm at 550 nm, l_coh ≈ 1 µm and t < 0.34 µm. Brij Lal's figure of a few hundred microns for ordinary sources (PDF p. 362) applies to quasi-monochromatic line sources such as a sodium lamp; white light needs films of a few wavelengths.

## Beginner traps

- Reflectivity of a transparent film is small, so the transmitted beam is the strong one.
- "Thin" is measured against the wavelength and the coherence length, not in everyday terms: a millimetre of glass is thick for ordinary light.

### 3. Core: path difference and fringe conditions for a plane parallel film in reflected light

*Objective:* Derive Δ = 2μt cos r for the two reflected beams, add the half-wave correction, and use the resulting conditions for brightness and darkness with the correct case of phase change.

## Set-up and assumptions

A transparent film of uniform thickness t and refractive index μ lies in air (index 1). Plane monochromatic waves fall on it at angle of incidence i (Brij Lal Fig. 15.3, PDF p. 358). The ray AB meets the top surface at B; part is reflected along BC, and part refracts into the film at angle r (Snell's law sin i = μ sin r), travels to the bottom surface at F, is reflected, and emerges at D along DE. Since the faces are parallel, BC and DE are parallel; the beams travel equal paths from H and D onwards.

## Derivation

1. The beam 2 path inside the film: in triangle BFD the two sides are equal, BF = FD = t / cos r, so BF + FD = 2t / cos r. Optical path in the film: μ(BF + FD) = 2μt / cos r.
2. The foot G of the normal from F gives BG = GD = t tan r, so BD = 2t tan r.
3. Beam 1 goes from B along BC. Draw DH perpendicular to BC; from H and D onwards the beams travel equal paths. The beam 1 path to H is BH = BD sin i = 2t tan r sin i.
4. Use Snell's law sin i = μ sin r: BH = 2μt sin²r / cos r. Optical path of beam 1: 1 × BH.
5. The difference of optical paths:

$$ Δ = μ(BF + FD) − BH = 2μt/cos r − 2μt sin²r/cos r = 2μt (1 − sin²r)/cos r = 2μt cos r   (Eq. 15.5) $$

6. **Phase-change correction** (bridge B2). For a film in air, beam 1 is reflected at an air-to-film boundary and gains π (equivalent to λ/2); beam 2 is reflected at a film-to-air boundary and does not. Therefore the effective path difference is

$$ Δ_eff = 2μt cos r − λ/2   (Eq. 15.6) $$

(+λ/2 is equally correct.)

*Checks:* every term is a length; normal incidence gives Δ = 2μt; Δ decreases as r increases.

## Conditions (film in air)

$$ Brightness (maxima):   2μt cos r = (2m + 1) λ/2 ,   m = 0, 1, 2, ...   (Eq. 15.8) $$
$$ Darkness (minima):   2μt cos r = m λ ,   m = 1, 2, 3, ...   (Eq. 15.10) $$

Brij Lal derives darkness as 2μt cos r − λ/2 = (2m + 1)λ/2, i.e. 2μt cos r = (m + 1)λ, and then renames (m + 1) as m. For a film of finite thickness m = 0 would give t = 0, so it is excluded. For other phase cases (bridge B2) the conditions exchange: with no net λ/2 (an anti-reflection coating on glass) brightness needs 2μt cos r = mλ and darkness (2m + 1)λ/2.

For a parallel film in a parallel beam only λ varies (Brij Lal PDF p. 360): monochromatic light shows the whole film uniformly bright or dark; in white light the wavelengths with 2μt cos r = mλ are absent and the film shows the others.

## Numbers (recomputed)

Soap film, μ = 1.33, t = 300 nm, normal viewing: 2μt = 798 nm. Bright at λ = 1596/(2m + 1) nm: m = 0 gives 1596 nm (infrared), m = 1 gives 532 nm (green), m = 2 gives 319 nm (ultraviolet). Dark at λ = 798/m nm: 798 nm (m = 1, infrared) and 399 nm (m = 2, violet edge). The film looks green with violet weakened.

*Limiting case:* t → 0 (much thinner than λ/(2μ)) gives Δ_eff → −λ/2: the beams are in antiphase and the film looks dark, as for a very thin soap film just before it bursts (standard observation; verify).

### 4. Core: transmitted light, colours, and fringes of equal inclination versus equal thickness

*Objective:* Derive the transmitted-light conditions, explain why they are complementary and weak, explain the colours of thin films numerically, and distinguish fringes of equal inclination (Haidinger) from fringes of equal thickness (Fizeau).

## Transmitted light

Brij Lal (PDF p. 362) takes a film in air and compares the beam that passes straight through the bottom face (BR) with the beam that is reflected at the bottom face and again at the top face from inside, then emerges (DQ). Both internal reflections are from film to air, so neither beam gains a π jump (bridge B2). The geometry is that of module 3: the delayed beam goes down, up and out, with film path μ(BC + CD) = 2μt/cos r, while the straight beam covers BN = 2μt sin²r/cos r in air (N is the foot of the perpendicular from D on BR). Subtracting, as before, gives

$$ Δ = 2μt cos r   (Eq. 15.15) $$

with no λ/2 term. Hence

- **Bright:** 2μt cos r = mλ (m = 0, 1, 2, ...; Eq. 15.16).
- **Dark:** 2μt cos r = (2m + 1) λ/2 (Eq. 15.17).

**Complementarity.** The reflected bright condition (2m + 1)λ/2 is the transmitted dark condition, and the reflected dark condition mλ is the transmitted bright one. This is energy conservation for a non-absorbing film: reflected and transmitted intensities add to the incident intensity (proved in module 8).

**Why transmitted fringes are faint.** For glass of index 1.52 the intensities are (1 − R)² = 0.917 and (1 − R)²R² = 0.0017, so the amplitude ratio is R = 0.043 and the fringe visibility V = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin) = 2R/(1 + R²) = 0.085. For the reflected pair the ratio is 0.957 and V = 0.999. Brij Lal says transmitted fringes are less distinct for this reason (PDF p. 363).

## Colours of thin films in white light

Soap bubbles, oil on water and oxide layers on heated metal show colours in reflection (Brij Lal PDF p. 368, credited to Young). White light is a mixture of wavelengths. At a given thickness t and angle r only the wavelengths meeting 2μt cos r = (2m + 1)λ/2 are strongly reflected; those meeting 2μt cos r = mλ are removed. Since t and r vary over the film, different colours are strong at different places, and each place carries a mixture, not a pure colour.

*Example (Brij Lal Example 15.1, recomputed):* a soap film of t = 500 nm and μ = 1.33 viewed at 35° has 2μt cos r = 1200 nm, so 600 nm and 400 nm are absent from the reflected light. The book prints 12.0 × 10⁻⁵ cm as "120 µm"; it is 1.2 µm, a source slip (full working in masterNotes).

## Equal inclination and equal thickness

Take the differential of Δ = 2μt cos r at fixed μ:

$$ δΔ = 2μ cos r δt − 2μ t sin r δr $$

- **Equal inclination (Haidinger) fringes**: the film has uniform thickness (δt = 0), so the path difference changes only through r. Each fringe joins rays of the same inclination; the beams from one incident ray are parallel, so the fringes are localised at infinity and seen with a telescope focused for parallel rays. They need an extended source and a film that is not too thin; they form concentric circles about the point where r is smallest (Brij Lal PDF pp. 363 to 364).
- **Equal thickness (Fizeau) fringes**: the film is very thin and almost the same ray inclination is used (δr ≈ 0), so the path difference changes with t. Each fringe joins points of equal thickness (wedge, Newton's rings), localised at or near the film (PDF pp. 367, 368).

### 5. Core: the wedge-shaped film, fringe width, wedge angle and spacer thickness

*Objective:* Derive the fringe width β = λ/(2μθ) for a thin wedge, list the five features of the pattern with reasons, and use fringe counts to find the wedge angle and the thickness of a spacer.

## The wedge and its path difference

A **wedge** is a film that has zero thickness at one end and increases steadily to a chosen thickness at the other. A thin air wedge is made from two glass plates resting on each other at one edge and separated by a spacer at the other (Brij Lal PDF pp. 364 to 365). The wedge angle θ is very small, a fraction of a degree. Under a parallel monochromatic beam from above, the beam reflected at the upper glass-to-air boundary and the beam reflected at the lower air-to-glass boundary come from one incident ray, so they are coherent. The optical path difference is

$$ Δ = 2μt cos r − λ/2   (PDF p. 365) $$

The λ/2 arises because the lower reflection is air to glass (π jump) and the upper is glass to air (none): the fourth row of the case table in bridge B2. At nearly normal incidence cos r ≈ 1.

## Conditions and fringe width

Darkness needs Δ = (2m + 1)λ/2, that is **2μt = mλ** (m = 0, 1, 2, ...). Brightness needs 2μt = (m + ½)λ. Let a dark fringe at A (distance x₁ from the edge) have thickness t₁ with 2μt₁ = mλ (Eq. 15.19), and the next dark fringe at C have t₂ with 2μt₂ = (m + 1)λ (Eq. 15.20). Subtracting:

$$ 2μ(t₂ − t₁) = λ   so   t₂ − t₁ = λ/(2μ) $$

The difference of thickness over the separation β of the fringes is t₂ − t₁ = β tan θ, so

$$ β = λ/(2μ tan θ) ≈ λ/(2μθ)   (Eqs. 15.24, 15.25) $$

since tan θ ≈ θ for small θ. *Dimension check:* β is a length (λ over a dimensionless number). *Limits:* θ → 0 makes β large and the fringes vanish as the faces become parallel; for θ near 1° the fringes are too close to resolve (Brij Lal PDF p. 366).

## The five features

1. **Dark at the edge:** t = 0 gives Δ = −λ/2, antiphase.
2. **Straight and parallel:** each fringe joins points of equal thickness, which lie on a line parallel to the contact edge.
3. **Equidistant:** β = λ/(2μθ) is constant for fixed λ, μ, θ.
4. **Localised** near the top surface of the wedge; seen with a microscope focused there.
5. **Fringes of equal thickness** (module 4).

## Wedge angle and spacer thickness

Count N dark fringes between two points at distances x₁ and x₂ from the edge. From 2μx₁θ = mλ and 2μx₂θ = (m + N)λ:

$$ θ = Nλ / (2μ (x₂ − x₁))   (Eq. 15.31) $$

If the spacer is at distance l from the edge, its thickness is t = lθ = Nlλ / (2μ(x₂ − x₁)) (Eq. 15.34). Equivalently, the number of dark fringes between the edge and the spacer is N_total = 2μt/λ, so the spacer thickness is t = N_total λ/(2μ).

*Numbers:* for β = 0.50 mm, λ = 589 nm and μ = 1 the wedge angle is θ = λ/(2β) = 5.89 × 10⁻⁴ rad, and a wire at l = 10 cm gives t = lθ = 58.9 µm (check: 200 dark fringes × λ/2 = 58.9 µm). Brij Lal Examples 15.2 and 15.3 (the printed 3 mm should be 0.30 mm) are worked in masterNotes.

*Use:* an air wedge between an optical flat and a surface gives straight equidistant fringes if the surface is flat; bending of the fringes shows hills or hollows (Brij Lal PDF p. 392).

### 6. Core: Newton's rings, geometry, radii of the dark and bright rings

*Objective:* Derive r² = 2Rt and the radii and diameters of the dark and bright rings in reflected light, explain the dark centre and the crowding of rings, and give the transmitted-light version.

## The arrangement

A plano-convex lens of large radius of curvature R (about 1 m in the experiment) lies convex face down on a flat glass plate and is lit from above by a parallel monochromatic beam sent downward by a glass plate inclined at 45° (Brij Lal PDF p. 369). The air film between lens and plate is zero thick at the contact point O and thickens outwards, so points of equal thickness lie on circles about O. Two beams, one reflected at the lens-to-air boundary and one at the air-to-plate boundary, interfere (division of amplitude). The fringes are therefore concentric rings: Newton's rings, fringes of equal thickness.

## Path difference and conditions

From module 5 with μ = 1 for air and cos r = 1 for normal incidence,

$$ Δ = 2t − λ/2   (Eq. 15.35) $$

The lens-to-air reflection has no jump and the air-to-plate reflection has a π jump (bridge B2, last row). Brightness needs Δ = mλ, so 2t = (2m + 1)λ/2 (Eq. 15.36), m = 0, 1, 2, ...; darkness needs Δ = (2m + 1)λ/2, so **2t = mλ** (Eq. 15.37), m = 0, 1, 2, ... At the centre t = 0, m = 0: the centre is dark (PDF p. 372, the two beams are in antiphase).

## Geometry: thickness against radius

Let the ring have radius r_m at a point where the film thickness is t. The lens surface is a sphere of radius R. In the right triangle with hypotenuse R (the radius of the lens surface), one side r_m and the other R − t (Brij Lal Fig. 15.17):

$$ R² = r_m² + (R − t)²   so   r_m² = 2Rt − t² $$

Since t is tiny compared with R, t² is negligible against 2Rt, giving r_m² ≈ 2Rt (Eq. 15.39). (For R = 1 m and t = 1 µm the neglected term is 5 × 10⁻⁷ of 2Rt.)

## Radii and diameters of the dark rings

With 2t = mλ, r_m² = mλR:

$$ r_m = √(mλR)   ,   D_m = 2 r_m = √(4mλR)   (Eqs. 15.40, 15.41) $$

The radii (and diameters) are proportional to √m, the square roots of the natural numbers 1, 1.41, 1.73, 2, ..., and to √λ (red rings are larger than blue). The diameters 1 : √2 : √3 : 2 are not equally spaced, so the rings crowd together outwards (Brij Lal PDF p. 371).

## Bright rings (reflected light)

2t = (2m + 1)λ/2 gives r² = (2m + 1)λR/2, or D² = 2(2m + 1)λR, with m = 0, 1, 2, ... for the first, second, third bright ring. Check: the first bright ring lies at t = λ/4 and the first dark ring at t = λ/2, so the first bright ring is inside the first dark ring.

## Transmitted light

In transmitted light the pattern is complementary (Brij Lal PDF p. 374): bright rings satisfy 2t = mλ and dark rings 2t = (2m + 1)λ/2, so the centre is **bright** and r_m² = mλR now refers to the bright rings.

## Numbers

*Dimension check:* D² = 4mλR has dimension length². *Limit:* R → ∞ (a plane face in contact with the plate) leaves a gap of zero thickness everywhere: there are no rings and the whole field takes the dark centre value (Δ = −λ/2).

### 7. Core: Newton's rings measurements, a liquid in the gap, two curved surfaces and the 2019 numerical

*Objective:* Use Newton's rings to find λ, R or μ; explain why the difference of squared diameters is used; and solve the problem of coincident dark rings for two wavelengths.

## Measuring the wavelength

Brij Lal (PDF pp. 372 to 373): a lens of radius about 1 m on a plate, a sodium lamp, light reflected down by a plate at 45°, and a travelling microscope reading the dark rings on each side of the centre. D_m² is plotted against m for rings such as the 5th to the 19th; the line has slope 4λR (Eq. 15.45), giving λ if R is known (spherometer), or R if λ is known.

**Why the difference.** At the contact point there may be dust or a flattened patch, so the film has an extra thickness ε there: 2(r²/2R + ε) = mλ, hence D_m² = 4mλR − 8Rε. The line does not pass through the origin, but its slope is unaffected. Equivalently, for rings p apart,

$$ D²_(m+p) − D²_m = 4pλR   so   λ = (D²_(m+p) − D²_m) / (4pR)   (Eq. 15.44) $$

*Brij Lal Examples 15.5 to 15.8* (λ = 5880 Å from D₁₅ and D₅; R = 106 cm; μ = 1.215) are worked in masterNotes.

## A liquid in the gap

If a liquid of index μ fills the gap, 2μt = mλ, so r_m² = mλR/μ and D_m² = 4mλR/μ (Eq. 15.46). The ratio of the squared diameters of the same ring in air and in the liquid is

$$ μ = D²_(m, air) / D²_(m, liquid)   (Eq. 15.50) $$

The rings shrink in the liquid because the optical thickness for a given geometric thickness has increased.

## Two curved surfaces and a bright centre

For two spheres in contact at O with radii R₁ (upper, convex downwards) and R₂ (lower), the film thickness at radius r is the difference of the two sags when the lower surface is concave (R₂ > R₁ needed), t = r²/2R₁ − r²/2R₂, and the sum when it is convex, t = r²/2R₁ + r²/2R₂ (Brij Lal PDF pp. 374 to 375). With 2t = mλ for dark rings (m = 0, 1, 2, ...):

$$ r²(1/R₁ ∓ 1/R₂) = mλ   (Eqs. 15.54, 15.56, with − for a concave lower surface and + for a convex one) $$

**Bright centre.** If a liquid fills the gap and its index lies between those of the lens and the plate, then for n_lens < n_liquid < n_plate both reflections are from lower to higher index and both gain π; for n_lens > n_liquid > n_plate neither gains a jump. Either way the net jump is zero and the centre is bright (standard physics; Brij Lal poses the question on PDF p. 400; verify). Transmitted light also gives a bright centre (module 6).

## The 2019 problem: coincident dark rings

Two wavelengths λ₁ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m and λ₂ = 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ m, R = 90 cm. The m-th dark ring for λ₁ coincides with the (m + 1)-th dark ring for λ₂ (same radius). Since λ₁ > λ₂, the rings of λ₁ are larger, so the m-th ring of λ₁ must meet a ring of higher order of λ₂. Dark rings: r² = mλ₁R = (m + 1)λ₂R.

1. m(λ₁ − λ₂) = λ₂, so m = λ₂/(λ₁ − λ₂) = 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ / 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ = 59.
2. r² = mλ₁R = λ₁λ₂R/(λ₁ − λ₂) (the general result Brij Lal asks to show on PDF p. 400).
3. D² = 4r² = 4 × 59 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.90 = 1.2744 × 10⁻⁴ m², so D = 1.129 × 10⁻² m = 1.13 cm.
4. Check with λ₂: 4 × 60 × 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.90 = 1.2744 × 10⁻⁴ ✓.

*Limit:* λ₁ → λ₂ makes m infinite: the rings of the two wavelengths never separate by a whole ring. (The answer to the application is in masterNotes.)

### 8. Core: multiple reflections from a plane parallel film and the total reflectivity (the 2025 derivation)

*Objective:* Derive the total reflectivity of a plane parallel film by summing all multiply reflected beams with the Stokes relations, reduce it to F sin²(δ/2)/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)), and check it against the two-beam result and energy conservation.

## Set-up and notation

A film of thickness h and index n₂ lies between media of indices n₁ (above) and n₃ (below). A wave of amplitude a meets the top face at angle θ₁ (refracted angle θ₂ inside). Write r₁, t₁ for the amplitude coefficients at the top face for light going n₁ to n₂, r₁′, t₁′ for the reverse direction (inside the film), and r₂ for reflection from n₂ into n₃ at the bottom face. The **phase** gained in one round trip inside the film is, from Δ = 2n₂h cos θ₂ (module 3),

$$ δ = 2π (2 n₂ h cos θ₂) / λ₀ = 4π n₂ h cos θ₂ / λ₀ $$

Brij Lal (PDF pp. 386 to 388) quotes the result (Eqs. 15.71, 15.74); the reflected derivation is written out below, with the π jumps built into r₁, r₂.

## Stokes relations

Take a wave of amplitude a meeting an interface from medium 1, giving a reflected wave ar and a transmitted wave at. Reverse the motion of both: a wave ar returns from side 1 and a wave at from side 2. They must rebuild the reversed incident wave alone. Medium 1 side: ar·r + at·t′ = a, so **t t′ = 1 − r²**. Medium 2 side: ar·t + at·r′ = 0, so **r′ = −r**. Hence r₁′ = −r₁.

## The sum of all reflected beams

Beam 1 is the reflection at the top face: a r₁. Beam 2 enters (t₁), is reflected at the bottom (r₂), and leaves (t₁′), with delay δ: a t₁t₁′ r₂ e^(−iδ). Each further round trip adds a reflection at the top face from inside (r₁′ = −r₁) and again at the bottom (r₂) and the delay δ, a factor −r₁r₂e^(−iδ). So

$$ E_R/a = r₁ + t₁t₁′ r₂ e^(−iδ) [1 + (−r₁r₂e^(−iδ)) + (−r₁r₂e^(−iδ))² + ...] $$

The bracket is a geometric series with ratio of modulus |r₁r₂| < 1, summing to 1/(1 + r₁r₂e^(−iδ)). With t₁t₁′ = 1 − r₁²:

$$ E_R/a = [r₁(1 + r₁r₂e^(−iδ)) + (1 − r₁²) r₂ e^(−iδ)] / (1 + r₁r₂e^(−iδ)) = (r₁ + r₂ e^(−iδ)) / (1 + r₁r₂ e^(−iδ)) $$

## Total reflectivity

The reflectivity is |E_R/a|². Using |r₁ + r₂e^(−iδ)|² = r₁² + r₂² + 2r₁r₂ cos δ and |1 + r₁r₂e^(−iδ)|² = 1 + r₁²r₂² + 2r₁r₂ cos δ:

$$ ρ_R = (r₁² + r₂² + 2r₁r₂ cos δ) / (1 + r₁²r₂² + 2r₁r₂ cos δ) $$

**Identical media above and below** (n₃ = n₁, so r₂ = −r₁ ≡ −r and R = r² is the single-surface reflectivity): the numerator is 2R(1 − cos δ) = 4R sin²(δ/2), and the denominator 1 + R² − 2R cos δ = (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2). Therefore

$$ ρ_R = F sin²(δ/2) / (1 + F sin²(δ/2)) ,   F = 4R/(1 − R)²   (Brij Lal Eqs. 15.72, 15.74) $$

The transmitted fraction is ρ_T = 1/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)) (Eq. 15.73) and **ρ_R + ρ_T = 1**: energy is conserved.

## Checks and limits

- **Small R:** ρ_R ≈ 4R sin²(δ/2), the two-beam result of module 3.
- **Maxima** at δ = (2m + 1)π, i.e. 2n₂h cos θ₂ = (m + ½)λ₀, the bright condition of module 3; value 4R/(1 + R)². **Minima** (zero reflection) at δ = 2mπ.

## Numbers

Glass plate, n₂ = 1.52 in air: R = 0.04258, F = 0.1858; ρ_R = 0.1567 at δ = π (= 4R/(1 + R)²), 0.0850 at δ = π/2 and 0 at δ = 2π; the two-beam value 4R = 0.170 overestimates by 9%. The coefficients r = (n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂) are normal-incidence values; oblique incidence depends on polarisation (a later topic). Further checks: masterNotes.

### 9. Core: anti-reflection and high-reflection coatings, quarter-wave films and multilayers

*Objective:* Derive the quarter-wave thickness and the index condition for an anti-reflection coating, explain why it works at one wavelength only, and explain the high-reflection quarter-wave film and multilayer stacks.

## Anti-reflection (AR) coatings

A transparent film put on a surface to suppress reflection is an **antireflection coating** (Brij Lal PDF p. 394: Smakula, 1935). It must satisfy two conditions.

**Phase condition and thickness.** Let air (1), film (μ_f) and glass (μ_g) have 1 < μ_f < μ_g. Both reflections are from a lower to a higher index, so both gain π and the net extra path is zero (bridge B2). Destructive interference of the two reflected beams at normal incidence needs

$$ 2μ_f t = (2m + 1) λ/2   so   t_min = λ/(4μ_f)   (Eq. 15.85) $$

an **optical thickness of one quarter wavelength** (the book writes Δ = 2μ_f t − λ/2 − λ/2 and drops the whole wave).

**Amplitude condition and index.** Complete cancellation needs equal amplitudes. At normal incidence the amplitude reflection coefficients are (1 − μ_f)/(1 + μ_f) at the top face and (μ_f − μ_g)/(μ_f + μ_g) at the bottom face (standard Fresnel values; verify). Equating them:

$$ (1 − μ_f)(μ_f + μ_g) = (μ_f − μ_g)(1 + μ_f) $$

Expand: μ_f + μ_g − μ_f² − μ_fμ_g = μ_f + μ_f² − μ_g − μ_gμ_f, so 2μ_g = 2μ_f², giving

$$ μ_f = √μ_g   (Eq. 15.88) $$

For μ_g = 1.5 this is 1.225. The book quotes 1.22 and notes magnesium fluoride (1.38) and cryolite (1.36) as practical materials that adhere well and resist scratching (PDF p. 395).

**One formula for both.** From module 8 the reflected amplitude is (r₁ + r₂e^(−iδ))/(1 + r₁r₂e^(−iδ)), which vanishes exactly when |r₁| = |r₂| and δ = π (mod 2π): the quarter-wave and square-root conditions together.

## Numbers

Glass μ_g = 1.50 uncoated: R = (0.5/2.5)² = 4.00%. MgF₂ (μ_f = 1.38) designed for 550 nm: t_min = 550/(4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm, and the exact film formula of module 8 gives R = 1.41% at 550 nm. With μ_f = √1.5 = 1.2247 the reflectivity would be exactly 0 at the design wavelength. Other wavelengths and Brij Lal Example 15.11 are in masterNotes.

**Single wavelength only.** The quarter-wave condition holds exactly at one λ. The design wavelength is usually 550 nm, where the eye is most sensitive (yellow-green); red and violet are reflected more, so a coated lens looks purple in reflected light (PDF pp. 395 to 396). Multilayer coatings widen the band; the three-layer design Brij Lal lists (PDF p. 396) is in masterNotes.

## High-reflection (HR) coatings and dielectric mirrors

If the film has a **higher** index than the substrate (μ_f > μ_g), the top reflection gains π and the bottom reflection (film to a lower index) does not. The net extra path is λ/2, so both reflected beams add when 2μ_f t − λ/2 = mλ, that is 2μ_f t = (2m + 1)λ/2, again t_min = λ/(4μ_f) (Eq. 15.89). Brij Lal names titanium oxide (2.8) and zinc sulphide (2.3) (PDF p. 397).

*Numbers:* a ZnS (2.3) quarter-wave film on glass 1.5 gives R = 31.2% at 550 nm, against 4% uncoated. **Alternating** quarter-wave layers of high and low index (H outermost) add reflections in phase, so R approaches 1 (own transfer-matrix values for 1 to 11 layers, verify, in masterNotes); modern filters and mirrors use up to 100 layers (PDF p. 397).

*Limiting cases:* μ_f = 1 or μ_f = μ_g removes the film surface, so R returns to the uncoated value 4% (δ-independent). *Dimension:* t is a length: nm.

### Notes: Definitions

- Thin film: a transparent medium of thickness of the order of a wavelength (about 0.5 to 10 micrometres), such as a soap film, mica, or an air film between plates (Brij Lal PDF p. 356).
- Division of amplitude: a wave is split by partial reflection into a reflected and a transmitted part which are later recombined (thin films, Newton's rings, Michelson).
- Division of wavefront: different parts of one wavefront are made to overlap (Young's slits, biprism, Lloyd's mirror).
- Fringes of equal inclination (Haidinger): fringes that join rays of equal inclination, localised at infinity (parallel film).
- Fringes of equal thickness (Fizeau): fringes that join points of equal film thickness (wedge, Newton's rings).
- Antireflection coating: a transparent film on a surface that suppresses reflection; high-reflection (dielectric mirror) film increases it.

### Notes: Equations to know

- Plane parallel film in air, reflected light: Delta = 2 mu t cos r - lambda/2; bright 2 mu t cos r = (2m + 1) lambda/2; dark 2 mu t cos r = m lambda.
- Transmitted light: Delta = 2 mu t cos r; bright 2 mu t cos r = m lambda; dark = (2m + 1) lambda/2.
- Wedge (dark at edge): 2 mu t = m lambda; fringe width beta = lambda/(2 mu theta); theta = N lambda/(2 mu (x2 - x1)); spacer t = N lambda/(2 mu).
- Newton's rings (air, reflected, normal incidence): r_m^2 = m lambda R, D_m^2 = 4 m lambda R (dark); D^2 = 2(2m + 1) lambda R (bright, m = 0, 1, ...); lambda = (D_(m+p)^2 - D_m^2)/(4 p R); mu = D_air^2/D_liquid^2.
- Multiple-beam reflectivity: rho_R = (r1^2 + r2^2 + 2 r1 r2 cos delta)/(1 + r1^2 r2^2 + 2 r1 r2 cos delta); identical outer media: F sin^2(delta/2)/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2)), F = 4R/(1 - R)^2, delta = 4 pi n2 h cos theta2/lambda0.
- Coatings: t_min = lambda/(4 mu_f); AR index mu_f = sqrt(mu_g).

### Notes: Phase change on reflection (case table)

- A pi jump (lambda/2) occurs on reflection at a boundary from a lower to a higher index; none from higher to lower; none on transmission (Brij Lal PDF pp. 359, 362).

### Notes: Equal inclination versus equal thickness

- Differential of the path difference: delta Delta = 2 mu cos r delta t - 2 mu t sin r delta r.

### Notes: Derivation skeletons

- Parallel film: BF + FD = 2t/cos r; BD = 2t tan r; BH = BD sin i = 2 mu t sin^2 r / cos r; Delta = 2 mu t / cos r - BH = 2 mu t cos r; correct for the pi jump.
- Wedge: 2 mu t1 = m lambda and 2 mu t2 = (m + 1) lambda give t2 - t1 = lambda/(2 mu) = beta tan theta, so beta = lambda/(2 mu theta).
- Newton's rings: r^2 = R^2 - (R - t)^2 = 2Rt - t^2 = 2Rt; 2t = m lambda gives r_m^2 = m lambda R.
- Total reflectivity: Stokes (r' = -r, t t' = 1 - r^2); series r1 + t1 t1' r2 e^(-i delta) sum_j (-r1 r2 e^(-i delta))^j; modulus; identical media gives F sin^2/(1 + F sin^2).
- AR coating: phase 2 mu_f t = (m + 1/2) lambda gives t = lambda/(4 mu_f); amplitude (1 - mu_f)/(1 + mu_f) = (mu_f - mu_g)/(mu_f + mu_g) gives mu_f^2 = mu_g.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- Film too thick: the path difference exceeds the coherence length and no steady fringes form (t < lambda^2/(2 mu delta lambda), Brij Lal Eq. 15.14).
- m = 0 in the dark condition 2 mu t cos r = m lambda would need t = 0; for finite thickness m starts at 1.
- A narrow source shows only a small patch of the film; use an extended source.
- Wedge angle near 1 degree: fringes too close to see; theta tends to zero: fringes spread and disappear.
- Transmitted fringes are faint (visibility about 0.085 for glass, reflected about 0.999).
- Quarter-wave coatings are exact at one wavelength only.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- r in 2 mu t cos r is the refracted angle inside the film, not the angle of incidence i.
- Bright and dark conditions swap between reflected and transmitted light, and between a film in air and an AR coating.
- D_m = 2 sqrt(m lambda R) uses the diameter; r_m = sqrt(m lambda R) the radius.
- The dark centre of Newton's rings in reflection comes from the phase jump, not from the film thickness being zero alone.
- Division of wavefront needs a narrow source; division of amplitude works with an extended source.

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - Thin-film fringes arise from division of amplitude; the two reflected beams from a parallel film differ in optical path by 2 mu t cos r, with a half-wave correction decided by the phase-change rule.
  - Brightness and darkness conditions exchange between reflected and transmitted light and between films in air and AR coatings.
  - A wedge gives equidistant straight fringes of width lambda/(2 mu theta), dark at the edge; Newton's rings give circles with D_m^2 = 4 m lambda R and a dark centre.
  - The multiple-beam sum with Stokes relations gives rho_R = F sin^2(delta/2)/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2)) for identical outer media.
  - A quarter-wave film with mu_f = sqrt(mu_g) is anti-reflecting; mu_f > mu_g raises reflectivity.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - Glass reflects about 4 percent per surface; the first two reflected beams are nearly equal in amplitude, so reflected fringes have good contrast.
  - Dark conditions: reflected 2 mu t cos r = m lambda (air film or soap film); transmitted 2 mu t cos r = (2m + 1) lambda/2.
  - Newton's rings: radii proportional to sqrt(m) and sqrt(lambda); the rings crowd outwards.
  - Equal inclination fringes are localised at infinity; equal thickness fringes at the film.
  - Visible range about 400 to 700 nm.
- **criticalDistinctions:**
  - Division of amplitude versus division of wavefront.
  - Fringes of equal inclination versus equal thickness.
  - Reflected versus transmitted conditions; film in air versus AR coating.
  - Radius versus diameter in Newton's rings.
  - Two-beam reflectivity 4R sin^2(delta/2) versus multiple-beam F sin^2/(1 + F sin^2).
- **formulae:**
  - Delta = 2 mu t cos r (- lambda/2)
  - beta = lambda/(2 mu theta)
  - D_m^2 = 4 m lambda R
  - lambda = (D_(m+p)^2 - D_m^2)/(4 p R)
  - rho_R = F sin^2(delta/2)/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2)), F = 4R/(1 - R)^2
  - t_min = lambda/(4 mu_f); mu_f = sqrt(mu_g)
- **traps:**
  - Using the angle of incidence in cos r.
  - Forgetting the half-wave jump or applying it to an AR coating.
  - Using the radius formula for a diameter or counting the centre as ring 1.
  - Treating a thick glass plate as a thin film.
- **blankPrompts:**
  - - **q:** For a film in air viewed by reflected light, the dark condition is 2 mu t cos r = ____ and the bright condition is 2 mu t cos r = ____.
    - **a:** m lambda; (2m + 1) lambda/2
  - - **q:** The fringe at the apex of an air wedge between glass plates is ____ in reflected light because ____.
    - **a:** dark; the lower reflection gains a pi jump and the upper does not
  - - **q:** The fringe width of a wedge is beta = ____.
    - **a:** lambda/(2 mu theta)
  - - **q:** The diameter of the m-th dark Newton's ring in air is D_m = ____.
    - **a:** 2 sqrt(m lambda R)
  - - **q:** The minimum thickness of an anti-reflection quarter-wave film is ____ and its ideal index is ____.
    - **a:** lambda/(4 mu_f); square root of the glass index
  - - **q:** For identical media above and below, the multiple-beam reflectivity is F sin^2(delta/2)/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2)) with F = ____.
    - **a:** 4R/(1 - R)^2
- **shortQuestions:**
  - - **q:** Why is a broad source needed to see thin-film fringes?
    - **a:** Rays from different source points reach the eye after reflection from different parts of the film, so the whole film is seen; a narrow source shows only a small area.
  - - **q:** Why do thick plates not show interference colours?
    - **a:** Their path difference exceeds the coherence length, so the two reflected parts belong to different wave trains and are incoherent.
  - - **q:** Why are Newton's rings crowded at large radius?
    - **a:** D_m is proportional to sqrt(m), so successive diameters differ by less and less.
  - - **q:** What are the two conditions for an AR coating?
    - **a:** Phase: quarter-wave optical thickness (antiphase beams). Amplitude: equal amplitudes, mu_f = sqrt(mu_g).
  - - **q:** State the Stokes relations.
    - **a:** r' = -r and t t' = 1 - r^2 for reflection and transmission coefficients at one interface seen from the two sides.
- **mnemonics:** - Air in the gap: the edge (and the centre) is dark; dense in the gap: a coating is dark when the optical thickness is a quarter wave.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Why is thin-film interference called interference by division of amplitude?
    - **a:** Each incident wave is divided by partial reflection into a reflected and a transmitted part of smaller amplitude, which later recombine.
  - - **q:** State the rule for the phase change on reflection.
    - **a:** A pi change (lambda/2 of path) occurs on reflection at a boundary from a lower to a higher refractive index; none from higher to lower; none on transmission.
  - - **q:** Write the conditions for brightness and darkness for a film in air in reflected light.
    - **a:** Brightness: 2 mu t cos r = (2m + 1) lambda/2; darkness: 2 mu t cos r = m lambda (m = 1, 2, ...), with r the angle of refraction in the film.
  - - **q:** Why are Newton's rings in reflected light dark at the centre?
    - **a:** Thickness is nearly zero but the air-to-plate reflection gains pi and the lens-to-air reflection does not, so the beams are in antiphase.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Explain why a thick glass plate does not show interference colours.
    - **a:** The optical path difference between the two reflected parts exceeds the coherence length, so the parts belong to different wave trains and cannot interfere.
  - - **q:** Distinguish fringes of equal inclination from fringes of equal thickness.
    - **a:** Equal inclination: uniform film, path difference varies with r, circular fringes localised at infinity. Equal thickness: very thin variable film, path difference varies with t, fringes follow contours of thickness (wedge: straight lines; Newton's rings: circles).
  - - **q:** Why are the diameters of the dark rings not equally spaced?
    - **a:** D_m = 2 sqrt(m lambda R) is proportional to sqrt(m), so the gap between successive diameters decreases outwards.
  - - **q:** Why does a quarter-wave anti-reflection coating work for only one wavelength, and how is the band widened?
    - **a:** The antiphase condition t = lambda/(4 mu_f) is exact at one lambda; other colours are cancelled only partly. Multilayer coatings of several quarter and half-wave layers widen the band.

      - Combine: E_R/a = [r1 + r1^2 r2 e^(-i delta) + r2 e^(-i delta) - r1^2 r2 e^(-i delta)]/(1 + r1 r2 e^(-i delta)) = (r1 + r2 e^(-i delta))/(1 + r1 r2 e^(-i delta)).
      - Modulus squared: rho_R = (r1^2 + r2^2 + 2 r1 r2 cos delta)/(1 + r1^2 r2^2 + 2 r1 r2 cos delta).
      - Identical media above and below: r2 = -r1, R = r1^2; numerator 4R sin^2(delta/2); denominator (1 - R)^2 + 4R sin^2(delta/2). Hence rho_R = F sin^2(delta/2)/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2)), F = 4R/(1 - R)^2.
      - Transmission rho_T = 1/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2)) and rho_R + rho_T = 1.
      - Numbers: glass n2 = 1.52 in air: R = 0.04258, F = 0.1858; rho_R = 0.157 at delta = pi, 0.085 at pi/2 and 0 at 2 pi.
    - **checks:**
      - Small R: rho_R is approximately 4R sin^2(delta/2), the two-beam result.
      - Maxima at delta = (2m + 1) pi match 2 n2 h cos theta2 = (m + 1/2) lambda0.
      - Numerical sum of 400 beams reproduces the closed form to 15 digits.
    - **traps:**
      - +r1 r2 in the ratio.
      - Skipping the Stokes relations.
      - Quoting the two-beam value as the total reflectivity.
  - - **id:** PH-050-D5
    - **task:** Obtain the thickness and the refractive index required of a single-layer anti-reflection coating on glass (10 marks; practice target about 12 minutes).
    - **steps:**
      - Assumptions: normal incidence; 1 < mu_f < mu_g; both reflections are from a lower to a higher index.
      - Both reflected beams gain pi, so the net extra path is zero; Delta = 2 mu_f t.
      - Destructive interference: 2 mu_f t = (2m + 1) lambda/2; the minimum thickness (m = 0) is t = lambda/(4 mu_f).
      - Equal amplitudes: (1 - mu_f)/(1 + mu_f) = (mu_f - mu_g)/(mu_f + mu_g) in magnitude and sign.
      - Cross-multiply: (1 - mu_f)(mu_f + mu_g) = (mu_f - mu_g)(1 + mu_f), so mu_f + mu_g - mu_f^2 - mu_f mu_g = mu_f + mu_f^2 - mu_g - mu_g mu_f, hence 2 mu_g = 2 mu_f^2.
      - Therefore mu_f = sqrt(mu_g) (Eq. 15.88).
      - Numbers: mu_g = 1.5: ideal 1.225; MgF2 (1.38) gives t = 99.6 nm at 550 nm and 1.41 percent reflectivity against 4 percent.
    - **checks:**
      - t is a length.
      - mu_f = sqrt(mu_g) gives zero reflectivity at the design wavelength.
      - Only one wavelength is cancelled exactly.
    - **traps:**
      - Applying the half-wave correction of a film in air.
      - Forgetting to divide by mu_f.

### Memory skeleton

- Division of amplitude: one ray split into two reflected beams; film thickness of order a wavelength; extended source
- Parallel film: Δ = 2μt cos r, plus λ/2 when exactly one reflection is lower-to-higher index
- Reflected and transmitted conditions swap; equal inclination (Haidinger) versus equal thickness (Fizeau)
- Wedge: β = λ/(2μθ), dark at the apex, spacer thickness
- Newton's rings: r_m² = mλR, D_m² = 4mλR, dark centre; measuring λ and μ
- Multiple beams: ρ_R = F sin²(δ/2)/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)), F = 4R/(1 − R)²
- Coatings: quarter-wave t = λ/(4μ_f); AR needs μ_f = √μ_g; HR needs μ_f > μ_g

### Last-week summary

- Film in air, reflected: dark 2μt cos r = mλ, bright (2m + 1)λ/2; transmitted swaps; r is the angle inside the film.
- π jump only on reflection from lower to higher index, never on transmission; an AR coating (1 < μ_f < μ_g) has two jumps, so no net λ/2.
- Wedge β = λ/(2μθ), dark apex; Newton's rings D_m² = 4mλR, dark centre, λ = (D²_(m+p) − D²_m)/(4pR), μ = D²_air/D²_liquid.
- Identical outer media: ρ_R = F sin²(δ/2)/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)), F = 4R/(1 − R)², δ = 4πn₂h cos θ₂/λ₀; ρ_R + ρ_T = 1.
- AR: t_min = λ/(4μ_f), μ_f = √μ_g (about 1.22 for glass; MgF₂ 1.38 used); HR film μ_f > μ_g, multilayers push R towards 1.
