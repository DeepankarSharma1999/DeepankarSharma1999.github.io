## PH-067: Ruby and He-Ne lasers

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Photon energy in electron-volts, kT and the Boltzmann ratio

Atomic energies are quoted in **electron-volts** (eV), where 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A photon of wavelength λ carries E = hc/λ, which is easy to remember as **E (eV) = 1239.8 / λ (nm)**. For the ruby line, λ = 694.3 nm gives E = 1.786 eV. For the red He-Ne line, λ = 632.8 nm gives 1.959 eV. The thermal energy scale at temperature T is kT, where k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV per kelvin; at 300 K it is 0.02585 eV.

When atoms are in thermal equilibrium, the populations of two levels E₁ < E₂ (with equal degeneracies) obey the **Boltzmann ratio** N₂/N₁ = exp(−(E₂ − E₁)/kT). For the ruby line at 300 K the exponent is 1.786/0.02585 = 69.1, so N₂/N₁ = 1.0 × 10⁻³⁰; for the He-Ne line the exponent is 75.8 and the ratio is 1.2 × 10⁻³³. The book rounds the ruby energy to 1.79 eV and prints 8 × 10⁻³¹ (rounding gives 8.5 × 10⁻³¹); the exponent is so large that a 0.2 per cent change in E alters the ratio by about 15 per cent. Either way, no visible-light transition can be inverted by heating, which is why a pump is needed.

### Bridge: Lifetime, exponential decay and why a long lifetime lets atoms pile up

If excited atoms leave a level at a rate proportional to their number, the number left after a time t is N(t) = N₀ e^(−t/τ), where τ is the **lifetime** of the level. After one lifetime a fraction e⁻¹ = 0.368 remains. The extract says ordinary excited levels last about 10⁻⁹ s while a **metastable** level, from which a single-photon return to the lower level is forbidden, lasts 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ s, that is 10³ to 10⁶ times longer (Brijlal PDF p. 628).

Compare what survives after one microsecond. For an ordinary level with τ = 1 ns the fraction is e^(−1000), effectively zero: the atoms are gone before the pump can fill the level. For a metastable level with τ = 3 ms (a typical value quoted for ruby, verify) the fraction is e^(−1/3000) = 0.9997: nearly all stay. If the pump delivers atoms at a steady rate R per second into a level of lifetime τ, the number held there tends to R τ. A larger τ therefore means a larger accumulation for the same pump, which is exactly what population inversion needs.

### 1. Orientation: what the examiner asks about the ruby and He-Ne lasers, and what PH-066 already gives you

## What a laser needs

Every laser has three parts (Brijlal Section 22.8, PDF pp. 628 to 629): an **active medium**, a **pump**, and an **optical resonator**. The active medium contains atoms ('active centres') with a suitable set of levels, among them a long-lived metastable upper level. The pump raises atoms to excited levels until the upper laser level holds more atoms than the lower one, a **population inversion**. The resonator is a pair of mirrors that returns the light through the medium so that stimulated emission builds up along the axis.

### 2. Foundation: three-level against four-level pumping, in numbers

*Objective:* Show by counting that a three-level scheme needs more than half of the active atoms excited and a four-level scheme does not, and attach numbers for the ruby rod.

## The two schemes (Brijlal Section 22.10, PDF pp. 632 to 634)

**Three-level.** Levels E₁ (ground), E₂ (metastable upper laser level) and E₃ (a broad pump level). The pump lifts atoms from E₁ to E₃; they fall to E₂ by non-radiative transitions (energy given to the lattice) in a very short time; at E₂ they wait because a single-photon return is forbidden. The laser transition is E₂ → E₁, so the **lower laser level is the ground level**.

**Four-level.** Levels E₁ (ground), E₄ (pump), E₃ (metastable upper laser level), E₂ (lower laser level, well above the ground: E₂ − E₁ ≫ kT). The pump lifts atoms E₁ → E₄, they drop quickly to E₃, the laser transition is E₃ → E₂, and atoms leave E₂ for E₁ by non-radiative transitions.

## The counting argument (the lesson's own, from the extract's statement on PDF p. 634 that more than half of the ground-level atoms must be pumped up; it writes N₂ > N₁/2 with N₁ meaning the ground population before pumping)

Take equal degeneracies and N active atoms in all, so that N₁ + N₂ ≈ N in a three-level scheme (E₃ is nearly empty because it empties at once).

- **Three-level:** inversion means N₂ > N₁ = N − N₂, so **N₂ > N/2**. Add a threshold excess ΔN_th (the threshold section of PH-066, 'Core: conditions for laser action, the gain coefficient and the threshold population inversion'): the inversion N₂ − N₁ = 2N₂ − N must reach ΔN_th, so N₂ ≥ (N + ΔN_th)/2. For N = 10²⁰ and ΔN_th = 10¹⁷ this is 5.005 × 10¹⁹: more than half, however small the threshold.
- **Four-level:** the lower level is empty, N₂ ≈ 0, so the inversion is N₃ − N₂ ≈ N₃ and only **N₃ ≥ ΔN_th** is needed: 10¹⁷ atoms instead of 5 × 10¹⁹, a fraction 0.2 per cent of the three-level requirement in this example.

The extract's own wording (PDF p. 634): in the four-level scheme inversion is 'readily established even if a smaller number of atoms arrive' and 'relatively small pumping power is required'.

## Numbers for the ruby rod

The book's practice problem takes 3 × 10¹⁹ Cr³⁺ ions emitting 6943 Å photons of 1.786 eV (the book prints 1.79 eV). Complete inversion would store 3 × 10¹⁹ × 1.786 eV = 8.58 J (the book prints 8.6 J). But the threshold needs N₂ > N/2, so at least half of the ions, 1.5 × 10¹⁹, must be excited: a stored energy of at least **4.29 J** before any inversion exists. Keeping that population against decay with a lifetime τ = 3 ms (a typical ruby value, verify; the extract gives only 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ s for metastable levels) would need energy delivered to the level at a rate of at least N/(2τ) × 1.786 eV = 1.4 kW continuously (the absorbed pump power is larger, because pump photons carry more energy than 1.786 eV). A flash delivers the energy in a time short compared with τ instead.

## The two consequences the extract draws (PDF p. 634)

1. **Pulsed or continuous.** In a three-level scheme stimulated emission returns atoms to the ground level, which restores the normal population and stops the lasing, so the output is pulsed. In a four-level scheme the lower level empties by itself and the output can be continuous (cw).
2. **Broad pump band.** A flash lamp emits over a wide range of wavelengths, so the pump level should be a broad band of close levels to use more of the light (Brijlal Section 22.10.4).

### 3. Core: the ruby laser - construction, energy levels and the role of the chromium ions

*Objective:* Describe the ruby rod, its resonator and flash lamp, draw the three-level diagram of Cr³⁺, and state what the chromium ions and the host crystal each do.

## The crystal and the rod

Ruby is aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃, in which a small fraction of the aluminium ions are replaced by chromium ions Cr³⁺: about 0.05 per cent by weight (Brijlal Section 22.14.1, PDF p. 639). The aluminium and oxygen atoms take no part in the lasing; they form the **host lattice**. The Cr³⁺ ions are the **active centres**, because only they have the set of three levels that lasing needs. The rod is a cylinder about 4 cm long and 0.5 cm across (the book's figures). Its ends are ground flat, polished, parallel to each other and perpendicular to the axis. One end is silvered for 100 per cent reflection, the other for about 90 per cent reflection and 10 per cent transmission, and the two silvered ends form the Fabry–Perot resonator (the instrument itself is Day 20 lesson PH-053). A helical **xenon flash tube** is wound round the rod; when its power supply is fired it gives a burst of white light lasting a few milliseconds.

## The three levels of Cr³⁺ (book Fig. 22.25)

- **E₁:** the ground level.
- **E₃ and E₃′:** two wide energy **bands** that absorb the green and the blue parts of the flash. Atoms stay there only about 10⁻⁹ s.
- **E₂:** a pair of closely spaced levels, **metastable**. The laser transition is E₂ → E₁, a red photon of wavelength 6943 Å.

The photon energy is 1239.8/694.3 = 1.786 eV (bridge B1). If the pump photon is green, say 550 nm (2.254 eV; the extract names only the colour, so the wavelength is illustrative), the laser photon keeps 1.786/2.254 = 79 per cent of its energy and the rest goes to the lattice as heat in the fast E₃ → E₂ step; for a blue photon of 420 nm (2.952 eV) the laser photon keeps 60 per cent. This is the energy price of a three-level scheme with a broad pump band.

## A source discrepancy to know

PDF p. 639 says the E₂ pair lives 'approximately 1000 times' longer than E₃. With E₃ at 10⁻⁹ s that implies about 10⁻⁶ s, the bottom of the book's own metastable range (10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ s, PDF p. 628) and about 3000 times shorter than the usual ruby value of about 3 ms (standard knowledge, verify). With 3 ms, E₂ outlives E₃ by about 10⁶, so atoms pile up there.

## The role of the chromium ions (the second part of 2017 Q4(c))

1. They **absorb** the pump: their broad bands E₃ and E₃′ catch the green and blue light of a white flash (Section 22.10.4 asks for a broad pump level for efficiency).
2. They provide a **metastable level** E₂: long-lived, localised in the crystal and not in competition with other filling processes (PDF p. 628 says such impurity levels lie in the forbidden gap and are localised).
3. They **emit** the laser line: E₂ → E₁ at 6943 Å, stimulated by a chance photon along the axis.
4. Because only 0.05 per cent of the sites carry Cr³⁺, each ion is isolated in the transparent host and keeps its own level structure (the same remark is made for Nd³⁺ in YAG on PDF p. 641).

The host crystal supplies a rigid, transparent, mechanically strong rod that holds the active ions in place; it contributes no levels of its own to the process.

### 4. Core: how the ruby laser works in time - pulses and laser spiking (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Narrate one flash of the ruby laser step by step, and derive from two rate equations why the output is a train of microsecond spikes.

## One flash in seven steps (Brijlal PDF pp. 639 to 640)

1. The flash tube gives a few milliseconds of white light.
2. Cr³⁺ ions absorb the green and blue parts and rise to the bands E₃ and E₃′.
3. In about 10⁻⁹ s they drop to E₂ without radiation, giving energy to the lattice.
4. E₂ is long-lived, so ions accumulate. When more than half of the ions are there, N₂ > N₁ and a population inversion exists (module 2).
5. A chance photon of 6943 Å emitted along the axis starts a chain of stimulated emissions; the mirrors send it back and forth.
6. When gain balances loss (PH-066 threshold) a strong red beam leaves through the 10 per cent end.
7. The output is **not** steady: it is a series of irregular pulses, each about a microsecond long, though the flash lasts milliseconds. This is **laser spiking**.

The book's reason: stimulated transitions occur faster than the pump can maintain the inversion, so each burst empties E₂ below the threshold value; the lasing stops until the pump has restored the inversion; then it repeats (PDF p. 640).

## Derivation 1: the spiking as a relaxation oscillation

This is the lesson's own simplified model (verify); the parameters are illustrative, not measured ruby data.

**Setting.** Let N be the excess population in E₂ (the inversion) and q the number of photons in the cavity. A constant pump rate P puts atoms into E₂. K is the stimulated rate per photon per atom, τ the lifetime of E₂ and τ_c the photon lifetime in the cavity.

$$ dN/dt = P − N/τ − K N q,   dq/dt = (K N − 1/τ_c) q $$

1. Threshold: dq/dt = 0 needs N_t = 1/(K τ_c). Define the pump ratio x = P τ/N_t (x > 1 above threshold).
2. Steady state: N_s = N_t and q_s = (x − 1)/(K τ).
3. Perturb: N = N_s + n, q = q_s + m. Using K N_s = 1/τ_c and K q_s = (x − 1)/τ, and dropping the product n m:

$$ dn/dt = −(x/τ) n − m/τ_c,   dm/dt = ((x − 1)/τ) n $$

4. Eliminate n by differentiating the second and substituting the first:

$$ m″ + (x/τ) m′ + ((x − 1)/(τ τ_c)) m = 0 $$

5. This is a damped oscillator: m ∝ e^(−x t/2τ) cos ωt with ω² = (x − 1)/(τ τ_c) − (x/2τ)² ≈ (x − 1)/(τ τ_c), since τ_c is nanoseconds and τ milliseconds.
6. Meaning: photons overshoot, burn the inversion below N_t, fall away, and the pump rebuilds the inversion: one spike per cycle. The oscillation dies in a time 2τ/x unless the pump varies.

**Numbers.** τ = 3 ms (typical, verify). With the book's 4 cm rod, a 90 per cent mirror (10 per cent loss per round trip) and a refractive index of ruby of about 1.76 (standard, verify): τ_c = 2 × 1.76 × 0.04/(3 × 10⁸ × 0.10) = 4.7 ns. For x = 3: ω = 3.77 × 10⁵ rad/s, a period of **16.7 µs**, and a decay time 2τ/x = 2 ms.

**Check by integration.** The two equations were integrated in Python with a spontaneous seed (relative size 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹²). Threshold is reached at 1.22 ms, matching τ ln(x/(x − 1)) = 1.216 ms. The first spike has a full width at half maximum of about 1.4 to 1.7 µs (2.3 µs for a seed of 10⁻⁶), consistent with the book's microsecond pulses. Early spike spacing is about 25 to 45 µs and shrinks to 17 to 19 µs as spikes decay, matching the formula's 16.7 µs. The first-spike width and early spacing depend on the assumed seed; the threshold time and small-signal period do not.

**Limiting case.** A longer τ_c slows the spikes; as x → 1, ω → 0.

### 5. Core: the He-Ne laser - construction, resonant transfer and the energy-level scheme

*Objective:* Describe the He-Ne tube and resonator, draw the energy-level diagram with the book's labels, and trace the pumping path from the discharge to the 6328 Å photon.

## Why a gas laser is not pumped with light

In a gas the energy levels of an atom are narrow, so only light of a very sharp wavelength could pump them, and a suitable lamp is hard to find. Gas lasers are therefore excited by an **electric discharge**: electrons in the discharge give energy to the gas atoms in collisions (Brijlal Section 22.14.3, PDF pp. 641 to 642).

## Construction

- A long **discharge tube** filled with helium and neon in the ratio 10 : 1. Neon atoms are the active centres; helium atoms help to excite them.
- **Electrodes** joined to a high-voltage supply (the book speaks of about 10 kV to ionise the gas).
- **Inclined windows** seal the ends (Brewster windows; module 9).
- **Two mirrors outside the tube** form the Fabry–Perot resonator, their spacing adjusted to a whole number of half-wavelengths, mλ/2, so that a standing wave fits. The output is continuous (cw).
- The He-Ne laser was the first gas laser (1961 in the book; first operated December 1960, published 1961: verify).

## The levels (book labels, Fig. 22.29)

- **Helium:** ground level and F₃ (2s) at **20.61 eV** above it, a metastable level.
- **Neon:** E₁ (2p) the ground level; E₂ (3s); E₃ (3p); E₆ (5s) at **20.66 eV**, a metastable level and the upper laser level.

## The path from discharge to light

1. Electrons accelerated by the field strike helium atoms and raise them to F₃.
2. F₃ is metastable: a helium atom cannot return to the ground level by emitting one photon, so helium atoms stay excited.
3. An excited helium atom collides with a ground-level neon atom. The two levels F₃ and E₆ are nearly equal (20.61 and 20.66 eV), so the energy passes across: **resonant energy transfer**. The 0.05 eV deficit is supplied from the kinetic energy of the helium atom. At 300 K kT is 0.0259 eV, so the deficit is about 1.9 kT, a modest multiple of the thermal energy. The helium atom drops to its ground level.
4. E₆ is metastable, so neon atoms accumulate there.
5. The lower laser level E₃ is almost empty. It lies at 20.66 − 1.959 = 18.70 eV above the neon ground level (the lesson's subtraction; the extract gives no value), so its thermal population exp(−18.70/0.0259) = e^(−723) is zero for all purposes: the inversion between E₆ and E₃ is established easily.
6. A chance photon starts stimulated emission E₆ → E₃: a red beam of wavelength **6328 Å** (1.959 eV). The book says other possible transitions give 3.39 µm (0.366 eV) and 1.15 µm (1.078 eV), but it does not draw them. They do not share the E₆ → E₃ pair: 3.39 µm starts at E₆ and ends on a different lower level about 0.37 eV below it; 1.15 µm starts from another neon level near 19.78 eV, fed by a second helium metastable near 19.82 eV (standard knowledge, verify).
7. Atoms fall E₃ → E₂ spontaneously; E₂ is also metastable, so they must be returned to E₁ (module 6).

## Why it is called a four-level scheme

The book calls it four-level: the pump brings neon atoms to E₆ (via helium), the lower laser level E₃ is virtually empty, and the atoms are returned to the ground level E₁. As in module 2, no more than a small excess in E₆ over E₃ is needed, so the output can be continuous.

### 6. Core: the role of helium, the narrow tube and what keeps the He-Ne laser running

*Objective:* Answer 'What is the role of helium?' and 'Why must the tube be narrow?' with the physical chain of reasoning, and say what would happen to the output if either were changed.

## The role of helium (2018 Q3(a) and 2022 Q3(c))

The book's answer: helium atoms excite neon atoms and cause the population inversion (PDF p. 643). The chain of reasons makes this a full answer:

1. **Helium stores the energy.** Electrons in the discharge raise helium atoms to the metastable level F₃. A single-photon return to the ground level is forbidden, so excited helium atoms live long enough to collide with neon atoms.
2. **The levels match.** F₃ (20.61 eV) lies within 0.05 eV of the neon upper laser level E₆ (20.66 eV), so collisions transfer the energy efficiently (resonant transfer).
3. **There is much more helium.** The ratio is 10 helium to 1 neon, so a neon atom is likely to meet an excited helium atom.
4. **The reverse transfer is negligible** (the book's statement): neon atoms in E₆ do not give their energy back to helium.
5. **The pumping is selective.** The transfer fills E₆, not E₃ or E₂, so the upper laser level fills and the lower one stays almost empty. That selectivity is what produces the inversion.

Neon itself is the lasing medium; helium never emits the laser line.

## The narrow tube (2018 Q3(a))

After the laser transition the atoms reach E₃, drop spontaneously to E₂ (3s), and E₂ is **metastable**. Atoms cannot leave it by radiating, so they accumulate there. If they stay there, the number of atoms in the ground level, which the discharge can excite again, decreases and the laser ceases. The only route back to the ground level E₁ is **collision with the wall of the tube** (book, PDF p. 643). A **narrow** tube makes wall collisions frequent, so E₂ empties quickly and the cycle continues. A wide tube makes them rare; atoms pile up in E₂ and the laser stops.

An order-of-magnitude check (the lesson's own estimate, verify): the rms speed of a neon atom at 300 K is √(3kT/m) = 612 m/s with m = 19.99 u. Crossing a bore of radius 1 mm takes about 1 mm/612 m/s = 1.6 µs, so the rate of wall visits is at most of order v/R and falls as R grows. The bore diameter is thus tied to how fast E₂ can be emptied.

## Why the output is continuous

Because E₃ is almost empty and E₂ is emptied at the wall, the population inversion between E₆ and E₃ is maintained as long as the discharge runs. Unlike the ruby laser (module 4) there is no self-termination, so the output is continuous (the book, PDF p. 643).

## What changes if you change the design?

| Change | Effect (as the extract's reasoning gives) |
|---|---|
| Remove helium | E₆ is no longer filled selectively by resonant transfer; inversion is lost or weak |
| Widen the tube | Atoms accumulate in E₂ and the ground level is depleted; the laser ceases |
| Reduce the helium : neon ratio | Fewer helium atoms per neon atom; transfer to neon becomes less likely |
| Switch off the discharge | No electron impact, no pumping, no laser |

### 7. Core: cavity modes, mode spacing and how many longitudinal modes oscillate (derivation 2; the 2018 numerical)

*Objective:* Derive the mode frequencies and spacing of a two-mirror cavity, count the modes inside a gain width, and solve the 2018 numerical stating how the printed 'half width' is read.

## Two kinds of mode

A **mode** is a standing-wave pattern that the cavity allows. **Longitudinal (axial) modes** are fixed by the length of the cavity; **transverse (TEM) modes** are fixed by its cross-section and describe the intensity pattern across the beam, labelled TEM_mn, where TEM₀₀ is the smooth single-peaked pattern (Brijlal Sections 22.12 and 22.13, PDF pp. 635 to 638). This module treats the longitudinal modes; PH-066 (section 'Master: stimulated emission and coherence, and where this lesson stops') quotes the same spacing.

## Derivation 2: frequencies, spacing and number

**Setting.** Two parallel mirrors a distance L apart, filled with a medium of refractive index n; light of wavelength λ (in vacuum) bounces between them.

1. A steady standing wave needs the wave to return to the start in phase after a round trip of optical path 2nL, so **2 n L = m λ**, m = 1, 2, 3, … (book Eq. 22.41).
2. The frequencies: ν_m = c/λ_m = m c/(2nL).
3. Neighbouring modes differ by **Δν = c/(2nL)** (Eq. 22.43), the same for every m.
4. Convert to wavelength: λ = c/ν gives |dλ| = (λ²/c) dν, so **Δλ = λ²/(2nL)** (Eq. 22.44).
5. Not every mode oscillates. The gain curve (the emission line, broadened by the motion of the atoms, module 8) rises above the threshold only over a width δλ. The number of modes that can oscillate is **N = δλ/Δλ** (Eq. 22.45), an average count: a window of width W holds the integer below or above W/Δλ, depending on where the comb of modes falls.

**Checks.** c/(2nL) has units s⁻¹ ✓. A long cavity has closely spaced modes; a short one fits a single mode. For L = 0.30 m and λ = 632.8 nm the mode number is m = 2L/λ = 9.48 × 10⁵.

## The 2018 numerical (also Brijlal Example 22.5)

**Question as printed:** 'How many longitudinal modes can be excited for an He-Ne laser in a cavity of length 30 cm and having half width of gain profile of laser material 2 × 10⁻³ nm? The emission wavelength is 6328 Å.'

1. Δλ = (632.8 × 10⁻⁹)²/(2 × 0.30) = 6.67 × 10⁻¹³ m = 0.667 × 10⁻³ nm (the book prints 0.66 × 10⁻³ nm).
2. N = 2 × 10⁻³/0.667 × 10⁻³ = 3.00 (more exactly 2.997), so **3 modes**.
3. In frequency: Δν = c/(2L) = 500 MHz and the width 2 × 10⁻³ nm is 1.50 GHz, again 3.0 mode spacings.

**Reading the data.** The stem says 'half width', a term commonly used for the full width at half maximum; Example 22.5 uses it in that sense. The book's method uses 2 × 10⁻³ nm as the width δλ in N = δλ/Δλ, and that is how it is solved here. If 2 × 10⁻³ nm were a literal half-width, the full width would be 4 × 10⁻³ nm and the count would be 5.99, about 6. Module 8 shows that the Doppler full width of neon at 373 K is 1.96 × 10⁻³ nm, so 2 × 10⁻³ nm is the size of a **full** width, which supports N = 3. Only the part above threshold counts, so this is an upper estimate. State the reading in the answer.

### 8. Master: Doppler broadening of the He-Ne line and the 2025 numerical (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Derive the Gaussian Doppler line shape and its full width at half maximum, and compute Δω_D for the 2025 data with the mass of the emitting atom stated.

## Why the gain line has a width

Atoms of one species radiate at the same frequency only if they are at rest. In the discharge the neon atoms move at random, and an atom moving along the axis with velocity component v_x emits a frequency shifted by the **Doppler effect**. The result is a spread of frequencies about the central value: the gain curve of Brijlal Fig. 22.21, with the extract noting only that 'various line broadening mechanisms' spread the line (PDF p. 637). The book's questions ask for 'Doppler and line broadening' and for the Doppler broadening of the 6328 Å line at 373 K for Ne-20 (PDF p. 653) but the extract gives no formula; the derivation below is standard physics (verify).

## Derivation 3: the Doppler full width

**Assumptions.** Atoms of mass m in thermal equilibrium at temperature T; speeds much smaller than c; each atom radiates at ω₀ in its own frame; other broadening is ignored.

1. First-order Doppler shift: ω = ω₀ (1 + v_x/c), so v_x = c (ω − ω₀)/ω₀.
2. One component of velocity in thermal equilibrium has the Gaussian distribution f(v_x) ∝ exp(−m v_x²/(2kT)).
3. Substitute step 1: the line shape is

$$ g(ω) ∝ exp[ −m c² (ω − ω₀)² / (2 k T ω₀²) ] $$

4. Half of the peak value is reached when the exponent equals ln 2: (ω − ω₀)² = 2 k T ω₀² ln 2/(m c²).
5. The full width at half maximum is twice that offset:

$$ Δω_D = ω₀ √( 8 k T ln 2 / (m c²) ) $$

**Checks.** The ratio kT/(mc²) is dimensionless, so Δω_D has the units of ω₀ (s⁻¹). As T → 0 the width goes to zero. A heavier atom gives a narrower line, a hotter gas a wider line (∝ √T). The width in ordinary frequency is Δν_D = Δω_D/2π.

## The 2025 numerical

**Question as printed:** 'For a He-Ne laser system, what will be the magnitude of Δω_D which represents FWHM of the line shape function g(ω), if resonant frequency ω₀ = 3 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹ and temperature T = 300 K?'

Helium does not emit this line (module 6). The natural mixture (20.18 u) changes the answer by only 0.5 per cent; helium's mass 4.00 u would give a wrong answer 2.2 times too large.

1. 8 k T ln 2 = 8 × 1.3806 × 10⁻²³ × 300 × 0.6931 = 2.297 × 10⁻²⁰ J.
2. m c² = 3.320 × 10⁻²⁶ × (2.998 × 10⁸)² = 2.984 × 10⁻⁹ J.
3. Ratio = 7.698 × 10⁻¹², square root = 2.774 × 10⁻⁶.
4. **Δω_D = 3 × 10¹⁵ × 2.774 × 10⁻⁶ = 8.3 × 10⁹ s⁻¹**, that is Δν_D = 1.32 GHz.

## Brewster windows: why the He-Ne beam is linearly polarised

The ends of the tube are closed by windows set at the polarising angle (Brijlal Section 20.6.1.1, PDF p. 507; Day 20 lesson PH-060, module 9, and Day 17 lesson PH-207 for Brewster's law). A beam polarised in the plane of incidence (p) meets each surface at the Brewster angle and suffers **no reflection loss**. The perpendicular component (s) is partly reflected out of the beam.

Numbers for glass of index 1.5 (recomputed): θ_B = tan⁻¹(1.5) = 56.31° (the figure in the book is labelled 57°, rounded). Each surface reflects R_s = ((μ² − 1)/(μ² + 1))² = 0.148 of s-light. A window has two surfaces: transmission (1 − 0.148)² = 0.726. A round trip crosses two windows twice, that is eight surfaces: (0.852)⁸ = **0.278**. The s component loses 72 per cent each round trip, while the p component loses nothing (ideal windows). With so little gain to spare, only the p polarisation oscillates. This gives a linearly polarised output (the book says the radiation is polarised, PDF p. 507) and removes the window losses for the oscillating light, which a plain window would add.

## A model paragraph (about 70 words) for 'role of helium'

Helium atoms, far more numerous than neon (10 : 1), are excited by electron impact to the metastable level F₃ at 20.61 eV. This is within 0.05 eV of the neon level E₆ at 20.66 eV, so on collision the energy passes to a ground-level neon atom. E₆ fills, the lower level E₃ stays empty, and inversion results. Helium itself never lases; its job is selective pumping of neon.

### Notes: Definitions

- Active medium: the material whose atoms (active centres) have levels suitable for inversion; the surrounding material is the host (Brijlal PDF p. 629).
- Pump: the agency that supplies energy to raise atoms to the upper level; optical pumping (flash lamp) for solid lasers, electric discharge for gas lasers.
- Metastable level: a level from which single-photon emission to the lower level is forbidden; lifetime 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ s, 10³ to 10⁶ times an ordinary level (PDF p. 628).
- Population inversion: N₂ > N₁ (equal degeneracies) between the upper and lower laser levels, a non-equilibrium state (negative-temperature state).
- Three-level scheme: the lower laser level is the ground level, so more than half of the active atoms must be excited; pulsed output (ruby).
- Four-level scheme: the lower laser level is above the ground level and almost empty, so a small inversion is enough; continuous output (He-Ne).
- Optical resonator: two mirrors that return the light through the medium; sets 2nL = mλ; the Fabry–Perot form is used here.
- Longitudinal (axial) mode: a standing wave along the axis, Δν = c/(2nL); transverse (TEM) mode: an intensity pattern across the beam.
- Laser spiking: the irregular train of microsecond pulses in the output of a ruby laser during a millisecond flash.
- Doppler broadening: spread of the emission frequency caused by the thermal motion of the atoms; Gaussian, with FWHM Δω_D = ω₀ √(8kT ln 2/(mc²)).

- E (eV) = 1239.8/λ (nm); kT = 0.02585 eV at 300 K; N₂/N₁ = exp(−ΔE/kT)

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Ruby (three-level, pulsed, 6943 Å, Cr³⁺ in Al₂O₃, flash pumping) against He-Ne (four-level, continuous, 6328 Å, Ne with He, discharge pumping).
- Helium does not lase: it stores and transfers energy; neon is the lasing atom.
- Narrow tube: for wall collisions that empty the metastable E₂, not to make the beam narrow.
- Spiking (relaxation oscillation of the inversion and photon number) against mode locking or Q-switching (not treated here).
- Mode spacing Δν = c/(2nL) against the Doppler width of the gain curve: the first depends on the cavity, the second on temperature and atomic mass.
- Δω_D (angular, s⁻¹) against Δν_D (Hz): they differ by 2π.
- FWHM against half width: the formula derived here is the full width.

### Core concepts

- A laser needs an active medium with a metastable upper level, a pump and a resonator; the pump creates the population inversion.
- The ruby laser is a three-level pulsed laser: Cr³⁺ in Al₂O₃, flash pumping, 6943 Å, more than half of the ions must be excited.
- The He-Ne laser is a four-level continuous laser: helium transfers energy to neon (20.61 eV to 20.66 eV), the lasing line is 6328 Å, and the tube is narrow so that wall collisions empty the metastable level.
- The number of oscillating longitudinal modes is the gain width divided by the mode spacing c/(2nL).
- The gain line of a gas laser is Doppler broadened: Δω_D = ω₀ √(8kT ln 2/(mc²)).

**Q.** Why does a ruby laser give spikes?

**A.** Stimulated emission empties the metastable level faster than the pump refills it, so the inversion falls below threshold, lasing stops, and the cycle repeats; the rate equations give a damped oscillation of period 2π√(τ τ_c/(x − 1)).

### Memory skeleton

- Every laser: an active medium with a metastable upper level, a pump that creates the inversion, and a two-mirror resonator
- Three-level (ruby): lower laser level is the ground, more than half excited, pulsed; four-level (He-Ne): lower level nearly empty, continuous
- Ruby: Cr³⁺ in Al₂O₃, xenon flash, bands E₃ and E₃′ → metastable pair E₂ → 6943 Å to the ground
- Spiking: irregular microsecond pulses in a millisecond flash, because stimulated emission outruns the pump
- He-Ne: discharge excites He F₃ (20.61 eV), collisions feed Ne E₆ (20.66 eV), 6328 Å to an empty E₃; narrow tube empties E₂ at the wall
- Longitudinal modes: 2nL = mλ, Δν = c/(2nL), Δλ = λ²/(2nL), N = δλ/Δλ
- Laser against ordinary light (directionality, divergence, intensity, coherence, monochromaticity); Brewster windows polarise the He-Ne beam
