## GS-029: Land revenue systems: Permanent, Ryotwari, Mahalwari

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words you need: zamindar, ryot, revenue, rent, assessment, settlement, mahal

Land revenue questions are a vocabulary test first. Learn these seven words and every system becomes a one-line sentence.

- **Revenue** is what the **state** takes from land: a share of the crop or its money value. **Rent** is what a **cultivator pays a landlord**. A zamindar collects rent from ryots and pays revenue to the state, and keeps the difference.
- **Zamindar** (landholder): a person with a hereditary or recognised claim to collect dues from a territory. NCERT Class XII notes that the Company classed the old rajas and taluqdars of Bengal as zamindars; one zamindari could contain hundreds of villages.
- **Ryot** (also spelt raiyat or ryot in British records): a peasant cultivator. NCERT says Bengal ryots often leased land onward to under-ryots.
- **Assessment**: working out how much revenue a piece of land or a village must pay, usually after a survey.
- **Settlement**: the agreement that fixes who pays the assessed revenue, how much and for how long. 'Permanent' means the amount is fixed for ever; 'temporary' means it is revised after a period.
- **Mahal**: in British records a revenue estate, which may be a village or a group of villages (NCERT Class VIII).
- **Intermediary**: anyone between the cultivator and the state, such as a zamindar or a revenue farmer.

**Reading rule.** The names of the systems tell you who signed the agreement: zamindari with the zamindar, ryotwari with the ryot, mahalwari with the mahal, meaning the village estate.

### 1. Orientation: three systems on one page

*Objective:* Name the three land revenue systems, who each dealt with, where each ran and what share of British territory each covered, as Spectrum gives it.

Spectrum opens its revenue section by saying that agriculture was the main occupation in rural India, that after the Diwani of 1765 the farm economy supported every other sector, and that British policy aimed to extract the maximum income from land with little care for the cultivators. The Company tried several arrangements. The three that matter for examinations are below.

| System | Main areas (Spectrum) | Who the Company dealt with | Fixed for ever or revised? | Share of British territory (Spectrum) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent Settlement (Zamindari) | Bengal and Bihar; extended to Orissa, Banaras and northern Madras | The zamindar | Fixed for ever (1793) | about 19 per cent |
| Ryotwari | Parts of Madras and Bombay, Assam and other parts | The ryot, directly | Revised periodically | about 51 per cent |
| Mahalwari | North Western Provinces, Central Provinces, Punjab (with variations) | The village or estate (mahal), through its headman | Revised periodically | about 30 per cent |

The three shares add up to 100 per cent, which is how Spectrum presents them; treat them as textbook approximations (verify in a standard text before quoting them as exact statistics).

## Hooks for memory

- **Permanent**: Cornwallis, zamindar, 1793, Bengal and Bihar.
- **Ryotwari**: Read and Munro, the ryot, Madras and Bombay.
- **Mahalwari**: Holt Mackenzie and Bird, the mahal, north India.

## The story in four steps

1. After 1765 the Company wanted revenue but built no regular system; the Bengal famine of 1769-70 showed the cost (module 2).
2. Cornwallis fixed the demand for ever with zamindars in 1793; the revenue was so high that many zamindaris were sold (modules 3 and 4).
3. In the south and west, officials chose to deal with the cultivator (module 5); in the north-west they chose the village (module 6).
4. All three proved heavy for cultivators; the result was debt, cash-crop farming and unrest (module 8).

**Warning.** Cornwallis belongs to the Permanent Settlement only. The 2017 and 2024 Prelims questions both use the name Cornwallis as bait (module 10).

### 2. Foundation: from the Diwani to Hastings' experiments

*Objective:* Explain why the Company needed a land revenue policy after 1765, what Hastings' farming system was and why it failed.

## Mughal background (standard knowledge, verify)

Not in the extracts, so read this as a flagged sketch. Under the Mughals the state's claim was a share of the produce, assessed by officials and collected with the help of local hereditary landholders. The British did not invent land revenue; they changed who owned what, how much was taken and how rigidly.

## The Diwani and the Company's wish for revenue

On 12 August 1765 the Mughal emperor appointed the Company Diwan of Bengal (NCERT Class VIII). As Diwan it became the chief financial administrator and had to organise revenue. NCERT adds a telling detail: the Company still saw itself mainly as a trader, wanted large revenue but was unwilling to set up a regular system of assessment and collection. Within five years the value of goods it bought in Bengal doubled. Before 1765 it had imported gold and silver to pay for Indian goods; now Bengal revenue financed the purchases (NCERT hess202 p. 6, hess203 pp. 1-2). The full story of the Diwani and dual government is GS-027 module 8; do not re-learn it here.

NCERT records the result: artisans deserted villages because they were forced to sell cheaply, peasants could not pay, and in 1770 a famine killed, in its words, about ten million people, one third of the population. Spectrum places the famine in 1769-70 and says the greed and corruption of the Company's servants were partly responsible.

## Hastings' system (Izaredari, or farming)

When Warren Hastings took charge of Bengal (Governor from 1772, Governor-General from 1774 under the Regulating Act of 1773; Spectrum simply says governor general, verify) he adopted the **Izaredari** or **farming system** to bring order (Spectrum):

- Collection rights were given to contractors, called farmers, who offered the highest bid for a district or subdivision. Land was 'farmed out'.
- The right ran for five years at a time (the quinquennial settlement). It was made annual in **1777**. The year the five-year system began, 1772, is standard knowledge outside the extracts (verify).
- It rested on a belief that traditional zamindars were mere tax gatherers with no ownership rights, so zamindars were discouraged from bidding and many hereditary zamindars were ousted.

**Why it failed (Spectrum).** First, the contractors wanted profit and cared nothing for the peasants. Second, the sums they promised were more than the land could produce. Third, corruption reduced the revenue that reached the government. The country was impoverished, and as agriculture shrank, items the British traded in, such as silk and cotton, also declined. The surplus the British expected never came.

## The idea of permanence arrives

In **1776** Philip Francis, a member of Hastings' council, recommended a permanent settlement of land revenue. After about two decades of debate (NCERT), the Company sent Lord Cornwallis as governor general with instructions that settlements with the zamindars be made permanent.

**Pattern.** Each system reacts to the failure of the one before: farming wrecked revenue, permanence later starved the Company of growth, so temporary settlements followed.

### 3. Core: the Permanent Settlement of 1793

*Objective:* State who devised the Permanent Settlement, its features (ownership, fixed demand, 10/11 share, sunset rule) and the area it covered.

## Who decided and why

Cornwallis set up a committee of himself, Sir John Shore and James Grant (Spectrum). Spectrum gives his reasoning: he came from the landed aristocracy of Britain, favoured giving ownership to the zamindars and hoped that they would improve the land as English landlords did; and since cultivators were very many, collecting from the few zamindars was simpler. NCERT Class XII adds the Company's own thinking: a fixed demand would give a regular flow of revenue to the Company and would let investors in agriculture feel sure that the state would not siphon away their profit, producing a class of improving landowners loyal to the Company.

## Features (Spectrum)

1. **Proprietary rights** were given to the zamindars. Every piece of land in the settled area became part of some zamindari.
2. **A ten-year settlement in 1790, made permanent in 1793.** The revenue demand was fixed for ever; NCERT Class VIII stresses that it was never to be increased.
3. **The zamindar collected the rent** from the cultivators, who were now tenants (ryots or raiyyats), and paid a fixed sum to the Company.
4. **The 10/11 idea.** The zamindar could keep one-tenth to one-eleventh of what he collected and hand the rest to the Company government. Spectrum gives it in this form; the usual textbook phrasing is that about ten-elevenths went to the state (verify for exact figure).
5. **Free transfer.** As owner he could sell, mortgage or transfer the land and his heirs inherited it.
6. **The sunset clause.** Under the sunset rule, which Spectrum dates to 1794, if the tax was not paid by sunset on the due date, the zamindari was taken over and auctioned, and the rights passed to the buyer. (Spectrum: introduced in 1794; most standard accounts treat the Sunset Law as part of the 1793 regulations, verify.)
7. **Distraint.** Regulations of 1793, 1799 and 1812 allowed the zamindar to seize a defaulting tenant's property without going to a court.

The 2024 Prelims question puts a statement on the sunset rule in plain words: if the zamindar failed to pay by the fixed date he would be removed from his zamindari. That matches feature 6.

## A tension to hold in your head

Spectrum says zamindars were made owners. NCERT Class XII says that in terms of the Company's definition the zamindar was not a landowner of the village but a revenue collector who contracted to pay a fixed sum for his estate. Reconcile the two this way: the zamindar got a heritable and transferable right in the estate, but he was bound to pay the fixed revenue or lose it, and in the early years the Company also curtailed his powers (his troops were disbanded, customs duties he used to levy were abolished, and his courts were brought under a Collector, NCERT Class XII). He was a property holder and a state contractor at once.

## Area covered

Spectrum: about 19 per cent of British territory. Introduced in Bengal and Bihar, and extended to Orissa, Banaras (Varanasi) and northern Madras.

### 4. Core: the Permanent Settlement in practice, 1793 to the 1800s

*Objective:* Explain why zamindars defaulted, what happened to zamindaris and ryots, and why the Company and the cultivator both lost.

## The hope and the first result

Officials expected a class of improving landlords. NCERT Class XII opens with a picture of the opposite result. In **1797** the estates of the Raja of Burdwan were put to auction because he had piled up arrears. Many buyers turned out to be the raja's own servants and agents, and over 95 per cent of the sales were fictitious, so the raja stayed in control. NCERT adds that over 75 per cent of zamindaris changed hands after the settlement.

## Why zamindars defaulted (NCERT Class XII)

1. **A very high initial demand.** Because the demand was fixed for ever, the Company pitched it high, to avoid losing the gains of rising prices later.
2. **Depressed prices in the 1790s**, so ryots could not pay the zamindar, and the zamindar could not pay the Company.
3. **An invariable demand with the sunset rule.** The sum was due on a date regardless of the harvest.
4. **Curbs on the zamindar's own powers.** The Company wanted to control him, so his troops were disbanded, customs duties abolished and his courts placed under a Collector.

Rent collection was also a problem: rich ryots and village headmen (jotedars and mandals, NCERT; see GS-031) were glad to see the zamindar in trouble, and Burdwan alone had over 30,000 pending rent suits in 1798.

## What the zamindars did (Spectrum)

- **Estates were sold** and absentee landlordism grew, as merchants, officials and other zamindars bought them.
- **Subinfeudation.** To meet the high demand zamindars cut estates into small lots called patni taluqs and let them out permanently, on a promise of fixed rent, to patnidars.
- **Pattas were not issued.** Zamindars were meant to give each cultivator a written agreement stating what he owed; they did not, so peasants were exploited, driven to moneylenders and in Spectrum's phrase reduced to serfdom.
- **No improvement.** Zamindars concentrated on extracting rent. NCERT Class VIII agrees: even when prices rose and cultivation spread in the early nineteenth century, zamindars preferred to let land to tenants and take rent without risking investment.

## The Company's own loss

When prices rose (NCERT Class VIII says by the first decade of the nineteenth century; Class XII says after 1810, so give 'early nineteenth century'), the zamindars' incomes rose but the Company, with a demand fixed for ever, gained nothing. Spectrum's point: there was no way to increase the tax, so revenue could not grow with the expanding costs of the Company's wars.

## Why it was not repeated widely

NCERT Class XII says the Permanent Settlement was rarely extended beyond Bengal: after 1810 prices and zamindars' incomes rose while the state could claim none of it, so temporary settlements were made in newly annexed territories, helped by a new economic idea (module 5).

**Balanced note.** It gave the Company a predictable income and a landed class tied to British rule, but agriculture was not improved, the cultivator lost, and the state lost the future rise.

### 5. Core: the Ryotwari system in Madras and Bombay

*Objective:* State who devised Ryotwari, how it worked in Madras and Bombay, the revenue shares used and its main defects.

## The idea and its authors

NCERT Class VIII: in the south the Company did not find traditional zamindars, Read and Munro argued, so the settlement had to be made directly with the ryots who had tilled the land for generations, after careful and separate survey of each field. **Captain Alexander Read** tried it on a small scale in areas taken over after the wars with Tipu Sultan; **Thomas Munro** developed it and it was gradually extended over south India. Spectrum says that Read and Munro, sent in **1792** to the newly acquired **Baramahal** region, first fixed the amount each village had to pay, and that the system known as Ryotwari came when Munro became governor of Madras in 1820; NCERT describes the trials by Read as settlements with the ryots. Treat Baramahal (1792) as the experimental start and 1820 as the presidency-wide start. (Munro was Governor of Madras from 1820 to 1827 in standard accounts; NCERT's caption 1819-26 appears to be a slip, verify.) Munro thought officials should act as paternal father figures to the ryots (NCERT). Spectrum's reason from the Company's side: with no intermediaries, whatever was taken from the cultivator went straight to the government, so revenue was larger.

## Madras

- Munro reduced the tax to **one-third of the gross produce** and extended the system across the presidency except where the Permanent Settlement already applied. Spectrum notes that one-third of gross was nearly equal to the economic rent, and that the state demanded it regardless of yield or market prices.
- In unsurveyed districts the tax was often fixed arbitrarily on what the ryot had paid earlier: the **putcut** assessment. Ryots were coerced and even tortured, and many fell to moneylenders.
- In **1855** the Madras Torture Commission report exposed coercion, bribery and corruption by subordinate collectorate officials. From then a scientific survey and fresh assessment began. The new rate was **half of the net produce**, settled for **thirty years**, in a reformed system of **1864**. Prosperity followed, interrupted by famines in 1865-66 and 1876-78.

## Bombay

- Direct collection from peasants began in Gujarat in 1813-14. After the Peshwa's territory was conquered in 1818 the Madras pattern was introduced, under **Elphinstone**, a disciple of Munro and Governor of Bombay from 1819 to 1827.
- After a survey of 1824-28 the state demand was fixed at **55 per cent of the net produce**. Surveys were faulty, estimates of produce erroneous, assessment too high, and cultivators deserted fields. NCERT Class XII adds that prices fell after 1832 and a famine of 1832-34 devastated the Deccan.
- About 1836 Wingate and Goldsmith improved the method: each field assessed by soil and location, revenue somewhat reduced. By 1865 this covered most of the Deccan.

## Features (Spectrum)

1. Ownership and occupancy rights lay with the ryot, who could sublet, transfer or sell.
2. The ryot paid directly to the Company, in a range of **45 to 55 per cent** of estimated produce.
3. Revenue was **not fixed**; settlement was revised periodically (NCERT Class XII: resurvey every 30 years in the Deccan).
4. Land could be confiscated for non-payment, and in practice the ryot was more or less forced to cultivate.

**Defects.** Over-assessment; rigid, often brutal collection; bribery at assessment; and non-cultivating landlords could be registered as owners, turning actual cultivators into tenants or bonded labourers.

**Idea behind it.** Spectrum and NCERT Class XII link ryotwari to Utilitarian thinking and Ricardo's theory of rent: the state should tax the surplus above the average rent; Bengal's zamindars seemed to have become idle rentiers, which officials took as confirmation.

### 6. Core: the Mahalwari system in north India

*Objective:* State who devised Mahalwari, the key dates (1819, 1822, 1833, 1855), the revenue shares and how it differs from the zamindari and ryotwari systems.

## Why a third system

Officials could not fix revenue permanently when the Company needed more money (NCERT Class VIII). In the North Western Provinces of the Bengal Presidency (mostly now Uttar Pradesh), **Holt Mackenzie** devised a new system.

## Dates and people

- **1819**: Holt Mackenzie, secretary to the board of commissioners (Spectrum), recommends it.
- **1822**: **Regulation VII of 1822** formalises it. Spectrum's text prints 1922, which is a misprint; NCERT says the new system came into effect in 1822. Teach 1822.
- Agricultural depression in **1828** and a complex survey, high demand and harsh methods caused it to break down in practice.
- **1833**: under governor general William Bentinck a Regulation simplified the estimating of produce. **Merttins Bird**, called the Father of Land Settlements in Northern India, supervised the new scheme.
- **1855**: Dalhousie issued fresh directions to settlement officers, limiting the state demand to 50 per cent.

## How it worked

NCERT Class VIII: collectors went from village to village, inspecting land, measuring fields and recording the customs and rights of different groups. The estimated revenue of each plot in a village was added up to give the revenue the whole village, the **mahal**, had to pay. The demand was **revised periodically**, not fixed permanently. Collection and payment to the Company was assigned to the **village headman**, not the zamindar. NCERT adds that Holt Mackenzie believed the village was an important social institution of north India that needed to be preserved.

Spectrum's features:

- The unit of assessment was the mahal, a village or group of villages.
- The village community was considered owner of the land, with individual ownership rights lying with the cultivator, and each farmer gave his share of the tax.
- Collection was by the village headman, the **lambardar**, or a community of village leaders.
- State share: **66 per cent of the rental value** under Bentinck, settlement for **30 years**, later reduced to **50 per cent**; average rents for different soil classes were introduced.
- Regional names: **mauzawar** in the North Western Provinces and **malguzari** in the Central Provinces. Spectrum calls it a dual system: settlement was made collectively with the community and with individual landlords. It is called a **modified zamindari system** because the headman linked cultivator and government but did not have the rights of the zamindar.

## Area and results

Spectrum says Mahalwari covered about 30 per cent of British territory: major parts of the North Western Provinces, Central Provinces and the Punjab, with variations. (The Punjab arrangements came after its annexation, which is a standard fact outside these pages; verify.)

**Shortcomings (Spectrum).** Officials had to record every right and fix tax on every plot, practically impossible; calculations were guesswork and open to corruption; heavy assessment ruined village communities; land passed to moneylenders and merchants and old proprietors became tenants. Spectrum links this to the uprisings of 1857 (GS-032) and says collection cost more than it yielded (one book's claim; verify).

### 7. Core: comparison table and confusion traps

*Objective:* Compare the three systems cell by cell and avoid the standard name and share traps.

The table is for revision (dates, people and regions are in the master notes). Every cell comes from Spectrum or NCERT as stated in modules 3 to 6.

| Feature | Permanent (Zamindari) | Ryotwari | Mahalwari |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit of settlement | The zamindari | The individual ryot's field | The mahal (village or estate) |
| Who paid the state | Zamindar | Ryot directly | Village headman or village leaders |
| Owner of land (Spectrum) | Zamindar | Ryot | Village community (individual rights with cultivator) |
| Fixed or revised | Fixed for ever | Revised periodically (30 years later) | Revised periodically (30 years) |
| Share taken | About 10/11 of the collection | One-third gross (Munro); 55 per cent net (Bombay); 45-55 per cent; half of net (Madras 1864) | 66 per cent of rental, cut to 50 per cent in 1855 |
| Share of area (Spectrum) | About 19 per cent | About 51 per cent | About 30 per cent |

## Traps

1. **Cornwallis and ryotwari.** Cornwallis is tied only to the Permanent Settlement. The 2017 Prelims question lists Cornwallis, Alexander Read and Thomas Munro for the introduction of Ryotwari; the extracts connect Read and Munro, not Cornwallis, to it. The 2024 Prelims question mixes a ryotwari statement with the phrase 'revenue collection by Cornwallis'; read what each statement says about its own system.
2. **'Ryotwari means no tax in a bad harvest.'** Spectrum says the opposite: the state wanted its money regardless of yield and prices, and the revenue could be raised when production rose. A positive reason, not just silence, rules out the 2024 statement 1.
3. **Ryotwari is not only Madras.** Spectrum also names Bombay, Assam and other parts. Mahalwari is not only Punjab; it started in the North Western Provinces.
4. **Munro versus Mackenzie.** Munro is south; Mackenzie is north-west. Elphinstone was Munro's disciple in Bombay. Thomas Munro and Merttins Bird do not belong to the same system.
5. **'Ryotwari had no middlemen.'** It aimed for none, but non-cultivating landlords could be registered as owners (Spectrum).
6. **Percentages.** The share differs by system, province and period. State the system and the period with the percentage: 'one-third of gross' is Madras under Munro, '55 per cent of net' is Bombay in the 1820s, 'half of net' is Madras after 1855-64, '66 then 50 per cent of rental value' is Mahalwari.
7. **Largest share.** Ryotwari, not the Permanent Settlement (about 51 per cent).

**Test the hook.** A simple rule, 'the name tells you who signed', works for all three: zamindar, ryot, mahal. Edge case: Mahalwari is called a modified zamindari system, but only because a headman stands in the middle; he is not a zamindar with ownership rights.

### 8. Core: consequences, debt, commercialisation and the famine link

*Objective:* Explain how the three systems produced indebtedness, commercialisation, absentee landlordism and unrest, and state carefully what the sources say about famines.

## Spectrum's verdict

Spectrum says all the settlements proved disastrous for farmers and, with revenue fixed too high, for many zamindars too. Land became saleable and mortgageable to protect government revenue, and the benefit of the new private ownership did not reach the cultivators.

## Consequence 1: debt and the moneylender

Where revenue was demanded in cash and on a fixed date, cultivators borrowed. NCERT Class XII describes the cycle in the Bombay Deccan: revenue could rarely be paid without a loan; once a loan was taken it was hard to repay; by the 1840s officials found alarming indebtedness everywhere. Spectrum says the systems exposed peasants to moneylenders and middlemen, and that landlords handed collection to middlemen who squeezed more.

## Consequence 2: commercialisation of agriculture

Spectrum says exorbitant demands led to the commercialisation of agriculture: farmers grew for sale to pay cash revenue. NCERT Class VIII adds that the Company pushed crops Europe wanted: opium and indigo, then jute, tea, sugarcane, wheat, cotton and rice in different regions. NCERT Class XII gives the cotton boom: when the American Civil War broke out in 1861, American cotton exports to Britain collapsed, Bombay merchants pushed credit into the Deccan (Rs 100 advance per acre of cotton), acreage in the Bombay Deccan doubled between 1860 and 1864, and by 1862 over 90 per cent of Britain's cotton imports came from India. By 1865 credit was cut and debts were called in. NCERT Class XII adds that in the next Deccan settlement the revenue demand was raised by 50 to 100 per cent just as prices fell and credit dried up.

## Consequence 3: new landlord and tenant relations

- **Absentee landlordism** grew where estates were bought by merchants and officials, and revenue-collecting rights were sublet.
- **Two hostile groups.** Spectrum says the village split into a landowning class and a landless peasantry, artisans became landless labourers, and courts and formal procedure replaced custom.

## Consequence 4: unrest

The extracts tie the revenue system to several risings. Spectrum chapter 6 names the Permanent Settlement among the causes of the Chuar rebellion (1798) and says heavy taxes, diku moneylenders and zamindars lay behind the Santhal hul of 1855-56. NCERT Class XII places the Deccan riots at Supa in May 1875, when ryots burned moneylenders' account books (causes: GS-031 modules 2 and 6). Spectrum also links north India's dispossession to the discontent of 1857 (GS-032 module 3).

## What the sources say about famines (2022 Mains theme)

Direct statements in the extracts are limited, so be exact:

- The Bengal famine of 1769-70 followed Company misrule and corruption in revenue collection (Spectrum).
- NCERT Class XII shows the Deccan famine of 1832-34 striking a peasantry already pressed by high revenue, falling prices and debt; Spectrum notes famines in 1865-66 and 1876-78 under the reformed Madras system.
- Grain exports, trade policy, drain and relief policy are not in these extracts; they are for GS-030 (Economic drain and deindustrialisation, to come on Day 15, not yet written). Treat any specific famine statistic as verify.

A safe answer links revenue policy to vulnerability (money demanded irrespective of the harvest, debt, loss of land, fall of crafts) and says plainly that rainfall failure was the trigger while policy decided how far a failed harvest became a famine.

### 9. Master: balanced evaluation of the three systems

*Objective:* Weigh intended aims against results and write a balanced verdict for each system.

## Aims and results

| Aim | Result |
|---|---|
| A regular, predictable revenue (Permanent Settlement) | Achieved in the sense of a fixed sum, but the demand was high and had to be collected through auctions, and the Company could not share in later price rises |
| An improving class of landowners | Not achieved: zamindars preferred rent to investment; sub-leasing multiplied |
| Maximum revenue with no intermediaries (Ryotwari) | Often over-assessed; revenue rose but at the cost of flight, debt and coercion |
| Preserve village institutions and record rights (Mahalwari) | Survey and records improved administration on paper; in practice unworkable assessment, guesswork, dispossession |

## Case for each system (balanced)

- **Permanent Settlement.** For: certainty for the Company and for landlords; simple to administer because there were far fewer zamindars than cultivators (Spectrum). Against: revenue frozen while the Company's costs rose; cultivators left unprotected; subinfeudation and absentee landlords. NCERT Class XII shows how the first decades were harsh even for zamindars.
- **Ryotwari.** For: rights vested in the ryot, revenue adjustable, later reforms (Wingate and Goldsmith, Madras 1864) eased the burden. Against: demand often beyond the land's capacity, harsh collection, faulty surveys.
- **Mahalwari.** For: it recognised village customs and records of rights and set limits (66 to 50 per cent, longer settlements). Against: the volume of record keeping, inaccuracy and heavy assessment.

## The shared logic

Spectrum's point: all three systems were built to extract the maximum from the land. The difference lay in the middleman chosen. Ricardian ideas shaped the 1820s Bombay Deccan settlement and the move away from permanence (NCERT Class XII); Spectrum notes the Utilitarian and Ricardian background of ryotwari.

## Historians and the larger frame

Whether ideas (Ricardo, the English landlord model), financial need or the lack of rural intermediaries mattered most is debated; the extracts show all three at work. A fuller historiography is outside these pages; treat any named 'school' as verify.

## Links forward

- The Moderates demanded a reduction of land revenue, and the early Congress said British rule's land revenue increases had impoverished peasants and zamindars (GS-036 module 7).
- Later tenancy law and zamindari abolition are standard topics outside the extracts (verify).
- Famine, drain and deindustrialisation are in GS-030 (Day 15, not yet written).

**Balanced conclusion template.** The systems gave the Company a legal, recorded and collectible revenue but turned land into a commodity and the cultivator into a debtor, and the later debates on peasant indebtedness and landlordism start here.

## 2022 Mains Q3 (10 marks): why a spurt in famines since the mid-eighteenth century?

**Frame (150 words).** Introduction: famine is triggered by crop failure but turned into mass death by policy and poverty. Body: (1) revenue demand fixed in cash and collected irrespective of yield (Spectrum), with over-assessment and coercion; (2) debt and landlessness left no reserves (NCERT Class XII on the Deccan 1832-34); (3) commercialisation shifted farm effort to export crops (module 8); (4) ruin of crafts pushed more people onto land (Spectrum); (5) Bengal 1769-70 showed the Company's early revenue greed; (6) points outside the extracts to add with 'verify': grain exports, the drain, weak relief until the Famine Commissions, and railways that carried grain away. Conclusion: policy, not rainfall alone, explains the intensity.

## A 15-mark frame for the systems

Introduction with Spectrum's aim (maximum extraction); table of the three (module 7); impact: debt, commercialisation, ownership change, unrest; balanced evaluation (module 9); conclusion.

### Notes: Chronology of land revenue policy

- 1765: Company becomes Diwan of Bengal (12 August 1765, NCERT).
- 1769-70 (NCERT: 1770): Bengal famine; Spectrum blames in part the rapacity and corruption of Company servants.
- Five-year farming settlement under Hastings (start year 1772, standard knowledge, verify); made annual in 1777.
- 1776: Philip Francis recommends a permanent settlement.
- 1790: ten-year settlement with zamindars; 1793: made permanent (Cornwallis).
- 1792: Read and Munro in the Baramahal (experimental start; Spectrum: village-wise fixing; NCERT: ryot-wise trials).
- 1794: sunset rule (Spectrum's date; books differ, verify).
- 1820: Munro governor of Madras and introduces ryotwari (Spectrum; verify the term, NCERT caption 1819-26).
- 1813-14: direct collection from peasants in Gujarat; 1819-27 Elphinstone governor of Bombay; 1824-28 survey with 55 per cent of net produce; about 1836 Wingate and Goldsmith.
- 1819: Holt Mackenzie recommends Mahalwari; 1822: Regulation VII; 1828 depression; 1833 Regulation (Bentinck) and Bird's scheme; 1855 Dalhousie limits demand to 50 per cent.
- 1855: Madras Torture Commission report; 1864: reformed Madras settlement (half of net, thirty years).
- 1859: Blue (indigo) rebellion (NCERT); 1861-65 cotton boom in the Deccan; May 1875 Deccan riots (NCERT Class XII).

### Notes: Key terms

- Izaredari: Hastings' farming system, revenue rights given to highest bidders for a fixed term.
- Patni taluq: a permanent sublease created by a Bengal zamindar to meet the high demand; the holder was a patnidar; the process is subinfeudation.
- Patta: the written agreement stating what a tenant must pay; zamindars were meant to issue it.
- Putcut: Madras assessment fixed arbitrarily on what the ryot had paid in earlier years.
- Lambardar: village headman who collected and paid the tax in the Mahalwari system.
- Mauzawar (North Western Provinces) and malguzari (Central Provinces): regional names for Mahalwari variants.
- Sunset rule: if the revenue was not paid by sunset of the due date, the zamindari was auctioned.
- Rentier (NCERT Class XII): a person who lives on rental income from property.

### Notes: Who is associated with what

- Warren Hastings: farming system, five-year then annual settlement (1777).
- Philip Francis: recommended permanence in 1776.
- Cornwallis, Shore, Grant: Permanent Settlement committee and settlement (1793).
- Alexander Read and Thomas Munro: Ryotwari (Baramahal, Madras).
- Elphinstone: Bombay, disciple of Munro, Governor 1819-27.
- Wingate and Goldsmith: improved Bombay assessment about 1836.
- Holt Mackenzie: Mahalwari proposal 1819 and 1822 system; Bentinck: 1833 Regulation; Merttins Bird: supervised; Dalhousie: 1855 limit of 50 per cent.
- H.T. Colebrook: 1806 description of Bengal under-tenants (NCERT).

### Notes: Cause and effect: from revenue demand to unrest

- High cash demand, rigid dates and collection regardless of yield lead to borrowing from moneylenders.
- Borrowing leads to loss of land to moneylenders, merchants and absentee landlords; cultivators become tenants or labourers.
- Cash demand and export demand lead to commercialisation: indigo, cotton, jute and others; the 1860s cotton boom ended in credit contraction in 1865.
- Ruin of crafts and more land pressure add to vulnerability.
- Vulnerability plus a failed harvest turns scarcity into famine; the extracts name Bengal 1769-70, Deccan 1832-34, Madras 1865-66 and 1876-78.
- Dispossession and debt feed risings: Chuar 1798, Santhal 1855-56, Deccan 1875, and the north Indian discontent of 1857.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Cornwallis versus Munro: Cornwallis (permanent, zamindar, Bengal); Munro (ryotwari, ryot, south).
- Mahalwari is not ryotwari: it settles with a village unit and a headman; ryotwari with the individual cultivator.
- Revenue (to the state) is not rent (to the landlord).
- Ryotwari is not 'fixed forever'; revision was built in.
- Raiyatwari and ryotwari are spellings of the same word; do not treat them as two systems.

### Notes: Source slips and differences between books

- Spectrum: 'Regulation VII of 1922'; correct year 1822 (NCERT: came into effect 1822).
- Spectrum: Munro introduced ryotwari as governor in 1820; NCERT caption 1819-26 appears to be a slip; standard 1820-27 (verify).
- Spectrum: 1855 Madras rate half of net; Bombay 55 per cent net; features list 45-55 per cent: attach period and place to each.
- Spectrum: sunset clause introduced in 1794; most standard accounts treat the Sunset Law as part of the 1793 Permanent Settlement regulations (verify).
- NCERT Class VIII: prices rose 'by the first decade of the nineteenth century'; Class XII: 'after 1810'.
- Spectrum: zamindars owners; NCERT Class XII: the zamindar was defined as a revenue collector for his estate; both views need stating (module 3).
- Famine of 1769-70 (Spectrum) versus 1770 (NCERT): both refer to the same famine.

### Core concepts

- Land revenue is the state's share of produce; each system chose a different person to deal with.
- Permanent: zamindar, fixed for ever, 1793, about 19 per cent; Ryotwari: ryot, revised, about 51 per cent; Mahalwari: mahal, revised, about 30 per cent.
- All three aimed at extraction and produced debt, commercialisation and unrest.

### Practice

**Q.** What is the difference between revenue and rent?

**A.** Revenue goes to the state; rent goes to a landlord. A zamindar collects rent and pays revenue.

**Q.** Name the person each system deals with: Permanent, Ryotwari, Mahalwari.

**A.** Zamindar; ryot (cultivator); village or estate (mahal) through its headman.

**Q.** Which of the three covered the largest share of British territory in Spectrum?

**A.** Ryotwari, about 51 per cent.

**Q.** Why did Cornwallis choose zamindars rather than cultivators?

**A.** He favoured the English landlord model and hoped zamindars would improve land, and collecting from a few zamindars was simpler than from many cultivators (Spectrum).

**Q.** Why did the Company move away from permanent settlement after 1810?

**A.** A demand fixed for ever gave the Company no share in the rise in prices and incomes, and it needed more revenue; so temporary settlements were made in newly acquired areas, and Ricardian ideas favoured taxing surplus rent (NCERT Class XII).

**Q.** How did the Mahalwari differ from ryotwari in unit and collector?

**A.** Unit was the village or estate (mahal), not the individual ryot's holding, and the village headman or leaders collected instead of each ryot paying separately.

**Q.** A statement says: 'Under the Permanent Settlement the revenue demand rose with every increase in prices.' Evaluate it.

**A.** Wrong: the demand was fixed for ever (NCERT), so the Company could not claim a share of the rise in prices; that is one reason it later preferred revisable settlements.

**Q.** Use the Deccan cotton boom to show how commercialisation raised peasant debt.

**A.** In 1860-64 cotton acreage doubled on easy credit during the American Civil War; when American supply returned in 1865, credit was cut and debts were demanded, leaving the majority of cotton growers heavily indebted (NCERT Class XII).

**Q.** Explain in four sentences how revenue policy and famine are linked in the sources.

**A.** Cash demand regardless of yield caused borrowing; debt and loss of land removed reserves; commercialisation and crafts' decline added vulnerability; rainfall failure then turned into famine, as in Bengal 1769-70 and Deccan 1832-34.
