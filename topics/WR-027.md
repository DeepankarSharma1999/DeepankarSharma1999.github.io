## WR-027: Comprehension: main idea and inference

English Paper B (qualifying) · Writing/Language · Reading and writing

### Bridge: Sentence and paragraph structure vocabulary

A passage is built from paragraphs, and a paragraph is built from sentences that do different jobs. Learn these names before you start, because the rest of this lesson uses them.

- **Topic sentence**: the sentence that states what a paragraph is about and the point it makes. It is often the first sentence, sometimes the last.
- **Supporting detail**: a reason, example, figure, quotation or comparison that backs up the topic sentence. Details are evidence; they are not the point itself.
- **Transition**: a word or phrase that links one sentence or paragraph to the next, such as *however*, *for instance* or *as a result*.
- **Concluding sentence**: a sentence that closes the paragraph by restating or drawing out the point.
- **Signal word**: a small word that tells you the direction of the author's thought. Contrast: *but, yet, however, although*. Addition: *moreover, also*. Cause and result: *because, since, therefore, so*. Example: *for instance, such as*. Concession (admitting a point before disagreeing): *admittedly, of course, it is true that*.
- **Paragraph map**: your own one-line note of what each paragraph does, written in the margin or on rough paper.

The tags used in the CSAT lesson (CT-001, module 3) are the same idea applied sentence by sentence: claim, support, contrast, qualification and conclusion. In this lesson the topic sentence of a paragraph is usually its local claim.

One caution: not every paragraph begins with its topic sentence. In argumentative prose the first sentence may state the view the author is about to reject, and the real topic sentence follows the word *however*.

### Bridge: Tone and attitude words

**Tone** is the attitude an author shows towards the subject or the reader, and it is carried by word choice: a writer who says *enslaved and distorted* feels differently from one who says *used*. Describing a tone needs precise words, so build this list now. The deeper treatment of tone and argument belongs to the next topic (WR-028); here you need the vocabulary and the habit of quoting a clue.

| Tone word | Meaning | Typical clue |
|---|---|---|
| appreciative / admiring | values something and says so | praise words: *admirable, remarkable* |
| sympathetic | shares or understands the feelings of others | attention to hardship |
| optimistic / hopeful | expects a good outcome | *will improve, promise* |
| enthusiastic | eager and energetic | strong positive verbs |
| objective / neutral | reports without showing feeling | facts, figures, no judgement words |
| analytical | examines parts and causes calmly | *because, consists of* |
| balanced / measured | weighs both sides | *however, on the other hand* |
| cautionary | warns of a risk | *may fail, beware, unless* |
| critical | points out faults | *flawed, fails to, distorted* |
| sceptical | doubts a claim until shown evidence | *it is claimed, doubtful* |
| cynical | assumes people act from bad motives | sneering explanations |
| ironic | says one thing and means the opposite | praise that is clearly not meant |
| indignant | angry at unfairness | strong accusing words |
| dismissive | treats something as unimportant | *merely, hardly worth* |
| nostalgic / wistful | longs gently for the past | memories, *once, used to* |
| pessimistic | expects a bad outcome | *no hope, decline* |

Notice the pairs that learners confuse: sceptical doubts, but cynical assumes bad motives; critical finds faults, but dismissive refuses to take the matter seriously; objective has no feeling, but balanced has a view and still weighs both sides.

### 1. What the English Paper B comprehension section looks like

*Objective:* Describe the status of Paper B, the comprehension clause and the shape of the ten years of verified comprehension questions.

## The paper and the clause

English is Paper B of the Mains written examination. It carries 300 marks, it is of matriculation (school-leaving) standard, and it is qualifying in nature. Its marks are not counted for your rank. The rule that matters is the cut-off: according to the project's syllabus registry (Plan-10, taken from the 2026 notification; re-check against each year's notification), the Essay, General Studies and Optional answer books of a candidate are taken into account only if the candidate scores at least 25 per cent in the English paper and 25 per cent in the Indian language paper. Twenty-five per cent of 300 is 75 marks. Paper B is therefore a gate, not a race.

The clause for this topic reads exactly: *Comprehension of given passages.* The aim of the language papers (Plan-15) is to test whether you can read and understand serious discursive prose (writing that discusses or argues a point rather than telling a story) and express ideas clearly and correctly. Other clauses of the same paper (Précis Writing, Usage and Vocabulary, Short Essays) are separate topics. The next topic, WR-028, deals with grammar, argument and tone in comprehension, and WR-029 deals with the précis. For the same idea in Hindi, see WR-013; for the multiple-choice version of the skill, see CT-001 and CT-002.

## What ten years of verified papers show

The project holds 50 official records from the English (qualifying B) papers of 2017 to 2026, each checked against the printed page images. Reading them gives these facts:

- Each year has one long passage followed by five questions, labelled Q2(a) to Q2(e). That is 10 passages and 50 questions.
- The passages run from about 600 to 1,200 words. They are serious prose.
- The questions are **descriptive**: you write sentences. There are no options to choose from.
- The record text carries no word limit, so this lesson does not assert one. Read the instructions on the printed paper.
- Each record carries the figure 15 in the project data (marks per sub-question or per question; verify on the printed paper).

| Year | Subject of the passage (Mission 90 description) |
|---|---|
| 2017 | the history and growth of tourism |
| 2018 | why north-Indian armies lost to the Turkish invaders, with a quotation from Alberuni |
| 2019 | what history is, how it is distorted, and what civilisation means |
| 2020 | the dignity of labour from Tolstoy and Gandhi to international work-camps |
| 2021 | breakdown of the ecological life-support systems |
| 2022 | how scientists related humans to the great apes |
| 2023 | rights and duties |
| 2024 | how British rule justified itself through the idea of civilisation |
| 2025 | an essay on reading books and finding arguments |
| 2026 | the empowerment of India and its standing abroad |

## What the records do not contain

None of the 50 questions says "main idea", "title" or "tone", yet the skills are needed: a *why* or *explain* question can be answered well only if you know what the passage argues, and *comment* needs alertness to the author's attitude. The lesson teaches the whole reading kit but targets the verified question types.

### 2. What each question verb asks you to write

*Objective:* Classify a descriptive comprehension question by its directive verb and say what shape of answer it needs.

## Why start with the question and not the passage

In a descriptive paper the marks go to an answer that does what the question says. A fine paragraph that answers a different question earns little. So before you read anything closely, learn the small set of directives that appear in the verified records.

## Five families of question

Mission 90 grouped the 50 verified questions by their directive. This is the project's own classification and some questions could fit two families; use it as a map, not as an official scheme.

| Family | Typical opening words in the records | What your answer must contain | Count of 50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieve and describe | *What was the state of...*, *How does the author describe...*, *What part did...* | A short, accurate description gathered from the passage | 15 |
| Cause or reason | *Why...*, *What made...*, *What led to...* | A reason, with the link from reason to result made clear | 9 |
| List or enumerate | *Enumerate...*, *What are the factors / suggestions / points...* | Separate numbered points, each complete in itself | 16 |
| Meaning | *Explain the statement...*, *Elaborate...*, *What does the author mean by...*, the meaning of an idiom | What the words mean, in your own words, and why the author says it | 7 |
| Judge or compare | *Comment on...*, *Give your critical observations...*, *How is X different from Y* | A stated view or difference, supported with evidence from the passage | 3 |

The counts are reproduced as the records are coded. Questions of the form *How is X related (or linked) to Y* (2020 b, 2022 b) are coded as retrieve and describe, because the passage states the relation and you report it; a few could also be read as comparison or cause questions, which is why the families are a map and not a scheme.

Two lessons follow from the counts. Lists and plain retrieval together make up well over half the questions, so the first skill is to gather points accurately. Meaning questions are fewer but have clear traps (module 5 and module 6). Comment questions are the rarest and the easiest to ruin by adding opinions the passage does not support.

## Parsing a question in ten seconds

Read each question and mark three things:

1. **The verb**: *enumerate*, *explain*, *comment*, *why*. It decides the shape of the answer.
2. **The topic words**: the nouns that tell you where in the passage to look.
3. **The limits**: phrases such as *according to the author*, *in post-Independence India*, *in the passage*. They say that you must answer from the passage and from that part of it.

Example from the records: *How does the author describe the state of tourism in the post-Independence India?* The verb is *describe*; the topic words are *state of tourism*; the limit is *post-Independence India*. A beginner who writes the history of world tourism has ignored the limit. The right answer lives in only one paragraph.

## According to the author

Many questions say *according to the author* or *in the passage*. They are not asking what is true; they are asking what the passage says. If the passage says something you believe to be wrong, you still report it, and you may add that it is the author's claim. If the passage is silent, do not fill the gap from memory: say what the passage does tell you, or answer from the passage's closest statement. Module 5 shows how.

## Link with the CSAT lessons

The multiple-choice stems in CT-001 ask for the same understanding, but the examiner supplies four options. Here you supply the sentence yourself, so every habit that CT-001 trains for *rejecting* wrong options (too broad, too narrow, outside knowledge) becomes a habit for *not writing* wrong sentences.

### 3. Reading the passage twice: claim, support, contrast

*Objective:* Read a long passage once for structure and a second time for the evidence a question points to, and build a one-line paragraph map.

## The problem with long passages

A 600-word to 1,200-word passage is too long to hold in your head. A beginner reads it once, hurriedly, and then answers each question by guessing where the answer lies. A better reader reads twice, with different jobs each time.

## Pass 1: read for structure (do not stop)

Read at normal speed to the end. As you finish each paragraph, write a four-to-eight-word note of its job, using the tags from the bridge module and CT-001: claim, support, contrast, qualification, conclusion. Circle every contrast word (*but, yet, however, although*) and every list word (*first, second, finally*). Do not underline phrases yet.

For example, the 2019 passage on history can be mapped this way:

| Paragraph | Job | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Claim | history covers everything human, all fields |
| 2 | Problem | history is distorted for selfish ends; truth sacrificed to patriotism |
| 3 | Change | the "new school" is broader and uses many sciences |
| 4 | Warning | the new history has dangers: popularising, losing depth, fiction |
| 5 | Difficulty | civilisation is hard to define; several definitions |

Now look at the five questions of 2019. They ask why history is all-inclusive (paragraph 1), how it has been distorted (2), how the author describes the new school (3), what the dangers are (4), and why civilisation is hard to define (5). The questions follow the paragraphs in order. The same is clear in 2017 (motives, Europe, technology, India, future), and largely in 2024 and 2026. Take this as a hint and not as a rule: 2020 and 2025 ask questions that gather material from several places.

## Pass 2: read for the question

Read question (a). Using its topic words, go straight back to the paragraph your map points to. Read that paragraph slowly, mark the sentences that answer the question, and note the signal words. Write your answer. Then go to question (b). You never reread the whole passage for each question.

## Three habits that protect your marks

1. **Map before you answer.** Two minutes spent on the map save more than two minutes of hunting.
2. **Trust the contrast word.** What comes before *but* is often the view the author sets aside; what comes after is usually the point. In the 2018 passage, the opening sentence reports what is "often said" about religious martyrdom, and the real argument follows with "But it is more likely".
3. **Stay inside the covers.** Answer from the passage. Module 2 called this the limit of *according to the author*.

## Reading speed

Reading speed with understanding varies widely from person to person. If you assume a practice figure of 150 to 200 words per minute (an assumption, not a measured or official figure), Pass 1 of a 700-word passage takes about 3.5 to 4.7 minutes and a 1,200-word passage 6 to 8 minutes. Time yourself on a real passage and use your own figure.

### 4. Main idea, theme, title and gist in written answers

*Objective:* Separate topic, main idea, theme, title and gist, and say how the main idea steers what you include in a written answer.

## The same ladder as in CT-001

CT-001 (module 2) taught a ladder of terms. WR-013 taught the Hindi equivalents. The English terms are used here in exactly the same way, so only a short refresher is needed.

| Term | What it is | Form | Example for a passage on rights and duties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic | What the passage is about | A noun phrase | rights and duties |
| Main idea (central idea) | What the author claims about the topic | One full sentence | Rights and duties cannot be separated, because society works on give and take |
| Theme | The larger concern behind the claim | An abstract noun phrase | the relation between citizen and society |
| Title | A short label | A phrase | "Two sides of one coin" |
| Gist | The passage compressed | One to three sentences | The main idea plus the key reason |

A topic names a subject; a main idea says something *about* it. A bare topic such as "rights and duties" is the commonest weak answer.

## The three-part test (from WR-013)

Test any candidate main idea:

1. Does it cover the whole passage, or only one paragraph?
2. Is it a claim with a verb and a judgement, not a bare noun?
3. Is it the author's idea, with no opinion or fact of yours added?

## What the main idea does for a descriptive answer

The verified questions rarely ask for the main idea directly. It still matters, in three ways.

- **It sorts the details.** A passage holds more facts than any answer needs. The main idea tells you which facts serve the argument. In the 2024 passage on British rule, the long list of British remarks about Indian society is support for the claim that the British thought India only half-civilised; if a question asks why they believed they were giving a gift, the main idea (justification through civilisation) tells you what to select.
- **It guards against distortion.** If your answer to a *why* question contradicts the passage's main idea, it has gone wrong somewhere.
- **It gives you your first sentence.** An answer that begins with the main point of the relevant paragraph is easier to read and to mark.

## Local and global main ideas

Every paragraph has its own main idea (its topic sentence), and the whole passage has a global one. Most verified questions point at one paragraph, so the local idea is what you need for each answer. The global idea is the check that all the local answers fit together.

## Common traps

- Writing the first sentence of the passage as the main idea. It may state the view the author rejects.
- Writing a detail, however striking, as the main idea.
- Writing a title when a sentence is required, or a sentence when a short phrase is required.
- Mixing in your own view: "...and this is very true." Examiners cannot reward it.

## A one-minute drill

After Pass 1, say to yourself: "The author's point is that ...". If you cannot finish the sentence without the word *and* three times, your idea is too broad; if it is only a noun, it is only a topic.

## The closest verified question to a main-idea task

The nearest thing in the records is 2018 (e): 'Enumerate the major view points of the given passage.' It asks for the passage's main positions as a list, so a firm grasp of the main idea and of each paragraph's job does the work.

### 5. Fact, inference, assumption and opinion in written answers

*Objective:* Distinguish what a passage states, what it implies, what it assumes and what is only the writer's opinion or guess, and write each with the right words.

## Five kinds of sentence

CT-002 gave the logic vocabulary: premise, conclusion, inference, assumption. In a written answer you need to handle five kinds of sentence.

| Kind | Meaning | Test | How to write it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stated fact | Said plainly in the passage | Can you point to the sentence? | "The passage states that ..." or simply report it |
| Opinion or claim | The author's judgement | Does it use words like *should*, *remarkable*, *the most critical*? | "The author holds that ..." |
| Inference | What follows from stated sentences | If the passage is true, must or likely must this be true? | "This suggests that ..." or "It can be inferred that ..." |
| Assumption | An unstated belief the argument rests on | If you deny it, does the argument collapse? | "The author takes for granted that ..." |
| Speculation | A guess offered with a hedge | Words such as *perhaps, possibly, appears, alternatively* | Keep the hedge: "possible reasons are ..." |

The distinction used in CT-001 holds here too: an assumption comes *before* the argument, and an inference comes *after* it.

## The rule of the traceable sentence

You may write an inference only if you can name the sentence in the passage that supports it. If you cannot point to one, you are bringing outside knowledge, and an answer built on it is a risk. A useful test: finish the sentence "I say this because the passage says ...".

## Keep the hedge

The 2018 passage on the North-Western passes shows why. The author does not say why the passes went undefended; the author offers possibilities (*perhaps* the need was not seen as urgent; *alternatively* the passes were left open for trade; local chiefs *derived an income* from that trade). The question asks why the rulers did not fortify the passes. A strong answer reports these as possible explanations. A weak answer states one of them as fact ("They did not fortify because it was too expensive"), which the passage never says, or turns the hedged reasons into certainty.

## Two worked inferences from the records

- The 2020 passage lists what Tolstoy, Gandhi, Thoreau and Ruskin did, and then says Pierre Ceresole used the same basic principles to promote *international reconciliation*. The question asks how Pierre's work was different. The answer is an inference from a contrast: the earlier figures demonstrated the dignity of labour for individuals and communities; Pierre used shared labour to bring people of former enemy nations together.
- The 2023 question on the duties of the state is answered by inference, because the passage talks mostly about citizens' duties. Module 8 treats it in detail.

## When the question and the passage use different words

Sometimes the question's wording is not the passage's wording. In 2021 the question asks for the factors responsible for the *evolution* of life on earth, while the passage speaks of the basic services that made life *possible* for millions of years. The sensible approach is to answer with the passage's services (climate and radiation regulation, decomposition by microbes, biological diversity, and productivity that can be advanced sustainably) and, if useful, to say in a phrase that this is how the passage describes the conditions of life.

## Fact versus opinion in the author's voice

Phrases such as *to my mind* (2026), *it appears* (2018) and *perhaps we can agree* (2019) mark the author's judgement. Report them as the author's view. Do not turn them into facts.

### 6. Vocabulary in context, idioms and tone

*Objective:* Work out the meaning of a word, phrase or idiom from its context, and name the tone of a passage with a clue.

## Why meaning-of-phrase questions appear

Several verified questions ask what a phrase means: *leave no stone unturned* (2023), *self-evident propositions* and *tautologies* (2025), and the statement beginning *their civilization, in their view* (2024). They test whether you understand the passage's language and can restate it simply.

## Five context clues

When you meet a word you do not know, look at the sentence and its neighbours for these clues:

| Clue | What it looks like | Example (original) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | The writer explains the word | "A *tautology*, that is, a statement true by definition, ..." |
| Contrast | An opposite word nearby | "Unlike the *frugal* mother, the son spent freely." |
| Example | Illustrations follow | "She loved *rodents*: mice, hamsters and rats." |
| Cause and result | The word names a cause or an effect | "Her *negligence* caused the fire: she left the stove on." |
| Tone of the sentence | Positive or negative surroundings | "The *dilapidated* house was on the verge of collapse." |

## A four-step method

1. Read the whole sentence and the next one.
2. Guess a meaning from the clues.
3. Put your guess into the sentence in place of the word. Does the sentence still make sense and say what the paragraph says?
4. Write the meaning in plain words, then say what it refers to in the passage.

## Idioms and phrases

An **idiom** is a group of words whose meaning is not the sum of the words. *Leave no stone unturned* does not concern stones; it means to try every possible means, to make every effort. In the 2023 passage it is used to describe the effort citizens should make for their country's strength. An answer that writes only "to try hard" is acceptable; an answer that adds the passage's use is better.

For a **phrase** the author coins or defines, use the author's own explanation. In the 2025 passage a tautology is a statement whose truth is contained in the meaning of its words, and the example given is that a father of a father is a grandfather. A *self-evident proposition* is one that neither writer nor reader can deny. The passage places them in different compartments, which module 9 explains.

## Answer pattern for meaning questions

Write: **X means** (plain meaning). **In the passage** it refers to (context). Two sentences are usually enough.

## Tone: reading the attitude

Tone is shown by word choice. A few examples from the verified passages, quoting only clue words:

- 2019: the words *enslaved and distorted* and *zealots* show a critical, disapproving tone about misuse of history.
- 2026: *marvels* and *admiration* show a proud, optimistic tone about India.
- 2024: *illicit logical move* and *absolutised* show an analytical but critical tone towards British claims.

Use the tone vocabulary in the second bridge module. A tone is named with a word, justified with a clue, and not asserted without one. Tone matters in *comment* and *critical observations* answers because your comment should respect the author's attitude: do not praise what the author is criticising.

The verified records do not contain a question that asks for tone directly. Treat it as a supporting skill, to be studied more fully in WR-028.

### 7. Writing the answer: structure, own words and common mistakes

*Objective:* Write a short answer with an answer sentence and a supporting reference, in your own words, and avoid the six commonest mistakes.

## The two-part answer

For most questions a good answer has two parts:

1. **Answer sentence**: one sentence that gives the direct answer in your own words, picking up the question's key words.
2. **Supporting reference**: one fact, reason or short example from the passage, paraphrased, that shows where the answer comes from.

For a list question, make each point its own numbered sentence. For a meaning question, state the meaning, then the reason the author says it. For a comment question, state your view and give two reasons drawn from the passage. Keep present tense when reporting the author: "The author argues ...".

## Own words, not copied words

Copying a sentence shows that you can find it, not that you understand it. Paraphrase this way: read the sentence, look away, say it to yourself as you would to a friend, and write that. Then compare: the meaning must be the same and the sentence structure different. Names, dates and technical terms (the Marshall Plan, haemoglobin) cannot be changed; keep them.

Original before-and-after (not from any paper):

- Source: "The committee postponed its decision because the figures were incomplete."
- Weak copy: "The committee postponed its decision because the figures were incomplete."
- Better: "Since it lacked full data, the committee put off deciding."

## Length

The verified records print no word limit, so no official limit is claimed here. If the printed paper gives a limit, keep close to it (as a practice habit, within about ten per cent). If none is given, let length follow the question: one or two sentences for a plain fact, a short numbered list for an enumeration, and a compact paragraph for explain or comment questions. A long answer is risky: the longer you write, the more room there is for error and for contradicting the passage.

## Six mistakes and their fixes

| Mistake | Why it costs marks | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Copying whole sentences | No evidence of understanding; padded answer | Paraphrase; keep only names and terms |
| Adding outside knowledge | The question says *according to the passage* | Test: "the passage says ..." |
| Over-long answers | More errors, wasted time | Stop after the point and one support |
| Missing the directive | A list is asked, a paragraph given | Re-read the verb before writing |
| Turning hedges into facts | Misreports the author | Keep *perhaps, suggests, appears* |
| Careless language | Errors in agreement, tense or spelling | Reread each answer once; check subject-verb agreement and tense |

## Language checks that take thirty seconds

Check that each sentence has a subject and a verb; that singular subjects take singular verbs; that you use one tense consistently; that your spelling of the passage's names is right; and that pronouns such as *they* and *it* clearly refer to something. Short sentences are easier to keep correct than long ones.

## A pattern to remember

Directive, answer sentence, support, stop. If you can say the answer aloud in two sentences, you can write it in two sentences.

### 8. Worked analyses, set one: correlation, state duties and hedged reasons

*Objective:* Analyse three official questions (2023 a and b, 2018 b), find what each asks and where its trap lies, and compare your own attempt with an original sample.

## How to use these analyses

Attempt each question first, using your own copy of the paper, then read the analysis. The samples are original Mission 90 answers that teach method; they are not official answers, no official key is asserted, and you should not memorise them. Only short phrases from the passages are quoted.

## Analysis 1: 2023 (a) "What is the correlation between rights and duties?"

**What it asks.** Correlation means a two-way relationship. The verb family is retrieve and describe.

**Where to look.** The opening of the passage says that rights and duties are "two sides of the same coin" and that the relationship is *two fold*. Each fold begins with a signal word: *Firstly* (society works on reciprocity) and *Secondly* (claims against the state imply a duty to contribute).

**Trap.** Quoting the figure of speech and stopping, or giving only the first fold; the marks lie in the two reasons.

**Original Mission 90 sample.** Rights and duties depend on each other. First, society works on give and take: my right requires others to respect it and requires me to respect theirs. Second, because the state creates the conditions in which I can develop, I owe it useful work in return. A society that stresses rights and neglects duties would drift into disorder.

## Analysis 2: 2023 (b) "Enumerate the duties of the state mentioned in the passage."

**What it asks.** A list. The phrase *mentioned in the passage* is the limit.

**Where to look.** The passage speaks mostly of the duties of citizens. The state appears in a few sentences: it "creates those conditions in which we can realise ourselves", and the phrase about the state "fostering a climate conducive to happiness of the individuals". The closing lines add that a society which does not secure a person's right lowers that person's status.

**Trap.** Importing a textbook list of a welfare state's duties (none is in the passage), or answering the neighbouring question about citizens.

**Original Mission 90 sample.** The passage says little directly about the state's duties, so the answer has to be gathered from a few statements. (1) The state creates the conditions in which citizens can realise themselves. (2) It helps to foster a climate in which individuals can be happy. (3) By implication, it should secure the rights of citizens, since the closing lines say that a society which fails to do so lowers a person's standing. Point 3 is an inference and is weaker than points 1 and 2; state it with that care.

## Analysis 3: 2018 (b) "According to the passage 'the Indian rulers did not find it necessary to fortify the North-Western Pass'. Why ?"

**What it asks.** A reason. The question's own phrasing is firmer than the passage's: the words in quotation marks in the question do not appear in the passage, which says that fortifying the passes "was not thought feasible" and then offers several hedged explanations.

**Where to look.** The third paragraph. The words *perhaps*, *alternatively* and *it appears* signal that the author is suggesting possibilities.

**Trap.** Presenting one reason as the only reason, or presenting guesses as facts. A subtler trap is accepting the question's word *necessary* and writing as if the passage settled the matter.

**Original Mission 90 sample.** The passage does not give one firm reason; it suggests several possibilities. The rulers may not have thought a line of forts along the passes feasible, or may not have given defence priority, treating the mountains as a natural frontier. The passes were also the routes of pastoralists and caravans, so leaving them open may have seemed wiser, and the local chiefs who controlled them earned an income from that trade. Familiarity with those who crossed may also have hidden the danger.

## What to take from the three

Name the verb first. Treat a neat phrase as a pointer to evidence, not as evidence. Where the passage is thin, report what it says and mark inferences as inferences.

### 9. Worked analyses, set two: an author's viewpoint, key terms and a single factor

*Objective:* Analyse three more official questions (2024 c, 2025 d and e, 2026 b) and practise separating the author's view from the view the author reports.

## Analysis 4: 2024 (c) "Explain the statement '..... their civilization, in their view attained the highest possible level of human existence .....'"

**What it asks.** A meaning question. You must say what the statement means, in plain words, and why the author says it.

**Where to look.** The first sentence of the third paragraph, and the sentences that follow it. The phrase *in their view* marks the belief as the British belief. The author is describing it and, as the later words *illicit logical move* show, criticising it.

**Trap.** Writing as though the author agrees that British civilisation was the highest. A second slip is repeating the sentence in other words without drawing out the consequences that the passage draws.

**Original Mission 90 sample.** The statement reports what the British believed: that their own way of life had reached the highest level a human society can reach and had developed qualities every person ought to develop. From this belief it followed that civilisation was desirable for all, that all societies should travel the same road, and that the more advanced had a duty to guide the others. The author presents this as the British view, and shows it as a way of treating their own habits as a universal standard.

## Analysis 5: 2025 (d) and (e) "self-evident propositions" and "tautologies"

**What they ask.** Meaning of two terms the author uses and separates.

**Where to look.** The paragraphs near the end. The author says an argument must start either from assumptions agreed between writer and reader or from self-evident propositions "which neither the writer nor reader can deny". Then comes the sentence that people now call such propositions tautologies, often with contempt. The author contrasts two examples: a father of a father is a grandfather, and the whole is greater than its parts.

**Trap.** Treating the two terms as synonyms. In the passage the label *tautology* is the term that critics apply, and the author argues that it fits one example but not the other.

**Original Mission 90 samples.** (d) Self-evident propositions are statements so plainly true that neither the writer nor the reader can deny them, so an argument may begin from them. (e) A tautology is a statement whose truth lies inside the meaning of its own words, so it adds nothing new. The essay's example is that a father of a father is a grandfather, which only repeats what the word grandfather is defined to mean. The statement that the whole is greater than its parts is self-evident but, the author says, is not merely a matter of definition.

## Analysis 6: 2026 (b) "What is the most important factor that has brought about a sea-change in India's standing abroad?"

**What it asks.** One factor, chosen by the author as the most important. The verb is a choice, not a list.

**Where to look.** The paragraph that opens with the question about factors. The phrase *to my mind, the most critical factor* names it: the way the country has nurtured its spirit of freedom and let it enliven its democratic institutions. The later factors are introduced as *another crucial element*, *the third factor* and *one last point*.

**Trap.** Listing all four factors. The question asks for the most important one. Another slip is picking the economy because its paragraph is long.

**Original Mission 90 sample.** The author names the nurturing of the spirit of freedom, and its presence in all democratic institutions, as the most critical factor. ## A self-marking rubric

Use this rubric after every attempt. It is a Mission 90 self-check tool, not UPSC's marking scheme, which the verified records do not describe. Score each criterion 0, 1 or 2, for a total out of 10.

| Criterion | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Answers the exact question and follows its directive | Partly on target, or the shape is wrong (a paragraph for a list) | Off the question |
| Own words | Paraphrased; only names and terms kept | A mixture of copied and own wording | Mostly copied |
| Accuracy | Every statement can be traced to the passage; hedges kept | One slip, or an inference not marked | Contradicts the passage or imports outside facts |
| Completeness | All the points the passage gives for this question | Some points missing | Only one point |
| Language | Clear, correct, consistent tense | A few errors that do not block meaning | Errors that block meaning |

Be strict with Accuracy. It is the criterion learners most often score themselves generously on. A score of 7 or more on three different passages, written under the conditions described below, is a sensible evidence target for a safe pass. Reading this lesson does not by itself provide that evidence.

## Qualifying strategy

The arithmetic is simple. Paper B has 300 marks; 25 per cent is 75 marks. The marks do not count for rank, but a candidate who falls below the cut-off has the Essay, GS and Optional answer books set aside. The strategy is therefore a safe pass, not a high score.

- **Do not over-invest.** Comprehension is one of four areas of the paper. Reach a reliable standard, keep it fresh, and spend your large blocks of time on the merit papers.
- **Protect the easy marks.** Questions that ask you to list, describe or give reasons are marks you can earn by careful reading and tidy writing.
- **Do not leave questions blank.** A blank earns nothing; a relevant two-sentence answer might.

### Notes: Key terms for written comprehension

- Topic: what the passage is about; a noun phrase with no claim.
- Main idea (central idea): the claim the author makes about the topic, written as one full sentence.
- Theme: the larger concern behind the claim; usually too broad to answer a main-idea question.
- Title: a short label for the passage; a phrase, not a sentence.
- Gist: the passage compressed into one to three sentences, in your own words.
- Inference: what follows from the passage without being written; it must be traceable to a sentence.
- Assumption: an unstated belief on which the author's argument rests; if it is denied, the argument collapses.
- Paraphrase: restating a sentence in your own words and structure with the same meaning.
- Directive: the verb in the question (describe, enumerate, explain, comment) that tells you the shape of the answer.
- Tone: the author's attitude towards the subject, shown by word choice.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Topic versus main idea: a topic is a noun phrase; a main idea is a claim about it.
- Main idea versus theme: the main idea is specific to the passage; the theme is a broad concern that fits many passages.
- Inference versus assumption: an inference comes after the argument; an assumption lies behind it.
- Paraphrase versus copy: a paraphrase changes words and structure and keeps the meaning; a copy keeps both.
- Self-evident proposition versus tautology (2025): the passage treats a tautology as true by the meaning of its words; a self-evident proposition may be undeniable without being merely a matter of definition.
- Sceptical versus cynical: doubts a claim versus assumes bad motives.
- Reported view versus author's own view: phrases like 'in their view' mark a belief that the author reports and may reject.

### Notes: Paraphrase before-and-after (original examples)

- Source: 'The library remained open an hour after closing time.' Paraphrase: 'The library stayed open for an extra hour after its normal closing time.'
- Source: 'Although the plan was costly, it was approved.' Paraphrase: 'The plan went ahead in spite of its high cost.'
- Source: 'Neglect of maintenance led to the collapse of the shed.' Paraphrase: 'The shed fell down because nobody looked after it.'
- Keep names, dates and technical terms unchanged: a proper noun cannot be paraphrased.

**A.** Original Mission 90 samples (other correct versions exist):
(1) Since it did not have enough money, the municipality put the project off.
(2) The road is narrow, yet a great deal of traffic uses it daily.
(3) According to the report, a majority of students find the evening the best time to study.
Self-check: meaning unchanged; sentence structure different; no new facts added; grammar correct.

**Q.** [original Mission 90 drill: tone words] Name the tone of each invented sentence in one or two words and quote the clue.
(1) 'The council, as usual, has promised to repair the road, and, as usual, nobody expects it to happen.'
(2) 'The new bridge will open next year and will cut the journey time by half.'
(3) 'How I miss the evenings when the whole street sat outside and talked!'
(4) 'The claim that the tablet cures every illness has not been supported by any study.'

**A.** Original Mission 90 answers:
(1) Ironic or sarcastic. Clue: 'as usual' repeated, and 'nobody expects it to happen'.
(2) Objective or informative (mildly positive). Clue: plain statement of fact with a figure.
(3) Nostalgic or wistful. Clue: 'How I miss the evenings'.
(4) Sceptical. Clue: 'has not been supported by any study'.
Self-check: for each item you must name a tone and quote a clue; a tone word with no clue is incomplete.

**Q.** Name the five question families of the verified Paper B comprehension records.

**A.** Retrieve and describe; cause or reason; list or enumerate; meaning; judge or compare.

**Q.** State the two parts of a standard written answer and the first thing to mark in a question.

**A.** An answer sentence in own words plus one supporting reference; mark the verb (directive) first, then topic words and limit.

**Q.** Write the five kinds of sentence in a passage and how to report a speculation.

**A.** Stated fact, opinion, inference, assumption, speculation; report speculation with its hedge, for example 'possible reasons are ...'.
