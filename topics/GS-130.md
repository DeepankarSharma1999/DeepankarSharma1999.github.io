## GS-130: Coastal processes and island groups

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Indian geography

### Bridge: Words of the coast

A **coast** is the zone where land meets the sea; the **coastline** is the line of that meeting. A **coastal plain** is low flat land beside the sea. A **delta** is a fan of river deposits at the river mouth, with the river splitting into branches called **distributaries**. An **estuary** is a funnel-shaped river mouth with no delta, where fresh and salt water mix. A **lagoon** is shallow water cut off from the sea by a bar or spit, and a **spit** is a ridge of sand attached to the land at one end. A **bar** is a ridge of sand or shingle parallel to the coast. A **cliff** is a steep rock face at the coast. **Erosion** is the wearing away of land; **deposition** is the laying down of material; **sediment** is the sand, silt and clay that is moved. **Accretion** is a coast gaining land. An **island** is land surrounded by water and an **archipelago** is a group of islands. Keep this list nearby; each term is used with an Indian example in the modules.

### Bridge: Submergence and emergence

A coast changes its shape when the **sea level** rises or falls, or when the **land** is lifted or lowered. If the land sinks or the sea rises, the lower parts of valleys are drowned and the coast is called a coast of **submergence**: it is irregular and indented, with inlets and natural harbours. If the land is lifted or the sea falls, the former sea bed becomes dry land and the coast is a coast of **emergence**: it is smooth and straight, with a gently sloping coastal plain and shallow water offshore. NCERT uses a related pair: a high rocky coast (submerged, erosion-dominated) and a low sedimentary coast (emerged, deposition-dominated). Notice that 'submerged' does not mean the whole coast is under water; it means the coast has been drowned in its lower parts. Keep this pair in mind in modules 3 and 4.

### Bridge: Degrees, nautical miles and sea distances

**Latitude** is the angle north or south of the equator and **longitude** the angle east or west of the Greenwich meridian. One degree of latitude is about 111 km everywhere, because lines of latitude are equally spaced. A degree is divided into 60 minutes (written with a prime), so 6 degrees 45 minutes N is 6.75 degrees N. One degree of longitude is about 111 km only at the equator and shrinks toward the poles. A **nautical mile** is the unit of distance at sea and equals about 1.852 km. A territorial limit of 12 nautical miles is therefore 12 x 1.852 = 22.2 km. When NCERT says an island group lies 'between 6 and 14 degrees N', subtracting gives its north-south extent in degrees, which you can multiply by 111 km to estimate kilometres. This is a rough method suited to the extracts' approximate bounds.

### 1. Orientation: India's coasts and islands on one page

*Objective:* State the two coasts and two island groups of India with the headline contrast of each, and the twelve official questions the topic has produced.

## The one-page picture

India's sea edge is made of two coasts and two island groups. The **mainland coast** is 6,100 km long; counting the islands the whole coastline is 7,517 km (NCERT Class XI, *India: Physical Environment*, chapter 1). The difference, 1,417 km (7,517 - 6,100), is what the island groups add when they are included. The two coasts differ in type: the **west coast** is narrow and rocky, a submerged coast with natural harbours and no deltas, while the **east coast** is broad and low, an emergent coast with large deltas and fewer harbours (NCERT, chapter 2). The two island groups differ too: **Andaman and Nicobar** in the Bay of Bengal are about 572 islands and islets, hilly, with one active volcano, while **Lakshadweep** in the Arabian Sea is a small group of coral islands.

## The three terms you must own first

A **coast** is where land meets the sea, and a **coastal plain** is the low flat land beside it. An **island** is land surrounded by water. A **delta** is a low fan of river deposits where a river meets the sea. Module 5 and the bridge modules define more.

## Scope

General wave action and the shapes of coastal landforms are in Day 17 lesson GS-102; salinity, tides and marine ecosystems, including coral reefs, are in Day 17 lesson GS-109. This lesson links to them rather than repeating them.

## What UPSC has asked

Twelve official questions are verified. The Prelims ones ask for a place where the tide retreats for kilometres (Chandipur is the usual answer), the island nearest Great Nicobar, a Barren Island statement set, the best example of marshland produced by repeated falls of sea level, and the climate of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. The Mains ones ask about sea-level rise and island nations, seawater intrusion, mangroves, coastal erosion and its management, the resource potential of the coast and hazard preparedness, and the Indian Ocean impact of a rise of about one metre by 2100. Module 10 maps them.

### 2. Foundation: measuring India's sea edge, coastline numbers and the maritime location

*Objective:* State India's coastline lengths, convert the territorial limit from nautical miles to kilometres, list the coastal states, and explain India's maritime position.

## Two coastline figures

NCERT Class XI (*India: Physical Environment*, chapter 1) gives the length of the coast as **6,100 km** for the mainland and **7,517 km** for the whole geographical coast of the mainland plus the Andaman and Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal and Lakshadweep in the Arabian Sea. Always quote both and say which you mean: 7,517 - 6,100 = 1,417 km is the amount added when the islands are counted. Caution: this is the NCERT figure; a newer official re-measurement of the coastline has been reported, so verify before quoting it as current. The peninsula extending into the Indian Ocean gives India its coast and a maritime location in south-central Asia, with the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal as the two arms of the Indian Ocean on either side.

## The territorial limit

India's territorial limit extends to **12 nautical miles** from the coast. A **nautical mile** is a unit used at sea; NCERT gives it as about 1.852 km (72,960 inches) against a statute mile of about 1.6 km. So 12 nautical miles is 12 x 1.852 = 22.2 km. NCERT prints "about 21.9 km", but 21.9 / 12 = 1.825 km per mile, which is not its own conversion. Teach 22.2 km, flag the slip. Other sea zones such as the exclusive economic zone are standard knowledge outside these extracts (verify).

## Latitude and distance

NCERT gives the north-south span of India as 3,214 km and the east-west span as 2,933 km, although both are roughly 30 degrees. The reason is that the distance between two lines of **latitude** stays the same, while the distance between two lines of **longitude** shrinks toward the poles. The mainland latitudes are 8 degrees 4 minutes N to 37 degrees 6 minutes N, an extent of 29.03 degrees, so 3,214 / 29.03 = about 110.7 km per degree of latitude. The southern boundary extends to 6 degrees 45 minutes N in the Bay of Bengal (NCERT), which is the southern tip of the Andaman and Nicobar group (standard knowledge: Indira Point; verify).

## Coastal states

Nine states of the mainland touch the sea: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala on the west, and Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and West Bengal on the east (standard knowledge, matching the NCERT map activity; verify). Union territories with a sea coast are Puducherry, Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep and the combined Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (as of 2020; verify). NCERT asks learners to identify these on a map; do so as a drawing exercise.

## Neighbours and size

Sri Lanka is separated from India by the **Gulf of Mannar** and the **Palk Strait** (a gulf is a large inlet partly enclosed by land, a strait a narrow passage joining two seas; standard definitions); the Maldives is the other island neighbour. India's area is 3.28 million sq km, 2.4 per cent of the world's land surface and seventh largest in the world (NCERT).

### 3. Core: the two coasts compared and the western coast

*Objective:* Compare the western and eastern coasts on type, width, rivers, ports and landforms using NCERT, and describe the divisions and features of the western coastal plain.

## Two coasts, one table

NCERT divides the coast into the western coastal plains and the eastern coastal plains. It also gives, in the landforms chapter, a general rule: high, rocky coasts (submerged) are dominated by erosion, while low, smooth, gently sloping sedimentary coasts (emerged) are dominated by deposition. NCERT then says plainly that the west coast of India is a high rocky retreating coast with erosional forms, and the east coast is a low sedimentary coast with depositional forms.

In words: the west coast is submerged, narrow, delta-free and full of natural harbours, and erosion dominates; the east coast is emergent, broader and deltaic, with fewer ports, and deposition dominates. The master notes hold the row-by-row table (West coast versus east coast).

## A variant to note

Leong (a Nigerian world text) lists "the western coast of the Deccan, India" as an example of an **emergent upland coast**, where land has been raised so cliffs stand back from the sea. NCERT's physiography chapter says the opposite: a submerged coast. Teach the NCERT position for the exam and remember Leong's wording only as a warning.

## The western coastal plain in detail

Extending from the Gujarat coast in the north to Kerala in the south, NCERT divides it into: the **Kachchh and Kathiawar coast** in Gujarat; the **Konkan coast** in Maharashtra; the **Goan coast**; and the **Malabar coast** in Karnataka and Kerala. The plain is narrow in the middle and broader toward the north and south. NCERT's explanation of the submerged origin includes the belief that the city of Dwaraka, once part of the mainland on the west coast, now lies under water. Because the coast is submerged and indented, it provides natural harbours; the ports NCERT names are Kandla, Mazagaon, JLN Port Navha Sheva, Marmagao, Mangalore and Cochin.

The rivers crossing the plain do not form deltas. The Malabar coast has **kayals** (backwaters), used for fishing, inland navigation and tourism; the Nehru Trophy Vallamkali (boat race) is held on Punnamada Kayal in Kerala (NCERT). Standard knowledge adds that the west-flowing Narmada and Tapi end in estuaries rather than deltas (verify).

## The Rann of Kachchh

NCERT says the southern part of the Indian desert slopes toward the Rann of Kachchh, and Prelims 2023 asked for the best example of marshland produced by repeated falls in sea level. By standard geography the Rann is a vast seasonally flooded salt marsh that was once an arm of the sea and became land through uplift and silting (verify, not in these extracts). Among the options in that question, Bhitarkanika is a delta mangrove forest, Marakkanam is a set of salt pans and Naupada is a swamp on the east coast; the Rann fits "repeated falls in sea level" best. This is analysis, not the official key.

### 4. Core: the eastern coast, deltas, lagoons and tidal flats

*Objective:* Describe the eastern coastal plain, its deltas and lagoons, explain why it has fewer ports, and place Chandipur and the Sunderbans.

## The eastern coastal plain

NCERT describes the eastern coastal plain as broader than the west and as an **emergent coast**. It has well-developed **deltas** formed by the rivers that flow east into the Bay of Bengal: the Mahanadi, the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri. Because the coast is emergent it has fewer ports and harbours, and NCERT links the difficulty of developing good ports to the continental shelf. NCERT prints up to 500 km; standard maps show the eastern shelf narrower than the western (verify). NCERT's question "Name some ports on the eastern coast" invites standard answers such as Visakhapatnam, Chennai, Paradip and Tuticorin (verify).

## How a delta forms

NCERT's landforms chapter explains that the load carried by a river is dumped and spread into the sea. If it is not carried far offshore or distributed along the coast, it accumulates as a low cone. Unlike alluvial fans, delta deposits are well sorted and stratified, because the coarsest materials settle first and the finer silt and clay are carried farther out. As the delta grows, the distributaries (branches of the river) lengthen and the delta builds into the sea. The flood plains within a delta are called delta plains. The Krishna delta of Andhra Pradesh is shown in an NCERT satellite view. In the north, the mouths of the Ganga-Brahmaputra form some of the largest deltas of the world, the famous **Sunderbans** (NCERT, chapter 2).

## Lagoons, spits and tidal flats

On a low sedimentary coast, waves churn the sea-bottom sediment into bars (long ridges of sand and shingle parallel to the coast), barrier bars (bars exposed above water) and spits (barrier bars attached to a headland). When bars or spits block a bay mouth, the enclosed water is a **lagoon**, which silts up into a swamp and finally a coastal plain (NCERT, chapter 6). Chilika in Odisha and Pulicat on the Andhra-Tamil Nadu border are the best-known Indian lagoons (standard knowledge; verify). Where the shore slopes very gently, a small vertical fall of the tide exposes a wide **tidal flat**. Prelims 2017 describes a place where the sea recedes a few kilometres twice a day and you can walk on the sea floor; the standard answer is Chandipur in Odisha (verify), which fits the gently sloping, depositional east coast. The tide itself is explained in Day 17 lesson GS-109.

## Weather and the east coast

PMF IAS, summarising a National Centre for Coastal Research (NCCR) report, gives three reasons erosion is more pronounced on the east coast: the Bay of Bengal has rougher seas and stronger tidal currents than the Arabian Sea; the east coast has frequent cyclones, while the west remained broadly stable; and besides the south-west monsoon (June to September) the east coast also gets the north-east monsoon from October to December, which brings rain to coastal Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu and keeps the sea rough for most of the year. Module 6 returns to the numbers.

### 5. Core: coastal processes on Indian coasts (recap with links)

*Objective:* Attach the general coastal processes taught in Day 17 lesson GS-102 to India's rocky west and sedimentary east, and apply longshore drift and groynes to an Indian case.

## What this module keeps

The mechanism of waves, erosion, transport and deposition, longshore drift and the formation of bars, spits and lagoons is taught once, in Day 17 lesson GS-102 (the sections on marine erosion and its landforms, and on marine deposition, bars, spits, lagoons and the sediment budget). Do not relearn it here; use this page to attach it to India.

## The India attachment

- **West coast (submerged, high rocky, retreating, NCERT):** erosional forms such as cliffs and wave-cut platforms. The Malabar kayals are shallow backwaters behind the coast; how they formed is not in the extracts (standard geography treats them as lagoon-like; verify).
- **East coast (emerged, low sedimentary, NCERT):** bars, spits, lagoons (Chilika, Pulicat), a tidal flat (Chandipur) and deltas (module 4); marshes and swamps are common.
- **Deposition at river mouths:** the Krishna delta and the Sunderbans. The Rann of Kachchh shows how a land and sea margin can shift (module 3); sea-level change is module 9.
- **Longshore drift and groynes:** waves arriving at an angle move sand along the shore, and a groyne or harbour wall traps it on the updrift side and starves the downdrift beach. Visakhapatnam in module 6 is the Indian case.
- **Sediment supply:** NCERT says a low sedimentary coast depends on a steady supply of sediment, while storm and tsunami waves cause drastic change whatever the supply.

## Exam habit

Tie every process to a place and to the coast type: cliffs and terraces to the rocky west; bars, spits, lagoons and deltas to the sedimentary east. Prefer an Indian example to a British or West African one.

### 6. Core: coastal erosion in India, the NCCR findings and management

*Objective:* State the PMF IAS summary of the NCCR coastal erosion findings, list natural and human causes, define accretion, and list management techniques.

## What the NCCR found

The National Centre for Coastal Research (NCCR) is an attached office of the Ministry of Earth Sciences and monitors shoreline change along the Indian coast. PMF IAS reports that 32 per cent of India's coastline underwent sea erosion and 27 per cent of it expanded between 1990 and 2018. If those two categories do not overlap, the remaining 100 - 32 - 27 = 41 per cent is neither eroding nor expanding on this measure (derived; verify the report's own categories). Other numbers in the same extract: the West Bengal coastline was the most vulnerable, with 60 per cent eroded; erosion on Odisha's coast expanded by 51 per cent; 28.7 per cent of Andhra Pradesh's coast is under varying degrees of erosion; and about 28 km of coast near Visakhapatnam is prone to erosion, with beach erosion visible since the Visakhapatnam Port was built in the 1930s. These are PMF IAS's summary and may differ across versions of the underlying report, so quote them with attribution.

## Why the east coast loses more

The three reasons (rougher Bay of Bengal, cyclones, the north-east monsoon) are in module 4. Add NCERT's coast types (module 3): a low sedimentary coast depends on a continued supply of sediment, so a fall in river sediment or a barrier to drift hurts it quickly. The general sediment-budget mechanism is in Day 17 lesson GS-102 (the section on coastal erosion in India, human causes and management); this module holds the NCCR state data.

## Causes, accretion and techniques

- **Natural**: waves, storm surges at cyclone landfall that strip the top layer of the beach, tidal bores in estuaries that damage banks (PMF IAS), rough seas and sea-level rise (module 9).
- **Human**: ports and coastal construction; dredging in port areas and dumping the dredged sediment in the deep sea rather than along the coast (PMF IAS says it ideally should be dumped along the coast); sand mining and loss of mangroves (standard; verify).
- **Accretion** is the expansion of a coastal area through an increase in the width or length of the coastline. It is not destructive in itself, but PMF IAS notes it can harm deltaic ecosystems by reducing inland water flow; one stretch may erode while a neighbour accretes.
- **Techniques (PMF IAS)**: treat beaches as dynamic landforms and allow continuous natural nourishment; a submerged breakwater about 500 m from the shoreline; seawalls, revetments and groynes; and beach nourishment (sand dredged from the sea and placed on an eroding beach). Hard structures work locally but can shift the problem down the coast (module 5). The master notes hold the technique table. The NCSCM is a research institute under the environment ministry mandated to manage the Indian coast sustainably; the NCCR monitors shorelines. Coastal Regulation Zone rules, mangrove belts and shelterbelts are standard knowledge (verify).

## How this answers Mains 2022

The 2022 GS III question asks for the causes and effects of coastal erosion in India and the available coastal management techniques. The structure is: numbers (NCCR), causes (natural, human), effects (loss of land and livelihoods, damage to ports and roads, saline intrusion; verify), techniques (hard, soft, ecological), and a balanced view that hard protection can shift the problem elsewhere.

### 7. Core: the Andaman and Nicobar Islands

*Objective:* Describe the location, structure, peaks, volcano, climate and vegetation of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and apply them to the three related Prelims questions.

## Location and make-up

NCERT: the Bay of Bengal island group consists of about **572 islands and islets** lying roughly between 6 and 14 degrees north and 92 and 94 degrees east. The two principal island clusters of islets named are Ritchie's archipelago and the Labrynth island (NCERT spelling). The entire group is divided into the **Andaman in the north** and the **Nicobar in the south**, separated by the **Ten Degree Channel**. The extent, 14 - 6 = 8 degrees of latitude, is about 890 km (8 x 111.2) by simple arithmetic on NCERT's rough bounds, so this is an upper estimate; the real chain is shorter. Other books give different counts of islands; use NCERT's 572 for the exam and note that counts vary with the definition of an islet (verify).

## How the islands were formed

NCERT: the islands are believed to be an elevated portion of submarine mountains, though some smaller islands are volcanic in origin. Standard geography also sees the chain as an arc continuing the mountain ranges of Myanmar (verify). **Barren Island**, the only active volcano in India, lies in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (NCERT).

## Peaks (NCERT)

From highest to lowest, NCERT names Saddle Peak (North Andaman, 738 m), Mount Thuiller (Great Nicobar, 642 m), Mount Diavolo (Middle Andaman, 515 m) and Mount Koyob (South Andaman, 460 m); the master notes hold the table.

NCERT spells the Great Nicobar peak "Thuiller" (often written Thuillier). NCERT prints 738 m for Saddle Peak; some references give about 732 m (verify). Do not memorise the exact figure; remember that Saddle Peak is the highest.

## Climate and vegetation

NCERT says the islands receive **convectional rainfall** and have an **equatorial type of vegetation**, with some coral deposits and beautiful beaches along the coast. The climate question in Prelims 2026 asks about three statements. A humid, tropical coastal climate (statement 1) fits NCERT's description. Receiving rain from both the south-west and the north-east monsoon (statement 2) is standard knowledge; the Bay of Bengal islands receive both (verify). Maximum rain between December and May (statement 3) does not fit: the heavy rain is brought by the south-west monsoon from about May to September, and the north-east monsoon adds rain in October and November (verify). By analysis the first two statements hold; this is not the official key.

## Prelims facts linked to the islands

1. **Closest to Great Nicobar (Prelims 2017)**: the southern boundary of India reaches 6 degrees 45 minutes N (NCERT), at the Great Nicobar end of the chain. Of Sumatra, Borneo, Java and Sri Lanka, Sumatra lies nearest to that southern tip; Sri Lanka is far to the west across the Bay of Bengal (standard geography, verify).
2. **Barren Island (Prelims 2018)**: statement 1, active volcano in Indian territory, matches NCERT. Statement 2 places it about 140 km east of Great Nicobar. Barren Island is, on standard maps, in the Andaman Sea east of the Andaman group at about 12.3 degrees N, roughly 138 km north-east of Port Blair, hundreds of kilometres from Great Nicobar in the south (verify). Statement 3 says it has been inactive since 1991, which clashes with statement 1, since the island has erupted repeatedly since then (verify). By analysis only statement 1 survives.
3. **The 2004 tsunami**: NCERT asks about the calamity of 26 December 2004 that devastated the islands; it was the Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami (standard).

### 8. Core: Lakshadweep, its channels and how the island groups compare

*Objective:* Describe Lakshadweep, learn its three channels with the NCERT misprint corrected, compare the two Indian island groups, and place Sri Lanka as a continental island.

## Lakshadweep in NCERT

The Arabian Sea island group includes **Lakshadweep and Minicoy**. NCERT gives their position as between 8 and 12 degrees north and 71 and 74 degrees east, at a distance of **220 to 440 km** off the Kerala coast. The whole group is built of **coral deposits**: approximately **36 islands, of which 11 are inhabited**. The islands have **storm beaches** of unconsolidated pebbles, shingle, cobbles and boulders on the eastern seaboard.

## The channels: an NCERT misprint to correct

NCERT prints that the group is 'broadly divided by the Nine Degree Channel', with Amini Island to the north and Canannore Island (NCERT's spelling) to the south. Standard geography (verify) names three channels in this sea: the Eleven Degree Channel between Amini and Canannore islands, the Nine Degree Channel between Minicoy and the rest of Lakshadweep, and the Eight Degree Channel between Minicoy and the Maldives (verify). So the gap NCERT assigns to the Nine Degree Channel is, in standard geography, the Eleven Degree Channel; treat NCERT's sentence as a misprint and learn the three-channel picture. None of these is the Ten Degree Channel, which separates the Andaman from the Nicobar (module 7). If a Prelims option says 'the Nine Degree Channel divides Lakshadweep into two groups', recognise NCERT's printed wording and check the other options before choosing.

## Flagged figures

NCERT calls Minicoy the largest island with an area of "453 sq km". That cannot be right: the whole group has a land area of only a few tens of square kilometres (about 32 sq km is widely reported; verify), and 453 is probably a misplaced decimal (about 4.5 sq km; verify). Do not memorise it. Some references treat Andrott as the largest island; the point to take away is that Lakshadweep is very small land on a wide sea area.

## Coral islands in brief

Coral polyps build calcium carbonate skeletons in warm, shallow, clear water, and some reefs become coral islands such as Lakshadweep (PMF IAS); an **atoll** is a ring reef enclosing a lagoon. Reef types, Darwin's subsidence theory and Daly's idea are in Day 17 lesson GS-102 (the section on islands and coral reefs as coastal and oceanic landforms); the conditions, the zooxanthellae partnership and bleaching are in Day 17 lesson GS-109 (the section on coral reefs, zooxanthellae, bleaching and acidification). India's reef areas are the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, the Gulf of Mannar and the Gulf of Kachchh (PMF IAS).

## The two groups side by side

The master notes hold the comparison table (sea, location, count, origin, channels, climate, special feature, peaks). In size terms, NCERT's rough bounds give Andaman and Nicobar 8 degrees of latitude (about 890 km) and Lakshadweep 4 degrees (12 - 8), about 445 km (4 x 111.2); the islands themselves are a tiny fraction of these areas.

## Other island ideas

Leong divides islands into **continental islands**, formerly part of the mainland and separated by a channel (it lists Sri Lanka separated by the Palk Strait) and **oceanic islands**, either volcanic or coral. Continental islands share structure, plants and animals with the mainland; oceanic islands have unrelated flora and fauna. Using this, Lakshadweep is an oceanic coral group; the Andaman and Nicobar chain is hilly with some volcanic islands (NCERT's description). Because they are low (coral) or exposed (volcanic), the islands are vulnerable to storms, tsunami and sea-level rise (module 9).

### 9. Master: sea-level rise, island nations, seawater intrusion, mangroves and the coast as a resource

*Objective:* Build Mains answers on sea-level rise and island nations, seawater intrusion, mangrove depletion and the resource and hazard profile of the Indian coast, flagging which parts are standard knowledge.

## Why this module is "master"

The extracts give the coastal setting but not the policy detail; the points below combine extract facts with flagged standard knowledge.

## Sea-level rise and island nations (Mains 2025 GS I, Mains 2023 GS III)

**Sea-level rise** is the long-term increase in the average height of the sea, caused mainly by warming oceans expanding and by melting ice (standard). The 2023 question quotes an IPCC prediction of about one metre by AD 2100 and asks for impacts on India and other Indian Ocean countries; the 2025 question asks how climate change and sea-level rise affect the existence of many island nations. Use the coral island case from module 8: low land, a small freshwater lens, exposure to storms. Impacts: land loss and inundation, saline intrusion into groundwater, coastal erosion, coral bleaching (see Day 17 lesson GS-109) that weakens natural protection, more intense storm surges, loss of livelihoods and displacement. Examples are the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati among island nations, and Lakshadweep and low parts of the Sunderbans in India (verify). Indian Ocean impacts include Bangladesh's delta, Sri Lanka and the Maldives (verify).

## Seawater intrusion (Mains 2025 GS III)

Fresh groundwater, being lighter, floats on denser salt water. **Causes** (standard, verify): over-pumping of coastal aquifers, reduced river flow to the sea because of dams and diversions, sea-level rise, storm surges and tsunami flooding, sand mining and, on low coasts, flat gradients that let the sea push inland. **Effects**: salty drinking water, loss of crop yield, damaged soil, abandoned wells and migration. **Remedies**: regulate and meter pumping, artificial recharge and recharge wells, rainwater harvesting, subsurface barriers, restore mangroves and wetlands, shift to less water-intensive crops, and use surface water where possible.

## Mangroves (Mains 2019)

Mangroves are salt-tolerant forests of the tidal zone. The extracts supply their carbon role: coastal systems, including mangroves, are blue-carbon stores that sequester carbon faster than forests and release it when damaged (PMF IAS). From standard knowledge (verify) the **causes of depletion** are clearing for shrimp farms and aquaculture, reclamation for ports and housing, salt pans, agriculture, pollution, wood cutting, reduced fresh-water flow and rising sea level. Their **importance**: a buffer against cyclones, storm surges and erosion; nurseries for fish and prawns; habitat; filter for sediment; carbon storage; livelihoods. Well-known stands are at delta mouths: the Sunderbans (West Bengal) and Bhitarkanika (Odisha) (standard; verify).

## The coast as a resource, and hazard preparedness (Mains 2023 GS I)

Resources: fisheries, ports and shipping on the 7,517 km coast (NCERT), tourism and beaches, offshore oil and gas and mineral sands, salt production, coral and mangrove ecosystems, and renewable energy such as wind and tidal power (NCERT Class XI, Movements of Ocean Water, mentions a 3 MW tidal power project at Durgaduani in the Sunderbans as under way; current status to verify). Hazards: cyclones that strike the east coast more often (PMF IAS), storm surges, the 26 December 2004 tsunami (NCERT), erosion (module 6), sea-level rise and seawater intrusion. Preparedness has three parts: monitoring (the NCCR tracks shoreline change), protection (natural barriers such as mangroves and coral, and engineering), and planning (zoning and early warning, outside these extracts; verify). A good answer ends with the trade-off between using and protecting the coast.

## Map skills for Prelims

1. Always locate before you choose. Draw the coast and mark the latitude of the place. Great Nicobar is near the southern boundary of India at 6 degrees 45 minutes N.
2. Use the two-coast rule: west means rocky, narrow, ports, no deltas; east means deltas, lagoons, tidal flats, cyclones.
3. Use the two-island rule: Bay of Bengal means hilly, volcanic, Ten Degree Channel (Andaman from Nicobar); Arabian Sea means coral, with the Eleven, Nine and Eight Degree Channels (NCERT prints the Nine Degree Channel for the Amini-Canannore gap; verify).
4. For a "which of the following" with a famous port or tourist spot among the options, test whether the property in the stem (tidal flat, marsh, delta, lagoon) belongs to the place.

## Prelims traps

- **Barren Island set**: statement 3 contradicts statement 1 (an active volcano cannot be inactive for decades).
- **Andaman climate**: the islands get rain from both monsoons; "December to May" is the drier period.
- **Rann of Kutch**: marsh from repeated sea-level falls is not a delta mangrove (Bhitarkanika) or a salt pan (Marakkanam).
- **Numbers and channels**: Minicoy's "453 sq km" and the Nine Degree Channel for the Amini-Canannore gap are NCERT misprints; the 21.9 km for 12 nautical miles is a slip; PMF IAS percentages vary with the report version.
- **Coast type**: NCERT says west is submerged; Leong says the western Deccan coast is emergent. Choose NCERT.

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Delta: low fan of well-sorted, stratified river deposits built into the sea; distributaries lengthen as it grows (NCERT).
- Lagoon: water enclosed behind a bar or spit that blocks a bay mouth; it silts up to a swamp and a coastal plain (NCERT).
- Spit: a barrier bar attached to one end of a bay or to a headland (NCERT).
- Accretion: expansion of a coastal area through an increase in the width or length of the coastline (PMF IAS).
- Groyne: shore protection structure built perpendicular to the shoreline to reduce longshore drift and trap sediment (PMF IAS).
- Beach nourishment: dredging sand from the sea and depositing it on an eroding beach (PMF IAS).
- Kayal: backwater of the Malabar coast, used for fishing, navigation and tourism (NCERT).

### Notes: Process chains

- Waves break to sediment churned to bars and spits to lagoon to swamp to coastal plain (NCERT).
- River carries load to the sea to coarse deposit first and fine carried farther to delta builds seaward (NCERT).
- Port or harbour wall built to longshore drift interrupted to downdrift beach starved to erosion (standard; PMF IAS on ports and dredging).
- Rough Bay of Bengal, cyclones and north-east monsoon to more erosion on the east coast (PMF IAS).
- Sea level rises to low island flooded and groundwater salted to livelihoods lost (standard, verify).

### Notes: Exceptions and source slips

- NCERT's 21.9 km for 12 nautical miles does not match its own conversion; use 22.2 km.
- NCERT's 453 sq km for Minicoy cannot be right; the whole group is a few tens of sq km and 453 is probably a misplaced decimal (about 4.5 sq km) (verify).
- Leong calls the western Deccan coast emergent; NCERT calls the west coast submerged.
- East-coast shelf: NCERT prints up to 500 km; standard maps show the eastern shelf narrower than the western (verify).
- Saddle Peak: NCERT 738 m, some references about 732 m.
- Lakshadweep largest island: NCERT says Minicoy; some references say Andrott (verify).
- NCERT prints the Nine Degree Channel between Amini and Canannore; standard: Eleven Degree Channel (verify). The Nine Degree Channel separates Minicoy from the rest; the Eight Degree Channel separates Minicoy from the Maldives.
- Barren Island is in the Andaman Sea east of the Andaman group (about 12.3 N, roughly 138 km north-east of Port Blair), not near Great Nicobar (verify).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Delta versus estuary: deposits build seaward; a drowned funnel-shaped mouth with no delta.
- Erosion versus accretion on the same coast: both occur; accretion is not automatically good.
- Bar versus spit versus barrier bar: offshore ridge; ridge exposed above water; bar attached to a headland or one end of a bay.
- Andaman versus Nicobar: northern group (Saddle Peak, Barren Island nearer) versus southern group (Great Nicobar, southern boundary).
- Ten Degree Channel (Andaman from Nicobar) versus Lakshadweep's channels: Eleven (Amini-Canannore), Nine (Minicoy from the rest), Eight (Minicoy-Maldives); NCERT prints Nine for the Amini-Canannore gap (verify).

### Notes: Indian examples

- Tidal flat: Chandipur, Odisha (standard, verify).
- Lagoons: Chilika, Pulicat (standard, verify).
- Deltas: Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Sunderbans.
- Backwaters: Malabar kayals, Punnamada Kayal.
- Erosion hot spots: West Bengal, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh including near Visakhapatnam (PMF IAS).
- Volcano: Barren Island.
- Coral reef areas: Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep, Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh (PMF IAS).

### Core concepts

- India's coast: 6,100 km mainland, 7,517 km with islands; two contrasting coasts and two island groups.
- West coast: submerged, narrow, rocky, natural ports, kayals, no deltas; east coast: emergent, broad, deltas, fewer ports.
- Waves shape coasts; rocky coasts erode, sedimentary coasts deposit; bars and spits make lagoons.
- Coastal erosion is mapped by the NCCR; the east coast loses more; management includes groynes, breakwaters and nourishment.
- Andaman and Nicobar are hilly with Barren Island; Lakshadweep is coral.

**Q.** Name the two island groups and the sea each lies in.

**A.** Andaman and Nicobar in the Bay of Bengal; Lakshadweep in the Arabian Sea.

**Q.** Why do the two coasts of India differ in landforms?

**A.** The west is submerged, rocky and erosion-dominated; the east is emergent, sedimentary and deposition-dominated (NCERT).

**Q.** How does a barrier bar create a lagoon?

**A.** It closes a bay mouth, enclosing shallow water behind it.

**Q.** Why are groynes both useful and risky?

**A.** They trap sand and slow erosion locally, but starve the downdrift beach.

**Q.** Why is Lakshadweep more vulnerable to sea-level rise than the Andaman hills?

**A.** It is built of low coral land; the Andaman chain has hills up to several hundred metres.

**Q.** A fishing village on a sandy east-coast beach has lost 40 m of beach in a decade after a port was built 15 km to the south. Suggest causes and two management measures.

**A.** Interrupted longshore drift, cyclones and rough seas; use beach nourishment and a submerged breakwater, and review dredged-sediment dumping (PMF IAS).
