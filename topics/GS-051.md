### Bridge: Words of the endgame: partition, two-nation theory, grouping, paramountcy, dominion

**Partition** here means the division of British India into two states, India and Pakistan, in 1947. It is not the partition of Bengal of 1905 (GS-038), a different event. The **two-nation theory** was the League's claim that Muslims were a nation, not a minority, and so needed a homeland of their own; the Congress held that India was one nation of many communities. The League's **Lahore resolution** of March 1940 asked for independent states in Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east (its text did not use the word Pakistan, per GS-053).

**Grouping** was the Cabinet Mission's device of joining provinces into sections that would draw up their own provincial or group constitutions. **Paramountcy** was the British Crown's supreme authority over the princely states, which were not ruled directly. **Dominion status** meant self-government within the Commonwealth with the Crown as head of state; here it was the quick way to hand over power in 1947. A **Governor-General** was the Crown's representative in a dominion. An **Interim Government** was a temporary executive, in 1946 formed from Indian leaders under the Viceroy. A **Constituent Assembly** is a body elected to write a constitution. A **boundary commission** draws the border.

### Bridge: Reading the map of 1946: majorities, sections and Punjab and Bengal

In 1946 British India had eleven main provinces (NCERT, PDF p11), plus Chief Commissioner's provinces and the princely states. Some provinces had a Muslim majority (Punjab, Bengal, Sindh and the North-West Frontier Province), others a Hindu majority (Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces and others). The key fact is that **the Muslim-majority areas were not neatly separate**. Spectrum gives the figure used by the Cabinet Mission: a Pakistan of the demanded kind would contain a non-Muslim population of about 38 per cent in the north-west and about 48 per cent in the north-east.

That is why the question of **Punjab and Bengal** dominates 1946-47. If a state was to be formed on a religious majority, then districts with non-Muslim majorities in those two provinces could claim to stay out, which the Mission said would disturb long-standing regional ties. The sources also show **separate electorates**: Muslims voted for reserved Muslim seats, others for general seats, so election results map community by community. Remember the narrow franchise: fewer than 10 per cent of the people could vote for the provinces and fewer than 1 per cent for the Central Assembly (Spectrum, PDF p520).

### 1. Orientation: 1945 to 1947 on one page

*Objective:* Hold the endgame as five stages: the post-war push, the Cabinet Mission, the breakdown, the 3 June plan and the Act.

## Where we start

GS-048 ends with the Quit India upsurge and its repression; GS-049 follows the INA and the Red Fort trials. GS-050 module 9 ends with the failure of the Wavell Plan at Shimla in June 1945. This lesson starts there: Britain has run out of offers that stop short of freedom, and the League has gained a virtual veto.

## Five stages

1. **The post-war push (1945 to early 1946).** Labour wins power in Britain in July 1945. Elections in India (winter 1945-46) show the Congress sweeping the general seats and the League the Muslim seats. The INA trials and the naval revolt weaken the sense that the Raj can last.
2. **The Cabinet Mission (March to June 1946).** Three British ministers produce their own plan on 16 May 1946: a union with a weak centre, provinces grouped in sections, and a constituent assembly.
3. **The breakdown (July to December 1946).** The League withdraws acceptance, calls for Direct Action on 16 August, and communal violence begins in Calcutta. An Interim Government under Nehru takes office on 2 September, the League joins in October and the Constituent Assembly meets without it on 9 December.
4. **The decision (February to June 1947).** Attlee announces on 20 February that power will be transferred by June 1948. Mountbatten arrives as Viceroy, and the plan of 3 June 1947 accepts partition and brings forward the date.
5. **The Act (July to August 1947).** The Indian Independence Act creates two dominions from 15 August 1947. Boundary commissions draw the lines in Punjab and Bengal, and the states are to accede.

### 2. Foundation: the road to 1946, communalism and the idea of Pakistan

*Objective:* Trace, from the sources, how separate electorates and communal politics led to the demand for a separate state, and link the earlier offers.

GS-034, GS-036, GS-038 and GS-040 teach the early part. This module only strings the thread together, using Spectrum's timeline (PDF pp535-538).

## The timeline

- **1906-09.** A Muslim delegation under the Aga Khan asked Minto for separate electorates; the **All-India Muslim League** was founded (1906) and separate electorates came in 1909 (GS-038 module 8, GS-050).
- **1916.** The Lucknow Pact: the Congress accepted separate electorates (GS-040 module 7).
- **1920s.** Muslims joined Khilafat and Non-Cooperation, but the decade also brought riots and the Shuddhi, Sangathan, Tabligh and Tanzeem movements; the Swarajists divided on communal lines (GS-044).
- **1928-32.** The Nehru Report and Fourteen Points (GS-044 module 7), the Round Table Conferences (GS-046) and the Communal Award (GS-052).
- **After 1937.** Spectrum says the League did badly in the 1937 provincial elections and then projected Muslims as a separate nation, an idea it credits to Rahmat Ali in the early 1930s and to Iqbal (verify details in a standard text). Jinnah is said to have demanded that the Congress recognise the League as sole representative of Muslims (1937-39).
- **23-24 March 1940.** The **Lahore resolution** (Spectrum: 24 March; commonly moved 23 March and adopted 24 March; verify) demanded independent states in the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east, with autonomous and sovereign units. Its text did not use the word Pakistan (GS-053 module 8).

## Spectrum's frame

Spectrum uses three stages: communal nationalism (people of one religion share all interests), liberal communalism (different religions have different interests even in secular matters) and extreme communalism (the interests are incompatible). It dates the shift to extreme communalism after 1937 and says it found an echo in Hindu organisations such as the Hindu Mahasabha and RSS (PDF pp528, 537). This is an analytical frame, not a settled consensus.

## The wartime offers, in one line each

The August Offer (1940) gave the League a minority veto; Cripps (1942) let provinces opt out; the Rajagopalachari Formula (1944) and the Gandhi-Jinnah talks failed because Jinnah wanted the two-nation theory accepted first; the Desai-Liaqat idea set a parity; at Shimla (1945) Wavell's breakdown gave the League a virtual veto (GS-048, GS-050; Spectrum PDF p538).

## Two readings to attribute

One stresses British policy and the structure of separate electorates and a narrow franchise (Spectrum); another stresses the League's leadership and the Congress's failure to draw in Muslim masses. Attribute such views; do not assert them.

### 3. Core: the post-war push and the elections of 1945-46

*Objective:* State why Britain changed course after 1945 and read the election results of 1945-46 correctly.

## The change in 1945

In June 1945 the government released the Congress leaders and expected a demoralised people; it found tumultuous crowds impatient for action (Spectrum, PDF p512). In **July 1945** the Labour Party formed the government in Britain, with **Clement Attlee** as Prime Minister and **Pethick-Lawrence** as Secretary of State for India. In August 1945 elections to the central and provincial assemblies were announced, and in September the government said that a constituent assembly would be convened after the elections, in the spirit of the Cripps offer (PDF p513).

## Seven reasons for the change (Spectrum, PDF p513)

1. Britain was weakened; the United States and the USSR favoured Indian freedom.
2. The new Labour government was more sympathetic.
3. A wave of socialist-radical governments swept Europe.
4. British soldiers were tired and the economy shattered (London owed India about £1.2 billion in 1945).
5. Vietnam and Indonesia were resisting the return of French and Dutch rule.
6. Officials feared another Congress revolt like 1942, with army and police disaffection and INA men present.
7. Elections were overdue (the last were in 1934 for the Centre and 1937 for the provinces).

Spectrum's graceful-withdrawal paragraph (PDF pp520-522) adds that after Cripps only full freedom was left to offer, wholly official rule was impossible for want of men, and the aim was a graceful withdrawal (PDF pp520-522); Wavell wrote in October 1946 that British power to control events was almost gone.

## The INA and the naval revolt

GS-049 module 7 tells the INA trials and the naval revolt. Their effect, in the Spectrum list (PDF p518): trials limited to men accused of murder or brutality (Spectrum prints 1 December 1946; standard 1 December 1945), sentences on the first batch remitted (Spectrum prints January 1947; standard January 1946; see GS-049 module 7), Indian troops withdrawn from Indo-China and Indonesia (Spectrum: by February 1947; verify), and the decision to send the Cabinet Mission.

## The elections, winter 1945-46

The Congress campaign built on the memory of 1942 and the INA cause (Spectrum, PDF p514). Results (PDF pp519-520):

| | Congress | Muslim League |
|---|---|---|
| Votes | 91 per cent of non-Muslim votes | 86.6 per cent of Muslim votes |
| Central Assembly | 57 of 102 seats | All 30 reserved Muslim seats |
| Provinces | Majorities in most provinces, including NWFP and Assam; not in Bengal, Sindh or Punjab | Majority in Bengal and Sindh |
| Punjab | Unionist-Congress-Akali coalition under Khizr Hayat Khan | (not in government) |

NCERT says the same in words: the Congress swept the general seats and the League captured most of the reserved Muslim seats, and the League then boycotted the Constituent Assembly (PDF p4).

## What the results mean

The League could now claim most Muslim voters, unlike in 1937; and the voting was **communal**, under separate electorates and a franchise of under 10 per cent (provinces) and under 1 per cent (Central Assembly; Spectrum PDF p520). The elections settled who spoke for each community, not what the country's structure should be.

### 4. Core: the Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946

*Objective:* State the Mission's members, the main points of the plan, the grouping dispute and how the plan fell apart.

## The Mission

The Attlee government announced in February 1946 (about 19 February; verify) a mission of three cabinet members: **Pethick-Lawrence** (Secretary of State for India and chairman), **Stafford Cripps** (President of the Board of Trade) and **A. V. Alexander** (First Lord of the Admiralty). It reached Delhi on **24 March 1946**. On 15 March Attlee said a minority could not be allowed a veto on the advance of the majority, a break from Shimla; Britain now preferred a united, friendly India (Spectrum, PDF p522).

## Why no Pakistan

The Mission rejected a full-fledged Pakistan (PDF pp522-523): the large non-Muslim population (about 38 and 48 per cent); the same principle would claim west Bengal and the Ambala and Jullundur divisions of Punjab; regional ties would be disturbed; east-west communication and administration would be hard; and the armed forces would be divided.

## The plan (statement of 16 May 1946)

- **A Union** controlling defence, communications and external affairs; provinces with full autonomy and residuary powers; a three-tier structure (province, section, union).
- **Three sections:** **A** (Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, United Provinces, Bihar, Orissa), **B** (Punjab, NWFP, Sindh) and **C** (Bengal and Assam). Spectrum labels B and C Muslim-majority; this holds for Section C as a whole (Bengal outweighed Assam), but Assam by itself had a non-Muslim majority (verify), which is why Assam and the Congress objected to compulsory grouping.
- **A Constituent Assembly** of 389: 292 from the provinces, 4 from Chief Commissioner's provinces, 93 from the princely states; provincial members elected by the provincial assemblies by proportional representation in three groups (General, Muslim, Sikh).
- **Procedure:** members of each section meet separately to frame provincial and, if possible, group constitutions; the whole assembly then frames the union constitution. Communal questions at the centre need a simple majority of both communities present and voting.
- **States:** paramountcy to lapse; states free to arrange with successor governments or Britain.
- **Opt-out:** a province could leave its group after the first general election, and after 10 years seek reconsideration of the group or union constitution.
- **An Interim Government** meanwhile (details in a 16 June statement; verify).

## The grouping dispute

The Congress read the plan as against Pakistan: grouping was **optional**, there was one assembly and no League veto. The League saw Pakistan implied in **compulsory grouping**. The Mission later clarified that grouping was compulsory (Spectrum, PDF p524; usually dated 25 May 1946, verify).

**Objections.** The Congress: provinces should not wait for the first election to leave a group (it had NWFP and Assam in mind); compulsory grouping contradicted provincial autonomy; states' representatives should be elected. The League: grouping must be compulsory so that B and C could become solid entities and later secede.

## Acceptance and collapse

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 6 June 1946 | League accepts the long-term plan |
| 24 June 1946 | Congress accepts it (about 25 June in some accounts; verify) |
| 10 July 1946 | Nehru says Congress is bound only by its decision to enter the Assembly and that grouping would probably not happen, since NWFP and Assam would object |
| 29 July 1946 | League withdraws its acceptance and calls for direct action from 16 August |

(NCERT's timeline says 16 June for the League's acceptance; 6 June is the standard date, verify.)



### 5. Core: Direct Action Day, the Interim Government and the Assembly of 1946

*Objective:* Narrate August 1946 to February 1947 with dates, state who sat in the Interim Government and describe the violence with care.

## Direct Action, 16 August 1946

The League called for 'direct action' from **16 August 1946** to achieve Pakistan (Spectrum, PDF p525). NCERT calls the **Great Calcutta Killings** of August 1946 the start of a year of almost continuous rioting across northern and eastern India (PDF p2). Spectrum speaks of riots on an unprecedented scale with 'several thousands' dead, worst in Calcutta, Bombay, Noakhali, Bihar and Garhmukteshwar (PDF p526); the months and the numbers are standard knowledge, and **estimates vary; verify**. The chain to remember: breakdown of the plan, direct action, riots that persuaded many that the two parties could not govern together.

## The Interim Government

Wavell now wanted the Congress in the Interim Government even if the League stayed out, a reversal of his stance at Shimla (PDF p526). A Congress-dominated Interim Government headed by **Nehru** (Vice-President of the Executive Council) was sworn in on **2 September 1946**, and it lasted until 15 August 1947 (PDF p527). Spectrum calls it little more than a continuation of the Viceroy's old executive.

Wavell brought the League in on **26 October 1946**; NCERT's timeline dates the League's decision to join to 13 October (PDF p26). The League joined without giving up direct action and despite rejecting the Mission's plans.

Of the 14 ministers, nine were from the Congress and others (Nehru, Patel with Home, Baldev Singh with Defence, Rajagopalachari, Rajendra Prasad, among them) and five from the League (Liaquat Ali Khan with Finance, Chundrigar, Nishtar, Ghazanfar Ali Khan and Mandal).

Spectrum calls the League's approach obstructive, with finance under Liaquat Ali Khan restricting other ministries, and its aim a foothold from which to fight for Pakistan (PDF pp527-528); this is Spectrum's judgement, which the League's supporters would dispute.

## The Constituent Assembly

The League did not attend the Assembly, which first met on **9 December 1946**. Spectrum says the Assembly confined itself to a general **Objectives Resolution** drafted by Nehru: an independent sovereign republic with autonomous units, minority safeguards and democracy (PDF p527). NCERT dates its introduction to 13 December 1946 (NCERT PDF p7); it was adopted on 22 January 1947 (standard; verify). London talks with Attlee on 3-6 December failed (NCERT timeline).

## February 1947

In February 1947 nine Congress members of the cabinet wrote to the Viceroy demanding the resignation of the League members and threatening to withdraw their own. The League demanded the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly; NCERT places this demand on 29 January 1947 (Spectrum: February; verify). A crisis seemed to be developing (PDF p528).

## A fair summary

Indians now ran the central departments, but the Viceroy's powers remained, and the two parties proved unable to work together. The record of 1946 holds both the riots and the unity of the INA and navy upsurges (module 3); do not let one erase the other.

### 6. Core: Attlee's statement, Mountbatten and the plan of 3 June 1947

*Objective:* State Attlee's announcement, how Mountbatten's plan emerged, its main points and why the date was advanced.

## Attlee's statement, 20 February 1947

Attlee announced in the House of Commons that Britain would leave (Spectrum, PDF pp541-542). Its main points:

- A deadline of **30 June 1948** for the transfer of power, even without agreement on a constitution.
- Power to pass to a central government or, in some areas, to existing provincial governments if the Constituent Assembly was not fully representative (if Muslim-majority provinces stayed out).
- British powers over the princely states to lapse, not to be handed to any successor government in British India.
- **Mountbatten** to replace Wavell (he took office on 24 March 1947; verify).

Spectrum says it hinted at partition and Balkanisation, in essence a reversion to the Cripps offer. The date was meant to shock the parties into agreement and avert a crisis. The League, emboldened, began civil disobedience against the coalition ministry in Punjab.

## Drifting towards partition

Spectrum says the riots and the failed coalition made many accept the 'unthinkable' (PDF p542). The loudest demand came from Hindu and Sikh groups in Bengal and Punjab, alarmed that compulsory grouping might place them in Pakistan. On **10 March 1947** Nehru said the Cabinet Mission plan was best if carried out and the only alternative was partition of Punjab and Bengal; in April the Congress president **Kripalani** told the Viceroy that Pakistan could be conceded if Bengal and Punjab were partitioned fairly.

## Mountbatten

Spectrum says he was firmer and quicker, informally given more powers. He found the Cabinet Mission Plan 'a dead horse' and Jinnah set on a sovereign state (PDF p543). Between March and May he drafted **Plan Balkan**: power to separate provinces (or a confederation), Punjab and Bengal able to vote on division, states free to join either or stand alone; it was dropped after Nehru reacted violently (PDF p546).

## The 3 June plan

The innovation, suggested by **V. P. Menon**, was the **immediate transfer of power on the basis of dominion status**, so there was no wait for agreement on a new structure (PDF p543). The main points:

1. The Punjab and Bengal assemblies would meet in two groups, Hindu and Muslim; if a simple majority of either group voted for partition, those provinces would be partitioned.
2. Partition would mean two dominions and two constituent assemblies; Sindh would decide for itself; referendums in the NWFP and Sylhet would decide their fate.
3. Independence for the states and for Bengal was ruled out, and Hyderabad's accession to Pakistan too (Mountbatten supported the Congress).
4. A boundary commission if partition occurred, and freedom on **15 August 1947**.

Spectrum: Pakistan was conceded but kept as small as possible, 'divide India but retain maximum unity' (PDF p544). The Bengal and Punjab assemblies then voted for partition (20 and 23 June, standard; verify), Sylhet went to East Bengal and the NWFP voted for Pakistan with the provincial Congress staying out (PDF p545).

**Why dominion status and an early date?** Spectrum: the Congress accepted it, against the spirit of Lahore 1929, for a quick, peaceful transfer and continuity in the services and army; Britain kept India in the Commonwealth for a time and escaped responsibility for the communal situation (PDF pp544-545).

### 7. Core: the Indian Independence Act 1947

*Objective:* State the Act's provisions and dates, and distinguish what it did from what the 1935 Act still did.

## The dates

Spectrum says the British Parliament passed the Act on 5 July 1947 and it received royal assent on **18 July 1947**, and took effect on 15 August (PDF p545). The Bill was introduced in the Commons on 4 July and the Act passed Parliament in mid-July; 5 July is not the usual date of passage (standard knowledge; verify). The date to learn is **18 July 1947** (assent) and **15 August 1947** (the appointed day).

## Provisions (Spectrum, PDF pp545-546)

- Creation of two independent **dominions**, India and Pakistan, from 15 August 1947.
- Each dominion to have a **Governor-General** responsible for the effective operation of the Act.
- The **constituent assembly of each dominion** would exercise the powers of the legislature of that dominion; the existing Central Legislative Assembly and the Council of State were to be automatically dissolved.
- For the transitional period, until each dominion adopted a new constitution, governments were to be carried on in accordance with the **Government of India Act 1935** (GS-047).

Standard accounts add (verify): the Act ended British paramountcy over the princely states, left them free to accede to either dominion, abolished the office of the Secretary of State for India and ended the British Parliament's power to legislate for the dominions, allowing each assembly to repeal or amend British laws. These points are not in the project extracts; use a textbook check before you rely on them in an answer.

## What happened on the day

Pakistan became independent on 14 August in Spectrum's account, while India obtained freedom on 15 August (PDF p546). NCERT's timeline records a Pakistan Independence ceremony at Karachi on 14 August and India's midnight celebration on 14-15 August; Jinnah was elected president of the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan on 11 August (PDF p26). The legal date for both dominions is 15 August 1947 under the Act (verify); the 14 August date reflects the Karachi ceremony.

**First Governors-General.** M. A. Jinnah for Pakistan. For India, the Congress requested Lord Mountbatten to continue (PDF p546).

## Dominion status, not yet a republic

India became a dominion with the Governor-General as the Crown's representative; the Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950 (NCERT, PDF p1).

## The transition problem

Spectrum lists three reasons why the early date failed to prevent the Punjab massacre: there were no institutions to handle partition problems; Mountbatten had hoped to be common Governor-General of both dominions but Jinnah wished the post for himself; and the boundary award, ready by 12 August, was published after 15 August so the British could escape responsibility for any disturbances (PDF p546). 

## Confusions to avoid

The Cabinet Mission Plan (united India) is not the 3 June plan (partition); the Independence Act (British statute, 1947) is not the Constitution (1950); the 1935 Act continued only as an adapted interim framework.

### 8. Core: the Radcliffe line, Punjab and Bengal, the states and the human cost

*Objective:* Describe how the borders were drawn, what followed in Punjab and Bengal, and where the princely states fit, with care for the human dimension.

## The boundary commissions

The plan provided for boundary commissions, one for each province (Spectrum, PDF p545). Standard accounts add that **Sir Cyril Radcliffe** chaired both, with four judges on each commission, two nominated by the Congress and two by the League (verify). Spectrum records that the award was ready by 12 August 1947 but Mountbatten decided to release it after 15 August so that the British would escape responsibility for disturbances (PDF p546). The usual date of publication is 17 August 1947 (verify). Millions found themselves on a side of a line they did not know had been drawn.

## What the line meant

East Bengal and West Punjab went to Pakistan; West Bengal and East Punjab stayed with India; the Sylhet referendum placed that district in East Bengal; Baluchistan and Sindh joined Pakistan (Spectrum, PDF p545). The Cabinet Mission had warned that dividing Punjab and Bengal would sever regional ties (module 4). Disputes over particular districts (for example Lahore, Gurdaspur, the Chittagong Hill Tracts, Murshidabad and Khulna) are standard knowledge outside the extracts; **verify** them before use.

## The movement of people and the violence

NCERT says millions of refugees were on the move: Muslims into East and West Pakistan and Hindus and Sikhs into West Bengal and the eastern half of Punjab. Many perished before reaching their destination, and 'innumerable' Muslims in India and Hindus and Sikhs in Pakistan faced a cruel choice between the threat of sudden death or the squeezing of opportunities on the one side and a forcible tearing from roots on the other (PDF pp2-3). Spectrum says the early date and the lack of institutions failed to prevent the Punjab massacre (PDF p546). Scholarly **estimates of the dead and displaced vary widely; verify** them, and do not quote one number as settled. Women, children and minorities suffered greatly, a theme in the memory and oral-history literature (the NCERT chapter in the project gives no figures).

## The princely states: a pointer

About a third of the subcontinent's area was held by princes (NCERT, PDF p3). With paramountcy lapsing, some dreamed of independence. Nehru declared in April 1947 that states refusing to join the Constituent Assembly would be treated as hostile; Patel took charge of the States Department in July 1947, and integration followed in two phases (Spectrum, PDF pp546-547):

- **Phase I (by 15 August 1947):** all states except Kashmir, Hyderabad and Junagadh signed an **instrument of accession** conceding defence, external affairs and communications, easily, since princes gave up only what they never had.
- **Phase II (within a year):** integration with neighbouring provinces or into new units (Kathiawar Union, Rajasthan, Himachal Pradesh), with privy purses as the principal bait.

Hyderabad, Junagadh and Kashmir are not in the extracts (deferred).

NCERT shows partition shaping the Constitution: Patel called separate electorates a poison, and most nationalists afterwards favoured a strong centre (PDF pp13, 21).

### 9. Master: why partition was accepted, and the comparison of the four plans

*Objective:* Give a balanced account of why the Congress accepted partition and compare the Cripps, Wavell, Cabinet Mission and Mountbatten plans.

## Spectrum's reasons (PDF pp548-550)

Spectrum frames the question as 'why Congress accepted partition':

1. **A long-term failure.** The Congress had a twofold task: to weld classes, communities and regions into a nation, and to secure independence for it. It built enough national consciousness to force the British out but failed to bring the Muslim masses fully into the movement.
2. **Only an immediate transfer could forestall the spread of direct action and communal violence**, and the virtual collapse of the Interim Government made Pakistan seem unavoidable.
3. **It prevented the Balkanisation of India:** the plan ruled out independence for the princely states.
4. **A step-by-step process:** Cripps (1942) accepted autonomy of Muslim-majority provinces; Gandhi (1944) accepted self-determination for Muslim-majority provinces; after the Cabinet Mission the Congress conceded a possible separate assembly and, in December 1946, accepted compulsory grouping (verify the date; Spectrum (p549) prints December 1946, and standard accounts place the AICC acceptance of the 6 December statement on 5-6 January 1947); a March 1947 CWC resolution said that if the country was divided Punjab and Bengal must be as well; the 3 June plan completed it.
5. **Wishful thinking.** Spectrum says the Congress underestimated communal feeling, with Nehru saying partition would be temporary and peaceful (Spectrum's judgement).

## Four debates

- **Was partition inevitable?** Spectrum's heading says so, but Plan Balkan and the Cabinet Mission show alternatives were tried; a balanced answer says it became likely after 1946.
- **Who is responsible?** The British (divide and rule, wartime veto, haste); the League (two-nation claim, Direct Action); the Congress (failure to mobilise Muslims, readiness for a quick transfer); the structure of separate electorates and a narrow franchise. Do not blame one party or person.
- **Was the haste justified?** Weigh Spectrum's reasons for the early date against its consequence, the Punjab massacre.
- **Was the Cabinet Mission Plan a lost chance?** One reading says so; Spectrum calls it a dead horse by 1947. Present both.

### Notes: Cabinet Mission Plan at a glance

- Members: Pethick-Lawrence (chairman), Stafford Cripps, A. V. Alexander; reached Delhi 24 March 1946.
- Rejected a full-fledged Pakistan: 38 per cent non-Muslims in the north-west, 48 per cent in the north-east; Punjab and Bengal would have to be divided.

- Assembly of 389: 292 from provinces, 4 from Chief Commissioner's provinces, 93 from princely states.
- Centre: defence, communications, external affairs; provinces hold residuary powers.
- Opt-out of a group after the first general elections; reconsideration of the union or group constitution after ten years.
- Paramountcy of the Crown to lapse; an interim government with the support of the major political parties (names in the 16 June statement, verify).

### Notes: Provisions of the Indian Independence Act 1947 (sourced and standard)

- Two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, from 15 August 1947 (Spectrum).
- A Governor-General for each dominion; Jinnah for Pakistan; Mountbatten asked to continue for India (Spectrum).
- The constituent assembly of each dominion exercises the legislature's powers; the Central Legislative Assembly and the Council of State dissolved (Spectrum).
- Until a new constitution, governments run under the Government of India Act 1935 (Spectrum).
- Royal assent 18 July 1947 (Spectrum). Standard additions, verify: lapse of paramountcy, abolition of the Secretary of State's office, end of British Parliament's legislative authority over the dominions, power to repeal British laws.

### Notes: Key definitions

- Two-nation theory: the League's claim that Muslims were a separate nation.
- Grouping: the Cabinet Mission's arrangement of provinces into sections A, B and C.
- Paramountcy: the Crown's supreme authority over the princely states.
- Dominion status: self-government within the Commonwealth with the Crown as head of state.
- Direct Action: the League's call of 16 August 1946 to achieve Pakistan.
- Instrument of accession: the document by which a prince ceded defence, external affairs and communications to the Indian government.

### Notes: Who is who

- Clement Attlee: British Prime Minister from July 1945.
- Pethick-Lawrence: Secretary of State for India and chairman of the Cabinet Mission.
- Lord Wavell: Viceroy until early 1947; Mountbatten his successor.
- V. P. Menon: credited with the dominion-status transfer idea.
- Cyril Radcliffe: chairman of the boundary commissions (standard; verify).
- J. B. Kripalani: Congress president in April 1947.
- Vallabhbhai Patel: head of the States Department from July 1947.
- Liaquat Ali Khan: League finance member of the Interim Government.

### Notes: Cause and effect

- War-weary Britain and Labour's victory led to a policy of graceful withdrawal.
- The elections confirmed the League as the dominant Muslim party and the Congress as the dominant non-Muslim party.
- The grouping dispute and Nehru's 10 July statement led to the League's withdrawal and Direct Action.
- Riots and the failure of the coalition led many to accept partition as a way out.
- Attlee's date and Mountbatten's plan led to the dominion route and the 15 August date; speed left little time for arrangements, and the Punjab massacre followed.

### Last-week summary

- Cabinet Mission (Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, Alexander): Delhi 24 Mar 1946, plan 16 May; union of defence, communications, external affairs; sections A, B, C; assembly 389 = 292 + 4 + 93; Pakistan rejected.
- Grouping optional (Congress) vs compulsory (League; Mission agreed); League withdrew 29 Jul; Direct Action 16 Aug; Interim Government under Nehru 2 Sep; League joined 26 Oct; Assembly met 9 Dec 1946 without the League.
