## PH-066: Einstein A and B coefficients

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Bridge: photon energy, the Boltzmann factor and big exponentials

Every result in this lesson is built from three small ideas.

**1. Photon energy.** Light of frequency ν (hertz, Hz, cycles per second) comes in packets of energy hν, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant. A two-level atom has a lower level of energy E1 and an upper level E2. It can exchange a photon with the light only if hν = E2 − E1 (the Bohr condition). For 500 nm light, ν = c/λ = 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz and hν = 2.5 eV (1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J).

**2. kT is an energy.** Boltzmann's constant is k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K. At T = 300 K, kT = 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J = 0.0259 eV, the typical thermal energy of one atom. The **Boltzmann factor** says that in thermal equilibrium the number of atoms in a level of energy E falls as e^(−E/kT). So for two levels, N2/N1 = e^(−(E2−E1)/kT) = e^(−hν/kT) (for single, non-degenerate levels). Write x = hν/kT: it compares the photon energy with the thermal energy.

**3. How big is e^x?** If x is small, e^x ≈ 1 + x. If x is large, e^x is astronomically big: e^10 = 2.2 × 10⁴, e^80 = 5.5 × 10³⁴. Scientific notation 5.5 × 10³⁴ means 55 followed by 33 zeros. In this lesson the whole story is the size of x: x ≈ 96 for visible light at 300 K, x ≈ 0.0016 for a 10 GHz microwave.

### Bridge: Bridge: radiation in a hot box, rho(nu) and the shape of Planck's law

Imagine a closed hot box at temperature T whose walls emit and absorb radiation until nothing changes. The light inside is **thermal (blackbody) radiation**. Its energy per unit volume in the narrow frequency band from ν to ν + dν is **ρ(ν) dν**. So **ρ(ν) is the energy density per unit frequency**, with units J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹ = J s m⁻³. (The book calls it the "photon density"; it is an energy density.)

Planck's law (statement here, derived in module 5) for empty space is

$$ ρ(ν) = (8π h ν³ / c³) · 1/(e^(hν/kT) − 1) $$

with c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. In a medium of refractive index n multiply by n³ (the book's μ). Two limits matter. If x = hν/kT ≪ 1 then e^x − 1 ≈ x and ρ ≈ 8π ν² kT/c³, the **Rayleigh-Jeans law**: it grows as ν² and would give infinite total energy, the "ultraviolet catastrophe" of classical physics. If x ≫ 1 then ρ ≈ (8π h ν³/c³) e^(−x), the **Wien law**: it dies off exponentially at high frequency. The real curve rises like Rayleigh-Jeans, peaks near x ≈ 2.8 (standard), and falls like Wien.

Why does this belong here? Einstein's 1916 argument takes this thermal radiation and demands that atoms be in balance with it. The balance condition tells us the relations among A and B.

### 1. Orientation: three ways light and an atom can exchange energy, and what Einstein did with them

*Objective:* Name the three radiative processes, say what each needs in order to happen, and state the three results this lesson will derive.

## The setting: a two-level atom
Take a gas of identical atoms. Look at just two of their energy levels: a lower level of energy E1 (the **lower level**, often the ground level) and an upper level of energy E2. The number of atoms per cubic metre sitting in each level is the **population**, N1 and N2. An atom and the light can swap energy only in packets of one photon, and only when the photon energy matches the gap: **hν = E2 − E1** (bridge B1). Everything below concerns that single pair of levels.

## The three processes
1. **Absorption.** An atom in E1 meets a photon of energy hν. The photon disappears and the atom jumps to E2. Written A + hν → A*. It needs a photon **and** a lower-level atom.
2. **Spontaneous emission.** An atom in E2 drops to E1 on its own and throws out a photon of energy hν. A* → A + hν. It needs only an upper-level atom; it happens whether or not any light is present. The book gives about 10⁻⁸ s as the usual wait before an ordinary excited level empties itself (PDF p. 618).
3. **Stimulated (induced) emission.** An atom in E2 meets a passing photon of energy hν. The atom drops to E1 and emits a second photon; the passing photon is not used up. A* + hν → A + 2hν. It needs a photon **and** an upper-level atom. The new photon is a clone of the old one: same frequency, direction, phase and polarisation (PDF p. 620).

Hand-drawn figure to make (three small panels, levels drawn as horizontal lines):
Absorption: photon arrow in, atom arrow up from E1 to E2. Spontaneous: atom arrow down, one photon arrow out in a random direction. Stimulated: one photon in, atom arrow down, two parallel photon arrows out.

## Why Einstein needed all three
Suppose only absorption and spontaneous emission existed. A steady state would still exist, because spontaneous emission grows as the upper level fills. Combined with the Boltzmann law it would force ρ to the Wien form, ρ ∝ e^(−hν/kT), which fails at low frequency, where ρ must grow like ν² (module 5). Einstein (1916, as the extract says on p. 614) saw that a third process is needed, in which light forces an excited atom down. That process is stimulated emission; with it the balance gives Planck's law at every frequency, and it is the process that a laser exploits. (The book's pp. 619 to 620 argument that the atoms would all end up excited is loose.)

## What this lesson will derive
- Definitions and units of the coefficients **A21, B12 and B21** (module 2).
- By demanding equilibrium with thermal radiation: **g1 B12 = g2 B21** (g is the number of states in a level) and **A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³**, and with them **Planck's law** (modules 3 to 5).
- How rare stimulated emission is in ordinary light and why the answer changes between visible light and microwaves (module 6).
- Why two levels alone cannot give laser action, and what a laser needs instead (modules 7 and 8).

### 2. Foundation: the coefficients A21, B12, B21, the rate of each process, and their units

*Objective:* Write the rate of absorption, spontaneous and stimulated emission, define A21, B12 and B21 and give each a unit that you can verify by dimensional analysis.

## Einstein's three postulates
These are the "postulates" the 2020 question asks about (the numbering is the lesson's own wording of the extract's Eqs. 22.5, 22.9, 22.13).

1. **Absorption** of a photon by an atom in E1 occurs with a probability per second proportional to the radiation density: P12 = B12 ρ(ν).
2. **Spontaneous emission** from E2 occurs with a probability per second that does not depend on the radiation: P(spont) = A21.
3. **Stimulated emission** from E2 occurs with a probability per second proportional to the radiation density: P21 = B21 ρ(ν); the emitted photon has the same frequency, phase, direction and polarisation as the stimulating photon.

Here ρ(ν) is the energy per unit volume per unit frequency of the radiation at the transition frequency (bridge B2). B12, B21 and A21 are constants of the atom; they depend on the two levels, not on the temperature or the amount of light.

## Rates per unit volume
A rate is the number of events per cubic metre per second. Probability per atom times number of atoms gives
$$ R_abs = B12 ρ(ν) N1 ,   R_sp = A21 N2 ,   R_st = B21 ρ(ν) N2 $$
(book Eqs. 22.8, 22.10, 22.14). Spontaneous emission from E1 up to E2 does not happen, so **A12 = 0** (Eq. 22.11 and 22.18): an atom in the lower level cannot spontaneously climb.

## Units
- **A21**: P(spont) is "per second", so A21 has the unit **s⁻¹**. If N2 atoms are left alone, dN2/dt = −A21 N2 and N2 = N2(0) e^(−A21 t). So **1/A21 = τ**, the radiative lifetime of the upper level. (Page 619 says the probability "is equal to the lifetime"; it is the reciprocal, 1/τ. The same page then correctly calls 1/A21 the lifetime.) A level that empties in 10⁻⁸ s has A21 ≈ 10⁸ s⁻¹; a metastable level of 10⁻³ s has A21 ≈ 10³ s⁻¹ (lifetimes quoted on PDF p. 628).
- **ρ(ν)**: J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹ = J s m⁻³.
- **B12, B21**: B ρ must be s⁻¹, so [B] = s⁻¹ / (J s m⁻³) = **m³ J⁻¹ s⁻²**. Using J = kg m² s⁻² this equals m kg⁻¹ (the book writes the answer of its Example 22.2 as m/kg; the two are the same unit).

## Dimension check of the key ratio
The relation A21/B21 = 8π h ν³/c³ (derived in module 5) must have the units of ρ: h ν³/c³ → (J s)(s⁻³)/(m³ s⁻³) = J s m⁻³ ✓. And the ratio A21/(B21 ρ) is (s⁻¹)/((m³ J⁻¹ s⁻²)(J s m⁻³)) = dimensionless ✓. This ratio is the ratio of spontaneous to stimulated emission rate, the star of module 6.

## Spontaneous versus stimulated, side by side
Spontaneous photons leave at random times, in random directions, with random phase and polarisation, so a crowd of N atoms gives total intensity N·I, the sum of intensities (PDF p. 619). Stimulated photons are in-phase copies, so in the book's field-addition argument the amplitudes add and the intensity in the common direction goes as N²·I (PDF pp. 620 to 622); the total energy released is still N hν, since each transition frees one photon, and coherence only concentrates it in one direction, phase and narrow band. Stimulated emission can be controlled from outside by controlling the light; spontaneous emission cannot.

## Common slip
Calling ρ "photon density" (the book does this). It is an **energy** density per unit **frequency**; a photon count would not have the units J s m⁻³.

### 3. Core: thermal equilibrium, the Boltzmann ratio and the derivation of g1 B12 = g2 B21 (derivation 1, part 1)

*Objective:* Derive the radiation density that keeps the atoms in balance and use the T → ∞ requirement to prove g1 B12 = g2 B21.

## Assumptions and conventions
A closed box at constant temperature T contains many identical atoms with two levels E1 < E2, hν = E2 − E1, filled with thermal radiation of energy density ρ(ν). Levels may be degenerate: level 1 has g1 states and level 2 has g2 states (**degeneracy**, the number of different states with the same energy; for a non-degenerate level g = 1). B12 is defined for the transition from the level 1 to the level 2 as a whole. N1 and N2 are the populations of the whole levels. Nothing changes in time: **steady state**. Each atom is at rest, a sharp line, no other levels.

## Step 1. Detailed balance
In steady state the number of upward transitions per second equals the number of downward ones (book Eq. 22.19):
$$ B12 ρ N1 = A21 N2 + B21 ρ N2 $$

## Step 2. Solve for ρ
Gather ρ: ρ (B12 N1 − B21 N2) = A21 N2. Divide the top and bottom by B21 N2:
$$ ρ = (A21/B21) / ( (B12 N1)/(B21 N2) − 1 ) $$

## Step 3. Insert the Boltzmann law
With degeneracy, the population of a level is proportional to g e^(−E/kT), so N2/N1 = (g2/g1) e^(−hν/kT), that is N1/N2 = (g1/g2) e^(hν/kT). Then
$$ ρ = (A21/B21) / ( (g1 B12 / g2 B21) e^(hν/kT) − 1 ) $$
This is the general form of the book's Eq. 22.22, which sets g1 = g2 = 1.

## Step 4. Require ρ → ∞ as T → ∞
At any fixed frequency the radiation energy density grows without limit as T rises (classically ρ ∝ ν² kT, module 5; the Stefan-Boltzmann law says the same of the total). As T → ∞, e^(hν/kT) → 1 and the denominator → (g1 B12 / g2 B21) − 1. If this were any non-zero number then ρ(T → ∞) would stay finite (or become negative, which is impossible), contradicting the physical fact. A and B depend only on the atom, not on T, so the only way to let ρ diverge is
$$ g1 B12 = g2 B21 $$
(book Eq. 22.25: B12 = B21 for non-degenerate levels). The same relation is forced independently by matching to Planck's law at all T (module 5).

## Step 5. The compact result
Putting it back,
$$ ρ(ν) = (A21/B21) / (e^(hν/kT) − 1) $$
Check: as T → 0, ρ → 0 ✓; the only unknown is the T-independent constant A21/B21, fixed in module 5.

## Interpretation
The result g1 B12 = g2 B21 says: per state, the atom is as good at absorbing a photon as at being stimulated into emitting one. The two induced transitions are mirror images; they differ only if the levels differ in how many states they contain. This symmetry is the origin of all the laser physics that follows: stimulated emission will always cancel absorption exactly when N2/g2 = N1/g1.

## Checks
Units: all three terms of B12 ρ N1 = A21 N2 + B21 ρ N2 are m⁻³ s⁻¹ ✓. Limits: g1 = g2 recovers B12 = B21. The book also gives a derivation by "demanding Planck" (Eqs. 22.22 to 22.26); this module reaches the same relation from the T → ∞ argument without assuming Planck.

### 4. Core: counting the modes of radiation in a box, and the Rayleigh-Jeans law (derivation 1, part 2)

*Objective:* Derive the number of standing-wave modes per unit volume per unit frequency, 8π n³ ν²/c³, and the Rayleigh-Jeans energy density from it.

## Why counting modes
Module 3 left one unknown constant, A21/B21. To pin it down we need to know how much radiation a hot box holds at low frequency, where classical physics works. That needs the number of independent ways light can stand in the box: its **modes** (a mode is one allowed standing wave, as in PH-039 for a string).

## Setup and assumptions
A cubical box of side L and volume V = L³, perfectly reflecting walls, filled with a medium of refractive index n (n = 1 for vacuum or, to good accuracy, air). Waves must vanish at the walls. The wave number is k = 2π/λ = 2π n ν/c.

## Step 1. Allowed wave vectors
A standing wave along x needs half-wavelengths to fit: kx = π n_x / L with n_x = 1, 2, 3, ... and the same for y and z (the integers n_x, n_y, n_z must not be confused with the refractive index n). The mode is labelled by the triple (n_x, n_y, n_z) of positive integers, and k² = (π/L)² (n_x² + n_y² + n_z²).

## Step 2. Count the triples up to a wave number k
In "integer space" the triples with magnitude at most K = kL/π fill one **octant** (the positive eighth) of a sphere of radius K. Each triple occupies unit volume, so the number of triples is
$$ (1/8)(4π/3) K³ = (π/6)(kL/π)³ = k³ V / (6π²) $$

## Step 3. Two polarisations
Light is a transverse wave, with two independent polarisations for each triple. Multiply by 2:
$$ N(k) = k³ V / (3π²) $$

## Step 4. Convert to frequency
Put k = 2π n ν/c: N(ν) = (8π V/3)(n ν/c)³. Differentiate: dN = 8π V n³ ν² dν / c³. Divide by V:
$$ number of modes per unit volume per unit frequency = 8π n³ ν² / c³ $$
(The same result holds for any shape of the box at high frequency; standard, verify.)

## Step 5. Dimension and size
[ν²/c³] = s⁻²/(m³ s⁻³) = s m⁻³ ✓ (per m³ per Hz). For vacuum light at 500 nm (ν = 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz): 3.35 × 10⁵ m⁻³ Hz⁻¹, so a band 1 GHz wide contains 3.4 × 10¹⁴ modes in every cubic metre.

## Step 6. Rayleigh-Jeans energy density
Classical statistical mechanics (equipartition) gives each mode an average energy kT (kT/2 from the electric and kT/2 from the magnetic field energy, as for an oscillator). Then
$$ ρ_RJ(ν) = 8π n³ ν² kT / c³ $$

## Interpretation
ρ_RJ grows as ν², so the total over all ν diverges: the ultraviolet catastrophe. Quantum theory cures it by allowing a mode of frequency ν energy only in lumps hν. A mode with energies 0, hν, 2hν, ... weighted by Boltzmann factors has mean energy hν/(e^x − 1), x = hν/kT: since Σ m e^(−mx) / Σ e^(−mx) = e^(−x)/(1 − e^(−x)) = 1/(e^x − 1). Replacing kT by this gives Planck's law directly. For x ≪ 1 it becomes kT.

Einstein's route in module 5 does **not** use the quantised mean energy; it needs only the classical Rayleigh-Jeans limit, which is a safe low-frequency check.

## Hand-drawn figure to make
Integer lattice (n_x, n_y) in two dimensions with a quarter-circle of radius K shaded; mention that in three dimensions the quarter-circle becomes an octant of a sphere.

## Common slip
Forgetting the factor 2 for polarisation, or the 1/8 for positive integers only: the product (1/8) × 2 = 1/4 appears in N(k) = (1/4)(4π/3)(kL/π)³.

### 5. Core: fixing A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³ and recovering Planck's law, with its limits (derivation 1, part 3)

*Objective:* Complete the Einstein derivation by fixing A21/B21 from the Rayleigh-Jeans limit, obtain Planck's law, check the Wien and Rayleigh-Jeans limits and explain the n versus c factor.

## Where we stand
Module 3 gave, in thermal equilibrium, ρ(ν) = (A21/B21) / (e^x − 1) with x = hν/kT, after using g1 B12 = g2 B21. Module 4 gave the classical low-frequency answer ρ_RJ = 8π n³ ν² kT/c³. The atom constants A21 and B21 do not know the temperature, so one comparison fixes their ratio for ever.

## Step 1. Expand for small x
For x ≪ 1 (low frequency or high temperature), e^x − 1 ≈ x, so
$$ ρ ≈ (A21/B21) · kT / (hν) $$

## Step 2. Match to Rayleigh-Jeans
Set (A21/B21) kT/(hν) = 8π n³ ν² kT/c³. The kT cancels:
$$ A21 / B21 = 8π h ν³ n³ / c³ $$
This is the book's Eq. 22.24 (the book writes μ for n). The same result written with B12 for non-degenerate levels is Eq. 22.26: A21 = (8π h ν³ μ³/c³) B12.

## Step 3. Substitute back: Planck's law
$$ ρ(ν) = (8π h ν³ n³ / c³) · 1/(e^(hν/kT) − 1) $$
This is Planck's law (book Eq. 22.23). Note what happened: Planck's law has been obtained from three ideas only, (i) equilibrium of atoms with radiation, (ii) the Boltzmann law with the Bohr condition, (iii) the correct low-frequency limit. No quantisation of the radiation oscillators was assumed (the Rayleigh-Jeans match does use the classical kT per mode).

## Step 4. Why the relation holds outside equilibrium
A21 and B21 belong to the atom. Having found A21/B21 in a box at temperature T we may use it for atoms in any radiation field, including a laser. This is why the ratio is the most-used relation in the whole subject.

## Step 5. The two limits again
- **Rayleigh-Jeans** (x ≪ 1): B21 ρ/A21 = 1/(e^x − 1) ≈ 1/x ≫ 1, so stimulated emission is far more frequent than spontaneous emission.
- **Wien** (x ≫ 1): B21 ρ/A21 = e^(−x) ≪ 1, so spontaneous emission dominates; ρ ≈ (8π h ν³ n³/c³) e^(−x), which is Wien's distribution (an approximation; not the displacement law), and has the form of A21/B21 times the Boltzmann factor, i.e. the stimulated term has dropped out of the balance.

## Dimension and size
[A/B] = s⁻¹ / (m³ J⁻¹ s⁻²) = J s m⁻³ ✓. For vacuum and 500 nm: A21/B21 = 8π h ν³/c³ = 1.33 × 10⁻¹³ J s m⁻³.

## The c versus n factor
In a medium, light moves at c/n and the mode density is multiplied by n³ (module 4). So A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³ = 8π h ν³ / v³, with v = c/n the speed in the medium, provided n does not change much over the line (no strong dispersion). For gases n ≈ 1. Exam answers usually quote the n = 1 form 8π h ν³/c³; the book keeps μ. If the question gives a medium, keep n³.

## The other frequency convention
If ρ is defined per unit angular frequency ω = 2πν, ρ(ω) = ρ(ν)/2π and the rate equations then define B_ω = 2π B_ν, with A unchanged. Then A21/B_ω = ħ ω³ n³/(π² c³) where ħ = h/2π. Numerically 8π h ν³/c³ divided by 2π equals 4 h ν³/c³; check ħ ω³/(π² c³) = 4π² h ν³/(π² c³) = 4 h ν³/c³ ✓. When you quote a formula, say which convention. The book and this lesson use ρ per unit frequency ν.

### 6. Core: how rare is stimulated emission? Visible light against microwaves, the temperature of equal rates, and why X-ray lasers are hard

*Objective:* Compute and interpret the ratio of spontaneous to stimulated emission for given ν and T, find the temperature at which the two are equal, and explain the ν³ difficulty for short wavelengths.

## The ratio
Divide the two emission rates (book Eq. 22.27, with the same N2 in both):
$$ R_st / R_sp = B21 ρ(ν) / A21 = ρ(ν) c³ / (8π h ν³ n³) $$
The last form uses module 5. For radiation in equilibrium at temperature T this is **1/(e^x − 1)**, x = hν/kT (book Eq. 22.28). Inverted:
$$ R_sp / R_st = e^(hν/kT) − 1 $$
Meaning: B21 ρ/A21 equals the **mean number of photons per mode**. When it is below 1, an upper-level atom is far more likely to decay on its own than to be stimulated. Also R_st/R_abs = N2/N1 (times g1/g2), so stimulated emission can never beat absorption unless the upper level is more populated (module 7).

## Numbers at T = 300 K (all recomputed with Python)
| Radiation | ν | x = hν/kT | R_sp/R_st |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visible, 500 nm | 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz | 95.9 | 4.5 × 10⁴¹ |
| Infrared, 10 μm | 3.0 × 10¹³ Hz | 4.80 | 120 |
| Crossover, 69 μm | 4.33 × 10¹² Hz | 0.693 | 1.0 |
| Microwave, 3 cm (10 GHz) | 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz | 1.60 × 10⁻³ | 1.6 × 10⁻³ |

So in a 300 K box, visible-light atoms emit spontaneously about 10⁴² times more often than they are stimulated. The book's own estimate for ν = 5 × 10¹⁴ Hz is R_st/R_sp = 10⁻⁵⁸ (Eq. 22.28 text); the correct value is e^(−80.0) = 1.8 × 10⁻³⁵ (a slip in the book, recorded in the master notes). Either way the conclusion stands: stimulated emission is negligible in thermal visible light. At 10 GHz the roles are reversed: stimulated emission is about 625 times more frequent than spontaneous, which is why the first stimulated-emission devices were microwave masers (the book, PDF p. 614, dates the first maser to 1954).

Caution: at 10 GHz and 300 K, R_st/R_abs = N2/N1 = e^(−x) = 0.9984, so absorption exceeds stimulated emission very slightly: nothing amplifies.

## The temperature at which the two rates are equal (the 2020 question)
Set R_sp = R_st: e^x − 1 = 1, so x = ln 2 = 0.693, so
$$ T = hν / (k ln 2) $$
For λ = 5000 Å = 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m: ν = c/λ = 5.996 × 10¹⁴ Hz, hν/k = 2.878 × 10⁴ K, T = 2.878 × 10⁴ / 0.693 = 4.15 × 10⁴ K (the book's Example 22.3 prints 41,558 K using ν = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz; same to 3 figures). So **yes**, but only at about 41,500 K, far above the Sun's surface (about 5800 K, where e^x − 1 = 142 at 500 nm). At 300 K the crossover frequency is ν* = kT ln 2/h = 4.33 × 10¹² Hz.

## Why higher frequencies are harder (the X-ray remark)
The book's statement (PDF p. 624): A21/B21 ∝ ν³. To make stimulated emission equal spontaneous emission you need ρ(ν) = A21/B21 = 8π h ν³/c³, which rises as ν³. Illustrative numbers (1 GHz bandwidth, vacuum): at 500 nm ρ = 1.3 × 10⁻¹³ J s m⁻³, intensity about 4 × 10⁴ W m⁻²; at 0.1 nm ρ is 1.25 × 10¹¹ times larger and the intensity needed is about 5 × 10¹⁵ W m⁻². Upper-level lifetimes also become very short at short wavelengths, so the pump power needed to hold an inversion rises steeply (the exact scaling is not in the extract: verify), and good mirrors are hard to make for X-rays (standard).

### 7. Core: why a two-level system cannot be population-inverted (derivation 2; the 2019 question)

*Objective:* Prove from the rate equations that radiation alone can at most equalise the populations of two levels, and state what is needed instead.

## What 'population inversion' means
**Population inversion** is the condition N2 > N1 for the upper and lower level of a transition (more precisely N2/g2 > N1/g1). It is a non-equilibrium state: the Boltzmann law at any positive T always gives N2 < N1, and N2 > N1 would need a "negative temperature" (book, PDF p. 627). It is needed because the net stimulated gain is proportional to N2 − (g2/g1) N1 (module 8): without inversion absorption wins.

## Assumptions
Identical atoms, only levels 1 and 2, constant total population N = N1 + N2 per unit volume. The only pump is incoherent radiation of density ρ at the line frequency, broad compared with the line, so the rate equations hold. First take g1 = g2.

## Step 1. Rate equation
$$ dN2/dt = B12 ρ N1 − (A21 + B21 ρ) N2 ,   dN1/dt = −dN2/dt $$

## Step 2. Steady state
Put dN2/dt = 0: N2/N1 = B12 ρ/(A21 + B21 ρ). With g1 B12 = g2 B21,
$$ N2/N1 = (g2/g1) · s/(1 + s) ,   s = B21 ρ/A21 $$
Since s/(1 + s) < 1 for every finite ρ, we get **N2/g2 < N1/g1: the upper level is never more populated per state**. For g1 = g2, N2 < N1.

## Step 3. The limit of strong pumping
As ρ → ∞, s → ∞ and s/(1 + s) → 1: N2 → N1 (g1 = g2). This is **saturation or transparency**: absorption and stimulated emission exactly cancel, and the medium neither absorbs nor amplifies.
Consistency check: for blackbody radiation s = 1/(e^x − 1) and N2/N1 = e^(−x), the Boltzmann law ✓.

## Step 4. It is not only a steady-state result
Let ΔN = N2 − N1 (g1 = g2). Subtracting the two rate equations, dΔN/dt = −2B ρ ΔN − 2 A21 N2. Start with the atoms in the ground state (ΔN < 0). Could ΔN rise to zero and cross? At the instant ΔN = 0, N2 = N/2 > 0, so dΔN/dt = −A21 N < 0: ΔN is falling, not rising. Hence ΔN can never become positive, whatever the (non-negative) ρ(t).

## Interpretation
A photon of frequency ν lifts an atom as readily as it knocks one down (g1 B12 = g2 B21), and spontaneous emission only pushes downward, so light at the transition frequency can level the populations from below but never overtake.

## What does work
Use a **third level**: pump atoms from level 1 into a high level 3, let them fall quickly into a long-lived (metastable) level 2, and use 2 → 1 as the lasing transition. The pump and the laser transition now differ, so the symmetry above does not apply. In a three-level scheme more than half of all atoms must reach level 2 (the book's 'N2 > N1/2' needs care; see the source notes). In a four-level scheme the lower laser level lies far above the ground level (energy gap much more than kT) and is empty, so a small N2 suffices (PDF p. 634). Ruby (three-level) and He-Ne (four-level) are in Day 20 lesson PH-067.

## Exceptions the question's word 'in general' leaves open (standard; verify)
- A short, strong, coherent resonant pulse (a "π pulse") can flip a two-level atom and give a transient inversion; the rate equations assume incoherent light and do not apply, and the inversion cannot be held.
- A sudden reversal of the magnetic field on a spin system can put spins in the upper level; the resulting negative spin temperature decays.
- **State selection**, the classic case: in the ammonia maser (inversion line about 23.87 GHz) an inhomogeneous electric field focuses upper-level molecules into the cavity and removes lower-level ones, so the beam is inverted by separation, not by light.
None is steady-state pumping of a closed two-level system by ordinary light.

### 8. Core: conditions for laser action, the gain coefficient and the threshold population inversion (derivation 3; the 2025 question)

*Objective:* State the three conditions for stimulated emission to dominate, derive the gain coefficient from the Einstein coefficients, and obtain the threshold inversion for a two-mirror resonator.

## Three conditions (book, PDF pp. 625 to 626)
- Stimulated beats **absorption**: R_st/R_abs = N2/N1 (g's equal) > 1, so **N2 > N1** (population inversion).
- Stimulated beats **spontaneous**: R_st/R_sp = B21 ρ/A21 > 1, so a **small A21 (a long-lived, metastable upper level)** and/or a **large ρ**.
- Large ρ comes from trapping the light: a **resonator** of two mirrors.

## Gain coefficient (the lesson's own derivation; verify)
**Assumptions:** a narrow collimated beam of frequency ν and intensity I (W m⁻²) along z in a medium of refractive index n; the atomic line has a normalised shape S(ν) (S, not g, to avoid a clash with the degeneracies g1, g2) (∫S dν = 1, unit s), so an upper-level atom is stimulated at the rate B21 u S(ν), where u is the beam energy density; degeneracies g1, g2.
1. Energy density of a beam travelling at c/n: u = nI/c.
2. Net photons added per m³ per second = B21 u S (N2 − (g2/g1) N1), because absorption removes at the rate B12 u S N1 = (g2/g1) B21 u S N1.
3. Each photon carries hν, so power per area gained per length: dI/dz = hν B21 S ΔN u, with ΔN = N2 − (g2/g1) N1.
4. Substitute u: dI/dz = γ I, with **γ = (hν n/c) B21 S(ν) ΔN = σ ΔN**.
5. Use B21 = A21 c³/(8π h ν³ n³): **σ = A21 λ0² S(ν)/(8π n²)**, where λ0 = c/ν is the vacuum wavelength. Unit check: s⁻¹ · m² · s = m² ✓. γ is positive only if ΔN > 0: the inversion condition again.

## Threshold in a two-mirror resonator (book Eqs. 22.33 to 22.39)
**Setup:** mirrors M1, M2 with intensity reflectivities r1, r2, separation L, the medium fills the gap, gain coefficient γ, distributed loss α_s (scattering, diffraction) per metre.
1. One pass: I → I e^((γ − α_s) L).
2. Round trip (two passes, two reflections): I → r1 r2 I e^(2(γ − α_s) L).
3. Oscillation needs round-trip gain G ≥ 1: r1 r2 e^(2(γ − α_s) L) ≥ 1.
4. Take logarithms: 2(γ − α_s)L ≥ ln(1/(r1 r2)), so
$$ γ_th = α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/(r1 r2)) $$
(Eq. 22.39).
5. With γ = σ ΔN, the **threshold population inversion** is
$$ ΔN_th = γ_th/σ = (8π n² / (A21 λ0² S(ν))) · [ α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/(r1 r2)) ] $$
For the peak of a line of width Δν, S ≈ 1/Δν (about 0.94/Δν for a Gaussian), so a broad line needs a bigger inversion.

## Checks
Dimension: [ΔN_th] = m⁻³; [8π n²/(A λ² S)] = 1/(s⁻¹ m² s) = m⁻² and the bracket is m⁻¹, product m⁻³ ✓. Limit: a perfect resonator with no loss (r1 = r2 = 1, α_s = 0) gives ΔN_th = 0, any inversion lases. Book Example 22.4: r1 = 1, γ_th = 0.0005 cm⁻¹, L = 15 cm, α_s = 0: r2 = e^(−2γL) = e^(−0.015) = 0.985 ✓.

## Illustrative numbers (original; not the data of any real laser)
λ0 = 632.8 nm, n = 1, A21 = 1.0 × 10⁶ s⁻¹, Gaussian line of width 1.5 GHz so S = 6.26 × 10⁻¹⁰ s, giving σ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ m². With L = 0.30 m, r1 = 1, r2 = 0.98, α_s = 0: γ_th = ln(1/0.98)/0.60 = 0.0337 m⁻¹ and ΔN_th = 3.4 × 10¹⁵ m⁻³. Adding α_s = 0.01 m⁻¹ raises it to 4.4 × 10¹⁵ m⁻³.

### 9. Master: stimulated emission and coherence, and where this lesson stops

*Objective:* Explain why stimulated emission produces coherent light, say what the resonator adds, and use coherence length to compare a laser with a lamp.

## The cloning property and N²
In stimulated emission the new photon has the same frequency, direction, phase and polarisation as the stimulating photon (book, PDF p. 620). Each stimulated photon is a copy, so the electric fields of the copies add with a fixed phase relation. If N atoms radiate with field amplitude E each:
- **Spontaneous (incoherent)**: random phases, intensities add: I_total = N I (book Eq. 22.12).
- **Stimulated (coherent)**: equal phases, amplitudes add: total amplitude N E, so the intensity in the common direction is I_total = N² I (Eq. 22.15).

This is the book's field-addition statement for fixed-amplitude, in-phase emitters (a peak or directional intensity). It does not mean the same atoms release more energy: N stimulated transitions free N hν, so the total power is still proportional to N. For N = 10⁶ the in-phase intensity in the common direction is 10⁶ times that of the incoherent sum; coherence concentrates the energy in one direction, one phase and a narrow band.

## What the resonator adds
Stimulation alone makes a copy of whatever photon it meets, but photons start from spontaneous emission in all directions and with random phase. Two mirrors (a Fabry-Perot resonator) select the axial direction, feed the light back, and enforce standing waves: 2 n L = m λ (book Eq. 22.41), with spacing between neighbouring longitudinal modes Δν = c/(2 n L). For L = 0.5 m, n = 1, and λ = 5000 Å the cavity supports m = 2nL/λ = 2 × 10⁶ half-wavelengths (the book's count), and Δν = 3.0 × 10⁸ Hz = 300 MHz. Only those modes lying inside the gain profile above threshold (module 8) can oscillate. The beam is then close to one frequency and one direction.

## Coherence length
**Temporal coherence** is how long the wave keeps a predictable phase; **coherence length** l_c ≈ c/Δν (order of magnitude, the Day 18 lesson PH-051 section 'Core: visibility, temporal coherence, coherence length and white-light fringes' shows its measurement with a Michelson interferometer). The book quotes about 0.3 mm for a sodium lamp and about 100 m for an ordinary He-Ne laser (PDF p. 649). Converting with l_c = c/Δν: 0.3 mm corresponds to Δν ≈ 1.0 × 10¹² Hz and 100 m to Δν ≈ 3 × 10⁶ Hz, a factor of about 3 × 10⁵ narrower. (The conversion is the lesson's, not the book's.)

## One comment
The photon-clone property is the opposite of what a thermal source does: for thermal visible light the stimulated fraction is about 10⁻⁴² (module 6), so thermal light is incoherent in this sense.

## Where this lesson stops
Three-level and four-level schemes (the pumping physics behind inversion), the ruby and He-Ne devices, modes of the beam and applications are in Day 20 lesson PH-067 (Ruby and He-Ne lasers) and the same book chapter; spatial and temporal coherence and the focusing of laser beams are Day 21 lesson PH-068 (Laser coherence and beam focusing). This module is only the recap needed to read the 2025 question, and the lesson ends with the threshold condition.

## Common slip
Writing 'coherent' for the spontaneous glow of a hot gas, or saying a laser has 'a single frequency'. A laser line is narrow but has a finite width Δν; coherence length is finite.

### Notes: Definitions

- Population N1, N2: atoms per unit volume (m⁻³) in the lower and upper level; g1, g2 are the numbers of states (degeneracies) of the levels.
- Bohr condition: a photon is exchanged only if hν = E2 − E1.
- ρ(ν): energy per unit volume per unit frequency of the radiation, unit J s m⁻³ (J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹). The book calls it 'photon density'; it is an energy density.
- A21: Einstein coefficient of spontaneous emission, unit s⁻¹; 1/A21 = τ, the radiative lifetime of the upper level. A12 = 0.
- B12, B21: Einstein coefficients of absorption and stimulated emission for ρ per unit frequency, unit m³ J⁻¹ s⁻² (= m kg⁻¹).
- Population inversion: N2/g2 > N1/g1 (N2 > N1 for non-degenerate levels), a non-equilibrium state; describable by a negative Boltzmann temperature, which is a label and not a temperature below absolute zero.
- Metastable level: an upper level with a small A21 (long lifetime, about 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ s in the book, PDF p. 628).
- Round-trip gain G = r1 r2 e^(2(γ − α_s)L); threshold G = 1.

### Notes: Equations to know

- R_abs = B12 ρ N1 ;  R_sp = A21 N2 ;  R_st = B21 ρ N2  (m⁻³ s⁻¹)
- Equilibrium: B12 ρ N1 = A21 N2 + B21 ρ N2 ;  N2/N1 = (g2/g1) e^(−hν/kT)
- g1 B12 = g2 B21  (book: B12 = B21 for non-degenerate levels)
- A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³  (n = 1: 8π h ν³/c³ ; book uses μ for n)
- ρ(ν) = (8π h ν³ n³/c³)/(e^(hν/kT) − 1) ;  modes per volume per Hz = 8π n³ ν²/c³
- R_sp/R_st = e^(hν/kT) − 1 ;  equal rates at T = hν/(k ln 2)
- Two-level steady state: N2/N1 = (g2/g1) s/(1 + s), s = B21 ρ/A21
- γ = σ [N2 − (g2/g1) N1], σ = A21 λ0² S(ν)/(8π n²) ;  γ_th = α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/(r1 r2)) ;  ΔN_th = γ_th/σ
- ω convention: ρ(ω) = ρ(ν)/2π, B_ω = 2π B_ν, A21/B_ω = ħ ω³ n³/(π² c³)

### Notes: Derivation skeleton 1: Einstein relations and Planck's law (15 marks, practice target about 18 minutes)

- 1. Two levels, hν = E2 − E1, thermal radiation ρ(ν), steady state, degeneracies g1, g2.
- 2. B12 ρ N1 = A21 N2 + B21 ρ N2, so ρ = (A21/B21)/((B12 N1)/(B21 N2) − 1).
- 3. Boltzmann: N1/N2 = (g1/g2) e^(hν/kT), so ρ = (A21/B21)/((g1 B12/g2 B21) e^(hν/kT) − 1).
- 4. T → ∞ with ρ → ∞ forces g1 B12 = g2 B21, so ρ = (A21/B21)/(e^(hν/kT) − 1).
- 5. Small x: ρ ≈ (A21/B21) kT/hν; equate to Rayleigh-Jeans 8π n³ ν² kT/c³ to get A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³.
- 6. Substitute: Planck's law; Wien limit (x ≫ 1) and Rayleigh-Jeans limit (x ≪ 1).
- 7. Checks: units J s m⁻³; g1 = g2 gives B12 = B21; T → 0 gives ρ → 0.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton 2: a two-level system cannot be inverted (10 marks, about 12 minutes)

- 1. dN2/dt = B12 ρ N1 − (A21 + B21 ρ) N2, N1 + N2 = N.
- 2. Steady state N2/N1 = B12 ρ/(A21 + B21 ρ) = (g2/g1) s/(1 + s), s = B21 ρ/A21.
- 3. s/(1 + s) < 1 for all finite ρ, so N2/g2 < N1/g1; ρ → ∞ gives N2/g2 → N1/g1 (transparency).
- 4. Time dependent (g1 = g2): dΔN/dt = −2B ρ ΔN − 2A21 N2; at ΔN = 0, dΔN/dt = −A21 N < 0, so ΔN cannot become positive.
- 5. Remedy: pump through a third level (three- or four-level schemes); exceptions: coherent π pulse, spin systems (verify).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton 3: threshold population inversion (20 marks, about 24 minutes)

- 1. Mirrors r1, r2, length L, loss α_s, gain γ. One pass e^((γ − α_s)L); round trip G = r1 r2 e^(2(γ − α_s)L) ≥ 1.
- 2. γ_th = α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/(r1 r2)) (book Eq. 22.39).
- 3. γ = σ [N2 − (g2/g1)N1] with σ = (hν n/c) B21 S(ν) = A21 λ0² S(ν)/(8π n²).
- 4. ΔN_th = γ_th/σ = (8π n²/(A21 λ0² S)) [α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/(r1 r2))].
- 5. Checks: m⁻³; lossless perfect cavity gives zero; a broad line (small S) raises the threshold.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- A21 (a rate, s⁻¹) versus B21 (a rate per unit energy density, m³ J⁻¹ s⁻²): the latter is multiplied by ρ to become a rate.
- Stimulated emission versus absorption: same B (per state); they differ only by the populations N2 and N1.
- Spontaneous/stimulated = e^x − 1 (≫ 1 for visible); stimulated/spontaneous = 1/(e^x − 1) is its reciprocal. Do not swap.
- Population inversion versus laser threshold: inversion is necessary; threshold also needs the inversion to cover all the losses.
- Negative temperature versus colder than absolute zero: a negative temperature describes a state hotter than any positive T in the sense of energy flow, not a lower temperature (label only).
- ρ per unit ν versus per unit ω: B differs by 2π.
- Spontaneous light is not coherent; stimulated light is coherent with the stimulating beam.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits of the argument

- The relations A21/B21 and g1 B12 = g2 B21 assume a sharp line and non-dispersive medium; in a dispersive medium n(ν) enters ν-dependently (the mode density becomes 8π n² n_g ν²/c³ with n_g the group index; standard, verify).
- The rate equations assume incoherent, broadband radiation; coherent pulses behave differently (Rabi flopping) and can invert a two-level atom transiently (verify).
- Ratios R_sp/R_st = e^x − 1 use blackbody ρ at temperature T; in a laser cavity ρ is vastly different and B21 ρ/A21 can be ≫ 1 even for visible light.
- Equilibrium with radiation is a derivation tool; the coefficients are valid also out of equilibrium.
- Degeneracy: use N2/g2 and N1/g1 whenever the levels are degenerate.
- Hydrogen maser (21-cm line, about 1.42 GHz): the upper hyperfine level is likewise selected by a magnetic state-selector before the atoms enter the cavity (standard; verify). The ammonia (about 23.87 GHz) and hydrogen frequencies are standard values not printed in the extract.

### Core concepts

- Three processes: absorption (photon + lower atom), spontaneous emission (upper atom alone), stimulated emission (photon + upper atom, produces a clone).
- Equilibrium argument: B12 ρ N1 = A21 N2 + B21 ρ N2 with the Boltzmann ratio gives g1 B12 = g2 B21 and, with Rayleigh-Jeans, A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³.
- A two-level system under radiation cannot be inverted (N2/g2 ≤ N1/g1); lasers need a third level, a metastable level and a resonator.

**A.** Absorption and stimulated emission depend on ρ; spontaneous emission does not.

**Q.** Estimate the energy density ρ (per unit frequency) and the intensity (for a 1 GHz band) needed in vacuum for B21 ρ = A21 at 500 nm and at 0.1 nm.

**Q.** Write the three rate expressions and the units of A21 and B21.

**A.** R_abs = B12 ρ N1; R_sp = A21 N2; R_st = B21 ρ N2; A21 in s⁻¹; B in m³ J⁻¹ s⁻².

**Q.** State the relation between A21 and B21 and the ratio of spontaneous to stimulated rates in thermal equilibrium.

**A.** A21/B21 = 8π h ν³ n³/c³; R_sp/R_st = e^(hν/kT) − 1.

**Q.** Why can a two-level system not be inverted by resonant radiation?

**A.** N2/N1 = (g2/g1) s/(1 + s) < g2/g1 for all ρ; absorption and stimulated emission are equally probable per state, spontaneous emission only depletes the upper level.

**Q.** Write the threshold gain of a two-mirror resonator and the threshold inversion.

**A.** γ_th = α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/r1 r2); ΔN_th = γ_th/σ, σ = A21 λ0² S/(8π n²).

**Q.** At what temperature are spontaneous and stimulated rates equal for 5000 Å?

**A.** T = hν/(k ln 2) = 4.15 × 10⁴ K.

### Memory skeleton

- Three processes for a two-level atom (hν = E2 − E1): absorption B12ρN1, spontaneous A21N2, stimulated B21ρN2 (a clone photon)
- Units: A21 in s⁻¹ (1/A21 = τ); B in m³ J⁻¹ s⁻²; ρ(ν) an energy density per unit frequency, J s m⁻³
- Equilibrium balance plus Boltzmann; ρ → ∞ as T → ∞ forces g1 B12 = g2 B21
- Mode count 8πn³ν²/c³ gives Rayleigh-Jeans; matching at small x fixes A21/B21 = 8πhν³n³/c³; substitute for Planck's law
- R_sp/R_st = e^(hν/kT) − 1: huge for visible, small for microwaves; equal rates at T = hν/(k ln 2)
- Two levels cannot invert: N2/N1 = (g2/g1) s/(1 + s); lasers need a third level, a metastable level and a resonator
- Threshold: round-trip gain r1 r2 e^(2(γ − α_s)L) ≥ 1 gives γ_th = α_s + (1/2L) ln(1/(r1 r2))
