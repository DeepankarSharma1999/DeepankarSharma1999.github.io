### Bridge: Bridge: orders, small angles and what 'resolving' means

A bright ring or fringe is described by its **order**, the whole number m for which the path difference equals m wavelengths. Order 0 is the central bright spot only if there is no extra path; in an air plate of thickness d the path difference at the centre is 2d, so the central order is m0 = 2d/lambda, a huge number (thousands) for a millimetre-thick gap. It need not be a whole number: if 2d/lambda is 3200.4, the centre is not exactly at a maximum.

For small angles theta (radians), cos theta is 1 minus theta squared over 2. Check: cos 0.05 = 0.9987503 and the formula gives 1 − 0.00125 = 0.99875, an error of 2.6 × 10⁻⁷ (theta⁴/24). Every ring-radius result below uses that replacement.

**Resolving** two close wavelengths means seeing them as two separate lines rather than one blurred line. A practical criterion used in this lesson: two equal peaks are resolved when their centres are separated by at least the full width of one peak at half its height. This is a convention (the Taylor-type criterion; the book does not state it in the extracts, so verify), not a law of nature.

### 1. Orientation: the instrument, the three numbers and the plan

*Objective:* State what the Fabry-Perot interferometer is, the three quantities that characterise it and the route of the lesson.

## What it is

The Fabry-Perot interferometer is two flat, parallel, highly reflecting surfaces facing each other across an air gap of thickness d. Light entering the gap bounces back and forth many times; a little escapes at each bounce on the far side, and all the escaping beams are brought together by a lens and made to interfere. Day 19 lesson PH-052 (Multiple-beam interference) derived the Airy intensity pattern and its sharpness for exactly this geometry; this lesson turns that theory into an **instrument** with a ring pattern, working formulae and applications. Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam (Chapter 15, section 15.12, PDF pp. 389 to 391) describe it as a high resolving power instrument that uses fringes of equal inclination from the transmitted light.

## The three numbers

Every use of the instrument is governed by three quantities, all derived in later modules:

1. **Free spectral range (FSR)**: the largest wavelength interval that can be examined without the orders of neighbouring wavelengths overlapping. At normal incidence FSR is lambda squared over 2 d.
2. **Finesse** (script F): the ratio of the spacing of the rings to their width; from PH-052, approximately pi times square-root R over (1 − R).
3. **Resolving power**: lambda over the smallest resolvable wavelength difference. It equals m times finesse, with m = 2d/lambda the order.

## A first example

For d = 0.8 mm and lambda = 500 nm (the 2026 question): m = 3200, FSR = 0.156 nm, and with R = 0.9 the finesse is 29.8, so the smallest resolvable difference is 0.156 nm / 29.8 = 5.2 picometres (5.2 × 10⁻¹² m). A single lens-and-screen set-up resolves lines 30 times closer than the FSR (the wavelength spacing of successive orders).

### 2. Foundation: construction, the etalon and why transmission is used

*Objective:* Describe the parts of the Fabry-Perot interferometer and the etalon and explain why the transmitted pattern is observed.

## The parts

Brij Lal (section 15.12, PDF pp. 389 to 390, Fig. 15.35) describes the interferometer as two optically plane glass plates A and B with their **inner surfaces silvered**, set accurately parallel to each other, with screws to restore the parallelism if it is disturbed. In use:

- S is a broad (extended) source of monochromatic light.
- A convex lens L1 makes the light parallel (collimating lens). The book says it is not drawn in the figure.
- The parallel light enters the gap between the plates and undergoes a large number of internal reflections between the silvered surfaces.
- Each time a small fraction passes through the far plate. Each incident ray therefore produces a group of coherent, parallel transmitted rays with a **constant path difference** between successive rays.
- A second convex lens L brings these rays together in its focal plane, where a screen (or an eyepiece) shows the interference pattern.

## The etalon

The book notes that the two-plate system is difficult to make and is no longer used; an **etalon** is used instead. An etalon is two semi-silvered plates rigidly held parallel at a fixed distance apart (the book, PDF p. 389). The reflectance of the surfaces can be as high as 90 to 99.9 percent. The word **interferometer** therefore often means the variable-gap version (one plate movable, d adjustable) and **etalon** the fixed-gap version. In practice the spacing d is the quantity that controls everything in this lesson, so both are treated together.

## Why use the transmitted light

Both reflected and transmitted beams interfere, but the instrument is usually used in the transmissive mode (PDF p. 390). PH-052 explains why: the transmitted pattern consists of sharp bright rings on a nearly dark background (for R = 0.9 the minimum is 0.28 percent of the peak), whereas the reflected pattern is the complement, a nearly bright field with narrow dark rings, which is much harder to see and measure.

## Words defined

- **Fringes of equal inclination**: bright rings, each belonging to one value of the angle theta the rays make with the axis. The book calls them such (PDF p. 389).
- **Order** m: the number of wavelengths in the path difference for a given ring. The central ring has the highest order.
- **Reflectance R**: fraction of intensity reflected at each surface (PH-052 defines R = r² and T = 1 − R for a lossless surface).

## Diagram-worthy ideas

A later hand-drawn figure should show: the source, lens L1, the two plates with the gap d, the lens L, the screen in its focal plane, a ray at angle theta entering and the first four transmitted rays emerging parallel to each other, and the circular ring pattern.

## Not to confuse

The etalon of Brij Lal section 15.8.5 (PDF pp. 381 to 383) is a different object: a length **substandard** made of two plane parallel mirrors, used with the Michelson interferometer to compare lengths. The Fabry-Perot etalon here is a fixed-spacing plate pair used for spectroscopy. Same word, different use.

### 3. Core: the ring condition, the central order and the ring radii

*Objective:* Derive the bright-ring condition and compute the angles and radii of the rings for given d and lambda.

## The ring condition

Brij Lal (section 15.12.1, PDF p. 390) takes d as the separation of the silvered surfaces and theta as the inclination of a ray to the normal. The path difference between two successive transmitted rays is 2 d cos theta (air gap, index 1), and a maximum needs

$$ 2 d cos theta = m lambda ,   m = integer $$

All rays leaving the extended source at the same theta lie on a cone and, brought to the focal plane of L, form a circle. The pattern is therefore a set of bright concentric rings on a dark background (the book's description).

## The central order

At the centre theta = 0, so m0 = 2d/lambda. For d = 0.8 mm and lambda = 500 nm: m0 = 1.6 × 10⁻³/5 × 10⁻⁷ = 3200. In general m0 is not an integer: write m0 = m1 + epsilon with m1 the whole number just below m0 and epsilon in [0, 1). If epsilon is not zero the centre of the pattern is not at the middle of a bright ring.

## Ring angles

The ring of order m1 − (p − 1) (counting p = 1 for the first bright ring from the centre) satisfies 2 d cos theta_p = (m1 − p + 1) lambda. Subtract this from 2d = (m1 + epsilon) lambda:

$$ 2 d (1 − cos theta_p) = (p − 1 + epsilon) lambda $$

Use 1 − cos theta = theta²/2 for small theta:

$$ theta_p = sqrt( (p − 1 + epsilon) lambda / d ) $$

If the central order is exactly an integer (epsilon = 0 and a bright central spot), count the central spot as p = 0 and the p-th ring has theta_p = sqrt(p lambda / d). For d = 0.8 mm and lambda = 500 nm, sqrt(lambda/d) = sqrt(6.25 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.025 rad:

| ring p | theta_p (rad) | theta_p (degrees) | radius on screen if f_L = 50 cm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.02500 | 1.432 | 12.50 mm |
| 2 | 0.03536 | 2.026 | 17.68 mm |
| 3 | 0.04330 | 2.481 | 21.65 mm |
| 4 | 0.05000 | 2.865 | 25.00 mm |
| 5 | 0.05590 | 3.203 | 27.95 mm |

The exact value of theta_1 from cos theta_1 = 1 − 1/3200 is 0.0250007 rad, and for p = 5 it is 0.055909 rad; the approximation is accurate to about 10⁻⁴ relative or better for the first five rings.

The radius on the screen is r_p = f_L theta_p (f_L is the focal length of the focusing lens L).

## Properties of the pattern

- Radii grow as the square root of p: **rings crowd together** towards the edge.
- The angular spacing between neighbouring rings is lambda/(2 d theta) (from 2 d sin theta Δtheta = lambda); it falls as 1/theta.
- Larger d: smaller theta_p (rings crowd towards the centre) and a larger central order.
- The width of each ring is not the spacing but the spacing divided by the finesse. For d = 0.8 mm, R = 0.9 (finesse 29.8) the local spacing formula gives 0.0125 rad at the first ring (the actual gap to ring 2 is 0.0104 rad, because the spacing shrinks outward) and a full width at half maximum of about 4.2 × 10⁻⁴ rad (0.21 mm on the screen at f_L = 50 cm). These sharp rings are what the instrument measures.

## Compared with Michelson

The Michelson circular fringes of PH-051 follow the same condition, 2 d cos theta = m lambda, but have a cos² profile with a width equal to about half the spacing (finesse 2 from PH-052). The Fabry-Perot rings use the same geometry but concentrate the light into narrow lines.

### 4. Core: sharpness and the finesse, recapped with what they buy

*Objective:* Recall the Airy half-width and finesse results from PH-052 and compute them for several reflectances.

## What is reused

Day 19 lesson PH-052 derived, from the geometric sum of the multiply reflected beams, the transmitted intensity

$$ I_T / I_0 = 1 / [ 1 + F sin²(delta/2) ] ,   F = 4R/(1 − R)² $$

(F here is the **coefficient of finesse**, Brij Lal Eq. 15.72, and delta = 4 pi d cos theta/lambda is the phase lag between successive beams). The peaks are at delta = 2 pi m. Brij Lal (PDF p. 388 to 389) measures sharpness by the half-width of the peak, and PH-052 turned it into the **finesse**, the ratio of the spacing of the peaks to their full width at half maximum:

$$ finesse = 2 pi / FWHM_delta ,   FWHM_delta = 4 arcsin(1/sqrt(F)) ≈ 2(1 − R)/sqrt(R) ,   finesse ≈ pi sqrt(R)/(1 − R) $$

Do not confuse F (coefficient) with the finesse; the former is 4R/(1 − R)², the latter pi sqrt(R)/(1 − R).

## Numbers (recomputed)

| R | F | finesse (exact) | finesse pi sqrt(R)/(1 − R) | I_min/I_max | visibility V = F/(F + 2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 8 | 4.35 | 4.44 | 0.111 | 0.800 |
| 0.8 | 80 | 14.02 | 14.05 | 0.0123 | 0.976 |
| 0.9 | 360 | 29.79 | 29.80 | 0.00277 | 0.994 |
| 0.95 | 1520 | 61.23 | 61.24 | 0.00066 | 0.9987 |
| 0.99 | 39600 | 312.6 | 312.6 | 0.000025 | 0.99995 |

Brij Lal (PDF p. 388) states that V reaches 0.8 when R = 0.5 and tends to 1 as R tends to 1; the table agrees (V = 0.800 at R = 0.5).

## What the finesse buys

1. **Narrow rings**: each ring is 1/finesse of the spacing wide. With R = 0.9 about 30 distinct ring positions fit within one order.
2. **High contrast**: the dark gaps are almost black, so a faint line next to a strong one can be seen.
3. **Resolving power** (module 7): the minimum resolvable wavelength difference is the free spectral range divided by the finesse.

The cost: a lossless pair still transmits fully at every peak, whatever R (PH-052), but real mirrors absorb, and absorption lowers the peak (PH-052 module 8).

## Why the high reflectance helps (the physical picture)

A beam reflected k times is out of step with the first transmitted beam by k times delta. With R = 0.9 about 1/(1 − R) = 10 round trips carry the sum, so even a small departure of delta from a multiple of 2 pi throws the later beams out of step with the first ones and the total collapses. The more bounces that matter, the narrower the peak: this is the same reason a diffraction grating with more slits has sharper lines (pointer to Day 23 lesson PH-056, stated as an analogy).

## Limit check

R → 0: F → 0, the finesse formula no longer applies (below R = 0.172 there is no half-maximum point) and the pattern fades to the weak cosine fringes of two beams (finesse 2 in the sense of PH-052). R → 1: width → 0 and finesse → infinity, an ideal limit; real mirrors have absorption and imperfect flatness (module 8).

### 5. Core: measuring a wavelength and a close wavelength difference

*Objective:* Derive the wavelength formula from fringe counting and the coincidence-method formula for a wavelength difference, and compute the book's numbers.

## Wavelength from fringe counting (Brij Lal 15.12.2, PDF p. 390)

Adjust the plates exactly parallel; circular rings appear. At the centre theta = 0, so a bright centre of order m has 2t = m lambda (t the plate separation; the book calls it t, we use d in the rest of the lesson). If the movable plate moves by lambda/2, the quantity 2t changes by lambda and a bright fringe of the next order appears at the centre. Moving the plate from x1 to x2 while N fringes appear at the centre:

$$ 2 (x2 − x1) = N lambda  ⟹  lambda = 2 (x2 − x1)/N $$

This is Eq. 15.77. It is the same fringe-counting rule as the Michelson interferometer (PH-051); the Fabry-Perot version has the advantage of sharper rings, so the fractional fringe can be read more precisely (stated as a general consequence of the finesse; verify).

**Book problem 9 (PDF p. 401)**: 'a shift of 200 fringes ... 0.0295 mm'. Recomputed: lambda = 2 × 0.0295 × 10⁻³/200 = 2.95 × 10⁻⁷ m = 295 nm, which is ultraviolet. The same displacement with 100 fringes (the book's Michelson Problem 7) gives 590 nm. As printed, the answer is not a visible wavelength; treat the data as probably misprinted and say so, not as a trick.

## Wavelength difference: the coincidence method (15.12.3, PDF pp. 390 to 391)

The book's argument: with two close wavelengths lambda1 > lambda2, each produces its own ring system, separated from the other, unlike the Michelson case (where the visibility cycles but no separate patterns appear). Start with the plates in contact; the two ring systems partly coincide. Move the plate slowly apart; the rings separate until, at a separation t1, the rings of lambda2 lie half-way between those of lambda1 (**maximum discordance**). At the centre:

$$ 2 t1 = m1 lambda1 = (m1 + 1/2) lambda2 $$

(lambda2 is smaller, so the same 2 t1 holds one-half more of its wavelengths.) Subtract: m1 (lambda1 − lambda2) = lambda2/2, so

$$ m1 = lambda2 / [2 (lambda1 − lambda2)] ,   2 t1 = m1 lambda1 ,   lambda1 − lambda2 ≈ lambda_mean² / (4 t1) $$

The book's Eq. 15.80 has this form (the extracted line is partly garbled). Moving further, the systems coincide again and then reach discordance again at t2, one more cycle:

$$ lambda1 − lambda2 = lambda_mean² / [ 2 (t2 − t1) ] $$

(Eq. 15.83.) Derivation: between two successive maximum discordances the extra path 2 (t2 − t1) must contain exactly one more wavelength of lambda2 than of lambda1: 2 (t2 − t1) = n lambda1 = (n + 1) lambda2. Then n (lambda1 − lambda2) = lambda2, so 2 (t2 − t1) = lambda1 lambda2/(lambda1 − lambda2) ≈ lambda_mean²/(lambda1 − lambda2).

## Sodium D lines, recomputed

Take 5890 and 5896 Angstrom, mean 5893 Angstrom: t2 − t1 = (5893 × 10⁻¹⁰)²/(2 × 6 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 2.894 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.289 mm, and t1 = lambda²/(4 Delta lambda) = 0.145 mm. These are the plate separations at which the sodium doublet is most discordant.

**Book problem 11 (PDF p. 401)**: plate separation 4 × 10⁻⁴ cm = 4 micrometres, lambda = 5000 Angstrom; central order = 2d/lambda = 8 × 10⁻⁶/5 × 10⁻⁷ = 16. The phrase 'find the order of the plates' is ambiguous; the order of the maximum at the centre is 16.

**Caution**: the method needs ring systems separate enough to see: with the instrument's finesse the criterion of module 7 sets the smallest Delta lambda for which this works at a given d.

### 6. Core: the free spectral range and overlapping orders

*Objective:* Derive the free spectral range exactly and approximately and compute it in wavelength, frequency and wavenumber.

## The idea

Every wavelength has its own set of bright rings. If the light holds two wavelengths that differ by just the right amount, the order m of one falls exactly on the order m − 1 of the other, and their rings land on top of each other: the ordering is ambiguous. The wavelength interval at which this first happens is the **free spectral range** (FSR). Within it the pattern can be read without confusion. (The extracts give the ring condition and the two-wavelength coincidence method; the FSR itself is standard result derived below from the ring condition. Verify the name in your class text.)

## Derivation (normal incidence, air gap)

Let wavelength lambda have a maximum of order m on the axis, 2 d = m lambda. A longer wavelength lambda' = lambda + Delta lambda has order m − 1 at the same place:

$$ m lambda = (m − 1)(lambda + Delta lambda)  ⟹  Delta lambda = lambda/(m − 1) $$

Substituting m = 2d/lambda,

$$ Delta lambda_FSR = lambda / (2d/lambda − 1) = lambda² / (2 d − lambda) ≈ lambda² / (2 d) $$

The approximation drops lambda against 2d, a relative error of lambda/(2d) = 1/m, about 0.03 percent for m = 3200.

Same in frequency: nu = c/lambda; the orders at fixed d are spaced by c/(2d) in frequency: Delta nu_FSR = c/(2d). In wavenumber (1/lambda): Delta sigma_FSR = 1/(2d). These are exact for fixed d at normal incidence, with no approximation, which is why spectroscopists quote FSR in GHz or cm⁻¹.

## Numbers (recomputed)

| d | lambda | FSR (wavelength) | FSR (frequency) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.8 mm | 500 nm | 0.15625 nm (approximate); 0.15630 nm (exact) | 187.5 GHz (6.25 cm⁻¹) |
| 4 micrometres | 500 nm | 31.25 nm approx.; 33.3 nm exact (m = 16) | 37.5 THz |
| 5 mm | 589.3 nm | 34.7 pm | 30 GHz |
| 10 mm | 546.1 nm | 14.9 pm | 15 GHz |

Check: 0.8 mm gives c/(2d) = 3 × 10⁸/1.6 × 10⁻³ = 1.875 × 10¹¹ Hz = 187.5 GHz. And 187.5 GHz at 500 nm (600 THz) corresponds to Delta lambda = lambda × Delta nu/nu = 500 nm × 187.5 × 10⁹/(6.0 × 10¹⁴) = 0.156 nm ✓.

## Reading

- FSR is **inversely proportional** to the plate separation: a large d (needed for resolution, module 7) gives a small FSR, so only a narrow band of wavelengths can be studied without overlap.
- For a wide spectrum use a small d (large FSR, lower resolving power) or put a filter or prism before the etalon to isolate the band (a standard practice; verify).
- A line spacing larger than FSR gives rings that look like a smaller spacing (aliasing). The fix is a shorter d or a pre-filter.

## Another angle

In ring terms: near the centre, the ring of order m for lambda + FSR sits where lambda has order m − 1. The 'maximum discordance' of module 5 is the half-way point of that cycle.

## Checks

- Units: lambda²/d has dimension length; c/d has dimension 1/time.
- d → 0: FSR → infinity.
- Larger lambda (red): FSR grows as lambda².

## The 2026 stem

'Two successive transmitted maxima correspond to lambda and lambda + Delta lambda, both satisfying the normal-incidence condition': this is exactly the FSR definition. For d = 0.8 mm and lambda = 500 nm the answer is Delta lambda = 0.1563 nm (exact 0.1563, approximate 0.15625). The separate question about the smallest **resolvable** difference needs the finesse, which the stem does not give; module 10 shows how to answer both readings.

### 7. Core: resolving power, the minimum resolvable difference and why it grows with d

*Objective:* Derive the minimum resolvable wavelength difference and the resolving power in terms of finesse and order, and compute them.

## The criterion

Two equal ring peaks are taken as just resolved when the distance between their centres equals the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of one peak. This is a convention (Taylor-type criterion; the book calls the instrument 'a high resolving power instrument' on PDF p. 389 but the extracts do not set the criterion, so verify). In phase terms, from PH-052, FWHM_delta = 2 pi/finesse.

## Derivation

At normal incidence the phase lag of the transmitted beams is delta = 4 pi d/lambda. Two wavelengths lambda and lambda + Delta lambda give peaks at phases that differ by

$$ |Delta delta| = 4 pi d Delta lambda / lambda² $$

Resolved when this equals the FWHM, 2 pi/finesse:

$$ 4 pi d Delta lambda/lambda² = 2 pi / finesse  ⟹  Delta lambda_min = lambda² / (2 d · finesse) $$

The right side is FSR divided by finesse. The **chromatic resolving power** is

$$ RP = lambda/Delta lambda_min = (2 d/lambda) · finesse = m · finesse $$

with m = 2d/lambda the central order. With finesse ≈ pi sqrt(R)/(1 − R) this is RP ≈ (2 pi d/lambda) sqrt(R)/(1 − R).

## Numbers for d = 0.8 mm, lambda = 500 nm (recomputed; m = 3200)

| R | finesse | RP = m × finesse | Delta lambda_min = lambda/RP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 4.44 | 1.42 × 10⁴ | 35.2 pm |
| 0.8 | 14.05 | 4.50 × 10⁴ | 11.1 pm |
| 0.9 | 29.80 | 9.54 × 10⁴ | 5.24 pm |
| 0.95 | 61.24 | 1.96 × 10⁵ | 2.55 pm |
| 0.99 | 312.6 | 1.00 × 10⁶ | 0.50 pm |

(The R = 0.9 row: RP = 3200 × 29.80 = 95,360 ≈ 9.54 × 10⁴.) Doubling d doubles RP and halves the minimum Delta lambda; going from R = 0.9 to 0.95 multiplies RP by 2.05.

**Why it depends on d (the 'physical explanation'):** a larger gap means a larger path difference between neighbouring beams, 2d, so a given change of wavelength produces a larger change of phase, 4 pi d Delta lambda/lambda². The peaks of the two wavelengths therefore separate faster in phase while their widths (set by R only) stay the same; they are resolved at a smaller Delta lambda. Equivalently, RP counts the number of waves, m, in the path difference, times the number of effective beams (finesse).

## The trade-off

FSR falls as 1/d while RP grows as d. Their ratio FSR/Delta lambda_min equals the finesse for **every** d: the instrument always distinguishes about 'finesse' separate wavelength elements across one FSR. A larger d moves those elements to a finer wavelength scale. This is why the finesse, not d, is the figure of merit of the plate pair.

## Examples

- d = 5 mm, lambda = 589.3 nm, R = 0.9: m = 16,969, RP = 5.06 × 10⁵, Delta lambda_min = 1.17 pm, FSR = 34.7 pm.
- d = 10 mm, lambda = 546.1 nm, R = 0.95: m = 36,623, RP = 2.24 × 10⁶, Delta lambda_min = 0.24 pm, FSR = 14.9 pm.

## Limit checks

- R → 0: the finesse of the Airy formula is not defined below R = 0.172; the 'two-beam' value 2 gives RP ≈ 2m, a small number (6400 for m = 3200).
- A real instrument has a lower finesse than the reflectance alone gives (module 8), so these RP values are upper bounds.
- Units: Delta lambda_min has dimension length; RP is dimensionless.

## Comparison in one line

A good grating has RP = N times the order (pointer to Day 23 lesson PH-056); a Fabry-Perot reaches 10⁵ to 10⁶ without a long ruled surface, but over a narrow FSR.

### 8. Master: real-instrument limits - coherence, flatness and mirror phase

*Objective:* State the practical limits that reduce the ideal finesse and resolving power and relate them to the book's coherence results.

## Coherence length and the number of useful beams

Brij Lal Chapter 16 (PDF pp. 402 to 410) defines the coherence length l_coh = c t_coh = lambda²/Delta lambda for a source of bandwidth Delta lambda, and lists typical values: sodium lamp about 1 mm, green mercury line about 1 cm, neon red line 3 cm, red cadmium line 30 cm, a commercial He-Ne laser about 15 m (PDF p. 408). Beams that differ in path by more than l_coh do not interfere.

In the Fabry-Perot, the k-th transmitted beam has made k extra round trips: its path exceeds the first by 2 k d, and about 1/(1 − R) beams matter (PH-052, module 5). The plates alone then set the ring width if

$$ l_coh ≳ 2 d / (1 − R) $$

(own reasoning from the book's results; verify). This is the same as saying that the source linewidth lambda²/l_coh is below about (1 − R) × FSR, roughly pi × Delta lambda_min. For d = 0.8 mm: R = 0.9 needs 16 mm; R = 0.99 needs 160 mm. When the condition holds the rings are instrument-limited. When it fails the instrument does not fail: the rings show the line's own width, which is what a spectroscopic Fabry-Perot measures. The book's 'about 1 mm' for a sodium lamp (Delta lambda about 0.35 nm) cannot be the width of each D line, since the same book says the Fabry-Perot separates D1 and D2, 0.6 nm apart; treat it as rough (verify).

## Flatness, parallelism and the finesse budget

The ideal finesse is set by the reflectance alone. Surface roughness, departures from flatness (a plate flat to lambda/N), non-parallelism and the finite angular aperture each spread the peak. The usual way to combine them is by adding the **inverse squares**:

$$ 1/finesse_total² = 1/finesse_R² + 1/finesse_flatness² + ... $$

with finesse_flatness about N/2 for lambda/N flatness (standard treatment; not in the extracts; verify). Example: R = 0.9 (29.8) with lambda/100 plates (50) gives 25.6; R = 0.95 (61.2) with the same plates gives 38.7. Beyond a point, raising R buys little unless the plates are flatter. The extract says 'screws are provided to secure parallelism'; the same concern is why the etalon (rigid spacer) replaced the adjustable pair.

## Phase change on reflection

Brij Lal's review question 20 (PDF p. 400) asks about the effect of the phase change at each reflection. If each reflection adds a phase phi, the round-trip phase becomes delta = 4 pi d cos theta/lambda − 2 phi (PH-052 module 8). Consequences: the central order is shifted from 2d/lambda by phi/pi (the centre can be a non-maximum even when 2d/lambda is a whole number), the ring radii shift slightly, but the **spacing** between rings, the FSR and the finesse are unchanged if phi does not depend on wavelength. Measurements that use **differences** of d or of fringe counts (module 5) are therefore unaffected; absolute measurements of d need the phase corrected.

## Absorption

Real mirrors absorb a fraction A: R + T + A = 1. The peak transmission falls to [T/(1 − R)]² (PH-052 module 8); the peaks stay narrow but less light passes, the practical cost of pushing R towards 1.

## Angular aperture

Keep theta well below sqrt(lambda/(d × finesse)): about 0.0046 rad for d = 0.8 mm, 500 nm, finesse 30 (rough; verify; derivation in the master notes). A small aperture on lens L keeps the rings sharp.

## Summary of what limits what

| Quantity | Ideal | Limiting factors |
|---|---|---|
| Finesse | pi sqrt(R)/(1 − R) | flatness, parallelism, aperture (a broad source line widens the observed rings; absorption lowers the peak, not the width) |
| FSR | lambda²/(2d) | not limited; set by d |
| Resolving power | m × finesse | the finesse budget above |

### 9. Master: comparison with Michelson, Lummer-Gehrcke and the grating, and the advantages of multiple beams

*Objective:* Contrast the Fabry-Perot interferometer with the Michelson interferometer, the Lummer-Gehrcke plate and the grating and list the advantages of multiple-beam interferometry.

## Fabry-Perot against Michelson (Brij Lal 15.12.3, PDF p. 390)

The book states the key difference: in the Michelson interferometer, two close wavelengths do not produce separate fringe patterns, so it cannot study fine structure; in the Fabry-Perot each wavelength produces its own ring pattern, and the patterns are separated, so it is suitable for the fine structure of spectral lines (the question on PDF p. 401, Q25, asks how it is superior). Reason in terms of PH-052: two-beam cos² fringes have finesse 2, so two nearby wavelengths overlap into one broad pattern whose visibility only wiggles; multi-beam fringes have finesse of tens, so two wavelengths produce separate narrow rings.

| Property | Michelson | Fabry-Perot |
|---|---|---|
| Number of interfering beams | 2 | many (about finesse/pi effective) |
| Fringe profile | cos² (broad) | Airy (narrow) |
| Finesse (PH-052) | 2 | ten to several hundred |
| Close wavelengths | not separated; visibility cycle | separate ring systems |
| Typical use | lengths, wavelength, coherence | fine structure, high resolution |
| Source requirement | moderate | long coherence length (module 8) |

## The Lummer-Gehrcke plate (15.13, PDF p. 391)

A plane parallel glass plate about 10 cm long and a few mm thick, with a prism cemented at one end. Light enters, is reflected up and down between the faces at an angle slightly below the critical angle, and at each reflection a beam leaves; the beams have equal path differences and are collected by a telescope, giving fringes. The book says it is used for fine structure and the Zeeman effect. It is a different instrument with the same multiple-beam idea.

## Against a diffraction grating

Both give high resolving power. A grating relies on N slits and the order of diffraction (RP = order × N, a result covered by Day 23 lesson PH-056); the Fabry-Perot relies on the order m and the finesse. The grating's FSR is large (many orders), the Fabry-Perot's is small, so the Fabry-Perot is used for narrow bands where high resolution is needed, usually with a prism, grating or filter selecting the band first (typical practice; verify).

## Advantages of multiple-beam over two-beam interferometry (the 2017 question)

1. Sharper fringes (narrow half-width, finesse of tens or more).
2. Much higher visibility: V = 2R/(1 + R²) (PH-052) approaches 1 as R approaches 1; two-beam fringes have V = 1 only for equal intensities.
3. Greater resolving power for close wavelengths (module 7).
4. Dark background of the transmitted pattern allows weak lines to be detected.
5. Precise measurement of small changes in the plate gap (a shift of the peak by a small fraction of its spacing is measurable).

Costs: need high reflectance and very flat parallel plates; small free spectral range; coherence length requirement; lower total transmitted energy for absorbing mirrors.

## Applications (as the extract and general physics give them)

- Fine structure of spectral lines (book, PDF p. 390); the book names the Zeeman effect for the Lummer-Gehrcke plate (PDF p. 391), and it is also a standard Fabry-Perot application (verify).
- Wavelength determination (Eq. 15.77) and wavelength difference (Eq. 15.83).
- A fixed etalon as a narrow-band filter or a laser-cavity element (standard, not in the extract; verify). The laser link will be seen in Day 20 lesson PH-067 (ruby and He-Ne lasers): an optical cavity formed by two mirrors is the same plane-parallel resonator geometry.

### Notes: Definitions

- Fabry-Perot interferometer: two optically plane, parallel, highly reflecting surfaces a distance d apart; the transmitted multiple beams are focused to give concentric rings (fringes of equal inclination).
- Etalon: the same plate pair rigidly spaced at a fixed d (do not confuse with the length-substandard etalon of Brij Lal section 15.8.5, used with the Michelson interferometer).
- Order m = 2 d cos theta / lambda of a bright ring; central order m0 = 2d/lambda (not necessarily an integer).
- Coefficient of finesse F = 4R/(1 − R)²; finesse = FSR over FWHM of a peak, approximately pi sqrt(R)/(1 − R) (PH-052).
- Free spectral range (FSR): wavelength (or frequency) interval at which the order m of one wavelength coincides with the order m − 1 of the next.
- Chromatic resolving power RP = lambda/Delta lambda_min = m × finesse (Taylor-type criterion: peak separation equals FWHM).
- Coherence length l_coh = c t_coh = lambda²/Delta lambda (Brij Lal Chapter 16).

### Notes: Equations to know (air gap, normal use)

- 2 d cos theta = m lambda (bright rings); m0 = 2d/lambda.
- theta_p = sqrt((p − 1 + epsilon) lambda/d), or sqrt(p lambda/d) for an integer central order; r_p = f_L theta_p.
- I_T/I_0 = 1/[1 + F sin²(delta/2)], delta = 4 pi d cos theta/lambda (PH-052).
- lambda = 2 (x2 − x1)/N (Eq. 15.77); lambda1 − lambda2 = lambda_mean²/(2 (t2 − t1)) (Eq. 15.83); at first discordance lambda1 − lambda2 ≈ lambda_mean²/(4 t1).
- FSR: Delta lambda = lambda²/(2d − lambda) ≈ lambda²/(2d); Delta nu = c/(2d); Delta sigma = 1/(2d).
- Delta lambda_min = lambda²/(2 d · finesse) = FSR/finesse; RP = m × finesse ≈ (2 pi d/lambda) sqrt(R)/(1 − R).
- Elements resolved per FSR = FSR/Delta lambda_min = finesse.
- Coherence condition for instrument-limited rings (own estimate): l_coh ≳ 2d/(1 − R), i.e. linewidth below about (1 − R) × FSR. Finesse budget: 1/finesse_total² = Σ 1/finesse_i² (standard; verify).
- Phase change phi on reflection: delta = 4 pi d cos theta/lambda − 2 phi (PH-052).

### Notes: Derivation skeletons (for revision)

- Ring condition: successive transmitted rays differ in path by 2 d cos theta; bright when this is m lambda.
- Ring radii: 2d(1 − cos theta_p) = (p − 1 + epsilon) lambda and 1 − cos x ≈ x²/2 give theta_p = sqrt((p − 1 + epsilon) lambda/d).

- Resolution: |Delta delta| = 4 pi d Delta lambda/lambda² set equal to FWHM_delta = 2 pi/finesse ⟹ Delta lambda_min = lambda²/(2 d finesse).
- Coincidence method: 2 (t2 − t1) = n lambda1 = (n + 1) lambda2 ⟹ n = lambda2/(lambda1 − lambda2) ⟹ Delta lambda = lambda²/(2 (t2 − t1)).

Moving mirror M1 a distance of half a wavelength changes the path difference by one wavelength, so one fringe passes; A mirror displacement that moves m fringes past a point is delta-d = m*lambda0/2 (Eq. 3.7).

- **F-HYP-FP-GEOM (verified):** The Fabry-Perot interferometer uses multiple reflections between two closely spaced partially silvered surfaces. Part of the light is transmitted each time it reaches the second surface, giving multiple offset beams that interfere; the large number of interfering rays gives extremely high resolution, somewhat like the multiple slits of a diffraction grating.
  Source: [HyperPhysics (R. Nave), 'Fabry-Perot Interferometer'](https://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/phyopt/fabry.html)

  Source: [HyperPhysics (R. Nave), 'Fabry-Perot Interferometer'](https://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/phyopt/fabry3.html)

- **F-HYP-FP-DOUBLET (verified):** In the HyperPhysics sodium-doublet demonstration the Fabry-Perot mirror spacing is increased with a micrometer and the two fringe systems merge and separate; when the pattern looks the same again, one fringe set has advanced a full order with respect to the other. The wavelength difference then follows from the mirror displacement, and the average wavelength of the doublet must be known from other measurements.

### Memory skeleton

- Construction: two optically plane plates, inner faces silvered, held parallel; collimator, focusing lens, screen
- Used in transmission: sharp bright rings on a dark ground (Airy law of PH-052)

### Last-week summary

- Two optically plane plates, inner faces silvered, parallel (screws); extended monochromatic source, collimating lens, focusing lens, screen in its focal plane; reflectance 90-99.9%.
- Transmitted pattern: bright concentric rings on a dark ground (reflected is the complement). Bright: 2d cos θ = mλ; m₀ = 2d/λ = 3200 for 0.8 mm, 500 nm.

- λ = 2(x₂ − x₁)/N (Eq. 15.77). Discordance cycle: 2(t₂ − t₁) = nλ₁ = (n + 1)λ₂, so Δλ = λ²/2(t₂ − t₁) (Eq. 15.83).
- Book: Michelson gives no separate patterns for close wavelengths; Fabry-Perot gives each its own ring system, hence fine structure.
