## GS-077: Buddhist stupas and cave architecture

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Buddhist stupas and cave architecture and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why and how stupas were built

Places linked with the Buddha's life — Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kusinagara — became sacred, and so did mounds holding his relics. The custom of raising such mounds may predate Buddhism, but it became closely tied to it. NCERT stresses that patronage was collective: inscriptions on railings and pillars name kings such as the Satavahanas, guilds such as the ivory workers who paid for part of a Sanchi gateway, monks, nuns and hundreds of lay donors with their home towns and occupations. Very few inscriptions name artisans, though Kanha at Pitalkhora and his disciple Balaka at Kondane are recorded. The Lomus Rishi cave in the Barabar hills, given by Asoka to the Ajivikas, shows the chaitya-arch entrance already in use in the third century BCE.

### Anatomy and sculpture of the stupa

The stupa began as a simple hemispherical earth mound, the anda, and grew into a structure balancing round and square forms: harmika above the anda, a yashti mast with a chhatri, and a railing (vedika) separating sacred from worldly space. Early Sanchi and Bharhut railings were plain and fence-like while the four gateways were richly carved. The Buddha was first shown through symbols such as footprints, the wheel, the lotus throne and the stupa itself; Jataka tales and life events were carved on railings and toranas. Amaravati, which also had a pradakshinapatha within a vedika, covered its dome with relief slabs and later carved Buddha images on drum slabs in the second and third centuries CE.

### Chaitya halls and viharas in the caves

Western Indian Buddhist caves from the second century BCE follow three plans: apsidal vault-roofed chaitya halls (Ajanta, Pitalkhora, Bhaja), apsidal pillarless halls (Thana-Nadsur) and flat-roofed square halls with a rear circular chamber (Kondivite). Every chaitya cave keeps a stupa at the back. Karla's chaitya is the largest, with courtyard, rain screen, veranda, pillared vaulted hall and stupa. Viharas have a veranda, hall and surrounding cells, as at Ajanta Cave 12 and Nashik Caves 3, 10 and 17. Ajanta's patrons included Varahadeva, minister of the Vakataka king Harishena (Cave 16). The fate of Amaravati, whose sculptures were dispersed, contrasts with Sanchi, preserved in situ after H.H. Cole's plea.

### Worked example

Q: 'Stupas were as much social monuments as religious ones.' Examine. Frame: (1) relic worship and the stupa as emblem of the Buddha; (2) structural parts and circumambulation; (3) donor inscriptions — kings, guilds, monks, nuns, lay men and women — showing collective patronage; (4) narrative carving of Jatakas for lay audiences; (5) afterlife: Amaravati dispersed, Sanchi preserved in situ. Conclude that stupas record networks of devotion and patronage.

### Recall

- - **q:** What part of the stupa represented the abode of the gods?
  - **a:** The harmika, the balcony-like structure above the anda.
- - **q:** Which guild paid for part of a Sanchi gateway?
  - **a:** The guild of ivory workers.
- - **q:** Where is the biggest rock-cut chaitya hall?
  - **a:** Karla, in the western Indian Buddhist cave tradition.

### Common trap

Treating chaitya and vihara as synonyms. A chaitya hall is a worship hall with a stupa at the rear; a vihara is a residence with cells around a hall. A vihara with a stupa added at the back became a 'chaitya-vihara'.
