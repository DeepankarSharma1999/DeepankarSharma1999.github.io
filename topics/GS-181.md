## GS-181: Parliamentary privileges and motions

### Bridge: Parliament vocabulary: session, sitting, adjournment, prorogation and dissolution

**Parliament** of India has three parts: the **President**, the **Lok Sabha** (House of the People) and the **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States). Each House has a **presiding officer** (the Speaker in the Lok Sabha, the Chairman in the Rajya Sabha). Most of the words below are standard textbook content that the extracts do not define; verify against Article 85 and the Rules.

| Word | Meaning | Who acts |
|---|---|---|
| Sitting | One meeting of a House on one day (it may have a break) | The House meets |
| Session | The series of sittings from the first meeting after summoning until the House is prorogued | President summons (Article 85) |
| Adjournment | Suspends a sitting for some hours, days or weeks; the session is not over | Presiding officer |
| Adjournment sine die | Ends a sitting without naming a date to meet again; the session is formally ended only by prorogation | Presiding officer |
| Prorogation | Ends a session | President |
| Dissolution | Ends the life of the Lok Sabha (five years, or earlier); Bills pending in it generally lapse (GS-179); the Rajya Sabha is a permanent House and is not dissolved | President |

**Article 85** requires that six months not pass between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. By convention there are three sessions in a year: Budget, Monsoon and Winter. The Constitution does not fix a minimum number of sittings or sessions. NCERT notes that the Rajya Sabha is a **permanent House** and continues even when the Lok Sabha is dissolved.

**Trap.** The device called an **adjournment motion** (module 4) has nothing to do with the adjournment of a sitting: it is a way for members to draw attention to an urgent matter.

### Bridge: A day in the Lok Sabha: the order of business

A sitting is not random talk; it follows an order. The order below is a teaching outline from Laxmikanth's description and standard practice (timings and exact order vary; verify with the current Rules).

1. **Question Hour.** The first hour of every sitting: members ask, ministers answer.
2. **Zero Hour.** Starts right after Question Hour and runs until the agenda for the day is taken up. Members raise matters without notice.
3. **Papers laid on the table, and statements by ministers.** Documents such as reports are formally placed before the House.
4. **Calling attention, special mentions and Rule 377 notices.** Short, focused matters of public importance.
5. **Legislative business.** Bills are taken up (see GS-179) and the budget when in session (GS-180). Motions and resolutions are discussed.
6. **Private members' business.** Time is set aside for members who are not ministers; resolutions of private members are discussed only on alternate Fridays in the afternoon (Laxmikanth).
7. **Adjournment** at the end of the day, or earlier if the House is adjourned or disrupted.

Two actors run the day: the **presiding officer**, who admits motions and decides what is in order, and the **Leader of the House** and the **Business Advisory Committee**, who agree the time table. Keep this outline in mind: each module shows where one device fits.

### 1. Orientation: why Parliament needs devices and privileges

*Objective:* Explain why a legislature in a parliamentary system needs devices to question the executive and privileges to protect itself, and map the lesson.

## The problem

In India the executive is drawn from the party or coalition that has a majority in the Lok Sabha. NCERT warns that with that majority an executive could slip into a **cabinet dictatorship**, in which the Cabinet leads and the House merely follows. Only an active and vigilant Parliament can keep a regular check on the executive.

## Four instruments of control (NCERT)

NCERT says the legislature ensures accountability at the stages of policy making, implementation and after implementation, using four kinds of device:

1. **Deliberation and discussion**: debates on Bills, Question Hour, Zero Hour, half-an-hour discussion, the adjournment motion.
2. **Approval or refusal of laws**: a Bill becomes a law only with Parliament's approval.
3. **Financial control**: the budget (see GS-180).
4. **No-confidence motion**: the most powerful weapon; its force is real only when the government's majority is shaky, as NCERT notes after 1989.

NCERT calls Question Hour perhaps the most effective method, noting that members are keenly interested and attendance is high. It also admits a weakness: in recent decades sessions and debating time have declined, and absence of quorum and boycotts reduce the House's power to control the executive.

## The second half of the topic: privileges

To question ministers fearlessly, a member needs protection. NCERT's example: no action can be taken against a member for what he says in the legislature. This is **parliamentary privilege**. Without privileges the Houses could neither maintain their dignity nor protect their members from obstruction (Laxmikanth).

### 2. Question Hour and Zero Hour

*Objective:* Distinguish starred, unstarred and short notice questions and questions to private members, and distinguish Question Hour from Zero Hour.

## Question Hour

The first hour of every sitting is Question Hour (Laxmikanth). Members ask questions and ministers usually answer. NCERT calls it perhaps the most effective method of keeping watch on the executive and the administration.

## Three kinds of question

| Kind | Answer | Supplementary questions? | Notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Starred** (marked with an asterisk) | Oral | **Yes** | Ordinary |
| **Unstarred** | Written | **No** | Ordinary |
| **Short notice** | Oral | Yes (it is oral) | Less than ten days |

A **supplementary question** is a follow-up put orally after the minister has answered. Only an oral answer allows one, so a written (unstarred) answer cannot be followed by supplementaries. A mnemonic: the **star** is a **speaking** question.

Questions can also be put to **private members** (members who are not ministers) if the subject concerns a Bill, resolution or other business for which that member is responsible. The procedure is the same. Lists of the four kinds are printed in different colours: green (starred), white (unstarred), light pink (short notice) and yellow (private members).

## Zero Hour

**Zero Hour** is **not mentioned in the Rules of Procedure**; it is an informal device (Laxmikanth). It starts right after Question Hour and lasts until the regular agenda is taken up, so it is the gap between the two. Members can raise matters without prior notice. It is an **Indian innovation**, in existence since **1962**. NCERT says members are free to raise any matter they think important, though ministers are not bound to reply.

## Quick comparison

- Question Hour: in the Rules; first hour; questions need notice; ministers answer.
- Zero Hour: not in the Rules; after Question Hour; no prior notice; ministers need not reply.

**Standard knowledge to verify.** Numbers of questions per day, the exact hour and the notice period for ordinary questions are not in the extract; do not quote them without checking the current Rules. Practice has also varied in recent sessions (verify before using any such detail).

### 3. Discussion devices without a vote: half-an-hour, short duration, special mention, calling attention

*Objective:* Describe the half-an-hour discussion, short duration discussion, special mention (Rule 377) and calling attention and say which are in the Rules and which carry a vote.

Question Hour and Zero Hour are daily. Other devices give members a slot to raise public issues. None of the four below ends in a vote, but each puts a minister on record.

## Half-an-hour discussion

Used for a matter of sufficient public importance which has already been subject to a lot of debate and where the answer needs elucidation on a matter of fact. The Speaker can allot **three days in a week** for such discussions. There is **no formal motion and no voting**.

## Short duration discussion (two-hour discussion)

Members raise a matter of **urgent public importance**; the time allotted should not exceed **two hours**. The Speaker can allot **two days in a week**. There is **no formal motion and no voting**. The device has existed since **1953**.

## Calling attention (since 1954)

A member calls the attention of a minister to a matter of **urgent public importance** and seeks an **authoritative statement** from him. It is an Indian innovation, in existence since **1954**, and, unlike Zero Hour, it is **mentioned in the Rules of Procedure**. It is described by Laxmikanth in the list of motions, although it works like a notice-based discussion (it appears in the 'Motions at a glance' master note).

## Special mention and Rule 377

A matter that is not a point of order and cannot be raised in Question Hour, half-an-hour discussion, short duration discussion, an adjournment motion, a calling attention notice or any other rule, can be raised as a **special mention** in the **Rajya Sabha**. The equivalent in the Lok Sabha is **'Notice (Mention) under Rule 377'**.

## How to tell them apart

| Device | Key words | Vote? |
|---|---|---|
| Half-an-hour discussion | A matter already debated; clarification of fact; 3 days a week | No |
| Short duration discussion | Urgent matter; 2 hours; 2 days a week; 1953 | No |
| Calling attention | Urgent matter; minister's statement; 1954; in the Rules | No |
| Special mention (RS) / Rule 377 (LS) | A matter that fits no other device | No |

The two innovations to link with years: **Zero Hour 1962** (not in the Rules), **calling attention 1954** (in the Rules), **short duration 1953**.

### 4. Motions I: the kinds of motion, adjournment motion, no-confidence, confidence and censure

*Objective:* State what a motion is, name the three principal categories, and compare the adjournment, no-confidence, confidence and censure motions.

## What is a motion?

No discussion on a matter of general public importance can take place except on a **motion** made with the consent of the presiding officer. A motion is a formal proposal; the House expresses its decision by adopting or rejecting it. Motions are moved by ministers or private members.

Laxmikanth puts motions into three principal categories: a **substantive motion** is a self-contained proposal on a very important matter (for example impeachment of the President or removal of the Chief Election Commissioner); a **substitute motion** replaces an original motion and, if adopted, supersedes it; a **subsidiary motion** has no meaning by itself and depends on an original motion or proceedings (it has ancillary, superseding and amendment sub-types).

## Adjournment motion

Introduced to draw the House's attention to a **definite matter of urgent public importance**; it needs the **support of 50 members** to be admitted. Because it interrupts normal business it is an **extraordinary device**. It involves an **element of censure** against the government, so the **Rajya Sabha is not permitted to use it**. The discussion should last not less than **two hours and thirty minutes**. Restrictions: it must be definite, factual, urgent and of public importance; not cover more than one matter; relate to a specific recent matter, not general terms; not raise a privilege question; not revive a matter already discussed in the same session; not deal with a matter under court adjudication; and not raise what can be raised on a distinct motion.

## No-confidence motion

Article 75 makes the council of ministers collectively responsible to the **Lok Sabha**. It stays in office only while it has the confidence of a majority of the House, so the Lok Sabha can remove it by passing a **no-confidence motion**. The motion needs the support of **50 members** to be admitted.

## Confidence motion

A newer device for hung parliaments, minority governments and coalitions. The President may ask a thinly-supported government to prove its majority on the floor; the government may also move a confidence motion itself. If it is negatived, the government falls.

## Censure versus no-confidence

| Point | Censure motion | No-confidence motion |
|---|---|---|
| Reasons | Must state reasons | Need not state reasons |
| Against | One minister, a group, or the whole council | The whole council only |
| Purpose | Censure specific policies or actions | Test the confidence of the Lok Sabha |
| If passed | Council need not resign | Council must resign |

**Traps.** Both motions work only in the Lok Sabha because the council is responsible to it. The adjournment motion is barred in the Rajya Sabha because of the element of censure. Fifty members' support appears for both the adjournment and the no-confidence motion.

### 5. Motions II: privilege motion, motion of thanks, dilatory, no-day-yet-named, point of order, resolutions

*Objective:* Describe the privilege motion, the motion of thanks, the dilatory and no-day-yet-named motions and the point of order, and distinguish resolutions from motions.

## Privilege motion

Concerned with the breach of parliamentary privileges **by a minister**. A member moves it when he feels a minister has committed a breach of privilege of the House or of its members by **withholding facts** of a case or giving **wrong or distorted facts**. Its purpose is to **censure** the minister. (More on privileges in modules 7 and 8.)

## Motion of thanks

The first session after each general election and the first session of every fiscal year is addressed by the President, who outlines the government's policies for the previous and coming year. This address (the counterpart of the 'speech from the Throne' in Britain) is discussed in **both Houses** on a **motion of thanks** and the motion is put to the vote. It **must be passed**; otherwise it amounts to the **defeat of the government**. The government falls only if it is defeated in the **Lok Sabha**, to which the council of ministers is responsible; amendments carried against the government in the Rajya Sabha (for example in 2001, 2015 and 2016; verify) did not bring it down. Trap: 'defeat in either House brings down the government' is wrong. It is also an occasion to criticise lapses.

## No-day-yet-named motion

A motion that has been **admitted by the Speaker** but for which no date has been fixed. The Speaker, after considering the business of the House and consulting the Leader of the House or the Business Advisory Committee, allots a day or days for it. (Think of a no-confidence motion that has been admitted and is waiting for a date.)

## Dilatory motion

A motion to **adjourn the debate** on a Bill, motion or resolution, or to retard or delay business. It can be moved at any time after a motion has been made; debate on it is limited to its own matter. If the Speaker thinks it is an abuse of the rules, he may put the question at once or decline to propose it.

## Point of order

Raised when the proceedings do not follow the normal rules of procedure. It must relate to the interpretation or enforcement of the Rules or of the Articles regulating the business of the House, and raise a question within the Speaker's cognizance. It is usually raised by an opposition member to control the government. It is an **extraordinary device** because it suspends the proceedings before the House, and **no debate** is allowed on it.

## Resolutions

Members move resolutions to draw attention to matters of general public interest. Three kinds:

1. **Private member's resolution**: moved by a non-minister; discussed only on alternate Fridays, in the afternoon.
2. **Government resolution**: moved by a minister; taken up Monday to Thursday.
3. **Statutory resolution**: moved by a member or a minister; always tabled under a provision of the Constitution or an Act.

**Resolution versus motion.** Every resolution is a type of **substantive motion** and is **always put to the vote**. Not every motion is substantive, and not every motion is put to the vote.

### 6. Closure and the guillotine; where cut motions fit

*Objective:* Describe the four closure motions and distinguish the guillotine from the others; link to cut motions in GS-180.

A **closure motion** is moved by a member to **cut short the debate** on a matter before the House. If the House approves it, debate stops at once and the matter is put to the vote. Laxmikanth lists four kinds:

| Kind | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Simple closure** | A member moves that the matter 'having been sufficiently discussed be now put to vote' |
| **Closure by compartments** | Before debate begins, the clauses of a Bill or a long resolution are grouped into parts; each part is debated and voted as a whole |
| **Kangaroo closure** | Only the important clauses are debated and voted; the clauses in between are skipped and taken as passed |
| **Guillotine closure** | When the allotted time is over, the **undiscussed** clauses are also put to the vote along with the discussed ones, for want of time |

A neat way to remember: the **kangaroo jumps** over clauses; the **guillotine cuts** the debate and votes the rest in one go. The guillotine is common at the end of the budget process, when demands for grants that have not been discussed are voted together (GS-180 explains the budget stages and cut motions).

## Where cut motions sit

A cut motion seeks to reduce the amount of a demand for grant. It is a motion on a financial item and belongs in the budget topic. Its three forms (policy cut, economy cut, token cut) are covered in GS-180; do not learn them here.

## Why these devices exist

The business of Parliament is large and time is short. Closure devices let the majority complete business; the opposition's tools (adjournment motion, point of order, calling attention) give it a voice. The House works well only when the two balance.

### 7. Parliamentary privileges: meaning, Articles 105 and 194, classification and sources

*Objective:* Define parliamentary privileges, list the collective and individual privileges, and explain the sources and the absence of a single code.

## Meaning

**Parliamentary privileges** are special rights, immunities and exemptions enjoyed by the two Houses, their committees and their members. They are needed to secure independence and effectiveness. The Constitution extends them to persons entitled to speak and take part in the proceedings, including the **Attorney General of India** and **Union ministers**. They do **not** extend to the **President**, although he is an integral part of Parliament.

## Articles

**Article 105** deals with privileges of the Houses of Parliament and their members. **Article 194** is the parallel provision for State legislatures (standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify). Article 105 originally named two privileges: **freedom of speech in Parliament** and the **right of publication** of proceedings. For other privileges it referred to those of the British House of Commons on 26 January 1950, until Parliament defined them. The 44th Amendment (1978) dropped the direct reference to the Commons and fixed the position as of its commencement (20 June 1979 per Laxmikanth) with only verbal changes in substance. **Article 361A** protects the publication in newspapers of true reports of proceedings (standard knowledge; verify). The 44th Amendment restored the freedom of the press to publish true reports without prior permission, except for a secret sitting.

## Collective privileges (each House as a body)

1. Publish its reports, debates and proceedings and prohibit others from publishing them (subject to the press freedom point above).
2. Exclude strangers and hold secret sittings.
3. Make rules for its own procedure and conduct of business and adjudicate on such matters.
4. Punish members and outsiders for breach of privilege or contempt by reprimand, admonition or imprisonment (and suspension or expulsion for members).
5. Receive immediate information of the arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment and release of a member.
6. Institute inquiries, order attendance of witnesses and send for papers.
7. Courts cannot inquire into the proceedings of a House or its committees.
8. No arrest and no legal process, civil or criminal, within the House precincts without the presiding officer's permission.

## Individual privileges (each member)

1. **Freedom from arrest** during the session and for **40 days before and after**, in **civil cases only** (not criminal or preventive detention).
2. **Freedom of speech** in Parliament and committees: no court proceedings for anything said or any vote given, subject to the Constitution and the rules regulating procedure.
3. **Exemption from jury service**, and the right to refuse to give evidence or appear as a witness in a pending case while Parliament is in session.

## Sources and codification

Parliament has **not made a special law to codify all privileges exhaustively**. They rest on five sources: (1) constitutional provisions, (2) various laws made by Parliament, (3) the rules of both Houses, (4) parliamentary conventions and (5) judicial interpretations.

### 8. Breach of privilege, contempt, the Committee of Privileges, courts and Fundamental Rights

*Objective:* Distinguish breach of privilege from contempt, and explain how courts and Fundamental Rights relate to privileges.

## Breach of privilege

Laxmikanth defines it as the disregard of or attack on the privileges, rights and immunities of a member individually or of the House collectively; it is punishable by the House.

## Contempt of the House

Any act or omission that obstructs a House, a member or an officer in the performance of their functions, or tends, directly or indirectly, to produce results against the dignity, authority and honour of the House, is **contempt of the House**.

## Breach versus contempt

The two phrases are used interchangeably, but contempt is **wider**. A breach of privilege may amount to contempt and contempt may include a breach, but there can be contempt without a breach of any specific privilege. Example in the source: **disobedience to a legitimate order of the House** is not a breach of a specific privilege but can be punished as contempt.

## How it is handled

A **privilege motion** (module 5) is moved against a minister who withholds or distorts facts. The presiding officer has the final say on matters of breach of privilege (NCERT). The **Committee of Privileges** examines the questions referred to it (standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify). The House can punish by reprimand, admonition or imprisonment, and suspend or expel members.

## Courts and privileges

Courts are prohibited from inquiring into the proceedings of a House or its committees (the source's collective privilege 7), and a member is not liable in court for anything said or any vote given in the House. But the rule has limits. Privileges are not one single code; they draw on the Constitution, laws, rules, conventions and **judicial interpretations**, so the courts do shape their meaning. Standard cases to verify before quoting:

- **Keshav Singh (1964):** the Supreme Court's special reference about a clash between a State legislature and a High Court; commonly cited to show that a House's punishment can be examined when it is said to violate Fundamental Rights such as personal liberty.
- **Raja Ram Pal (2007):** expulsion of members in the cash-for-query case; commonly cited for limited judicial review of the exercise of privileges.
- **P. V. Narasimha Rao (1998)** held that a member who took a bribe to vote or speak in the House was protected by Article 105(2)/194(2); **Sita Soren v Union of India (4 March 2024, seven judges)** overruled that decision: bribery is not protected by Article 105(2)/194(2) (verify the exact holding and bench).

## Fundamental Rights and the Constitution's limits

Freedom of speech in Parliament under Article 105 is itself **subject to the Constitution and the rules of procedure**: it is a speech protection inside the House, separate from the citizen's freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a), which is a Fundamental Right with its own limits. Where the House acts against an outsider (for example for a publication) the person can raise Articles 19 and 21. This is why the codification debate matters: with no single code, the line between the House's dignity and individual freedom is drawn by practice and by courts.

## The codification debate

Case for a code: certainty, protection of citizens' rights, and avoiding disputes between legislatures and courts. Case against: a code may be rigid, and Parliament may prefer to keep flexibility. This is a Mains discussion, not a fact to state as settled.

### 9. How UPSC tests this topic: patterns and a Mains frame

*Objective:* Recognise the question shapes on this topic, reason through statements and plan a Mains answer.

## Prelims shapes

1. **Instruments of control** (2017 Q50): which devices let Parliament control the Council of Ministers. Lists in modules 1 to 4: Question Hour, supplementaries, the adjournment motion, no-confidence, and so on.
2. **Exclusive powers of the Lok Sabha** (2022 Q15): the no-confidence motion belongs to the Lok Sabha (Article 75: responsibility to the Lok Sabha). Impeachment of the President involves both Houses (see GS-174), and the approval of an Emergency proclamation involves both Houses (standard knowledge; verify). A resolution disapproving the continuation of a National Emergency, by contrast, is a Lok Sabha-only power (Article 352(7)-(8); verify).
3. **Types of question** (2026 Q58): the most common swap is oral and written: starred is oral, unstarred is written; supplementaries follow only oral answers.
4. **Vocabulary swaps** in original MCQs: breach versus contempt, closure types, which devices are barred in the Rajya Sabha.

## Thought-aloud on 2026 Q58

Statement 1 says unstarred questions are those for which a Member desires an oral answer: this reverses the definition. Statement 2 says starred questions are those for which a written answer is desired: reversed again. Statement 3 says no supplementary question can be asked on an unstarred question: consistent with module 2. So only one statement is correct, and the option 'only one correct statement' fits. Reasoned analysis, not an official answer key.

## Mains shape (2021 Q4)

'To what extent is Parliament able to ensure accountability of the executive?' A 150-word frame: the instruments (Question Hour, Zero Hour, motions, committees, budget), the strongest tool (no-confidence, effective only without a stable majority), the limits (disruptions, declining sittings, quorum failures, party discipline), and one or two reforms (more committee work, predictable Question Hour, penalties for disruption). Keep a view: 'to a limited extent' with reasons.

## Common errors

Calling an adjournment motion the same as adjournment of the House; thinking the Rajya Sabha can use the adjournment motion; saying a censure motion forces the council to resign; confusing prorogation (ends a session, President) with adjournment sine die (presiding officer); and claiming that Parliament has a single code of privileges.

### Notes: Vocabulary of Parliament: definitions

- Sitting: one meeting of a House on a day. Session: the series of sittings from summoning to prorogation (standard knowledge; verify).
- Adjournment: suspends a sitting; sine die: no date fixed. Prorogation: ends a session (President). Dissolution: ends the Lok Sabha (President).
- Motion: a formal proposal moved with the consent of the presiding officer. Resolution: a substantive motion always put to the vote.
- Privilege: special right, immunity or exemption of Houses, committees and members. Contempt: any act that obstructs or lowers the dignity of the House.
- Supplementary question: an oral follow-up on a starred question. Closure: cutting short a debate.

- Adjournment (of a sitting) versus adjournment motion (a device for a matter of urgent public importance).

- Censure versus no-confidence; confidence motion versus motion of thanks.
- Breach of privilege versus contempt; privilege motion (against a minister) versus the Committee of Privileges (verify).
- Question Hour (in the Rules; notice) versus Zero Hour (informal; no notice).

- Recent use of privilege notices, suspension of members and disruptions during sessions.

### Core concepts

- Parliament controls the executive by deliberation, approval of laws, financial control and the no-confidence motion.
- Question Hour is the first hour and is in the Rules; Zero Hour is informal.
- Starred is oral with supplementaries; unstarred is written without supplementaries.
- The adjournment motion is barred in the Rajya Sabha because it carries censure.
- Privileges are collective and individual; they rest on five sources and have not been codified in one law.
- Breach of privilege and contempt differ: contempt is wider.

### Practice

**Q.** Which hour comes first in a sitting?

**A.** Question Hour.

**Q.** Who prorogues a session and who adjourns a sitting?

**A.** The President prorogues; the presiding officer adjourns.

**Q.** Name two kinds of question in Parliament.

**A.** Starred and unstarred (also short notice and questions to private members).

**Q.** Why is a no-confidence motion moved only in the Lok Sabha?

**A.** Because the council of ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75.

**Q.** Why do members need freedom of speech in the House?

**A.** To question the executive fearlessly and represent the people without fear of court action.

**Q.** Why is a resolution different from a motion?

**A.** Every resolution is a substantive motion and is always voted upon; not every motion is voted upon.

**Q.** An MP is detained under a preventive detention law during a session. Does the freedom from arrest apply? Why?

**A.** No; the freedom is limited to civil cases and does not cover criminal or preventive detention cases.

**Q.** The time for the debate on the Finance Bill is over and clauses 5 to 20 have not been debated. They are put to vote together. Which device and which house? Why are cut motions not involved?

**A.** The guillotine closure in the Lok Sabha; cut motions are a separate budget device (GS-180).
