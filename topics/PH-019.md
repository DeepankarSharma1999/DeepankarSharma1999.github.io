### Bridge: Matrices and eigenvalues: finding principal axes

The inertia tensor of PH-015 is a 3×3 table of numbers. Multiplying it by a column vector ω gives the angular momentum L: each output component is a row of the table multiplied term by term with ω and added. Usually L points in a different direction from ω.

For a **symmetric** matrix (entry in row 1 column 2 equals row 2 column 1, as for the inertia tensor) there are always three mutually perpendicular directions along which the matrix only stretches a vector without turning it. These directions are the **eigenvectors**; the stretch factors are the **eigenvalues**. For the inertia tensor they are the **principal axes** and the **principal moments**. Along such an axis L ∥ ω.

How to find them for a flat (planar) molecule lying in the xy-plane: the tensor has the form with a 2×2 block [[a, d], [d, b]] and a separate entry c for the z-axis (the third row and column contain zeros except c). The eigenvalues of the block are (a+b)/2 ± √(((a−b)/2)² + d²), and c is the third eigenvalue. If d = 0 the axes already are principal. A computer (numpy.linalg.eigh) does the same job for any size. Always check that the sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace a + b + c, because the trace does not change when the axes are turned.

### Bridge: Atomic masses and bond lengths in SI units

Moments of inertia come out in kg m², so masses must be in kilograms and lengths in metres. **Atomic mass unit:** 1 u = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (about the mass of one hydrogen atom, 1.0078 u). **Lengths:** 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m; 1 angstrom (Å) = 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.1 nm, so a bond length of 0.1275 nm is 1.275 Å. Mathur and Upadhyaya also use older units (g cm², Å, erg), so check units before comparing answers: 1 g cm² = 10⁻⁷ kg m².

Typical sizes: bond lengths are about 0.1 nm, atomic masses 1 to 35 u (nitrogen 14, oxygen 16, chlorine 35), so moments of inertia are of order 10⁻⁴⁷ to 10⁻⁴⁵ kg m². A result of 10⁻²⁷ or 10⁻³⁰ kg m² means a unit has been missed. **Worked example:** hydrogen molecule, m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg per atom, r = 0.77 Å = 0.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ m; I = μr² = (m/2) r² = 0.835 × 10⁻²⁷ × 5.93 × 10⁻²¹ = 4.95 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kg m² (this is Mathur's example 2, PDF p. 615, whose printed 0.495 × 10⁻⁴⁷ is the same number). In this lesson masses use 1H = 1.00783 u, D = 2.01410 u, C = 12 u, N = 14.003 u, O = 15.995 u, F = 18.998 u, 35Cl = 34.969 u, 32S = 31.972 u (standard values, flagged for verification).

### 1. Orientation: what 'molecular rotations (as rigid bodies)' asks of you

*Objective:* Say in two sentences what the clause asks, what the rigid-rotor model keeps and freezes, and where each earlier lesson supplies a tool.

## What the clause asks

The Paper I clause is 'Molecular rotations (as rigid bodies); Di and tri-atomic molecules'. In plain words: treat a molecule as a tiny rigid body whose parts are the atomic nuclei (point masses), then use the rigid-body theory you already have (PH-015 to PH-018) to find how it spins, how much energy the spin stores and what its moments of inertia are. **Di-atomic** means two atoms (HCl, CO, N₂); **tri-atomic** means three (CO₂, H₂O).

This lesson is an application, not a new theory. For that reason the plan is: one-line recaps with links, and the time goes into what is new (triatomic molecules, symmetric tops, the numbers, and where the model breaks).

## Three motions of a molecule

A molecule can do three kinds of motion at once: it can **translate** (the centre of mass moves), it can **rotate** (the whole arrangement of atoms turns), and it can **vibrate** (bonds stretch and angles flex about their equilibrium values). The **rigid-rotor model** keeps the first two and freezes the third: every bond length and angle is treated as one fixed number.

## Why freezing the vibration is allowed

Two reasons, both numerical. **Energy:** for carbon monoxide (CO) the first rotational step is ħ²/I = 7.7 × 10⁻²³ J, which is 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ eV. A vibrational quantum of CO is about 0.27 eV (standard value, verify), roughly 550 times larger. **Speed:** the hydrogen chloride (HCl) bond vibrates at 8.7 × 10¹³ Hz (PH-009, module 8), i.e. ω = 5.5 × 10¹⁴ rad s⁻¹, while HCl in a fast rotational state (J = 10, an idea explained in module 8) turns at only 4.2 × 10¹³ rad s⁻¹. About thirteen vibrations fit into each turn, so the rotating molecule effectively sees an average bond length.

The nuclei may be treated as points because the electrons add under 0.06% of a hydrogen atom's mass (1/1837 of a proton) and the nuclei are tiny compared with the bond length. Module 9 shows where the model fails: a very fast spin stretches the bond, and some molecules (ammonia) have motions that are not rigid at all.

### 2. Foundation: degrees of freedom, 3N = translation + rotation + vibration

*Objective:* Count translation, rotation and vibration for any di- or tri-atomic molecule and explain why a linear molecule has two rotational degrees of freedom and a non-linear one three.

## The counting rule

Recall from PH-015 (module 3) that **degrees of freedom** are the independent coordinates needed to fix a configuration. N separate atoms need 3N numbers. When the atoms are bound into a molecule, the total is still 3N, but the numbers are regrouped by what kind of motion they describe:

$$ 3N = 3 (translation) + R (rotation) + V (vibration) $$

Three numbers always describe where the centre of mass is. The rotational count R comes from the rigid-body theory of PH-015 and the vibrational count is whatever is left.

## Why R = 2 for a linear molecule and R = 3 for a non-linear one

Recap (PH-015 module 3): a rigid body with three or more non-collinear points has 6 degrees of freedom and a collinear set has 5, so a linear molecule is 3 translational + 2 rotational and a non-linear one 3 + 3. The kinetic-theory count of quadratic energy terms is a different count and depends on which vibrations are active (module 9).

## Vibrational count

Subtract: V = 3N − 5 for a linear molecule and V = 3N − 6 for a non-linear molecule (N ≥ 3). A diatomic molecule (always linear, N = 2) has V = 6 − 5 = 1: just the bond stretch.

| Molecule | N | 3N | Shape | Trans | Rot | Vib |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HCl, CO, N₂ | 2 | 6 | linear | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| CO₂ | 3 | 9 | linear | 3 | 2 | 4 |
| H₂O | 3 | 9 | bent | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| NH₃ | 4 | 12 | pyramid | 3 | 3 | 6 |
| CH₄ | 5 | 15 | tetrahedral | 3 | 3 | 9 |

A single atom (N = 1) has no orientation to change: 3 translational, nothing else. The H₂O case matters most for the optional paper: bending gives a non-linear molecule, so H₂O has three rotational degrees of freedom and, as module 5 shows, three different moments of inertia.

## Why the bond axis does not count

Recap (PH-015 module 3; the tensor is in module 3 below): for point nuclei on the axis I about the axis is exactly zero, so a spin about it stores no energy or angular momentum. **New caveat:** real electrons carry angular momentum about the axis in some molecules (for example nitric oxide, NO), and the nuclei are not exact points; these are quantum corrections outside this lesson (flagged: standard knowledge, not in the extracts).


### 3. Core: the diatomic rotor, I = μr₀², and the vector model

*Objective:* Recap I = μr₀² with links, then derive the new results: the tensor I = μr₀²(1 − n̂n̂ᵀ), L perpendicular to the axis for any ω, and the free-rotor motion of n̂.

## Recap (links, not repeated)

Two point masses m₁ and m₂ a fixed distance r₀ apart have their centre of mass C at distances r₁ = m₂r₀/(m₁ + m₂) from m₁ and r₂ = m₁r₀/(m₁ + m₂) from m₂, and about C

$$ I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² = μ r₀² ,   μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂) $$

This is derived in PH-017 (module 6, the rigid diatomic molecule) and applied to HCl and CO in PH-009 (module 8); Mathur derives it in section 11.18 (PDF pp. 611-612) and Upadhyaya in example 25 (pp. 187-188). The 2017 question (module 10) asks exactly this derivation. A neat meaning: the rotating pair acts like one particle of mass μ moving on a circle of radius r₀ about a fixed centre, which is the one-body reduction of PH-009 (module 3) applied to rotation.

## New: the full inertia tensor of the diatomic rotor

Let n̂ be the unit vector along the bond (from m₂ towards m₁). With C as the origin, atom 1 is at **r**₁ = r₁n̂ and atom 2 at **r**₂ = −r₂n̂. The tensor of PH-015 (module 6) is I_jk = Σ m (r² δ_jk − r_j r_k). For each atom r² = (distance)² and r_jr_k = (distance)² n_jn_k, so

$$ I_jk = (m₁r₁² + m₂r₂²)(δ_jk − n_jn_k) = μ r₀² (δ_jk − n_jn_k) $$

Choose axes so that n̂ = e₃: the matrix is diag(μr₀², μr₀², 0). Check: the third entry is the moment about the bond axis, zero as module 2 said; the two equal entries are the two perpendicular axes.

## Consequences: the vector model

1. **L is always perpendicular to the molecular axis.** For any angular velocity ω, L = Iω = μr₀²(ω − (ω·n̂)n̂). The bracket removes the part of ω along the axis, so n̂ · L = 0. Even if ω has a component along the bond (it cannot be seen physically), it adds nothing to L.
2. **Energy.** K = ½ ω·L = ½ μr₀² |ω⊥|² where ω⊥ is the part of ω perpendicular to n̂. Again, the axial part contributes nothing.
3. **Free rotation.** The axis n̂ is carried by the rotation: dn̂/dt = ω × n̂. Then n̂ × (dn̂/dt) = n̂ × (ω × n̂) = ω − (ω·n̂)n̂ = ω⊥, so **L = I (n̂ × dn̂/dt)**, with I = μr₀². With no torque L is constant. Since L ⊥ n̂ at all times, n̂ always lies in the plane perpendicular to L, and L = I |dn̂/dt| gives a constant angular speed ω = L/I: the axis turns uniformly in a plane, with L fixed along the plane's normal.

## Energy, period and frequency

$$ K = L²/(2I) = ½ I ω² ,   ω = L/I ,   f = ω/2π = L/(2πI) ,   T = 2πI/L $$

(Mathur section 11.19 writes E = j²/(2μr₀²), PDF p. 612.) For a given L everything depends on the single number I, which is why the quantum levels of module 8 depend on one number.

## Diagram

```
                L  (constant, out of the page)
                ⊙
        m₂ ●───── C ─────● m₁        n̂ points from m₂ to m₁
                 ↺                   n̂ turns in the page at ω = L/I
     (page = plane of rotation; L ⟂ page, so L ⟂ n̂ at every instant)
```

## Dimensions and limits

[I] = M L² = kg m². If r₀ → 0, I → 0 and K → ∞ for fixed L: a point has no moment of inertia. If m₂ → ∞, μ → m₁ and C sits on m₂: the light atom circles a fixed heavy one at radius r₀, I = m₁r₀².

### 4. Core: linear molecules, the sum rule and CO₂

*Objective:* Derive I = (1/M) Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼrᵢⱼ² for any linear molecule and apply it to CO₂ (I = 2m_O r²) and OCS.

## From two atoms to any number on a line

Any linear molecule (all nuclei on one line) is a rigid rotor with I = 0 about the line and the same value I about every perpendicular axis through the centre of mass C. Let atoms of masses mᵢ sit at positions zᵢ along the line (a coordinate z measured along it from any origin), M = Σ mᵢ and z_cm = Σ mᵢzᵢ / M. Then

$$ I = Σ mᵢ (zᵢ − z_cm)² = Σ mᵢzᵢ² − M z_cm² $$

(the standard expansion of Σ m(z − z_cm)², using Σ mᵢzᵢ = M z_cm, and equal to the parallel-axis theorem of PH-017).

## The pair formula (derivation)

Claim: I = (1/M) Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ (zᵢ − zⱼ)², the sum over distinct pairs. Steps:

1. Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ − zⱼ)² = ½ Σᵢ Σⱼ mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ² − 2zᵢzⱼ + zⱼ²) (the full double sum counts each pair twice; the i = j terms are zero).
2. Split: ½ [Σᵢ mᵢzᵢ² Σⱼ mⱼ − 2 (Σᵢ mᵢzᵢ)(Σⱼ mⱼzⱼ) + Σⱼ mⱼzⱼ² Σᵢ mᵢ] = ½ [2M Σ mᵢzᵢ² − 2 (M z_cm)²].
3. This is M Σ mᵢzᵢ² − M² z_cm² = M (Σ mᵢzᵢ² − M z_cm²) = M I.

Divide by M. **Check:** two atoms, one pair: I = m₁m₂r₀²/(m₁ + m₂) = μr₀² ✓. **Advantage:** the origin and the position of C never need to be found.

## CO₂ (O=C=O, linear and symmetric)

Let each C–O bond have length r, with C at the origin and the O atoms at z = ±r. C lies at the centre of mass, because the two equal O masses balance. C is on the axis through C, so it adds nothing:

$$ I = m_O r² + m_O r² = 2 m_O r² $$

**Check by the pair formula:** pairs O–C and C–O give m_O m_C r² each, and O–O gives m_O² (2r)² = 4 m_O²r²: I = (2 m_O m_C r² + 4 m_O² r²)/(m_C + 2 m_O) = 2 m_O r² (m_C + 2 m_O)/(m_C + 2 m_O) = 2 m_O r² ✓. A consequence: the mass of the **central** atom drops out, so ¹²CO₂ and ¹³CO₂ have the same I (a testable isotope statement, flagged: the spectral consequence is standard knowledge, not in the extracts).

**Numbers** (flagged data: r = 0.1162 nm, 16O = 15.995 u): m_O = 2.656 × 10⁻²⁶ kg, so

$$ I = 2 (2.656 × 10⁻²⁶)(1.162 × 10⁻¹⁰)² = 7.17 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² $$

This is about five times the CO value (1.449 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m²), because both oxygen atoms sit 0.1162 nm from the centre, while in CO the two atoms sit 0.048 nm and 0.064 nm from the centre of mass.

## OCS, an unsymmetric linear triatomic

For O=C=S the masses differ, so C is not the centre of mass. Take r(OC) = 0.1156 nm and r(CS) = 0.1560 nm (flagged data, verify): atoms at z = 0, 0.1156 and 0.2716 nm. The centre of mass is at 0.168 nm from O. Direct sum and the pair formula give the same result, I = 1.374 × 10⁻⁴⁵ kg m² (computed both ways by Python; they agree to all digits shown).

## Why the molecule type matters later

A linear molecule is described by **one** moment of inertia. Its classical rotation is the diatomic motion of module 3 (L ⊥ axis, K = L²/2I). Whether it also absorbs microwave radiation depends on having an electric dipole moment: CO₂ has none (symmetric), HCl and OCS do. That is a Paper II (quantum and spectroscopy) fact, flagged as outside the extracts.

### 5. Core: the bent molecule H₂O, principal moments by the tensor method

*Objective:* Find the three principal moments of H₂O from its geometry (symmetric frame and an explicit tilted-frame diagonalisation) and use the arbitrary-axis formula and the planar sum rule as checks.

## Data and set-up

Take the bond length r = 0.0958 nm and the H–O–H angle θ = 104.5° (standard values 0.0958 nm and 104.5°; flagged, verify), with m_O = 15.995 u and m_H = 1.00783 u. The three atoms lie in a plane, so the molecule is a **planar** rigid body (PH-017, perpendicular-axis theorem).

**Frame** (right-handed): z along the C₂ axis (the line bisecting the angle), x in the molecular plane perpendicular to z, y perpendicular to the plane (into the page when x is drawn to the right and z up, so that x × y = z). With O at the origin:

- O at (0, 0, 0)
- H₁ at (+r sin(θ/2), 0, +r cos(θ/2)) = (+0.07575, 0, +0.05865) nm
- H₂ at (−0.07575, 0, +0.05865) nm

(sin 52.25° = 0.7906, cos 52.25° = 0.6122.)

## Centre of mass

By symmetry x_cm = y_cm = 0. z_cm = 2m_H(0.05865)/(m_O + 2m_H) = 2(1.00783)(0.05865)/18.0106 nm = **0.006564 nm** above O. Relative to the centre of mass the H atoms are at z = 0.05865 − 0.00656 = 0.05209 nm and O at z = −0.00656 nm.

## Moments (symmetric frame)

With I_xx = Σ m (y² + z²), I_yy = Σ m (z² + x²), I_zz = Σ m (x² + y²) and products Σ mxy, Σ myz, Σ mzx (PH-015 and PH-017):

- I_xx = 2m_H(0.05209 nm)² + m_O(0.00656 nm)² = **1.0225 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m²** (about an in-plane axis perpendicular to the C₂ axis)
- I_zz = 2m_H (0.07575 nm)² = **1.9205 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m²** (about the C₂ axis)
- I_yy = I_xx + I_zz = **2.9430 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m²** (about the axis perpendicular to the plane)

All products of inertia vanish: y = 0 for every atom, and x → −x maps the molecule onto itself, so Σ mxz = 0. The tensor is already diagonal, so these are the **principal moments**. The relation I_yy = I_xx + I_zz is the perpendicular-axis theorem for a planar body (PH-017, module 3). The three moments are all different, so H₂O is an **asymmetric top**. Order them as I_a < I_b < I_c = 1.0225, 1.9205, 2.9430 (× 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m²). The C₂ axis therefore carries the **intermediate** moment, the case of PH-018 module 7.

## Explicit tilted-frame diagonalisation (the tensor method)

To see the tensor method with non-zero products of inertia, take a frame with x along the bond O–H₁ and the molecule in the xy plane. In units of 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m² the tensor about the centre of mass (computed numerically from the atomic coordinates) is

$$ I = [[1.359, 0.435, 0], [0.435, 1.584, 0], [0, 0, 2.943]] $$

The off-diagonal element is I_xy = −Σ mxy. Block eigenvalues: mean (1.359 + 1.584)/2 = 1.4715, half-difference 0.1125, so eigenvalues 1.4715 ± √(0.1125² + 0.435²) = 1.4715 ± 0.449 = **1.9205 and 1.0225**, and the third, 2.943 (numpy.linalg.eigh gives 1.0225, 1.9205, 2.9430). The eigenvector of 1.9205 points 52.25° from the O–H bond, which is half the bond angle: the C₂ axis, as it must be. Trace check: 1.359 + 1.584 + 2.943 = 5.886 = 1.0225 + 1.9205 + 2.9430 ✓.

**Arbitrary-axis formula (PH-017, module 9):** for an axis through C parallel to the O–H bond, n̂ = (sin 52.25°, 0, cos 52.25°) in the symmetric frame (x is the in-plane direction perpendicular to z), and I = I_xx n_x² + I_zz n_z² = 1.0225(0.625) + 1.9205(0.375) = 1.359 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m², the same as the diagonal element of the tilted tensor ✓.

## Rotational constants

In the cm⁻¹ units of module 8 these moments give 27.4, 14.6 and 9.51 cm⁻¹ (observed values are close, about 27.9, 14.5 and 9.3, flagged: not in the extracts).

### 6. Core: symmetric tops and the three-fold-axis proof

*Objective:* Classify rotors by their principal moments, prove that a three-fold axis forces I_xx = I_yy, compute I for NH₃, CH₃Cl and CH₄ and connect the free motion to PH-018.

## The four types

Order the principal moments as I_a ≤ I_b ≤ I_c.

| Type | Moments | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Linear rotor | I_a = 0, I_b = I_c | HCl, CO₂ |
| Spherical top | I_a = I_b = I_c | CH₄, SF₆ |
| Symmetric top | two equal, one different | NH₃, CH₃Cl, benzene |
| Asymmetric top | all different | H₂O |

A symmetric top is **prolate** (cigar-like) when the odd moment is the smallest and **oblate** (disc-like) when it is the largest. Upadhyaya (PDF p. 193) calls I₁ = I₂ a symmetric top (his example is the Earth) and I₁ = I₂ = I₃ a 'totally symmetric top' (sphere or cube); the usual molecular name for the latter is spherical top.

## Why a three-fold axis gives a symmetric top (derivation)

Let the molecule have a rotation axis z of three-fold symmetry: turning by 120° about z maps it onto itself. Place the three equivalent atoms (mass m each) at (ρ cos φ_k, ρ sin φ_k, z₀) with φ_k = φ₀ + 2πk/3 (k = 0, 1, 2). Atoms on the axis have x = y = 0.

1. The unit vectors (cos 2φ_k, sin 2φ_k) are three vectors 120° apart (the doubled angles 2φ_k are again three directions 120° apart), so they sum to zero: Σ cos 2φ_k = Σ sin 2φ_k = 0.
2. Σ m x² = mρ² Σ cos²φ_k = mρ² (3/2 + ½ Σ cos 2φ_k) = 3mρ²/2. In the same way Σ m y² = 3mρ²/2. So Σ m x² = Σ m y².
3. Σ m xy = (mρ²/2) Σ sin 2φ_k = 0, and Σ m x = Σ m y = 0 (three vectors at 120° sum to zero) so the products Σ m xz and Σ m yz vanish.
4. The tensor is diag(I_⊥, I_⊥, I_∥) with I_∥ = Σ m(x² + y²) = 3mρ² and I_⊥ = Σ m(y² + z²), referred to the centre of mass (which lies on the axis).

The same argument for a symmetry axis of order 3 or higher in general shows I_xx = I_yy.

## Geometry of an XY₃ pyramid

If the bond length is r and each bond makes the angle α with the axis, the angle θ between two bonds satisfies r² cos θ = r²[cos²α + sin²α cos 120°], so cos θ = (3cos²α − 1)/2 and

$$ cos²α = (2 cos θ + 1)/3 ,   ρ = r sin α ,   z = r cos α $$

Checks: θ = 120° gives α = 90° (a planar molecule); θ = 109.47° gives cos α = 1/3.

### 7. Core: NH₃, CH₃Cl and CH₄ with numbers, and the free motion

*Objective:* Compute the principal moments of NH₃, CH₃Cl and CH₄, classify each, and connect the free motion of a symmetric-top molecule to the Euler-equation result of PH-018.

## NH₃ (r = 0.1012 nm, θ = 106.7°, flagged data)

cos²α = 0.14176, α = 67.88°, ρ = 0.09375 nm, hydrogens 0.03810 nm above N. Centre of mass: z_cm = 3 m_H(0.03810)/(m_N + 3m_H) = 0.006766 nm above N. Then

$$ I_∥ = 3 m_H ρ² = 4.413 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m² ,  I_⊥ = m_H[3ρ²/2 + 3(z_H − z_cm)²] + m_N z_cm² = 2.806 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m² $$

(a numerical diagonalisation of the full tensor agrees). Since I_∥ > I_⊥, NH₃ is an **oblate** symmetric top: (I_∥ − I_⊥)/I_⊥ = +0.573.

## CH₃Cl (C–Cl 0.1781 nm, C–H 0.1096 nm, ∠HCH = 110.5°, flagged data)

The axis is the C–Cl line. The heavy chlorine is on the axis, so only the light H atoms give I_∥ = 3 m_H ρ² = 5.43 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m²; I_⊥ = 6.30 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² because Cl is far from the centre. Here I_∥ < I_⊥: **prolate**, with (I_∥ − I_⊥)/I_⊥ = −0.914.

## CH₄ (r = 0.1087 nm, flagged data)

Place C at the origin and H atoms at (d, d, d), (d, −d, −d), (−d, d, −d), (−d, −d, d) with d = r/√3. Each H has y² + z² = 2r²/3, so I_xx = (8/3) m_H r² = 5.273 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m². By the symmetry of the four positions I_yy = I_zz is the same, and Σ m xy = m d²(1 − 1 + 1 − 1) = 0 (also for yz and zx). So the tensor is I × (unit matrix): a **spherical top**. Then L = Iω for every ω (L ∥ ω always).

## Free motion (link to PH-018)

PH-018 (module 6) shows for a torque-free symmetric top that ω₃ is constant, ω turns about the symmetry axis at Ω = (I₃ − I₁)ω₃/I₁ and the axis circles L at L/I₁. With I₃ = I_∥ and I₁ = I_⊥: Ω/ω₃ = +0.573 for NH₃ (same sense as the spin) and −0.914 for CH₃Cl (opposite sense). The molecules wobble at a rate comparable to their spin, unlike the Earth where it is about 1/305. For a spherical top, Ω = 0 and ω is constant.

The quantum energy of the symmetric top is quoted in module 8.

### 8. Core: rotational energy, constants, microwave frequencies and isotope effects

*Objective:* Write the classical rotational energy of each rotor, state the quantum levels as a forward link, compute B and the first microwave line for HCl, DCl, CO and ¹³CO, and relate classical and quantum frequencies.

## Classical energy

On principal axes K = ½ΣIᵢωᵢ² = ΣLᵢ²/(2Iᵢ) (PH-018, module 5). So:

- linear rotor: K = L²/(2I) (module 3);
- spherical top: K = L²/(2I);
- symmetric top (L² = L₃² + L⊥²): K = L²/(2I_⊥) + L₃²(1/(2I_∥) − 1/(2I_⊥)).

## Quantum levels (forward link to Paper II, quoted not derived)

Quantum mechanics replaces L² by J(J + 1)ħ² with J = 0, 1, 2, … Mathur (section 11.19, PDF pp. 612-613) writes E = A_M j(j + 1) with A_M = h²/(8π²I) (Mathur's A, renamed A_M here; A_M = hB in the notation below), levels 0, 2A_M, 6A_M, 12A_M, … and the rule Δj = ±1. In modern notation the **rotational constant** is B = h/(8π²I) (frequency units) or B̃ = h/(8π²cI) (cm⁻¹, with c in cm s⁻¹), and

$$ E_J = h B J(J+1) = J(J+1)ħ²/(2I) ,   ν(J → J+1) = 2B(J+1) $$

so successive lines are equally spaced by 2B (Mathur p. 614, 'common difference 2A_M/h' in the present notation), in the microwave or far-infrared region. A measured spacing gives I = h/(8π²B), then r₀ = √(I/μ). For the symmetric top the standard result adds a term, E = hB J(J + 1) + h(A_∥ − B) k² with A_∥ = h/(8π²I_∥) (flagged: standard knowledge, not in the extracts; the quantum number k is the projection of J on the symmetry axis, written K in spectroscopy texts and renamed here because K means kinetic energy, and A_∥ is not Mathur's A_M). Whether a molecule absorbs depends on a permanent dipole moment: HCl yes, H₂ and CO₂ no (flagged).

## Classical meaning of the spacing

The classical frequency in a state with |L| = √(J(J+1)) ħ is f = L/(2πI) = 2B√(J(J+1)). Since J < √(J(J+1)) < J + 1, this lies between the lines 2BJ and 2B(J+1) that bracket the level, and for J = 50 in CO it agrees with 2BJ to about 1% (5851 against 5793 GHz): the classical rotor of module 3 and the quantum levels correspond at large J.

## Isotope effect

Substituting an isotope changes the nuclear mass but, to a very good approximation, not the bond length (the electrons that bind the atoms are almost unchanged: flagged standard assumption, as in PH-009 module 8). Hence I ∝ μ and B ∝ 1/μ:

$$ I(DCl)/I(HCl) = μ(DCl)/μ(HCl) = 1.9044/0.9796 = 1.944 ,  ν(DCl)/ν(HCl) = 0.514 $$

For ¹³CO against ¹²CO, ν(¹³CO)/ν(¹²CO) = μ(¹²CO)/μ(¹³CO) = 6.856/7.172 = 0.956. Heavier isotopologues have lower and more closely spaced lines: this is how isotopes are identified from spectra.

### 9. Master: limits of the rigid rotor and the rotational specific heat

*Objective:* Estimate the centrifugal stretching of HCl, list the other failures of the model, and derive the rotational contribution to molar specific heat (R for linear, 3R/2 for non-linear) with its range of validity.

## Failure 1: centrifugal stretching

A fast-spinning molecule is pulled outward and the bond lengthens. Model the HCl bond as a spring of force constant k = 4.83 × 10² N m⁻¹ (the value used in PH-009). With angular momentum L the stretched radius r satisfies k(r − r₀) = μrω² = L²/(μr³). For a small stretch put r = r₀(1 + δ):

$$ δ ≈ L²/(μ k r₀⁴) $$

For L² = J(J+1)ħ², HCl gives: J = 1, δ = 0.011%; J = 5, 0.16%; J = 10, 0.58% (solving the cubic exactly; the small-stretch formula gives 0.59%); J = 20, 2.1% (formula 2.2%). Because I ∝ r², I rises by about 2δ and the true levels lie below the rigid-rotor values (standard correction −D J²(J+1)², flagged).

## Other failures (named, not developed)

- **Vibration–rotation coupling:** the average of 1/r² over the vibration is not 1/r₀². Mathur (p. 361; taken from PH-009's extract of PDF 358-361, not in the day-05 extracts, so unverified here) attributes the departure of the potential curve from a parabola to large amplitude and to rotation.
- **Non-rigid motion:** the NH₃ pyramid can turn inside out (inversion), and groups such as CH₃ can rotate internally; neither is a rigid-body motion (flagged: standard knowledge).
- **Electronic angular momentum** and nuclear spin statistics (for example in H₂) change the allowed levels (flagged; Paper II).

## The rotational specific heat

**Equipartition (flagged forward link to Paper I thermal physics):** in classical statistical mechanics each term in the energy that is quadratic in a coordinate or momentum has mean value ½kT per molecule at temperature T. For a linear rotor K = (L_x² + L_y²)/(2I) has two such terms (module 2: two rotational degrees of freedom), so ⟨K⟩ = kT and the molar rotational heat capacity is C_rot = R. For a non-linear rotor three terms give ⟨K⟩ = 3kT/2 and C_rot = 3R/2. Adding the translational 3R/2:

| Gas | Rotation | C_V (vibration frozen) | γ = C_p/C_V |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monatomic | none | 3R/2 = 12.47 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ | 5/3 |
| Linear (HCl, N₂) | R | 5R/2 = 20.79 | 7/5 = 1.40 |
| Non-linear (H₂O, NH₃, CH₄) | 3R/2 | 3R = 24.94 | 4/3 |

(γ = (C_V + R)/C_V.) **CO₂ is not in the linear row:** its low-frequency bending mode (Θ_vib about 960 K) is partly active at 300 K, so the measured C_V is about 3.4R (about 28.5 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) and γ about 1.29-1.30, not 7/5 (flagged, verify). Each fully active vibration adds R, so a classical CO₂ would reach 5R/2 + 4R = 13R/2 = 54.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; this is the high-temperature limit and is not reached at room temperature.

## When is classical rotation valid?

The rotational level spacing is of order ħ²/(2I); define the **rotational temperature** Θ_rot = ħ²/(2Ik_B). The classical result needs T ≫ Θ_rot. Computed: HCl 15.2 K, CO 2.78 K, CO₂ 0.56 K, H₂ 87.6 K. So at 300 K rotation of HCl, CO and CO₂ is classical, while for H₂ it freezes out when T falls toward 100 K (heat capacity below 5R/2; flagged, standard). The fall actually starts well above Θ_rot: C_rot of normal H₂ is already dropping at about 200-300 K, and ortho/para nuclear-spin statistics apply (flagged, verify). The vibrational temperature is hν/k_B = 4.2 × 10³ K for HCl (ν = 8.7 × 10¹³ Hz), so at 300 K vibration is frozen: this is why a diatomic gas shows 5R/2 and γ ≈ 1.4 at room temperature.

## A source example

Upadhyaya (example 27, PDF p. 188) takes the average rotational energy of an N₂ molecule at room temperature as about 4 × 10⁻²¹ J. That is kT = 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K, the equipartition value for a linear rotor. With the printed bond length 1.3 Å (the accepted N₂ bond is about 0.110 nm, flagged), I = 1.975 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² and the rotation frequency f = (1/2π)√(2K/I) = 1.0 × 10¹² Hz. Upadhyaya prints the result as ω = 6.36 × 10¹² rad s⁻¹ (recomputed: 6.36 × 10¹²) and calls it the rotational frequency; f = ω/2π. For the accepted bond length 1.10 Å the same K gives I = 1.41 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² and f = 1.2 × 10¹² Hz.

## The verified 2017 question (Physics Optional I, Q2(b), 15 marks)

Text (verified against the official paper): “A diatomic molecule can be considered to be made up of two masses m₁ and m₂ separated by a fixed distance r. Derive a formula for the distance of centre of mass, C, from mass m₁. Also show that the moment of inertia about an axis through C and perpendicular to r is μr², where μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂).” Practice target, not an official allotment: 15 marks, about 18 minutes. Per the project brief, Q1 and Q5 are compulsory with several 10-mark short parts; verify against the paper instructions.

## Answer layout (analysis, not an official key)

1. **Set-up and assumption.** Draw the two masses on a line, C between them. State: masses are points (nuclei), the distance r is fixed (rigid rotor), C is the origin of moments, and the axis of rotation passes through C perpendicular to r.
2. **Centre of mass.** Let r₁ and r₂ be the distances of m₁ and m₂ from C, so r₁ + r₂ = r. The moments about C balance: m₁r₁ = m₂r₂. Substituting r₂ = r − r₁: m₁r₁ = m₂(r − r₁), so r₁(m₁ + m₂) = m₂ r and

$$ r₁ = m₂ r/(m₁ + m₂) ,   r₂ = m₁ r/(m₁ + m₂) $$

The distance of C from m₁ is r₁. The heavier mass is closer to C.
3. **Moment of inertia.** I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² = [m₁m₂² + m₂m₁²] r²/(m₁ + m₂)² = m₁m₂(m₂ + m₁) r²/(m₁ + m₂)² = m₁m₂ r²/(m₁ + m₂) = μr².
4. **Interpretation and checks.** Dimension: M L². If m₂ → ∞ then μ → m₁ and C moves to m₂, so I → m₁r² (a light atom circling a fixed heavy one). If m₁ = m₂ = m, μ = m/2, r₁ = r/2 and I = mr²/2 (the hydrogen molecule of Mathur example 2, I = 4.95 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kg m² for r = 0.77 Å and m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). About the line joining the atoms I = 0 (module 2).
5. **Optional one-line alternative:** the relative-motion argument of PH-009: the pair rotates like a single particle of mass μ at distance r from a fixed centre, so I = μr².

The full derivation appears in PH-017 (module 6) and PH-009 (module 8); the task here is to present it cleanly, not to rediscover it.

# Guided practice

## Standard procedure for any molecular-rotation problem

1. **Count:** N atoms, linear or not. Give R (2 or 3), V (3N − 5 or 3N − 6).
2. **Coordinates:** put the symmetry axis along z, the molecule in a plane if planar, origin at a convenient atom.
3. **Centre of mass:** compute it first; shift to it. All moments refer to the centre of mass.
4. **Tensor:** I_jk = Σ m(r²δ_jk − r_jr_k). Look for symmetry that kills the off-diagonal products.
5. **Principal moments:** diagonal entries if products vanish, otherwise diagonalise (bridge module B1).
6. **Checks:** dimensions (kg m²), trace invariance, planar sum rule, a limiting case.
7. **Use:** E = L²/2I, B = h/(8π²I), Θ_rot, C_V.

### Notes: Definitions

- Rigid rotor: a molecule modelled as point nuclei at fixed mutual distances, free to translate and rotate but not to vibrate.
- Degrees of freedom of a molecule: 3N coordinates for N atoms, regrouped as 3 translation + R rotation + V vibration, with R = 2 and V = 3N − 5 for a linear molecule, R = 3 and V = 3N − 6 for a non-linear one.
- Reduced mass: μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂) (PH-009).
- Principal moments I_a ≤ I_b ≤ I_c: the eigenvalues of the inertia tensor about the centre of mass.
- Linear rotor, spherical top, symmetric top (prolate or oblate), asymmetric top: classes of molecule by the pattern of the principal moments.
- Rotational constant: B = h/(8π²I) in frequency units, B̃ = h/(8π²cI) in cm⁻¹ (c in cm s⁻¹).
- Rotational temperature: Θ_rot = ħ²/(2Ik_B).

### Notes: Key equations

- Diatomic: r₁ = m₂r₀/(m₁ + m₂) (distance of C from m₁); I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² = μr₀².
- Tensor of a linear rotor: I_jk = I(δ_jk − n_jn_k), L = I(ω − (ω·n̂)n̂), K = L²/(2I), ω = L/I, f = L/(2πI).
- Linear molecule: I = Σ mᵢ(zᵢ − z_cm)² = (1/M) Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ − zⱼ)²; CO₂: I = 2m_O r².
- Planar molecule: I_perpendicular-to-plane = I_in-plane1 + I_in-plane2.
- Three-fold axis: I_xx = I_yy; XY₃ pyramid: cos²α = (2cos θ_YY + 1)/3, I_∥ = 3mρ² with ρ = r sin α.
- Classical energy: K = ΣLᵢ²/(2Iᵢ); symmetric top K = L²/(2I_⊥) + L₃²(1/(2I_∥) − 1/(2I_⊥)).
- Quantum rotor (quoted, Paper II): E_J = J(J+1)ħ²/(2I) = hBJ(J+1), ν(J → J+1) = 2B(J+1), Δ J = ±1.
- Heat capacity (classical, vibration frozen): C_V = 3R/2 + R (linear) or 3R/2 + 3R/2 (non-linear).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: diatomic centre of mass and I = μr₀²

- State: points m₁, m₂ at distance r₀ on a line, C between them, r₁ + r₂ = r₀.
- Balance of moments about C: m₁r₁ = m₂r₂, so r₁ = m₂r₀/(m₁ + m₂), r₂ = m₁r₀/(m₁ + m₂).
- I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² = m₁m₂(m₂ + m₁)r₀²/(m₁ + m₂)² = μr₀².
- Checks: dimension M L²; m₂ → ∞ gives I → m₁r₀²; equal masses give mr₀²/2; I = 0 about the axis of the line.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: the pair formula for linear molecules

- Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ − zⱼ)² = ½ΣᵢΣⱼ mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ² − 2zᵢzⱼ + zⱼ²).
- = M Σ mᵢzᵢ² − (Σ mᵢzᵢ)² = M (Σ mᵢzᵢ² − M z_cm²) = M I.
- Therefore I = (1/M) Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ − zⱼ)²; the origin and the position of C are not needed.

- Classical frequency of rotation 2B√(J(J+1)) versus the quantum line 2B(J+1).
- Spherical top versus symmetric top; prolate (I_∥ < I_⊥) versus oblate (I_∥ > I_⊥).

### Notes: Limits and exceptions

- Moment of inertia about the bond axis of a linear molecule is zero only in the point-nuclei rigid model; electronic angular momentum can matter in some molecules (flagged).
- Rotation is not rigid at high J: centrifugal stretching lowers the levels (module 9).
- NH₃ inversion and internal rotation of methyl groups are non-rigid motions.
- Equipartition for rotation needs T ≫ Θ_rot; H₂ freezes out near 100 K, though C_rot of normal H₂ already starts falling at about 200-300 K, well above Θ_rot = 87.6 K, and ortho/para statistics apply (flagged, verify).
- Homonuclear molecules and CO₂ have no permanent dipole and show no pure rotational microwave absorption (flagged).
- Asymmetric-top quantum levels have no closed formula (not covered).

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - A molecule treated as a rigid rotor keeps translation and rotation and freezes vibration.
  - 3N = 3 + R + V with R = 2, V = 3N − 5 for linear and R = 3, V = 3N − 6 for non-linear molecules.
  - Diatomic: I = μr₀² about an axis through the centre of mass perpendicular to the bond; zero about the bond axis.
  - Linear molecules have one moment; planar molecules obey I_⊥plane = I_a + I_b; symmetry forces equal moments (three-fold axis, tetrahedron).
  - Rotational energy K = L²/2I classically; E_J = J(J+1)ħ²/2I quoted from quantum theory.
  - Rotation adds R (linear) or 3R/2 (non-linear) to the classical molar heat capacity.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - CO₂ is linear with I = 2m_O r²; the mass of C does not appear.
  - H₂O is an asymmetric top with three different moments, 1.02, 1.92 and 2.94 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg m² for r = 0.0958 nm and 104.5° (flagged data).
  - NH₃ is an oblate symmetric top and CH₃Cl a prolate symmetric top; CH₄ is a spherical top.
  - CO: ν(0 → 1) = 115.9 GHz from I = 1.449 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² (observed near 115.27 GHz, flagged).
  - DCl has about 1.94 times the moment of HCl, so its first line is at 0.514 of the HCl frequency.
  - At J = 10 the HCl bond stretches by about 0.6% under rotation.
- **criticalDistinctions:**
  - μ versus total mass versus single-atom mass in I.
  - r₀ (bond) versus r₁, r₂ (distances from the centre of mass).
  - Rigid-body degrees of freedom versus kinetic-theory degrees of freedom.
  - Prolate (cigar, I_∥ < I_⊥) versus oblate (disc, I_∥ > I_⊥) symmetric tops.
  - Classical rotation frequency versus the quantum line frequency.
- **formulae:**
  - r₁ = m₂r/(m₁ + m₂);  I = μr²;  μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)
  - I = (1/M) Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ rᵢⱼ² (linear molecules)
  - I_jk = I(δ_jk − n_jn_k) for a diatomic
  - cos²α = (2 cos θ + 1)/3 for an XY₃ pyramid; I_∥ = 3mρ²
  - K = L²/2I;  B = h/(8π²I);  ν(J → J+1) = 2B(J+1)
  - C_V (vibration frozen) = 5R/2 (linear), 3R (non-linear)
- **traps:**
  - Using m₁ or m₁ + m₂ in place of μ.
  - Writing a non-zero moment about the molecular axis of a linear molecule.
  - Forgetting to convert nm or Å to metres.
  - Calling H₂O a symmetric top because it has a C₂ axis: its three moments differ.
  - Reading the line spacing as B rather than 2B.
  - Using 3 rotational degrees of freedom for a linear molecule (3N − 6 instead of 3N − 5).

    - **a:** the sum of the two in-plane moments.
  - - **q:** Successive microwave lines of a rigid diatomic rotor are spaced by ___ in frequency.
    - **a:** 2B = h/(4π²I).
  - - **q:** A symmetric top with I_∥ > I_⊥ is called ___.
    - **a:** oblate.
- **shortQuestions:**
  - - **q:** Why does a linear molecule have two rotational degrees of freedom?
    - **a:** With all nuclei on a line there is no third point off the line to fix; spinning about the line moves no mass (I = 0), so only the direction of the line (2 angles) specifies the orientation.
  - - **q:** State the type of rotor of CH₄, NH₃, CH₃Cl and H₂O.
    - **a:** Spherical top, oblate symmetric top, prolate symmetric top, asymmetric top.

    - **a:** Carbon sits at the centre of mass on the axis, so its distance from the rotation axis is zero.

  - - **q:** When is the classical rotational heat capacity valid?
    - **a:** When T is much greater than the rotational temperature ħ²/(2Ik_B).

    - **a:** Centrifugal force stretches the bond, increasing I and lowering the level spacing.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-OSU-EQUIP (verified):** Equipartition theorem: the energy of a thermodynamic system in equilibrium is partitioned equally among its degrees of freedom.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, section 2.3 'Heat Capacity and Equipartition of Energy'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-3-heat-capacity-and-equipartition-of-energy)
- **F-OSU-DIATOMIC-DOF (verified):** A diatomic molecule has three translational degrees of freedom, two rotational degrees of freedom (rotation perpendicular to its axis), and vibration along its axis.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, section 2.3 'Heat Capacity and Equipartition of Energy'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-3-heat-capacity-and-equipartition-of-energy)
- **F-OSU-DIATOMIC-CV (verified):** From about room temperature to about 600 K the rotational degrees of freedom of a diatomic gas are fully active and the vibrational ones are not, so d = 5 and the molar heat capacity at constant volume is C_V = 5R/2.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, section 2.3 'Heat Capacity and Equipartition of Energy'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-3-heat-capacity-and-equipartition-of-energy)
- **F-HP-ROTOR-SEL (verified):** For a rigid-rotor diatomic molecule the rotational selection rule is delta J = +/-1. The allowed transitions are regularly spaced at interval 2B (B the rotational constant defined on the page), and the line for J to J+1 has energy 2(J+1)B.
  Source: [HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), 'Rotational Spectra of Rigid Rotor Molecules'](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/molecule/rotrig.html)

### Answer Lab diagrams

#### Rigid diatomic rotor: I = μ r₀² (draft)

Rotation about the centre of mass, perpendicular to the bond, reduces to one body of reduced mass μ at distance r₀.

A horizontal bond with a larger mass m1 on the left and smaller mass m2 on the right, the centre of mass between them nearer m1, a dashed perpendicular axis through it, and distances r1, r2 and r0.

1. Draw the bond with a larger circle m₁ on the left and a smaller circle m₂ on the right.
2. Mark the centre of mass nearer m₁ and draw the dashed axis through it perpendicular to the bond.
3. Dimension r₁, r₂ and the whole bond r₀ = r₁ + r₂.
4. Write r₁ = m₂r₀/(m₁+m₂), r₂ = m₁r₀/(m₁+m₂) and I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² = μr₀².

#### Bent H₂O: three principal axes (draft)

A planar bent molecule has three different principal moments, so it is an asymmetric top; the one perpendicular to the plane is the sum of the other two.

A bent three-atom molecule with oxygen at the top and two hydrogens below left and right, a vertical symmetry axis a and a horizontal axis b through the centre of mass, and axis c out of the page.

1. Draw the bent molecule: O at the apex, an H at each lower end.
2. Mark the centre of mass on the symmetry axis just below O and draw axis a (vertical, dashed) and axis b (horizontal, dashed) through it.
3. Mark axis c perpendicular to the plane as a dot in a circle at the centre of mass.
4. Write I_c = I_a + I_b (planar body) and note that all three moments differ: asymmetric top.

### Memory skeleton

- Rigid rotor: point nuclei, fixed distances, vibration frozen
- 3N = 3 + R + V: linear R = 2, V = 3N − 5; non-linear R = 3, V = 3N − 6
- Diatomic: centre of mass and I = μr₀²
- Linear molecules: pair formula; CO₂ I = 2m_O r²
- Planar and symmetric molecules; H₂O asymmetric top by the tensor
- Rotor classes: linear, spherical, symmetric (prolate, oblate), asymmetric

### Last-week summary

- Linear: R = 2, V = 3N − 5; non-linear: R = 3, V = 3N − 6.
- m₁r₁ = m₂r₂, r₁ = m₂r₀/(m₁ + m₂); I = μr₀², zero about the bond; I = (1/M) Σ_{i<j} mᵢmⱼ(zᵢ − zⱼ)².
