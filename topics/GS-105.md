### Bridge: Wind names, the 'from' rule and a map in words

Meteorologists name a wind by the direction it blows **from**. A **westerly** blows from the west towards the east. An **easterly** blows from the east towards the west. A **south-westerly** blows from the south-west towards the north-east. This is the opposite of how a storm is described: a depression 'moves eastwards' means its centre travels towards the east, and the winds that carry it are westerlies. Day 19 lesson GS-104 uses the same 'from' rule.

Two other tools are needed. First, **latitude bands**: 30 degrees North is the edge of the tropics' subtropical high; 60 degrees North is the sub-polar low. India lies between about 8 and 37 degrees North, and the Tropic of Cancer crosses its middle (NCERT kegy104 p3). Second, **height**: the troposphere is the lowest layer, where weather happens; its top is the tropopause. NCERT's table (kegy209 p1) gives about 265 millibars and minus 49.7 degrees Celsius at 10 km in a standard atmosphere, compared with 1,013.25 millibars and 15.2 degrees at sea level; so at 10 km only about a quarter of the air is above you in pressure terms (265 / 1,013.25 = 0.26).

Worked example. A weather bulletin says 'a westerly jet over the north Indian plain'. The wind comes from the west, so over Delhi it blows towards Bihar, high in the troposphere. A surface depression 'moving eastwards' over Punjab is travelling the same way, towards Delhi.

### 1. Orientation: two puzzles and two fast-moving systems

*Objective:* Say in one minute what a jet stream and a western disturbance are, which two puzzles of Indian weather they explain, and what this lesson owns.

## Two puzzles from one NCERT chapter

NCERT's chapter on the climate of India leaves two puzzles.

1. **Why does the dry north-west, far from any sea, get a little rain and snow in the cold season?** NCERT answers that weak cyclones from the Mediterranean sea cause rainfall in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and western Uttar Pradesh, and snow in the lower Himalaya (kegy104, PDF p7).
2. **Why does the summer monsoon arrive as a sudden 'burst'?** One part of NCERT's answer is that the movement of the ITCZ is tied to the westerly jet stream leaving the north Indian plain and the easterly jet stream setting in (PDF p5).

Both answers involve fast winds high above the ground and storms that travel from west to east.

## The two ideas in one line each

- A **jet stream** is a narrow, fast current of air high in the troposphere. The NCERT extracts use the term without defining it, so the definition in module 3 is standard knowledge and carries a verify flag.
- A **western disturbance** is the name commonly used for the winter depressions that reach north-west India from the west. NCERT calls them 'shallow cyclonic depressions' and 'temperate cyclones' (PDF pp7 and 12); the phrase 'western disturbance' itself does not occur in the extracts, so treat it as the standard name for the same thing (verify).

The extracts are short on this topic: two sentences on the jets, one paragraph on the depressions and the figures for winter rain. ### 2. Foundation: the words and the three ideas you need from earlier lessons

*Objective:* Define troposphere, tropopause, cyclone, depression, front, disturbance, rabi and snowfall, and recall the pressure, Coriolis and belt-shifting ideas from GS-103 and GS-104.

## Eight words

1. **Troposphere and tropopause**: the lowest layer of the atmosphere, where weather happens, and its upper boundary. NCERT says air rising at the ITCZ reaches the top of the troposphere 'up to an altitude of 14 km' (kegy209 p5); Day 18 lesson GS-103 gives a higher equatorial figure, so treat 14 km as NCERT's number (check).
2. **Westerly and easterly**: winds named by the direction they blow **from** (bridge GS-105-B1).
3. **Cyclone**: a low-pressure system with winds circling the centre, **anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere** and clockwise in the southern (NCERT kegy209 p4, Table 9.2).
4. **Depression**: Leong's word for a temperate cyclone, with a low-pressure centre and close-set isobars (PDF p201). NCERT kegy104 also uses 'cyclonic depression' for both the winter systems from the west and the retreating-monsoon systems from the Andaman Sea (PDF pp7 and 11), so read the origin before assuming which is meant.
5. **Front**: the boundary zone where two different air masses meet; cold, warm, stationary and occluded fronts occur in the middle latitudes (NCERT kegy209 pp6-7). The **polar front** is the middle-latitude boundary between warmer air from the south (carried by the westerlies) and cold polar air from the north; extra-tropical cyclones form along it (NCERT kegy209 p7).
6. **Disturbance**: in weather talk, a system that breaks up a spell of settled weather. A **western** disturbance comes from the west.
7. **Rabi**: crops sown in the cold season and harvested in spring, such as wheat (standard meaning; NCERT p12 simply calls winter rain 'highly beneficial for rabi crops').
8. **Snowfall**: precipitation as snow, which in India NCERT restricts to the Himalayan region (kegy104 p12).

## Three ideas to carry over

**One: Coriolis direction.** The rotation of the earth deflects moving air to the **right** in the northern hemisphere and to the **left** in the southern (kegy209 pp3-4). It is absent at the equator and grows towards the poles.

**Two: friction-free upper winds.** Winds 2-3 km above the surface are free from the friction of the ground and are controlled mainly by the pressure gradient and the Coriolis force. Where isobars are straight, these two balance and the wind blows **parallel to the isobars**: the **geostrophic wind** (NCERT p4). Jet streams are the fastest examples of this balance.

**Three: belts shift with the sun.** The pressure belts follow the sun: they move **southwards** in the northern winter and **northwards** in summer (kegy209 p3). Leong adds that the Mediterranean-type regions of Europe and California come under the westerlies in winter for that reason (PDF p196). This is the key to why disturbances from the west reach India in winter.

## Why the order matters

Jet streams explain the **upper** half of the story (fast westerlies aloft); disturbances explain the **surface** half (rain, snow, cold). The two are linked: NCERT itself asks what role the westerly jet streams play in steering the depressions.

### 3. Core: what a jet stream is, and why it blows from the west

*Objective:* Define a jet stream, explain in three steps why the westerly jets blow from the west in both hemispheres, and use the result against the 2020 statement that jet streams occur only in the northern hemisphere.

## Definition (standard knowledge; verify)

The NCERT extracts use the words 'jet stream' without defining them. The standard definition is: a **narrow, fast-moving current of air high in the troposphere, near the tropopause**, flowing mostly from west to east, with its fastest core in the middle and slower air on either side. It is often described as hundreds of kilometres wide and a few kilometres deep, with speeds above about 110 km per hour (about 30 metres per second) in the core. These figures are **standard knowledge, verify**. Picture a river of fast air in a sea of slower air.

## Why it forms and why it is a westerly: three steps

**Step 1. A pressure slope aloft.** Warm air expands; a column of warm tropical air is taller than a column of cold polar air. At the same height high up, the air pressure is therefore higher over the tropics than over the pole. The pressure-gradient force aloft points **from the tropics towards the pole** (standard reasoning, verify). NCERT p5 gives the supporting picture: air rises at the ITCZ to the top of the troposphere and moves towards the poles.

**Step 2. The Coriolis force turns the motion.** NCERT p3: deflection is to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern.

**Step 3. Balance.** High above the ground there is no friction (NCERT p4), so the wind settles into the geostrophic balance: the pressure force on one side, the Coriolis force on the other, and the wind along the isobars.

Check the direction in each hemisphere, because this is where mistakes happen.

- **Northern hemisphere.** The pressure force points north (towards the pole). Suppose the wind blows towards the **east**. Facing east, your right is south, so the Coriolis force points south, exactly opposite the pressure force. The two balance. Wind blowing towards the east is a **westerly**.
- **Southern hemisphere.** The pressure force points south (towards the pole). Suppose the wind blows towards the **east**. Facing east, your left is north, so the Coriolis force points north, opposite the pressure force. They balance. Again the wind is a westerly.

So **the upper westerlies and the westerly jet streams exist in both hemispheres.** A statement that jet streams occur in the northern hemisphere only (the 2020 Prelims item) fails on this reasoning; the reasoning is standard physics, so verify it against a current source.

## Why it is narrow and fast

The pressure slope aloft is steepest where warm and cold air lie side by side. So jets sit above the boundaries between air of different temperature: at the edge of the tropics and along the polar front (module 4). The stronger the contrast, the faster the wind; the contrast is larger in the winter half of the year, so the jets are stronger then (standard, verify).

## Three common mistakes

- A jet stream is **not** a surface wind, and it is **not** a storm.
- It is **not** a straight line: it meanders north and south and its position changes from day to day and season to season.
- 'Jet' means narrow and fast. It has nothing to do with aircraft engines; pilots do use it to save time when flying eastwards (standard).

### 4. Core: the named jets, and where they sit

*Objective:* Name the subtropical westerly, polar-front and tropical easterly jets, state each one's position and direction, and say which positions come from NCERT and which are standard knowledge to verify.

The extracts name only two jets: a **westerly jet stream** over the north Indian plain in winter and an **easterly jet stream** along 15 degrees North at the start of the summer monsoon (kegy104 p5). The wider family below is **standard knowledge; verify every number**.

## The three jets compared

| Jet | Where (typical) | Height (typical) | Direction | Season |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Subtropical westerly jet** | Near 25-35 degrees North and South, at the poleward edge of the tropics | Highest of the westerly jets, around 12 km | From the west | All year; strongest in winter |
| **Polar-front jet** | Higher latitudes, roughly 40-65 degrees, very variable | Lower, around 9 km | From the west | All year; wavy and changeable |
| **Tropical easterly jet** | Near 15 degrees North over southern Asia (NCERT) | Very high, near the top of the troposphere | From the east | Summer monsoon months |

## Why those places

- NCERT p5 describes the Hadley circulation: air rises at the ITCZ to the top of the troposphere, moves towards the poles and accumulates near 30 degrees North and South, where part of it sinks as the subtropical high. The **subtropical jet** lies on the poleward side of this poleward-flowing, descending air, where tropical and mid-latitude air meet (standard, verify).
- NCERT p7 says extra-tropical cyclones form along the **polar front**, the boundary between warm air from the south and cold air from the north. The **polar-front jet** sits above this boundary.
- The **tropical easterly jet** is a summer feature. Standard accounts link it to the strong summer heating of the Asian land mass and the Tibetan region, which reverses the upper-air flow over the tropics (verify). NCERT does not give a cause; it gives only the date and the role.

## A rule, and its limit

In the NCERT story every jet is a **westerly** except the **tropical easterly jet**. Test the rule: the subtropical and polar-front jets are westerly (module 3's balance), the tropical easterly jet is easterly (NCERT p5). A near-surface current such as the Somali jet over the Arabian Sea is a different, low-level feature of the monsoon, belonging to Day 20 lesson GS-119, and is not an upper-air jet. So the rule holds only for upper-air jets.

## A word on meanders (outside the extracts)

Because the polar-front jet follows the changing boundary between warm and cold air, it does not run straight. It develops large north-south waves (called Rossby waves in standard texts) that carry cold air equatorwards in some places and warm air polewards in others. The extracts do not mention Rossby waves; learn the one-line idea and mark it verify.

### 5. Core: the jets and India - NCERT's seasonal story

*Objective:* Tell NCERT's sequence of the westerly and easterly jets over India through the year, state the caution on cause and link, and place the winter jet in the steering of western disturbances.

## Winter: the westerly jet over the north Indian plain

In the cold season the westerly jet stream lies over the north Indian plain, **south of the Himalayas** (NCERT kegy104 p5 describes its 'position over the north Indian plain, south of the Himalayas'). In the same season the surface weather is pleasant, with a feeble high pressure over the northern plain and winds moving out from the north-west (PDF p7). At intervals shallow depressions arrive from the west (module 6). NCERT leaves a question to the reader: what is the role of the westerly jet streams in steering these depressions (PDF p7)? The standard answer is that the depressions are carried along in the westerly flow aloft, so the jet acts as the guide and the conveyor of the systems (**standard knowledge, verify**; the extract poses the question and does not answer it).

Standard texts add that the winter jet is split into two branches by the Tibetan Plateau and that one branch passes south of the Himalayas. NCERT does not say this; mark it verify.

## Summer: the changeover and the burst

NCERT's sequence for the start of the southwest monsoon (PDF pp4-5), in order:

1. In April-May the sun is vertically over the Tropic of Cancer; the land in the north-west heats up and an **intense low pressure** forms there.
2. The low attracts the south-east trades across the equator and the **ITCZ moves north** (centred near 25 degrees North in July, NCERT p8; the p4 box gives 20-25 degrees North for the monsoon trough).
3. The shift of the ITCZ is related to the **withdrawal of the westerly jet** from its position over the north Indian plain.
4. The **easterly jet stream sets in along 15 degrees North only after the westerly jet has withdrawn**.
5. The easterly jet is **held responsible for the burst** of the monsoon.
6. The monsoon reaches the Kerala coast by about 1 June, Mumbai and Kolkata between 10 and 13 June, and the whole subcontinent by mid-July (PDF p5).

Day 20 lesson GS-119 teaches the monsoon in full. Here only the jet-stream links matter.

## The caution: cause or companion?

NCERT's wording is 'related to' and 'held responsible'. Present-day research treats the shift of the jets, the heating of the land, the ITCZ and the wind reversal as parts of one connected change, and does not always treat the jet as the single cause (standard knowledge, **verify**). In an answer write the NCERT sequence, then add one line: 'the jet change accompanies and strengthens the onset'. That keeps you inside the textbook and shows awareness.

## Putting the seasons side by side

| Season | Jet over India (NCERT) | Surface link |
|---|---|---|
| Cold weather | Westerly jet over the plain south of the Himalayas | Depressions from the west bring light rain and snow |
| Onset (June) | Westerly jet withdraws; easterly jet sets in along 15 degrees North | The monsoon bursts and advances |

A calendar strip with two bars: the westerly jet in winter over the plain, the easterly jet from June; mark the burst between them, and add the dates of arrival in Kerala, Mumbai and Kolkata.

### 6. Core: western disturbances - origin, path and moisture

*Objective:* State NCERT's account of where the depressions originate, which regions they cross and where they gain moisture, and explain why they reach India in winter.

## NCERT's paragraph (kegy104 p7), unpacked

During the winter the pleasant weather of India is 'at intervals disturbed by shallow cyclonic depressions' that **originate over the east Mediterranean Sea** and **travel eastwards across West Asia, Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan** before they reach the **north-western parts of India**. On the way their **moisture content is augmented** from the **Caspian Sea in the north** and the **Persian Gulf in the south**.

Keep the five facts as a chain: *origin - direction - regions crossed - moisture sources - arrival*.

## The path in words, for a hand-drawn map

Start at the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea. Move east over West Asia, then Iran, then Afghanistan, then Pakistan, and finish over north-western India. The Caspian Sea lies to the north of this line and the Persian Gulf to its south, which is why NCERT says the depressions gather moisture from both sides. Draw one arrow along the path, a small wave symbol at the Caspian Sea and one at the Persian Gulf.

## Why 'shallow' and 'cyclonic'

**Cyclonic** means winds circulate around a low-pressure centre, anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere (kegy209 Table 9.2). **Shallow**, in NCERT's wording, tells you the systems are weak compared with tropical cyclones: NCERT calls the rainfall they bring 'meagre'. They are **temperate cyclones** and NCERT names them 'weak temperate cyclones' (kegy104 pp7 and 12).

## Why from the west, and why in winter

- **Direction.** Extra-tropical cyclones move from west to east (kegy209 p8), carried by the westerlies. Day 19 lesson GS-104 explains the westerlies as the surface winds of the middle-latitude cell, and module 3 explained the westerly jet above them.
- **Season.** The belts move south in the northern winter (kegy209 p3). Leong notes that Mediterranean-type regions come under the westerlies only in winter for the same reason and so receive winter rain (PDF p196). The standard explanation (verify) is that the same southward shift puts the westerlies and the subtropical westerly jet far enough south to carry Mediterranean depressions to the north-west of India in winter; the extracts do not state this as a sentence.
- **Steering.** The westerly jet over the north Indian plain (module 5) is the upper-air guide (standard knowledge, verify).

## What else comes from the north-west

NCERT adds that around February 'cold winds coming from the Caspian Sea and Turkmenistan bring cold wave along with frost and fog over the north-western parts of India' (kegy104 pp6-7). The same north-west gateway admits both the cold air and the depressions. Two things to keep apart: the cold wave is a cold-air flow; the disturbance is a rain-bearing depression.

## How a disturbance works (link to fronts)

An extra-tropical cyclone has a warm sector wedged between cold air, a **warm front** with a sequence of clouds ahead of it and a **cold front** that lifts the warm air and builds cumulus (kegy209 p7). A western disturbance is a weaker relative of this picture, so clouds first thicken ahead of it, and rain or snow follows as the front passes (standard reasoning; verify). A later lesson (GS-107, air masses, fronts and cyclones) deals with fronts in detail.

## Frequency and timing (standard knowledge, verify)

Standard sources say the disturbances arrive between roughly December and March, a few in a month, and reach the plains and mountains in turn. Do not quote counts or dates without checking.

### 7. Core: what western disturbances do, and the other winter rain of India

*Objective:* State the effects NCERT gives for the winter depressions, apply the west-to-east and north-to-south pattern with the check on the book's own numbers, and separate western disturbances from the other winter rain sources.

## What NCERT says (kegy104 pp7 and 12)

- **Rain** in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and western Uttar Pradesh from weak temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean. The amount is meagre, but it is 'highly beneficial for rabi crops'.
- **Snow** in the lower Himalayas. 'It is this snow that sustains the flow of water in the Himalayan rivers during the summer months.'
- **Pattern.** Precipitation goes on decreasing **from west to east** in the plains and **from north to south** in the mountains.
- **Figures.** The average winter rainfall in Delhi is around 53 mm; in Punjab and Bihar it remains between 25 mm and 18 mm respectively.

## Testing the pattern against the book's numbers

The numbers should fall from west to east if the sentence is applied to places: Punjab is west of Delhi, and Delhi is west of Bihar. But the figures are Punjab 25 mm, Delhi 53 mm and Bihar 18 mm: Delhi, in the middle, has the **most**. Delhi to Bihar falls (53 to 18, a factor of about 3), yet Punjab to Delhi rises (25 to 53, a factor of about 2). So the three figures do **not** fall steadily from west to east. They may come from different stations or averaging periods; the lesson marks this **check**, not 'slip'. In answers, use the sentence about the broad pattern and quote at most one figure, saying 'NCERT gives about 53 mm for Delhi'.

## Why the benefit is large even if the rain is small

Winter is the dry season in the north-west, and the **rabi** crops grow on stored soil moisture and irrigation. A small, timely rain at the right stage adds moisture without raising the cost of irrigation (standard reasoning, verify). Snow in the mountains is a store of water that is released in the summer, which is when the rivers of the plains need it most. That is the second benefit NCERT names.

## The other side of the ledger (standard knowledge; verify)

NCERT gives only the beneficial effects. Standard sources add that a strong disturbance can bring heavy snow and avalanches in the mountains, hailstorms and untimely rain that damage standing rabi crops near harvest, fog and cold-wave conditions after the system passes, and disruption of travel. In a Mains answer give these as 'adverse effects reported (verify)' beside the sourced benefits. Western disturbances also act outside the winter core: pre-monsoon hail and, as reported, interaction with the monsoon that can intensify rain in the western Himalaya (verify).

## Do not confuse the winter rain sources of India

| Source | Where (NCERT) | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Western disturbance** (weak temperate cyclones) | Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, western UP; snow in the lower Himalayas | Winter |
| **Other occasional winter rain** | Central India and northern parts of the southern peninsula | Winter, occasionally |
| **Winter rain in the north-east** | Arunachal Pradesh and Assam, between 25 and 50 mm; NCERT names no cause | Winter |
| **North-east monsoon** crossing the Bay of Bengal | Tamil Nadu coast, southern Andhra Pradesh, south-east Karnataka and south-east Kerala | October-November |
| **Depressions from the Andaman Sea** | East-coast deltas of the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri; the Coromandel coast | Retreating monsoon, October-November |

The Tamil Nadu coast and the east-coast deltas do **not** get their winter rain from western disturbances.

### 8. Master: western disturbances and tropical cyclones compared, and the eye trap

*Objective:* Compare extra-tropical (temperate) and tropical cyclones feature by feature using NCERT, and explain why the eye of a tropical cyclone is not cooler than its surroundings.

## Where the comparison comes from

NCERT kegy209 (PDF pp7-8) compares **extra-tropical cyclones**, the systems of the middle and high latitudes, with **tropical cyclones**. A western disturbance belongs to the first family: it moves from west to east, gathers moisture along the way and is far weaker than the second.

## Feature by feature

| Feature | Extra-tropical cyclone (the family of western disturbances) | Tropical cyclone |
|---|---|---|
| Where formed | Mid and high latitudes beyond the tropics, along the polar front | Over warm tropical oceans only (sea surface warmer than 27 degrees C) |
| Over land or sea | Can originate over land and sea | Originates only over the seas; dissipates on reaching land |
| Frontal system | A clear frontal system with warm and cold fronts | No front |
| Area affected | Much larger | Smaller |
| Wind velocity | Lower | Much higher; up to about 250 km per hour in the eye wall |
| Movement | West to east | East to west (NCERT's general rule; many Bay of Bengal storms move north-west and some recurve north-east, verify) |
| Eye | None described by NCERT | A calm eye with subsiding air, ringed by the eye wall of torrential rain |
| Destructiveness | Less | More |

On sizes, the books disagree and their numbers are not comparable: NCERT gives 150-250 km for the circulating system of a mature tropical cyclone and 600-1,200 km for the storm over the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea; Leong gives 80-320 km for a typhoon and 240-3,200 km for a temperate depression (PDF pp199 and 201). Use the qualitative ordering, not the numbers.

## Why India's winter rain is not 'cyclonic' in the tropical sense

The depressions of the retreating monsoon from the Andaman Sea are tropical (kegy104 p11) and are 'very destructive' on the deltas. The depressions of winter from the west are extra-tropical and weak. The name 'cyclone' in NCERT therefore needs its adjective: look for **temperate** or **tropical**.

## The eye trap, step by step (the 2020 item)

NCERT describes the eye as a calm region with **subsiding air** (kegy209 p8). Air that sinks is compressed and warms; NCERT uses the same idea for dry air descending a mountain slope (adiabatic warming, kegy209 p6). The expected result is that the eye is **warmer** than its surroundings, not colder. The extract gives no temperature difference, and the standard-knowledge reading (a warm core, verify) rejects 'about 10 degrees cooler'.

## The Mediterranean connection (Leong)

The Mediterranean climate's winter rain arrives when the westerlies and their depressions shift towards the sub-tropics (PDF p196). The same swing explains why a depression born over the east Mediterranean can later reach India. The argument is a reasoned link rather than a book sentence, so state it with 'because the wind belts shift south in winter'.

## Anticlockwise or clockwise?

Around a low in the northern hemisphere the winds circulate anticlockwise (kegy209 Table 9.2). A western disturbance, being a northern-hemisphere low, has anticlockwise circulation. A cyclone of the southern hemisphere circulates clockwise.

### Last-week summary

- NCERT names two jets only: the westerly jet over the north Indian plain, south of the Himalayas, in winter; the easterly jet along 15 N after it withdraws, held responsible for the burst.
- Onset: sun over the Tropic of Cancer in April-May, intense low in the north-west, ITCZ north; Kerala about 1 June, Mumbai and Kolkata 10-13 June, all India by mid-July.
- Shallow cyclonic depressions (weak temperate cyclones) from the east Mediterranean via West Asia, Iran, Afghanistan, Pakistan; moisture from the Caspian (north) and Persian Gulf (south).
- Meagre rain in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, western UP, good for rabi; snow in the lower Himalaya feeds summer rivers; decreases west to east in plains, north to south in mountains. February cold wave from the Caspian and Turkmenistan.
- Extra-tropical: fronts, land or sea, west to east, larger, weaker, no eye. Tropical: warm seas only, no fronts, east to west, eye. Tamil Nadu's Oct-Nov rain: north-east monsoon and Andaman Sea depressions.
