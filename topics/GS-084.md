## GS-084: Hindustani and Carnatic music basics

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Hindustani and Carnatic music basics and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Ancient foundations

NCERT divides Indian music into ancient (to about 1200 CE), medieval (to 1800) and modern periods. Vedic hymns were chanted, and the Samaveda set selected Rigvedic verses to svaras and metre. Panini's Shiksha adds two more pitches and explains how seven notes arise from the three Vedic ones. The epics mention terms such as svara, laya, tala, murchana and jati and instruments like the vina, bheri and vallaki. The Natyashastra treats shruti, grama, jati and rasa and gives the first classification of instruments into four classes. Music was transmitted orally through the guru-shishya parampara, the forerunner of the later gharana.

### Medieval divergence

Sharangadeva's thirteenth-century Sangita Ratnakara described drums and rhythm instruments in detail. As Persian music and culture entered the north from the late twelfth century, temple and court (darbari) music both developed there, while the southern classical tradition stayed anchored in temples. Dhrupad, dhamar, khayal and tarana grew out of the older prabandha style, and instrumental styles such as Masitkhani and Razakhani appeared. Amir Khusro is credited with forms like qawwali and with raga Yaman; Tansen, Swami Haridas, Baiju Bavra and Raja Man Singh Tomar shaped Hindustani music. Bhakti poets from Jayadeva to Meerabai and Tulsidas deeply influenced practice.

### Gharanas and the modern turn

Royal patronage encouraged artists to refine their forms, and lineages of practice crystallised into gharanas — belonging defined by descent or by adopting a particular style, each with its own mannerisms, sustained by the guru-shishya bond. Pandit Somnath's Raga Vivodha described a raga's mood and tonal structure; poetic descriptions of ragas led to dhyana verses later painted as ragamala. Instruments such as the sitar and tabla developed. In the modern period, European scholars such as William Jones wrote on Hindustani music, and Muhammad Raza's Naghmat-e-Asafi (1813) adopted Bilawal as the basic scale. Bhatkhande and Paluskar moved music into formal education with notation and institutions.
