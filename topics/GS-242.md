## GS-242: Liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### The 1991 crisis and response

NCERT traces the crisis to inefficient fiscal management in the 1980s: spending persistently exceeded revenue, borrowed foreign exchange was sometimes used for consumption, and exports lagged imports. By 1991 reserves could not cover two weeks of imports or interest owed abroad, and prices of essentials were rising. India obtained a $7 billion loan from the World Bank and IMF, which expected liberalisation, a smaller role for government and freer trade. The New Economic Policy combined two kinds of measures. Stabilisation measures were short-term, aimed at restoring reserves and curbing inflation. Structural reforms were long-term, aimed at efficiency and international competitiveness by removing rigidities across the economy.

### What each 'L', 'P' and 'G' changed

Liberalisation deregulated industry by ending most licensing, opening reserved sectors and dereserving many small-scale products, and let markets set most prices. Financial reforms aimed to turn the RBI from regulator into facilitator, allowed private and foreign banks, and admitted foreign institutional investors. Tax reform lowered income and corporate tax rates to improve compliance and later led to GST. The rupee was devalued and its value increasingly market-determined, while trade reform dismantled quantitative restrictions and cut tariffs. Privatisation meant shedding government ownership or management, mainly through disinvestment, while profitable enterprises gained autonomy as Maharatnas, Navratnas and Miniratnas. Globalisation integrated India with the world, visible in outsourcing and WTO membership.

### A balanced appraisal

NCERT credits reforms with faster GDP growth led by services, foreign investment growing to about US$23 billion in 2022-23 from roughly US$100 million in 1990-91, and reserves climbing to about US$646 billion in 2023-24, along with strong exports of auto parts, pharmaceuticals, engineering goods and software. Critics point to growth without enough jobs, falling public investment in agricultural infrastructure, higher input costs after subsidy cuts, import competition hurting small industry, and disinvestment proceeds used to plug deficits while assets were allegedly undervalued. Tariff cuts and tax incentives for foreign investors also limited revenue for welfare. A Mains answer should present both sides and link outcomes to sectors.

### Worked example

Classify: (a) rupee devaluation 1991 — stabilisation; (b) abolition of industrial licensing — structural/liberalisation; (c) sale of PSU equity to the public — privatisation via disinvestment; (d) removal of QRs from April 2001 — liberalisation of trade/globalisation. Then write one gain and one criticism for each.

### Recall

- - **q:** Distinguish stabilisation from structural reform measures.
  - **a:** Stabilisation: short-term steps to fix the balance of payments and inflation. Structural: long-term steps to improve efficiency and competitiveness by removing rigidities.
- - **q:** Which activities remain reserved for the public sector?
  - **a:** A part of atomic energy generation and some core activities in railway transport.
- - **q:** When were QRs on manufactured consumer goods and agricultural imports fully removed?
  - **a:** April 2001.

### Common trap

Treating disinvestment and privatisation as identical in every case: disinvestment is the sale of part of the equity of public enterprises, while privatisation can also mean transfer of ownership or management.
