## GS-076: Prehistoric rock paintings

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Prehistoric rock paintings and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Discovery and spread of sites

India's first recorded rock-painting discovery came in 1867–68 through Archibold Carlleyle, earlier than Altamira in Spain. Painted shelters are known from Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Bihar and the Kumaon hills. At Lakhudiyar ('one lakh caves') beside the Suyal river, motifs fall into humans drawn as sticks, animals such as a long-snouted animal, a fox and a many-legged lizard, and geometric patterns; a hand-in-hand dance is a notable scene. In Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, Neolithic painters used granite surfaces at Kupgallu, Piklihal and Tekkalkota, painting bulls, elephants, sambhars and stylised humans in white and red ochre. The richest concentration lies in the Vindhyas and their Kaimur extensions, above all Bhimbetka.

### Bhimbetka phases and themes

Bhimbetka's shelters were classified by style, technique and superimposition into seven periods. Upper Palaeolithic work shows large linear animals such as bison, tigers and rhinos in green and dark red, with green figures of dancers and red figures of hunters. The Mesolithic phase has the largest number of paintings, but they are smaller: group hunts with barbed spears, bows and traps, masked or head-dressed men, women grinding food, children at play, honey collection and community dance. Animals are drawn naturalistically while humans are stylised. Some shelters carry up to twenty overlapping layers, which may reflect sacredness, dissatisfaction or use by successive generations.

### Materials and meaning

Painters ground rocks and minerals into powder and probably mixed it with water and a binder such as animal fat, gum or resin. Haematite gave red, chalcedony gave green and limestone likely gave white; white and red were the favourite colours. Their survival is attributed to a chemical reaction with oxides on the rock surface. Some paintings sit in living shelters, others in places that were perhaps ritually important, and some are placed high near ceilings, possibly to be seen from afar. Scenes of injured hunters around a bison or dancing near a dying animal suggest the images may have expressed a sense of power over animals, comparable to rites still practised by some communities.
