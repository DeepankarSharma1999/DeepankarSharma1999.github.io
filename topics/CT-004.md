### Bridge: If-then, only if, necessary and sufficient

A **condition** links two things: 'if A then B'. Four relatives of that sentence look alike, and only one of them says the same thing.

| Form | Example | Same claim as 'if A then B'? |
|---|---|---|
| Original | If it rains, the match is cancelled | - |
| Converse (swap) | If the match is cancelled, it rained | No: it may have been cancelled for another reason |
| Contrapositive (swap and negate) | If the match was not cancelled, it did not rain | Yes: it says the same thing in reverse |
| Inverse (negate only) | If it does not rain, the match is not cancelled | No: it may be cancelled for another reason |

**Sufficient** means 'enough on its own': rain is sufficient for cancellation. **Necessary** means 'cannot do without': fees paid is necessary for a hall ticket if the rule says 'a hall ticket is issued only if fees are paid'. 'Only if' always marks the necessary condition. It does not promise that meeting the condition is enough: a student who paid fees may still lack another document.

Two habits follow. When a passage states 'only if X', you may conclude that anyone who got the benefit satisfied X, but you may not conclude that everyone who satisfied X got the benefit. When a passage says 'if X then Y', you may conclude 'no Y, therefore no X' but you may not conclude 'Y, therefore X'. Many 'it follows that' traps in this lesson are the converse in disguise.

### Bridge: Percent, fraction and 'most': reading numbers in a passage

Passages often give a number and then ask what can be concluded. Four rules cover the cases in this lesson.

1. **Always find the base.** '60 per cent of the 80 applicants were women' means 0.6 x 80 = 48 women and 32 men. The same 60 per cent of a different group is a different number.
2. **A share is not a count.** 'The share of young patients rose' does not say that their number rose, and it does not say which other group's share fell.
3. **'More than half' has a floor.** In a group of 120, more than half means at least 61 (60 is exactly half). 'Most' is read as more than half in CSAT passages, as CT-002-B2 taught.
4. **Percentage points are not percent.** A rise from 20 per cent to 30 per cent is 10 percentage points. Relative to the 20 per cent it is a 50 per cent increase, because 30 divided by 20 is 1.5.

A passage may also give a share of a group and then be silent about the other group: 'most patients were under 30' says nothing about the chance that a young person falls ill, because the chance needs the number of young people in the whole district. A conclusion about a chance, a risk or a likelihood needs a count of those who did *not* fall ill as well as those who did.

## Where this lesson sits

## A one-minute illustration

> A school reports that 80 of its 100 Class X students passed a mock test, and that all 20 who failed had missed more than a week of classes.

Safe: most students passed; every student who failed had missed more than a week. Unsafe: missing classes *caused* the failures (side by side; module 4), or any student who misses a week fails (a converse, CT-004-B1).

### 2. The strength ladder: a conclusion may not outrun its evidence

*Objective:* Place evidence words and conclusion words on a four-rung ladder and apply the weakest-link and match rules.

## A conclusion has a size

CT-002 module 3 gave the five degrees and CT-002-B2 explained all, some and most. The rule that makes them usable: **a conclusion may be no stronger than the evidence behind it.** A passage that says 'may' cannot give 'will'.

| Rung | Evidence words | Conclusion words |
|---|---|---|
| Top | proves, establishes, is, all, always | must, certainly, all, always |
| Upper | shows, indicates, most, often | probably, most, generally |
| Middle | suggests, some, sometimes | may, could, some, sometimes |
| Lower | is consistent with, a few, rarely | might, a few, rarely |

The ladder is a Mission 90 teaching scale, not an official one; its use is to compare a passage's words with an option's words. Negative universals (none, never, cannot, rules out) are Top-strength claims pointing the other way: the passage must rule the thing out.

**Rule 1, weakest link.** A conclusion from two sentences is no stronger than the weaker of them, and often weaker; two 'most' statements chained through a middle group give no conclusion at all. 'Most farmers use pumps' and 'pumps often fail' support at most 'some pump-using farmers sometimes face failure', not 'farmers always face failure'.

**Rule 2, match, do not merely minimise.** A weaker option is safe only if it still says the same thing. 'Some' under 'most' is fine; 'incomes grew slowly' beside 'incomes were unchanged' is a different claim (some growth versus none).

**Rule 3, the passage sets the rung.** Where the author writes 'likely', an option with 'definitely' fails however sensible it sounds; where the author writes 'must', an option with 'may' is not false, only weaker, and can lose to a better match.

## Worked contrast

> Surveys show that most commuters in the city say they would switch to trains if fares were lower.

'Commuters will switch to trains if fares fall' is too strong ('will', and all commuters). 'Some commuters might ride more' is safe but weak. 'A fare cut would probably raise train use' matches the rung: 'most' gives probably, and survey answers are intentions (and the step from stated intention to behaviour holds only if the intentions hold), so 'certainly' is not earned.

### 3. Over-generalisation and the group test

*Objective:* Spot an option that stretches a small group into a larger one, using the group test and its edge cases.

## What over-generalisation is

An **over-generalisation** stretches a claim about a small group (one village, one trial, one decade) into a claim about a larger one (all villages, everyone, always). The giveaways are 'all', 'every', 'any', 'in general'. The stretch can also be silent: 'farmers who sow early tend to earn more', drawn from one farm's good year, sounds soft but moves from one farm to a whole type.

## The group test

1. Mark the group the passage talks about (the **evidence group**).
2. Mark the group the option talks about (the **claim group**).
3. If the claim group is larger than, or different from, the evidence group, the option over-generalises, unless the passage itself widens the group.

Four stretches to watch:

- **Part to whole**: from digital trade to 'globalisation' (2019 Q22, pyqNotes).
- **Sample to population**: from 300 households in three villages to 'all farmers'.
- **One case to a rule**: from one farmer's year to 'all farmers'. A real item can be a close call when the passage itself widens the group (2026 Q54 statement 3; see edge case 3).
- **Some to only**: from 'small farms produce 41 per cent of the country's grains' to 'only small farmers can ensure food security' (2019 Q24, pyqNotes).

## Edge cases that keep the rule honest

1. **Narrowing is safe.** From 'most of the 300 households' to 'some households' stays inside the evidence group.
2. **A narrower claim can still be wrong.** A message stem asks for the point of the whole passage, not one example (CT-001).
3. **The passage may itself generalise.** If it says a rule holds for all States, an option saying so is not a stretch. Test against the author's group, not your own sense of what is reasonable.
4. **A soft word does not rescue a stretch.** 'Tends to' attached to a bigger group is still a bigger group.

### 4. Causal slips and the link test

*Objective:* Separate a cause the passage states from facts it merely places side by side, and name the six causal slips.

## Side by side is not 'because'

CT-002 module 5 named one trap (cause from sequence or correlation); here it is split into six forms.

**The link test.** Does the passage itself state the link ('because', 'leads to', 'results in', 'is driven by'), or does it only place two facts side by side ('also', 'meanwhile', 'since then', 'in the same year')? If the link is not on the page, a conclusion saying 'caused' goes beyond the passage.

## Six causal slips

| Slip | What the option does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Post hoc / correlation | After X, or alongside X, came Y, so X caused Y | Accidents fell after crossings were painted, or wells fell while rice spread, so that caused it |
| Single cause | 'solely', 'the only reason' | Falling wells are 'solely' due to rice |
| Reverse cause | Swaps cause and effect | Hospitals are full of ill people, so hospitals cause illness |
| Hidden third factor | A third thing may drive both | Phone owners have bigger farms; farm size may explain results |
| Necessary taken as sufficient | 'is needed' becomes 'guarantees' | 'Funds are needed' becomes 'funds will eliminate poverty' |
| Cause taken as remedy | 'who caused it' becomes 'who must fix it' | The poor contributed little to warming, so they have no role in mitigation |

## When the passage does state the cause, use it

In 2018 Q8 the author says that globalising food production makes food harder to regulate and trace, raising consumers' vulnerability. A conclusion that more options for food bring more risk takes one paraphrase step ('world on our plates' read as 'more options for food') and then rests on a link the passage states. Distrust only the link that is not on the page.

## The inserted 'therefore'

An option may attach a reason the passage never gives. In 2026 Q63 followers adopt the new technology and close the income gap; a statement that leaders stagnate and *therefore* followers close it adds a cause.

### 5. Combining statements: chains, overlap, shares and relationships

*Objective:* Combine two or more given statements validly and classify the relationship between numbered statements.

## Putting two sentences together

Four combinations occur (bridges CT-004-B1 and CT-004-B2 give the prerequisites).

1. **Chain.** 'If A then B' and 'if B then C' give 'if A then C', at the weaker rung. A chain breaks if a link is read backwards or a property moves: 'schools with libraries score higher' and 'libraries are costly' do not give 'schools that score higher are costly' (a higher score does not imply a library, and 'costly' belongs to the library, not the school).
2. **Overlap by 'more than half'.** If more than half of a group does X and more than half does Y, at least one member does both. For 120 people, 61 + 61 = 122, which is 2 more than 120, so at least 2 do both. The rule fails for exactly half (60 + 60 can be separate) and for 'some' ('some do X, some do Y' allows no overlap).
3. **Shares versus counts.** '70 per cent of the patients were young' does not give the share of young people who fall ill; that needs the population of each age group. A rise in one share does not force a fall in a particular other share.
4. **Necessary and sufficient.** 'Only if' makes a condition necessary, never sufficient.

## Relationships between statements

In 2026 Q71 three statements are followed by 'relationships'. Use five labels: **supports**; **could be a reason for** (only plausible, since 'could' is a low rung); **counterpoint** (qualifies without contradicting); **contradicts**; **unconnected**. 'Unconnected' is a strong claim: statements on one subject that use each other's terms are rarely unconnected. A counterpoint to the *form* of a cause (extreme activism) is not an attack on the cause.

## Code elimination

Settle the statement you are surest about first. Every code containing a statement you know is wrong falls away, and every code omitting one you know is right falls away (CT-002 module 4). Never choose by the look of the code.

### 6. What each stem allows, and the Group-Strength-Link check

*Objective:* State what a conclusion may and may not do under each stem family, and run the Group-Strength-Link check on every option.

## What each stem allows

| Stem | The conclusion may... | It may not... |
|---|---|---|
| 'corollary' | follow directly, with soft wording, or draw a mild 'should' or 'necessary' step that the passage's own concern points to (as the 2017 Q65, 2019 Q22 and 2019 Q24 options do) | add a remedy the passage's reasoning does not point to, or a stronger word |
| 'message', 'crux' | sum up the whole passage | rest on one example or an extreme |
| 'implication', 'plausible' | take one safe step | need the world's help |
| 'suggestion', 'action', 'solution' | recommend the step that fits the problem as the passage states it; name a tool only if it answers that problem's shape | name a tool that answers a different problem (moving water across space) or carries upgrades ('huge', 'across the country') |
| 'conclusions ... correct' | be judged statement by statement (module 2) | be chosen by the pattern of the code |

CT-002 module 4 defines a corollary strictly, but the verified UPSC corollary items do offer normative options: a mild step the passage's concern points to is allowed; a new remedy is not.

In practical stems the passage defines a problem and the answer is the step that fits its shape. In 2022 Q73 the problem is timing (about 75 per cent of rain in four months), so the fitting step carries water across time; (d), storage and recharge, against (b), a crop-pattern change that cuts dry-season demand, is the closer pair, and the discriminators are fit to timing and the words 'huge' and 'across the country'. In 2023 Q1 the problem is how to divide water, so the step is a rule for dividing, not more supply. In 2021 Q43 the passage calls resolution and recapitalisation 'only a part', so a step that is 'alone' enough does not fit. The shape rule is a Mission 90 reading, not an official rule.

## The Group-Strength-Link check

Run three questions on every option.

- **G (group)**: same group, place and time as the passage? (module 3)
- **S (strength)**: same rung as the passage's own words? (module 2)
- **L (link and direction)**: is every link in the option on the page and running the passage's way, with nothing the passage does not say added? (module 4)

An option that fails one is out. G-S-L is CT-003's scope-degree-direction for conclusions (and maps onto CT-002's PASS check, module 5): G is scope, S is degree, L covers direction, cause and added content. If two survive, choose the one that matches the passage's rung and group, not the one that merely claims less.

### 7. Worked analyses of four official questions

*Objective:* Apply the strength ladder, group test, link test and G-S-L check, with code elimination, to four verified official questions. Analysis only: no official key is asserted.

Try each question from the official paper first. These analyses name the failure in each option; they are not an answer key. The other twelve items are in the pyqNotes.

**1. 2017 Q65 (grain and hunger).** Record output and stocks sit beside a quarter of the world's undernourished, so the tension is about access, not output. 'Automatically eliminate' (b) and 'eliminate' (d) fail on S; (c) denies something the passage never says. Option (a), a better farm-to-consumer chain, contains 'necessary', itself a strength word, yet it survives S because it is the only option without an outcome promise, and it is the mild step the passage's concern points to (module 6).

**2. 2021 Q42 (driverless cars).** Statements 1 ('too much globalisation... any country') and 4 ('every country should...') widen to every country and prescribe (G). Codes (a), (b) and (d) all contain statement 1, so once statement 1 is rejected only one code survives and statement 2 need not be settled.

**3. 2026 Q53 (customer-service AI).** Statement 3 ('inevitably leads to stressful outcomes') reverses the passage (effects 'may be salutary'): a reversal, caught by L as direction. Once it is rejected, the two codes left both contain statement 2, so the item turns on statement 1 alone: test 'invariably' against the passage's 'previous waves ... stoked concerns'. A close call.

**4. 2026 Q71 (relationships).** S3 mentions climate change deniers (whom S2's missing consensus touches) and activism (S1's subject), so 'unconnected' fails; every code containing statement 2 then falls away, leaving '3 only' whatever you decide on statement 1. Statement 3 uses 'could be', a low rung.

### Notes: Terms in one place

- Conclusion (in this lesson): the statement a careful reader may safely end with after reading only the passage; it is not the same as the author's own conclusion, which is the claim the author argues for (CT-003 module 4).
- Corollary: a direct consequence that follows from the passage with little added; the strictest conclusion stem in CT-002 module 2, although the verified corollary items also offer mild 'should' or 'necessary' options (module 6).
- Message: the point of the whole passage (CT-001); a conclusion about one example is not the message.
- Over-generalisation: stretching a claim about a small group into a claim about a larger one.
- Causal slip: treating facts placed side by side, or a necessary condition, as a stated cause.
- Converse: the swapped statement ('if B then A' from 'if A then B'); it is a new claim and needs its own support.
- Contrapositive: the swapped and negated statement ('if not B then not A'); it says the same as the original.
- Necessary condition: cannot be done without; 'only if' marks it. Sufficient condition: enough on its own.

### Notes: The strength ladder (Mission 90 teaching scale)

- A conclusion may be no stronger than the passage's own words; compare rungs before comparing content.
- Weakest-link rule: a conclusion from two sentences is no stronger than the weaker of them, and often weaker; two 'most' statements chained through a middle group give no conclusion. Match rule: a weaker option is safe only if it still says the same thing.
- Negative universals (none, never, cannot, rules out) are Top-strength claims pointing the other way; the passage must rule the thing out.

### Notes: Over-generalisation: the group test

- 1. Mark the evidence group (whom or what the passage talks about).
- 2. Mark the claim group (whom or what the option talks about).
- 3. If the claim group is larger or different, the option over-generalises unless the passage itself widens the group.
- Four stretches: part to whole; sample to population; one case to a rule; some to only.
- Edge cases: narrowing is safe; a narrower claim can still miss the message; a passage may itself generalise; a soft word ('tends to') does not rescue a stretch.

### Notes: Six causal slips and the link test

- Link test: is the cause stated on the page ('because', 'leads to'), or only placed side by side ('also', 'in the same year')?

### Notes: Combining statements

- Chain: 'if A then B' and 'if B then C' give 'if A then C', at the weaker rung; it breaks if the middle term shifts.
- Overlap: more than half do X and more than half do Y means at least one does both. For N people the smallest overlap is 2 x (floor(N/2) + 1) - N. For N = 120 it is 2 x 61 - 120 = 2. It fails for exactly half and for 'some'.
- Shares versus counts: a share does not give a count, a chance or a risk without the base.
- Percentage points versus percent: 20 per cent to 30 per cent is 10 points, a 50 per cent relative rise.
- Necessary versus sufficient: 'only if' gives necessary, never sufficient. Converse is not the same claim; contrapositive is.
- Relationship labels for numbered statements: supports, could be a reason for, counterpoint, contradicts, unconnected.

### Notes: What each stem allows

- Practical stems allow a recommendation, but only the step that fits the problem's shape as the passage states it (a Mission 90 reading of 2021 Q43, 2022 Q73 and 2023 Q1).
- CT-002 module 4 defines a corollary strictly, but the verified corollary items (2017 Q65, 2019 Q22, 2019 Q24) offer normative options.

### Notes: The Group-Strength-Link check

- G (group): same group, place and time as the passage?
- S (strength): same rung as the passage's own words?
- L (link and direction): is every link in the option on the page and running the passage's way, with nothing the passage does not say added?
- An option that fails any one is out; if two survive, choose the one that matches the passage's rung and group, not the one that merely claims less.
- G-S-L is CT-003's scope-degree-direction rewritten for conclusions (and maps onto CT-002's PASS check): G is scope, S is degree, L covers direction, cause and added content.

### Notes: Examples of passage-to-conclusion at the right strength

- Passage: 350 of 500 patients were under 30. Safe: most patients were under 30 (70 per cent). Unsafe: young people are more likely to catch the disease (no population base).
- Passage: fishermen in one village say the devices helped. Safe: the fishermen of that village report help. Unsafe: technology raises incomes in every community.
- Passage: accidents fell after crossings were painted, in the same year as a drink-driving drive. Safe: accidents fell after the painting. Unsafe: the crossings caused the fall.
- Passage: 'only if income is below the limit'. Safe: the awardee's income was below the limit. Unsafe: everyone below the limit gets the award.

### Notes: Exceptions and edge cases

- A strong word is not automatically wrong: 'all' or 'only' in the passage can license the same word in the option (CT-001, CT-002 and CT-003 all say so).
- A weaker option is not automatically right: 'slow growth' is not a softer form of 'no growth'.
- 'Could be a reason' needs only plausibility, because 'could' is a low rung.
- 'Unconnected' is a strong claim; two statements on one subject that use each other's terms are rarely unconnected.
- A causal conclusion is acceptable when the passage states the link (2018 Q8 passage on traceability).
- Practical stems allow a recommendation; plain inference stems do not (CT-002 module 4).

  - Counterpoint versus contradiction; 'could be a reason' versus 'is the reason'.
  - A weaker option versus a different option.
- **formulae:**
  - Net marks = 2.5 x (correct) - (5/6) x (wrong)
  - Smallest overlap of two 'more than half' groups in N people = 2 x (floor(N/2) + 1) - N
  - EV of a guess among k options = 2.5 / k - (5/6) x (1 - 1/k); zero at k = 4
- **traps:**
  - Choosing the option that is true or interesting rather than the one the passage supports at that strength.
  - Treating 'more likely' or 'tends to' as if a bigger group were allowed.
  - Reading 'after' as 'because'.
  - Taking the converse of an 'only if' rule.
  - Choosing a weaker option that is really a different claim.
  - Choosing a code by its pattern ('Both', 'Neither') instead of statement by statement.
- **blankPrompts:**
  - - **q:** A conclusion may be no ____ than the passage's own words.
    - **a:** stronger
  - - **q:** A conclusion built from two sentences is no stronger than the ____ of the two sentences.
    - **a:** weaker
  - - **q:** The group test compares the evidence group with the ____ group.
    - **a:** claim
  - - **q:** The link test asks whether the cause is stated on the page or only placed ____.
    - **a:** side by side
  - - **q:** 'Only if' marks a ____ condition, never a sufficient one.
    - **a:** necessary
  - - **q:** More than half of 120 people is at least ____ people.
    - **a:** 61
  - - **q:** The G-S-L check stands for group, ____, link.
    - **a:** strength
  - - **q:** A rise from 20 per cent to 30 per cent is 10 ____ points.
    - **a:** percentage
- **shortQuestions:**
  - - **q:** What does the weakest-link rule say?
    - **a:** A conclusion built from two sentences is no stronger than the weaker of the two.
  - - **q:** Name the four stretches that over-generalise.
    - **a:** Part to whole; sample to population; one case to a rule; some to only.
  - - **q:** Name the six causal slips.
    - **a:** Post hoc or correlation, single cause, reverse cause, hidden third factor, necessary taken as sufficient, cause taken as remedy.
  - - **q:** How do you find the smallest overlap of two 'more than half' groups?
    - **a:** Add the two minimum group sizes and subtract the total: for 120 people, 61 + 61 - 120 = 2.
  - - **q:** What may a practical stem allow that a corollary stem does not?
    - **a:** A recommendation that fits the problem as the passage states it; a corollary may offer only a mild step the passage's own concern points to.
  - - **q:** Why can a weaker option still be wrong?
    - **a:** If it says a different thing (for example slow growth against no growth) it is not a softer version of the passage's claim.
- **mnemonics:**
  - G-S-L: Group, Strength, Link.
  - Side by side is not because.

- **conceptsTested:**
  - The most logical corollary or message supported by a passage, at the passage's strength.
  - Practical step, solution or action that fits the problem the passage states.
  - Numbered conclusions judged against quantifiers, chains and ordered facts.
  - Relationships between statements (reason, counterpoint, unconnected).
- **reasoningRequired:**
  - Place the passage's key sentence on the strength ladder before reading the options.
  - Apply the group test and the link test to every option.
  - Combine statements only by valid chains, overlaps and necessary or sufficient conditions.
  - Choose the option that matches the passage's group and rung, not simply the weakest or the most interesting.
- **examinerTraps:**
  - Strength upgrades ('automatically', 'eliminate', 'only', 'inevitably').
  - Stretching a part into a whole or one case into a rule.
  - Cause from side-by-side facts, or an inserted 'therefore'.
  - Prescriptions, or tools that answer a different problem from the one the passage states.
  - Ranking or comparing groups the passage does not compare.
  - A weaker-sounding option that is a different claim.
- **distractorAnalysis:**
  - In the 11 single-answer items, wrong options are typically drawn from these types: one extreme ('only', 'eliminate', 'stopped immediately'), one prescription or named tool, one reversal and one comparison the passage does not make.
  - The attractive wrong option is usually something a well-read person agrees with; the passage must still support it at that strength.
  - In coded items, an option pairing one supported statement with one unsupported statement is a common decoy.
- **prelimsElimination:**
  - Predict the rung and the group of the passage before looking at options.
  - Remove options by a named G, S or L failure; remove coded options by the statements you are sure of.
  - Guess only after a named elimination and an honest chance above 25 per cent.
- **commonMistakes:**
  - Choosing a true but unsupported option.
  - Taking the converse of an 'only if' rule.
  - Using the weakest option by default instead of matching the rung.
  - Choosing 'Both' or 'Neither' by pattern.
  - Treating a share as a count.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** State in one sentence the rule that links a conclusion to the passage's own words.
    - **a:** A conclusion may be no stronger than the evidence words of the passage; 'may' cannot give 'will'.
  - - **q:** What are the evidence group and the claim group, and when does an option over-generalise?
    - **a:** The evidence group is whom the passage talks about; the claim group is whom the option talks about. If the claim group is larger or different, the option over-generalises unless the passage widens the group.
  - - **q:** What does the link test ask?
    - **a:** Whether the passage states the cause ('because', 'leads to') or only places two facts side by side.
  - - **q:** In 'a certificate is issued only if fees are paid', which is the necessary condition?
    - **a:** Fees paid. 'Only if' marks the necessary condition; it is not sufficient.
  - - **q:** What are the three letters of the check run on every option?
    - **a:** G (group), S (strength), L (link).
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is 'slow growth' not a safe, softer version of the passage's 'income was unchanged'?
    - **a:** It says a different thing: some growth against none. A weaker option is safe only if it keeps the same claim at a lower strength.
  - - **q:** Why can 'some do X and some do Y' not give an overlap, while 'more than half do X and more than half do Y' does?
    - **a:** 'Some' allows two separate groups; two groups each above half the total cannot both fit without a shared member.
  - - **q:** Why is 'unconnected' a risky label for two statements on one subject?
    - **a:** It is a strong claim: statements that mention each other's terms or the same actors are rarely unconnected, so a single link defeats it.
  - - **q:** Why may a practical-step stem allow a recommendation when a plain inference stem does not?
    - **a:** The stem asks what to do, so a step is part of the answer; but it must fit the problem as the passage states it, and any tool it names must answer that problem, not a different one.
  - - **q:** How does a share differ from a count in a numerical conclusion?
    - **a:** A share is a proportion of a base; a count is a number of people. A rise in a share does not give a rise in a count, and a share of patients is not a chance of falling ill.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A passage says: 'After the new bus route opened, shop sales in the area rose. In the same month, a festival began.' Write one safe and one unsafe conclusion and name the slip in the unsafe one.
    - **a:** Safe: sales rose after the route opened (and the festival began in the same month). Unsafe: the new route caused the rise; this is post hoc, with a hidden third factor (the festival).
  - - **q:** In a hall of 90 people, more than half are teachers and more than half are parents. What is the least number who are both? Show the working.
    - **a:** More than half of 90 is at least 46 each; 46 + 46 = 92, which is 2 more than 90, so at least 2 are both.
  - - **q:** A passage says 'most households in the sample own a phone'. Option: 'phones are common in the country'. Run G-S-L on it.
    - **a:** G fails: the claim group (the country) is larger than the evidence group (the sample). S is partly met ('most' matches 'common'), but the group failure removes the option.
  - - **q:** A passage states a rule: 'A loan is sanctioned only if a guarantor signs.' Kavita's loan was sanctioned. State one conclusion that follows and one that does not.
    - **a:** Follows: a guarantor signed for Kavita's loan. Does not follow: every loan with a guarantor is sanctioned (the condition is necessary, not sufficient).

### Memory skeleton

- Conclusion = what a careful reader may safely end with; not the author's conclusion, not the message
- Strength: a conclusion may be no stronger than the passage's own words
- Group test: evidence group versus claim group; four stretches
- Link test: cause stated on the page, or only side by side? Six causal slips
- Combining statements: chain, overlap, shares versus counts, necessary versus sufficient
- Coded statements: judge one at a time, never by code pattern

### Blank-page recall

On a blank page, write the group test with its four stretches, the six causal slips, and the rules for combining two statements.

### Last-week summary

- A conclusion may not outrun its evidence: passage 'may' never licenses option 'will'; a strong word is fine if the passage uses it.
- Group test: claim group larger or different than evidence group = over-generalisation (part to whole, sample to population, case to rule, some to only).
- Side by side is not because: correlation, single cause, reverse cause, third factor, necessary as sufficient, cause as remedy.
- Chain if A then B and if B then C; two 'more than half' groups overlap; a share is not a count; points are not percent.
- Converse is a new claim; contrapositive says the same; a weaker option can be a different claim.
