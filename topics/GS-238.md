## GS-238: Labour markets, formalisation and skilling

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Labour markets, formalisation and skilling and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Who works and how

NCERT counts as workers not only wage and salary earners but also the self-employed, such as vendors, shop owners and professionals, because all contribute to producing goods and services. The worker-population ratio shows what share of people actively produce; in 2023-24 it was higher in rural areas because poorer households cannot afford to stay out of work. The status of employment is revealing: a little under three-fifths of workers are self-employed, about one-fifth are casual wage labourers and roughly one-fifth hold regular salaried jobs. Women's unpaid household and farm work is often not counted, which NCERT invites students to question. Sectorally, about 46 per cent of workers were still in the primary sector in 2023-24, down from about 74 per cent in 1972-73.

### Informality and jobless growth

Formal-sector workers, in public establishments and in private units with ten or more hired workers, enjoy social security, higher pay and union bargaining. Everyone else, including farmers, small enterprise owners and casual workers serving several employers, is informal, with no protection from dismissal and little regulation. NCERT estimates that in 2019-20 only about 11 per cent of roughly 535 million workers were formal. It also shows GDP growing faster than employment over 1951-2023, with the gap widening from the late 1990s, which scholars call jobless growth. The Ahmedabad mill closures show how a formal workforce can be pushed back into informality. Disguised unemployment on farms and seasonal unemployment are typical rural forms of underemployment.

### Labour Codes and the skilling gap

The Economic Survey 2025-26 describes four Labour Codes, on wages, industrial relations, social security and occupational safety, that consolidate 29 central laws; their implementation was notified on 21 November 2025. They promise appointment letters for all, a national floor wage, portable social security for migrants, equal benefits for fixed-term workers and legal recognition of gig and platform workers, aiming to balance flexibility with protection. On supply, PLFS data show the share of people with some vocational or technical training rising sharply to 34.7 per cent in 2023-24, yet only 4.9 per cent of youth had formal training. The Survey argues gig policy should make such work a choice, not a fallback forced by weak demand or skill mismatch.
