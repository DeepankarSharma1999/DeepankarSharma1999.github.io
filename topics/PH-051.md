### Bridge: Images in a plane mirror and why two mirrors can act like two sources

**One mirror.** A point source S in front of a plane mirror sends rays that reflect as if they came from a point S′ behind the mirror, at the same distance behind as S is in front. S′ is a **virtual image**: no light actually passes through it, but every reflected ray points back to it. Draw the mirror as a vertical line; S is at distance a to the left; S′ is at distance a to the right of the line.

**Two parallel mirrors.** Take two parallel plane mirrors, A and B, with B a distance d behind A, and let a ray reflect from only one of them (as in the interferometer, where each beam meets one mirror). S is a distance a in front of A. The image in A is a distance a behind A. S is a distance a + d in front of B, so its image in B is a distance a + d behind B, which is a + 2d behind A. The two images are separated by

$$ (a + 2d) − a = 2d $$

along the axis. **Two parallel mirrors a distance d apart give two virtual sources a distance 2d apart.**

**Why this matters here.** In a Michelson interferometer one mirror is M₁ and the other is the *virtual image* M₂′ of the second mirror (module 1). Light that reflects from M₁ and light that reflects from M₂′ appears to come from two virtual sources 2d apart, and both are images of the same real source, so they are coherent. The path difference at a distant point follows from the geometry of these two sources (module 2).

**Beginner traps**

- The gap between the images is 2d, not d.
- Virtual does not mean unreal for the calculation: the path lengths from the images to the observer give the correct optical paths.

### Bridge: Intensity of two beams and the visibility of fringes

**Two beams.** For two coherent beams of intensities I₁ and I₂ and phase difference φ the resultant intensity is

$$ I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁ I₂) cos φ $$

(PH-038 module 3 gives the amplitude form A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2a₁a₂ cos δ; intensity is proportional to A², so I₁ ∝ a₁² and I₂ ∝ a₂²). The largest intensity is I_max = (√I₁ + √I₂)² and the smallest is I_min = (√I₁ − √I₂)².

**Visibility.** The contrast of fringes is measured by the **visibility** (Brij Lal PDF p. 349, Eq. 14.40)

$$ V = (I_max − I_min) / (I_max + I_min) $$

Substituting the extremes gives (Eq. 14.41)

$$ V = 2√(I₁ I₂) / (I₁ + I₂) $$

*Numbers (recomputed):* equal beams, I₁ = I₂: V = 1 and I_min = 0 (perfectly dark). A beam splitter that sends 70 percent one way and 30 percent the other gives V = 2√0.21 = 0.9165. If the beams are incoherent the cosine term averages to zero, I_max = I_min and V = 0: uniform brightness (PDF p. 350).

**Reading V.** V = 1 means black dark fringes; V near 0 means the fringes are washed out. In the Michelson interferometer V changes with the mirror position because of the finite spectral width of the source (modules 6 and 7), so V is the quantity that links the instrument to coherence.

**Beginner traps**

- Brij Lal PDF p. 350 says V "is always equal to 1 when monochromatic light is used". That is true only when the two beams have equal intensities (V = 2√(I₁I₂)/(I₁ + I₂)); with unequal beams V < 1 even for a perfectly monochromatic source.
- V is a ratio of intensities, not of amplitudes.

### 1. Orientation: what a Michelson interferometer is, its parts and its virtual air film

*Objective:* Name the parts of the Michelson interferometer, trace the two beams, explain the purpose of the compensating plate and picture the virtual air film between M₁ and the image M₂′.

## What it is

An **interferometer** is an instrument that uses interference of light to make precise measurements of wavelength or distance (Brij Lal PDF p. 375). In the Michelson interferometer a beam from an extended source is divided into two beams of nearly equal intensity by partial reflection; they travel along two perpendicular arms, are reflected by plane mirrors, return to the same plate and overlap. This is interference by **division of amplitude**, the same family as thin films (Day 17 lesson PH-050 module 2).

## The parts (PDF p. 376)

- **Source S** (extended, monochromatic) and a **collimating lens L** that makes the light parallel.
- **Beam splitter G₁**: a plane parallel glass plate, partially silvered on one face, set at 45° to the beams.
- **Compensating plate G₂**: an identical but unsilvered plate, parallel to G₁, in the arm to M₂.
- **Mirror M₁**: movable on a carriage by a micrometer screw, so that it moves parallel to itself; displacements of the order of 0.1 µm can be read.
- **Mirror M₂**: fixed, with fine tilt screws to make M₁ and M₂ exactly perpendicular.
- **Telescope T** (or the eye) to observe the fringes.

## How the light goes

Light from S meets G₁. One part is reflected towards M₁ along one arm, returns the same way and passes through G₁ towards T. The other part passes through G₁ and G₂ towards M₂, is reflected back and, at G₁, is reflected towards T. The two parts reach T together. Because both come from one incident wave by division of amplitude, they are coherent.

## Why the compensating plate

The beam to M₁ passes through the thickness of G₁ three times; the beam to M₂ passes through it only once (PDF p. 376). The extra plate G₂, of the same thickness and parallel to G₁, is placed in the M₂ arm (traversed twice) so that both beams have the same path in glass. For a single wavelength a distance could be adjusted instead, but for white light the glass path must be equal for every colour; this is standard knowledge, flagged 'verify': the refractive index of glass depends on wavelength, so unequal glass paths would move the zero-path position differently for different colours.

## The virtual air film

Looking into the instrument from T you see M₁ directly and also the **virtual image M₂′** of M₂ formed in the reflecting surface of G₁ (PDF p. 377). Depending on the position of M₁, M₂′ may lie in front of, behind or exactly on M₁. The two interfering beams then behave as if they were reflected from two surfaces, M₁ and M₂′, separated by a distance d: an **air film of thickness d**. The difference from a real film is that only two reflections occur, not many (PDF p. 377).

Everything in the next modules follows from this picture: the film thickness d is set by turning the micrometer screw, and the angle between M₁ and M₂′ is set by the tilt screws.

**Hand-drawn figure to practise:** a top view with S and L on the left, G₁ at 45° in the middle, M₁ above, M₂ to the right with G₂ before it, and T below.

### 2. Foundation: the path difference 2d cos θ and the circular fringes of equal inclination

*Objective:* Derive Δ = 2d cos θ from the two virtual sources, write the bright and dark conditions with the half-wave term, and derive how ring angle and ring number depend on d.

## The path difference

By bridge B1 the two mirrors M₁ and M₂′, a distance d apart, produce two virtual images S₁ and S₂ of a source point, a distance **2d** apart along the axis (the line perpendicular to the mirrors). Take a distant observer in a direction making angle θ with the axis. The rays from S₁ and S₂ to the observer are essentially parallel, so the difference of the two path lengths is the projection of the separation S₁S₂ on that direction:

$$ Δ = 2d cos θ $$

(Brij Lal PDF p. 377.) Check with the thin-film result: for a film of index μ the path difference is 2μt cos r (PH-050 module 3); here μ = 1, t = d and r = θ. Dimension: length. Limit: θ = 0 gives Δ = 2d, the extra path of the double trip to M₁ and back.

## The half-wave term and the order of the central ring

Brij Lal states that the beam from M₂ undergoes a rare-to-dense reflection (a π jump) while the other does not, so the effective path difference is Δ = 2d cos θ + λ/2 and brightness needs 2d cos θ + λ/2 = mλ, m = 0, 1, 2, … With this convention d = 0 gives Δ = λ/2: the centre is **dark** when M₁ coincides with M₂′ (PDF p. 377). *Flag:* which of the two outputs has the extra π depends on how the beam splitter is coated; with a different coating the centre is bright at d = 0. The counting formulas of module 4 do not depend on this. This lesson follows the book.

Define m₀ = 2d/λ + ½, the order at the centre (θ = 0).

## Why the fringes are circles

For fixed d, λ and order m, the condition fixes cos θ, hence θ. All rays at the same angle θ to the axis lie on a cone, so the fringe is a circle about the axis. These are **fringes of equal inclination** (the same name as in PH-050 module 4, where the film is real). They are formed at infinity, so the telescope is focused at infinity or the eye is relaxed.

## Ring angles

Use cos θ ≈ 1 − θ²/2 for small θ. A bright ring of order m = m₀ − p (p = 1, 2, … counted from the centre, taking m₀ an integer so that the centre is bright) satisfies

$$ 2d (1 − θ²/2) + λ/2 = (m₀ − p) λ ,   with   2d + λ/2 = m₀ λ $$
$$ d θ_p² = p λ ,   θ_p = √( p λ / d ) $$

*Numbers (recomputed):* λ = 589 nm, d = 1 mm: θ₁ = 0.02427 rad = 1.39°, θ₂ = 1.97°, θ₃ = 2.41°, θ₄ = 2.78°. The exact formula cos θ_p = 1 − pλ/(2d) agrees to 0.01°. For d = 5 mm the first ring is at 0.62°.

## What happens when d changes

A ring of fixed order m obeys cos θ = (m − ½)λ/(2d), so as d increases cos θ decreases and θ increases: each ring moves **outwards**, and a new ring is born at the centre every time the central order increases by 1, that is every time d grows by λ/2 (Brij Lal PDF p. 378). The number of rings inside a half-angle θ_max is about d θ_max²/λ: for d = 1 mm and 2° this is 2.07, for d = 5 mm it is 10.3. The rings crowd as d grows; the spacing near ring p is Δθ ≈ λ/(2dθ_p). When d is reduced the rings shrink into the centre; at d = 0 the field is uniform.

**Beginner traps:** using θ in degrees inside θ²; forgetting the factor 2 in 2d.

### 3. Core: tilted mirrors, straight and curved fringes, localisation and white-light fringes

*Objective:* Explain how the fringe shape changes with the tilt of M₁ relative to M₂′ and with d, state where the fringes are localised, and describe the white-light fringes used to find zero path difference.

## Parallel mirrors: circles at infinity

When M₁ and M₂′ are exactly parallel the fringes are the circles of module 2, formed at infinity. They do not depend on where in the field one looks except through the angle θ, which is why a large extended source can be used and the central region is observed through a telescope.

## Inclined mirrors: a wedge film

If the tilt screws leave M₁ and M₂′ slightly inclined, the air film between them is **wedge-shaped** and the fringes appear straight (Brij Lal PDF p. 378, §15.7.5). For a wedge of small angle ε, the thickness varies as d(x) = d₀ + εx across the field. In the regime where the cos θ term is negligible, fringes are the loci of constant d(x), so they are straight lines parallel to the edge of the wedge and equally spaced. As for any air wedge (PH-050 module 5 with μ = 1) the fringe width is

$$ β = λ / (2ε) $$

*Numbers (recomputed):* λ = 589 nm, ε = 10⁻⁴ rad gives β = 2.945 mm.

If one mirror is moved, the straight fringes slide across the field; the position of any bright fringe is taken by the next. Counting those that pass a reference mark gives the displacement: m fringes correspond to d = mλ/2 (Eq. 15.58, module 4).

## In between: curved fringes

The exact condition is 2d(x) cos θ = constant. Write cos θ = 1 − θ²/2: the fringe condition is d(x)(1 − θ²/2) = constant, so the field-angle term θ² competes with the position term εx. Near d ≈ 0 the θ² term is negligible (d is tiny) and the fringes are straight; as d grows the θ² term becomes important and bends the fringes into arcs, in the standard treatment hyperbolas. This is why the book advises locating the position of M₁ where it intersects M₂′ by looking for **straight** fringes with monochromatic light (PDF p. 378).

## Localisation

Fringes of the wedge are localised near the film, i.e. near the mirrors, and the telescope must be focused on them; the circular fringes of equal inclination are formed at infinity. This agrees with the thin-film rule of PH-050 module 4: equal thickness fringes are at the film, equal inclination fringes at infinity.

## White-light fringes

With a white source, tilt the mirrors slightly (as for straight fringes) and move M₁ very slowly near the position where it cuts M₂′ (PDF p. 378). A few coloured fringes appear with a **central dark fringe**, the book says surrounded by 8 to 10 coloured fringes on either side. The central fringe is the zero path difference position for every wavelength at once. White fringes are useful precisely because they mark *zero* path difference, which monochromatic fringes cannot (all orders look the same).

The **number of visible white fringes is small** because white light has a very short coherence length: with a spectral width of about 300 nm around 550 nm, λ²/Δλ ≈ 1 µm, about two wavelengths (module 7). A few coloured bands (book: 8 to 10) remain visible because colour differences persist after the black-and-white contrast, which lasts only about 1 to 2 wavelengths (about 1 µm; Ch 16 Problem 1 gives 0.86 µm), has gone.

**Beginner traps:** expecting a bright centre at d = 0 (the book's convention gives dark); thinking white-light fringes can be found far from zero path difference.

### 4. Core: fringe counting, the wavelength of light and small displacements

*Objective:* Derive that one fringe corresponds to a mirror movement of λ/2, use N fringes to find the wavelength or the displacement, and estimate the precision.

## The counting law

With the circular-fringe arrangement the order at the centre is m₀ = 2d/λ + ½ (module 2). Move M₁ so that d changes by Δd. The order at the centre changes by

$$ ΔN = 2Δd / λ $$

so every time Δd grows by λ/2, one more fringe appears at the centre (and, going the other way, one disappears). If N fringes pass the centre while M₁ moves through Δd,

$$ Δd = N λ / 2 ,   λ = 2 Δd / N   (Brij Lal Eq. 15.58, 15.59) $$

The book writes this for d₁ and d₂, the film thicknesses at the start and the end (orders m₁ and m_n): 2d₁ = m₁λ and 2d₂ = m_nλ in the same bright-ring position, so 2(d₂ − d₁) = (m_n − m₁)λ (PDF pp. 378 to 380). For straight (wedge) fringes the same law holds: N fringes cross a reference mark while M₁ moves Δd = Nλ/2.

## Procedure

Adjust M₁ and M₂ for circular fringes. Mark the centre. Turn the micrometer screw slowly and count the rings that appear (or vanish) at the centre while reading the drum before and after.

## Worked numbers (recomputed)

- Brij Lal Example 15.9: 200 fringes for 0.0589 mm: λ = 2 × 0.0589 × 10⁻³ m/200 = 5.890 × 10⁻⁷ m = 5890 Å. ✓
- Problem 1: 100 fringes for 2.948 × 10⁻³ cm = 2.948 × 10⁻⁵ m: λ = 5.896 × 10⁻⁷ m = 5896 Å.
- Problem 7: 100 fringes for 0.0295 mm: λ = 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ m = 5900 Å.
- Reverse use: 412 fringes counted with the mercury green line 546.1 nm means Δd = 412 × 546.1/2 nm = 0.1125 mm.

## Sensitivity and precision

One fringe corresponds to λ/2 = 294.5 nm of mirror movement for sodium light; 1 mm of travel is 2 × 10⁻³ m/589 nm = 3396 fringes. The book says displacements of the order of 0.1 µm can be read on the drum (PDF p. 376), i.e. about 0.34 of a fringe. If N = 1000 fringes are counted over Δd = 0.2945 mm and Δd is uncertain by 0.1 µm, the fractional uncertainty is 0.1/294.5 = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴, about 0.2 nm in the wavelength. The instrument therefore measures lengths in units of the wavelength to a fraction of a fringe: this is its reason for being used to compare a length standard with the wavelength of light.

## The standard metre (reading)

Michelson and Benoit (1895) measured the metre in terms of the cadmium red line. Because fringes are visible only up to a path difference of about 20 cm, the mirror was not moved more than 10 cm, and nine etalons, each twice the length of the previous one, were compared in turn: 0.390625 mm × 2⁸ = 100 mm (PDF pp. 381 to 383). The book gives λ_R = 6438.4722 Å and 1 m = 1 553 163.5 λ_R; recomputed, 1/(6438.4722 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) = 1 553 163.5 ✓.

**Beginner traps:** forgetting the factor 2 (the path changes by twice the mirror movement); counting rings instead of fringes crossing the centre; mixing Å, nm and µm.

### 5. Core: the refractive index of a gas and the thickness of a thin sheet (the 2018 question)

*Objective:* Derive 2(μ − 1)l = Nλ for a gas cell and for a thin sheet, solve the 2018 numerical, and explain why white light is used to locate the zero.

## The gas cell

Put a tube of length l, with transparent end windows, in the path of the beam going to M₁. The beam passes through the tube twice, there and back. Let the tube be evacuated at first (refractive index 1) and then filled with gas of index μ. The optical path of the beam in the tube changes from 1 × l to μ × l on each pass, so the **extra optical path difference** between the two arms is

$$ 2(μ − 1) l $$

(Brij Lal §15.8.4, PDF p. 381). If N fringes cross the centre of the field while the gas is admitted (or removed),

$$ 2(μ − 1) l = N λ ,    μ = 1 + N λ / (2l) $$

*Dimension check:* Nλ/(2l) is dimensionless. *Limiting case:* μ = 1 gives no shift. The same quantity, (μ − 1) l, appears for a single pass in the Jamin refractometer (N λ = (μ − 1) l, PDF p. 351); the factor 2 here is the double pass.

## The 2018 question (20 marks)

"Describe how Michelson interferometer can be used to determine refractive index of a gas. In a Michelson Interference experiment, a tube of length 25 cm containing a gas of refractive index μ is introduced between the upper mirror and the beam splitter. 150 fringes cross the centre of the field of view when the wavelength of light used is 5890 Å. Find the value of μ."

Solution: l = 0.25 m, N = 150, λ = 5890 Å = 5.890 × 10⁻⁷ m. Then μ − 1 = Nλ/(2l) = 150 × 5.890 × 10⁻⁷/0.50 = 1.767 × 10⁻⁴, so

$$ μ = 1.0001767 $$

*Assumption to state:* the 150 fringes are the shift relative to the evacuated tube (the standard measurement in the book). The stem does not say whether the shift is relative to vacuum or to air in the same path; with vacuum as the reference the answer above follows. If the count were relative to air (μ_air ≈ 1.000293, verify), it would give μ_gas ≈ 1.00047. *Check:* omitting the factor 2 gives 1.000353 (twice as large above 1), which is a standard error.

*Book check:* Example 15.10 (140 fringes, 5460 Å, 20 cm) gives μ = 1 + 140 × 5.46 × 10⁻⁷/0.40 = 1.000191 ✓ (printed 1.00019). Real gases at atmospheric pressure have μ − 1 of order 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³ (standard knowledge, verify); our value is of that order. For a gas at a given temperature, μ − 1 is proportional to the pressure (Brij Lal PDF p. 386), so plotting N against pressure gives a straight line.

## A thin transparent sheet

A sheet of thickness t and index μ in one beam adds 2(μ − 1)t to the path difference (double pass). The fringe system shifts by N with 2(μ − 1)t = Nλ. With monochromatic light all fringes look alike, so a sudden shift cannot be counted (PDF pp. 380 to 381). The book's method: with white light, bring the central dark fringe to the cross-wire; insert the sheet; move M₁ by a distance d until the central fringe is back (so 2d = 2(μ − 1)t, d = (μ − 1)t); then, with monochromatic light, count the fringes N in the same movement: d = Nλ/2, so

$$ t = Nλ / (2(μ − 1)) $$

*Numbers (recomputed):* mica μ = 1.58, N = 10, λ = 589 nm gives t = 10 × 589 nm/(2 × 0.58) = 5.08 µm. Conversely a sheet 5 µm thick causes 9.85 fringes of shift.

**Beginner traps:** using (μ − 1)l instead of 2(μ − 1)l; confusing wavelength in vacuum and in the gas; taking the 2018 data as "tube inserted into air" without stating the reference.

### 6. Core: two close wavelengths, the sodium D lines and the visibility cycle

*Objective:* Derive the visibility of Michelson fringes for two close wavelengths, the repeat distance Δd = λ̄²/(2Δλ) and the formula for Δλ, and evaluate it for the sodium D doublet.

## The idea

A sodium lamp emits two lines close together, the D lines. Each wavelength makes its own set of fringes. When the mirror position is such that the bright rings of one coincide with the bright rings of the other, the fringes are sharp. At another position the bright rings of one fall on the dark rings of the other and the fringes fade. Moving the mirror, the fringes **sharpen and fade periodically**; the period gives the wavelength difference (Brij Lal §15.8.2, PDF p. 380).

## Derivation of the visibility

Let x = 2d be the path difference. For two lines of equal intensity I₀, wavelengths λ₁ and λ₂ (λ₁ > λ₂), the intensity is the sum of two fringe patterns:

$$ I = I₀ [1 + cos(2πx/λ₁)] + I₀ [1 + cos(2πx/λ₂)] $$

Use cos A + cos B = 2 cos[(A + B)/2] cos[(A − B)/2] with A = 2πx/λ₁ and B = 2πx/λ₂:

$$ I = 2 I₀ [ 1 + cos(π x Δλ/λ̄²) cos(2π x/λ̄) ] $$

where Δλ = λ₁ − λ₂ and λ̄ is the mean (1/λ₂ − 1/λ₁ = Δλ/(λ₁λ₂) ≈ Δλ/λ̄²). The fast factor cos(2πx/λ̄) is the fringe pattern; the slow factor is an **envelope**. The visibility (module B2) is

$$ V(x) = | cos(π x Δλ/λ̄²) | $$

V = 1 when x = kλ̄²/Δλ (k = 0, 1, 2, …); V = 0 when x = (k + ½)λ̄²/Δλ.

## The repeat distance

Successive positions of maximum distinctness differ by Δx = λ̄²/Δλ in path difference, i.e. by a mirror movement

$$ Δd = λ̄² / (2Δλ) ,   Δλ = λ̄² / (2Δd)   (Brij Lal Eq. 15.60) $$

The book derives the same result by coincidence: the m-th order of the longer wavelength coincides with the (m + 1)-th of the shorter, 2d = mλ₁ = (m + 1)λ₂, so m = λ₂/(λ₁ − λ₂) and a coincidence recurs each time the order difference grows by one. The first disappearance occurs at d = Δd/2.

## The sodium doublet (recomputed)

Using λ(D₂) = 5889.95 Å and λ(D₁) = 5895.92 Å (standard values, verify): Δλ = 5.97 Å, λ̄ = 5892.9 Å, so Δd = λ̄²/(2Δλ) = 0.2908 mm. The number of fringes crossing the centre between consecutive best-distinctness positions is 2Δd/λ̄ = 987 (equal to λ̄/Δλ). With the book's rounded 5890 and 5896 Å (Problem 3) the same formula gives 0.2894 mm. Conversely, if the best-visibility positions are 0.289 mm apart, Δλ = (5893 × 10⁻¹⁰)²/(2 × 0.289 × 10⁻³) = 6.01 Å. The book's Problem 10 (best visibility at 0.03 cm, λ̄ = 5893 Å) gives Δλ = 5.79 Å.

## Unequal intensities

If the two lines have intensities I₁ and I₂ the envelope is

$$ V² = ( I₁² + I₂² + 2 I₁ I₂ cos φ ) / ( I₁ + I₂ )² ,   φ = 2π x Δλ/λ̄² $$

with minimum |I₁ − I₂|/(I₁ + I₂) (Brij Lal PDF p. 379). For D₂ : D₁ = 2 : 1 (an approximate, standard figure, verify) the minimum is 1/3: the fringes never vanish but cycle between V = 1 and V = 1/3.

**Michelson versus Fabry-Perot.** The Michelson interferometer does not show two separate ring systems; it detects the doublet through the visibility cycle. The book prefers the Fabry-Perot interferometer for fine structure, where each wavelength has its own rings (PDF p. 390).

### 7. Core: visibility, temporal coherence, coherence length and white-light fringes

*Objective:* Derive the visibility curve for a spectral line of finite width, relate its first zero to the coherence length λ²/Δλ, and use it to explain why Michelson fringes fade as d grows and why white light gives only a few fringes.

## From two lines to a line of width

Module 6 summed two fringe patterns. A real spectral line contains a continuous range of wavenumbers σ = 1/λ. Suppose the line has uniform intensity over a band of width Δσ centred on σ₀ (a rectangular profile, chosen for simplicity). With path difference x = 2d, each wavenumber gives I_σ [1 + cos(2πσx)], so the total intensity is

$$ I(x) = ∫ I_σ [1 + cos(2πσx)] dσ ,   σ from σ₀ − Δσ/2 to σ₀ + Δσ/2 $$

Let the total be I_T = I_σ Δσ. Integrating cos(2πσx) over the band:

$$ ∫ cos(2πσx) dσ = [ sin(2πσx) / (2πx) ] = (1/(2πx)) · 2 cos(2πσ₀x) sin(πΔσx) = Δσ · cos(2πσ₀x) · sin(πΔσx)/(πΔσx) $$

so

$$ I(x) = I_T [ 1 + sinc(πΔσ x) cos(2πσ₀ x) ] ,   sinc(u) = sin u / u $$

The fast factor cos(2πσ₀x) is the fringe pattern; the slow factor is the envelope, so the visibility is

$$ V(x) = | sin(πΔσ x) / (πΔσ x) | $$

*Limiting cases:* Δσ → 0 gives V = 1 for every x (a perfectly monochromatic source has no limit on path difference). At x = 0, V = 1 for every source. The first zero of V is at πΔσx = π, that is

$$ x = 1/Δσ = λ̄²/Δλ ,   using   Δσ = Δλ/λ̄² $$

## Coherence length

This path difference λ̄²/Δλ is the **coherence length** l_coh = λ²/Δλ ≈ cΔt (module 2 of PH-050; Brij Lal Eq. 16.11, PDF p. 405, and §16.7, PDF p. 408). The instrument therefore measures it: move M₁ until the contrast disappears; the path difference 2d at which this happens is l_coh.

*Numbers (recomputed).* For the mercury green line (λ = 546.1 nm) with l_coh = 1 cm the implied bandwidth is Δλ = (546.1 nm)²/10⁷ nm = 0.030 nm. For a sodium lamp with l_coh = 1 mm (Brij Lal PDF p. 408) it is 0.35 nm. For white light, Δλ ≈ 300 nm around 550 nm: l_coh ≈ (550)²/300 nm = 1.0 µm, about 1.8 wavelengths. Brij Lal's worked examples (PDF p. 410) recompute correctly: 1.2 mm for a 4 × 10⁻¹² s emission, 11.2 km for the CO₂ laser line (10.6 µm, Δλ = 10⁻⁵ nm) and 0.3 mm and 11.6 Å for 5893 Å with 10⁻¹² s.

## A source-dependent value

The book quotes about 2 to 3 cm (Ch 14, PDF p. 341) and about 1 mm (Ch 16, PDF p. 408; Ex 16.1 gives 1.2 mm); the value depends on lamp pressure and temperature. Both exceed the doublet repeat of 0.58 mm path, which is why several visibility cycles are seen. Ch 14 also calls white light's coherence length "a fraction of a cm", which is vague; the recomputed value is about 1 µm. The lesson uses the recomputed values.

## Why white light gives few fringes

Every colour has fringes of its own spacing but all share the zero-path position, where the zero-order fringes coincide. As x grows the colours separate and the fringes become tinted bands and then wash out. Brij Lal's 8 to 10 coloured fringes on either side (PDF p. 378) are not a slip. A few coloured bands (book: 8 to 10) remain visible because colour differences persist after the black-and-white contrast, which lasts only about 1 to 2 wavelengths (about 1 µm; Ch 16 Problem 1 gives 0.86 µm), has gone.

## Extended sources

For an extended source each point gives the same rings, since 2d cos θ depends only on the direction θ (standard knowledge, verify), so the patterns add; a double slit needs a narrow source (Brij Lal PDF pp. 408 to 409).

**Beginner trap:** calling any fading of V "poor alignment"; it may be the finite linewidth.

### 8. Master: Michelson fringes compared with thin-film fringes, and the other interferometers

*Objective:* State the similarities and differences between the Michelson fringes and thin-film fringes and place the Michelson interferometer among the Fabry-Perot, Twyman-Green and Mach-Zehnder instruments.

## Same mathematics, different object

The Michelson fringes are the fringes of an air film of thickness d (module 1), with the path difference 2d cos θ of PH-050 module 3 for μ = 1. Day 17 lesson PH-050 treats the real film; this table gives only what changes.

| Feature | Real thin film (PH-050) | Michelson interferometer |
|---|---|---|
| The two reflecting surfaces | Two faces of one film, fixed | Mirror M₁ and the virtual image M₂′ of M₂ |
| Film thickness | Fixed by the object | Chosen by the micrometer screw, and can be reduced to zero or reversed in sign |
| Beams | Two beams matter only if the surfaces reflect weakly; with strong reflection many beams (PH-050 module 8) | Exactly two beams, equal intensities, so the intensity follows cos² and V can reach 1 |
| Medium | Refractive index μ, angle r inside | Air, μ = 1, angle θ of the ray itself |
| Phase term | π jump decided by the indices (PH-050 bridge B2) | Fixed by the beam splitter; the book gives Δ = 2d cos θ + λ/2 |
| Equal inclination fringes | Haidinger fringes at infinity | Circular fringes at infinity (same name, same origin) |
| Equal thickness fringes | Wedge, Fizeau fringes | Straight fringes when M₁ and M₂′ are inclined |
| Use | Colours, coatings, thickness | Measuring wavelengths, displacements, indices, coherence |

The difference to remember is that the Michelson instrument is a **variable** film in which d can be changed by a known amount and counted fringe by fringe, which a real film cannot do. The compensating plate G₂ and the large separation possible for d are what let the instrument produce the visibility curves of modules 6 and 7. Brij Lal (PDF p. 377) also reminds us that in a real film multiple reflections occur whereas here only two reflections take place.

## Other interferometers in the extract (reading, not taught)

- **Twyman-Green** (PDF p. 384): a Michelson interferometer with a point source and a collimating lens; used to test prisms, lenses and glass plates; fringes are contours of the deformed wavefront. Uniform intensity means a perfect component.
- **Mach-Zehnder** (PDF p. 385): two beam splitters and two mirrors, with cells in both arms; N fringes cross when the refractive index changes by Δμ = Nλ/l for a single pass of cell length l. Contrast with the Michelson double pass 2(μ − 1) l.
- **Fabry-Perot** (PDF pp. 389 to 391): multiple-beam interference with highly reflecting plates; sharp rings; each wavelength gives its own ring system, so the doublet can be resolved; the book says the Michelson cannot study fine structure the same way (PDF p. 390). Multiple-beam theory is in PH-050 module 8 (the Airy form).

**Beginner traps:** transferring the formula 2μt cos r to the Michelson interferometer without setting μ = 1 and r = θ; assuming the Michelson fringes are many-beam fringes like Fabry-Perot fringes.

## The 2018 question (Paper I, Q3(c), 20 marks)

Describe how a Michelson interferometer can be used to determine the refractive index of a gas; a tube of length 25 cm containing the gas is placed between the upper mirror and the beam splitter; 150 fringes cross the centre for λ = 5890 Å; find μ.

## Part 1: the description (about 14 minutes)

1. **Figure** (practise the figure in module 1): source and collimating lens, beam splitter G₁ at 45°, compensating plate G₂, mirrors M₁ and M₂, telescope T, and the gas cell of length l between G₁ and M₁ with windows.
2. **Principle in two sentences:** the two beams are formed by division of amplitude at G₁, reflect from M₁ and M₂ and recombine; they are coherent and give circular fringes of equal inclination; the path difference is 2d cos θ, so at the centre the order is m₀ = 2d/λ + ½.
3. **Method:** adjust for circular fringes with the cell evacuated; admit the gas slowly (or evacuate the filled cell) and count the N fringes that cross the centre.
4. **Theory:** each pass through the cell adds (μ − 1)l to the optical path; the beam passes twice, so the extra path difference is 2(μ − 1)l = Nλ; μ = 1 + Nλ/(2l).
5. **Precautions:** equal windows; compensation of the glass; slow filling to avoid losing count; temperature and pressure noted (μ − 1 ∝ pressure); using the fringes at the centre for sharp counting.

## Part 2: the numerical (about 8 minutes)

l = 25 cm = 0.25 m; N = 150; λ = 5890 Å = 5.890 × 10⁻⁷ m.

$$ μ − 1 = Nλ/(2l) = 150 × 5.890 × 10⁻⁷ / 0.50 = 1.767 × 10⁻⁴ $$
$$ μ = 1.0001767 ≈ 1.000177 $$

Show the unit conversion, the factor 2 and the reference (the shift is relative to vacuum, as in the book's §15.8.4); the stem does not state the reference, so state your assumption. If the count were relative to air (μ_air ≈ 1.000293, verify), it would give μ_gas ≈ 1.00047. Without the double pass the answer would be 1.000353, wrong.

### Notes: Definitions

- Interferometer: an instrument that uses interference of light to make precise measurements of wavelengths or distances (Brij Lal PDF p. 375).
- Beam splitter G1: partially silvered plane parallel plate at 45 degrees; compensating plate G2: identical unsilvered plate in the arm to M2 so that both beams have equal glass paths.
- M2': the virtual image of M2 in the reflecting surface of G1; d = distance between M1 and M2'; the interference is that of an air film of thickness d with two reflections only.
- Fringes of equal inclination: circles for which 2d cos theta + lambda/2 = m lambda; localised at infinity. Fringes of equal thickness: straight fringes when M1 and M2' are inclined; localised near the mirrors.
- Visibility V = (I_max - I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = 2 sqrt(I1 I2)/(I1 + I2) for two beams.
- Coherence length l_coh = lambda squared/delta lambda: the path difference at which the visibility of a line of width delta lambda first vanishes (rectangular profile).

### Notes: Equations to know

- Path difference: Delta = 2d cos theta (book: + lambda/2); bright 2d cos theta + lambda/2 = m lambda.
- Ring angles (centre bright, small angles): d theta_p squared = p lambda, theta_p = sqrt(p lambda/d); rings within half-angle theta_max about d theta_max squared/lambda.
- Counting: N fringes cross the centre for a mirror movement Delta d: Delta d = N lambda/2, lambda = 2 Delta d/N.
- Gas cell (double pass): 2(mu - 1) l = N lambda, mu = 1 + N lambda/(2l). Sheet: 2(mu - 1) t = N lambda, t = N lambda/(2(mu - 1)).
- Two close lines: V(x) = |cos(pi x delta lambda/lambda_mean squared)|, x = 2d; repeat distance Delta d = lambda_mean squared/(2 delta lambda); delta lambda = lambda_mean squared/(2 Delta d).
- Unequal lines: V squared = (I1 squared + I2 squared + 2 I1 I2 cos phi)/(I1 + I2) squared, minimum |I1 - I2|/(I1 + I2).
- Rectangular line: V(x) = |sin(pi delta sigma x)/(pi delta sigma x)|, first zero x = lambda squared/delta lambda.
- Wedge fringe width with straight fringes: beta = lambda/(2 epsilon) (epsilon = angle between M1 and M2').

### Notes: Counting formulas at a glance

- Every measurement is a count of fringes against a known path change; the factor 2 comes from the double trip.

### Notes: Derivation skeletons

- Path difference: M1 and M2' separated by d give two virtual sources 2d apart; at angle theta the difference is the projection 2d cos theta.
- Ring angles: cos theta = 1 - theta squared/2; 2d(1 - theta squared/2) + lambda/2 = (m0 - p) lambda with 2d + lambda/2 = m0 lambda gives d theta squared = p lambda.
- Counting: central order m0 = 2d/lambda + 1/2; Delta m0 = 2 Delta d/lambda.
- Doublet: I = I0[1 + cos(2 pi x/lambda1)] + I0[1 + cos(2 pi x/lambda2)] = 2 I0[1 + cos(pi x delta lambda/lambda_mean squared) cos(2 pi x/lambda_mean)].
- Line of width: integrate I_sigma[1 + cos(2 pi sigma x)] over delta sigma; result I_T[1 + sinc(pi delta sigma x) cos(2 pi sigma0 x)].

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- The centre at d = 0 is dark in the book's convention; with another beam-splitter coating it is bright. Fringe counts do not depend on this.
- The ring-angle formula uses cos theta = 1 - theta squared/2 (better than 0.001 degree at 2.8 degrees).
- Visibility V = 1 requires equal beam intensities as well as a monochromatic source.
- Fringes disappear when 2d is comparable to the coherence length: the mirror cannot be moved far (about 10 cm for cadmium red).
- For the doublet with unequal intensities the minimum visibility is not zero.
- White light: the book's 8 to 10 coloured fringes (PDF p. 378) is not a slip. lambda squared/delta lambda is about 1 micrometre (Ch 16 Problem 1: 0.86 micrometre), the black-and-white contrast range; coloured bands persist for several orders beyond it.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- d (film thickness between M1 and M2') is not the arm length; it is the difference of the two arm lengths.
- Path difference 2d versus mirror movement d: one fringe per lambda/2 of mirror movement.
- Fringes of equal inclination (circles, at infinity) versus equal thickness (straight, near the mirrors).
- Coherence length of a line (set by its width) versus the repeat distance of the doublet visibility (set by the line separation).
- Michelson (two beams, cos squared) versus Fabry-Perot (many beams, sharp rings).

### Memory skeleton

- Construction: beam splitter G₁ at 45°, compensating plate G₂, mirrors M₁ and M₂; division of amplitude
- Virtual air film between M₁ and M₂′ of thickness d: Δ = 2d cos θ, circular fringes of equal inclination
- Ring angles dθ_p² = pλ; tilted mirrors give straight fringes; white-light fringes only near zero path
- Fringe counting: one fringe per λ/2 of mirror movement, λ = 2Δd/N
- Gas cell 2(μ − 1)l = Nλ (2018); sheet t = Nλ/(2(μ − 1))
- Two close lines: visibility cycles, Δd = λ²_mean/(2δλ)
- Visibility V and coherence length λ²/δλ limit the usable path difference

### Last-week summary

- M₁ and M₂′ form an air film of thickness d (difference of arm lengths): Δ = 2d cos θ; circles at infinity; inclined mirrors give straight fringes near the mirrors.
- Rings dθ_p² = pλ: θ_p ∝ √p and 1/√d, so they crowd as d grows; one new fringe per λ/2 of mirror movement, λ = 2Δd/N.
- Gas cell (double pass) μ = 1 + Nλ/(2l): 25 cm, 150 fringes, 5890 Å gives 1.0001767; dropping the 2 gives 1.000353. Sheet t = Nλ/(2(μ − 1)).
- Doublet: V = |cos(πxδλ/λ²_mean)|, x = 2d; best-visibility positions Δd = λ²_mean/(2δλ) apart, about 0.29 mm for sodium.
- V = 2√(I₁I₂)/(I₁ + I₂), 1 only for equal beams; fringes vanish when 2d reaches the coherence length λ²/δλ.
