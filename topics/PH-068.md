## PH-068: Laser coherence and beam focusing

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Phase, interference and fringe visibility

A light wave at a point has an amplitude and a **phase**, the stage it has reached in its cycle. When two waves of the same frequency overlap, the brightness depends on their phase difference: in step (difference 0) they add and give a bright fringe, half a cycle out of step they cancel. A steady pattern of fringes needs the phase difference to stay **fixed** for a time much longer than the eye or detector takes to look. If the phase difference changes randomly many times in that time the bright and dark places wander and average to a uniform glow. Waves with a fixed phase relation are called **coherent**.

The quality of fringes is measured by the **visibility** V = (I_max - I_min)/(I_max + I_min), where I_max and I_min are the brightest and darkest intensities. V = 1 is perfect (the dark fringes are fully dark) and V = 0 means no fringes. *Example:* I_max = 1.0 and I_min = 0.2 (arbitrary units) give V = 0.8/1.2 = 0.67. Coherence means a steady phase relation, and a loss of coherence appears as a fall of V.

### Bridge: Why a beam of width D spreads: diffraction and the 1.22 lambda/D rule

Light is a wave, and a wave that passes through an opening spreads (diffracts) behind it. The narrower the opening the more it spreads. For a circular opening of diameter D and light of wavelength lambda the pattern far away is a bright central disc (the **Airy disc**) surrounded by faint rings (Day 17 lesson PH-057). The angle from the axis to the first dark ring is theta = 1.22 lambda/D (Brijlal gives the focal-plane radius 1.22 f lambda/d, PDF p. 451). So the rough rule is: **angular spread = wavelength divided by aperture**.

*Numbers:* green light (lambda = 550 nm) through a hole of D = 1 mm spreads by 1.22 x 5.5 x 10^-7/10^-3 = 6.7 x 10^-4 rad, about 0.67 mm per metre. Through D = 10 mm it is ten times smaller. This spreading is not a defect of the source: it is set by the size of the aperture even for a perfect beam, and it is the reason that no beam can be both narrow and parallel for ever.

### 1. Orientation: in step along the beam, in step across the beam, and how small a beam can be made

*Objective:* Name the two kinds of coherence and the two beam questions (spread and focus), and say which verified questions fall where.

## Three questions about one beam

Suppose you hold a laser pointer and a candle. Three questions arise.

1. **Does the light stay in step with itself along the beam?** A wave that keeps a predictable phase over a long distance has high **temporal** (longitudinal) coherence. The distance over which it does so is the **coherence length**. A candle flame, with millions of atoms radiating at random, keeps its phase over a fraction of a millimetre; a good laser, over metres or kilometres.
2. **Does the light stay in step across the beam?** Two points side by side on the same wavefront can be in step (high **spatial**, or lateral, coherence) or not. A small distant source has high spatial coherence; a large lamp has low.
3. **How narrow can the beam be made, and how much does it spread?** The spread is set by diffraction at the aperture (bridge B2), and the smallest focal spot by diffraction at the lens. A beam that is spatially coherent can be squeezed to this limit; a lamp cannot, however good the lens.

### 2. Foundation: wave trains, coherence time and coherence length, with numbers

*Objective:* Describe the wave-train model of a lamp and compute coherence time, coherence length and the number of oscillations in a train.

## The model

An excited atom gives up its energy by emitting light and the emission lasts only for a brief time, about 10^-8 s (Brijlal Section 16.1, PDF pp. 402 to 403). During that time it radiates a finite burst of waves, a **wave train** or wave packet. The atom is then excited again later and emits another train with a new, random phase. The atoms in a lamp act independently, so the light of the lamp is a jumble of finite trains with random phases, a **quasi-monochromatic** source (PDF pp. 403 to 404). The phase is predictable only within one train; between trains it jumps.

## Length, time and number of oscillations

Let a train last for a time Delta t. Its length in vacuum is

$$ l = c Delta t $$

(PDF p. 403, Eq. 16.1) and the number of oscillations in it is N = l/lambda (Eq. 16.2). *Numbers (recomputed):* Delta t = 10^-8 s gives l = 3 x 10^8 x 10^-8 = 3 m, and for lambda = 5000 Angstrom (5 x 10^-7 m) N = 3/(5 x 10^-7) = 6 x 10^6. The book rounds this to "about a million" oscillations (an order-of-magnitude phrase); the exact count is six million.

## Coherence time and coherence length

The **coherence length** l_coh is the length over which the wave is nearly sinusoidal with a predictable phase, roughly the length of the train, c Delta t. The **coherence time** t_coh is the time over which the phase of the wave can be predicted, so that

$$ l_coh = c t_coh $$

(Eq. 16.5, PDF p. 404). The book remarks that two overlapping waves from different atoms keep a fixed phase for at most about 10^-8 s, and that the phase relation of two beams from independent sources changes about 10^8 times per second. That is why interference between two independent lamps is never seen.

## Worked example 16.1 (recomputed)

A sodium atom radiates for 4 x 10^-12 s: l_coh = 3 x 10^8 x 4 x 10^-12 = 1.2 x 10^-3 m = 1.2 mm (PDF p. 410). This is far shorter than the 3 m of the 10^-8 s model, because in a real lamp collisions and atomic motion shorten the effective train (standard physics; verify). The book's other sodium numbers (about 1 mm in Chapter 16 and 0.3 mm in Chapter 22) are of the same order; they differ with lamp conditions, as module 4 lists.

## A picture to keep

Think of each train as a short stick of fixed length; coherence length is the length of the stick. Two points on the beam are in step if they lie on the same stick and not if they lie on different ones.

## Units

If you must compare 10^-8 s with a coherence time of 4 x 10^-12 s, say it in words: the lamp's effective train lasts about two and a half thousand times less than the book's 10^-8 s atomic emission time (10^-8/(4 x 10^-12) = 2500).

### 3. Core: bandwidth, coherence length and the formulas l_coh = c/Delta nu = lambda^2/Delta lambda (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive l_coh = c/Delta nu, l_coh = lambda^2/Delta lambda and N = lambda/Delta lambda, and solve the book problems with units.

## Derivation 1

**Assumptions.** A source emits a train of duration Delta t = t_coh; the frequency spread (bandwidth) is Delta nu; light in vacuum; Delta lambda small compared with lambda.

1. A pulse of duration Delta t cannot have a single frequency: Fourier analysis gives a spread Delta nu of about 1/Delta t (Brijlal p. 404, Section 16.3). So Delta nu = 1/t_coh.
2. With l_coh = c t_coh: **Delta nu = c/l_coh**, the book's Eq. 16.7.
3. Use nu = c/lambda. Differentiating, Delta nu = -c Delta lambda/lambda^2; the sign only says that a shorter wavelength has a higher frequency, so use magnitudes: Delta nu = c Delta lambda/lambda^2.
4. Equate steps 2 and 3: c/l_coh = c Delta lambda/lambda^2, so

$$ l_coh = lambda^2/Delta lambda $$

(Eq. 16.11).
5. The number of oscillations in a train is N = l_coh/lambda = lambda/Delta lambda (Eq. 16.13). The **degree of monochromaticity** is xi = Delta nu/nu = Delta lambda/lambda = 1/N (Eq. 16.14); a perfectly monochromatic wave has xi = 0 and an infinite train.

*Dimension checks:* Delta nu is per second; c/l is metres per second per metre, per second. lambda^2/Delta lambda is a length. *Limit:* Delta lambda -> 0 gives l_coh -> infinity.

## Reading the table

White light has a coherence length of less than a micrometre, about 1.6 wavelengths (0.86 um/550 nm), which is why its fringes die out after a few; a CO2 laser line has 11 km. A narrower line means a longer train: the table spans ten orders of magnitude (0.86 um to 11 km) in length and more in bandwidth.

## A warning about the two formulas

l_coh = c/Delta nu and l_coh = lambda^2/Delta lambda are the same statement in two variables; use whichever matches the data. The factors of order one (such as 1/2 or 2) depend on the line shape; the book's formulas are order-of-magnitude and the lesson uses them exactly as the book does. Module 4 shows where the line shape enters the visibility.

### 4. Core: measuring temporal coherence with the Michelson interferometer, and the coherence lengths of real sources

*Objective:* Explain how fringe visibility in a Michelson interferometer measures l_coh, use V = |sinc| for a rectangular line, and convert the book's coherence lengths into bandwidths.

## The experiment (Brijlal Section 16.7, PDF p. 408)

In a Michelson interferometer a half-silvered plate splits the light into two beams that return from two mirrors and are recombined. If the mirror M1 is a distance d from the image M2' of the other mirror, the path difference is **2d**. When 2d is much smaller than l_coh the two beams are parts of the same train and give distinct fringes; when 2d is much larger the beams come from different trains with unrelated phases and the fringes vanish. To measure l_coh, move one mirror and watch the contrast fade; the path difference 2d at which the fringes disappear is the coherence length. The details of the instrument and its fringes are Day 18 lesson PH-051.

## How the contrast falls

Day 18 lesson PH-051 (the module on visibility, temporal coherence, coherence length and white-light fringes) integrates the fringe patterns of all wavenumbers in a rectangular band of width Delta sigma (sigma = 1/lambda) and finds

$$ V(x) = | sin(pi Delta sigma x) / (pi Delta sigma x) | ,   x = 2d $$

with the first zero at x = 1/Delta sigma = lambda^2/Delta lambda, equal to l_coh. In terms of l_coh, V = |sin(pi x/l_coh)/(pi x/l_coh)|:

| x/l_coh | 0 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V | 1 | 0.90 | 0.64 | 0 |

So the contrast has already dropped to 0.64 when the path difference is half the coherence length: "l_coh" is the scale at which fringes vanish, not a sharp edge. Real lines are not rectangular and have tails, so the first zero may not be so sharp (verify); the book's measurement idea is the same.

## Coherence lengths quoted by the book

Brijlal (PDF p. 408) lists: sodium lamp about 1 mm, green mercury line about 1 cm, neon red line 3 cm, red cadmium line 30 cm, orange krypton line 80 cm, a commercial He-Ne laser about 15 m, and some lasers up to a few km. Chapter 22 (PDF p. 649) gives 0.3 mm for a sodium lamp and about 100 m for an ordinary He-Ne laser. Converting with Delta nu = c/l_coh (the lesson's own conversion):

| Source | l_coh | Delta nu = c/l_coh |
|---|---|---|
| sodium lamp (Ch 16) | 1 mm | 3 x 10^11 Hz |
| green mercury | 1 cm | 3 x 10^10 Hz |
| red cadmium | 30 cm | 1 x 10^9 Hz |
| He-Ne (Ch 16) | 15 m | 2 x 10^7 Hz |
| He-Ne (Ch 22) | 100 m | 3 x 10^6 Hz |

The Chapter 16 and Chapter 22 figures for the same sources differ (He-Ne 15 m against 100 m; sodium 1 mm against 0.3 mm). Both are order-of-magnitude statements and the value depends on the particular lamp or laser (pressure, temperature, how many modes oscillate; verify), so quote the book you are asked about and give the order of magnitude.

## What the numbers tell you

The mirror distance d at which the fringes vanish is half the coherence length: for the cadmium line, 15 cm; for green mercury, 5 mm; for the sodium lamp, about 0.5 mm. A Michelson interferometer can therefore display fringes with a laser at path differences where a lamp would show none.

**Beginner trap:** reading "fringes disappear at 2d = l_coh" as "the mirror moved l_coh". The mirror moves by l_coh/2 because the path difference is 2d.

### 5. Core: spatial coherence, the source size and the two-slit visibility V = |sinc| (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Explain spatial coherence, derive the two-slit visibility for a source of width b and define the coherence width lambda/theta_s.

## The idea (Brijlal Section 16.6.2, PDF p. 407)

**Spatial** (lateral) coherence refers to a fixed phase relation between two points across the wavefront, in a plane perpendicular to the beam. An ideal point source gives equal phases at equal distances; an extended source, made of many independent emitters, gives less (the book says points more than about one wavelength apart on the source behave independently). The contrast of fringes produced by a source is therefore a measure of its spatial coherence (PDF p. 408).

## The Young experiment (Section 16.8, PDF pp. 408 to 409)

A lamp lighting two slits S1 and S2 directly gives no fringes; a narrow slit S in front of the lamp restores them, and widening S makes them fade. The deciding quantities are the **width b of the source slit** and the **separation d of the two slits**.

## Derivation 2: the visibility for a uniform source slit

**Assumptions.** A uniform source slit of width b at distance D from the double slit; monochromatic light of wavelength lambda; small angles; each point of the source emits independently so intensities add.

1. A source point at transverse position y (measured across the slit) has a path difference to the two slits of d y/D. Its fringe pattern on the screen is I_y [1 + cos(phi + 2 pi d y/(lambda D))], with phi the phase that depends on the screen position: the pattern is **shifted** in proportion to y.
2. Add over y from -b/2 to +b/2 (uniform intensity). With k = 2 pi d/(lambda D) the integral of cos(phi + k y) is cos(phi) sin(k b/2)/(k/2), so the total is

$$ I = I_T [ 1 + sinc(beta) cos(phi) ] ,   beta = pi b d/(lambda D) ,   sinc(beta) = sin(beta)/beta $$

3. The fringe visibility is therefore

$$ V = | sin(beta)/beta | ,   beta = pi b d/(lambda D) $$

This is the same mathematics as the Michelson result of module 4: there the quantity spread over a band was the wavenumber, here it is the position across the source.
4. The first zero is at b d = lambda D, that is d_c = lambda D/b = **lambda/theta_s**, where theta_s = b/D is the angular size of the source seen from the slits. We call d_c the **coherence width**. Good fringes need d much less than d_c; V = 0.9 when d = 0.25 d_c.

*Check:* b d and lambda D are both areas; b -> 0 or d -> 0 gives V = 1.

## Reading the book's equations

The book's two-edge argument says the patterns from the slit edges A and B cancel when shifted by half a fringe, b/D = lambda/(2d), and coincide when b/D = lambda/d (Eqs. 16.17 and 16.18 as read from the garbled text layer; check the page image). A uniform slit has its first zero at twice the two-edge value, lambda D/b. Both say: d must be small compared with about lambda divided by the angular size of the source.

## Numbers (recomputed)

lambda = 550 nm, D = 0.5 m. A source slit b = 0.1 mm gives d_c = 5.5 x 10^-7 x 0.5/10^-4 = 2.75 mm, and slits 0.5 mm apart give V = |sinc(0.571)| = 0.95. Widen the source slit to b = 1 mm: d_c = 0.275 mm and the same slits give beta = 5.71 and V = 0.095. For the Sun (angular diameter about 9.3 mrad; standard value; verify) the slit formula gives d_c about 59 um; for a circular disc the first zero is at 1.22 lambda/theta_s, about 72 um. So, to order of magnitude, only points within about 0.06 mm of one another on direct sunlight are well correlated.

A laser has a very small effective source angle: fringes appear with the slits lit directly (Brijlal p. 410).

### 6. Core: why laser light is coherent, and how coherent it really is (modes)

*Objective:* Explain, using stimulated emission, the resonator and the modes, why a laser has long coherence length and high spatial coherence, and give the numbers.

## Three ingredients

1. **Stimulated emission clones the photon.** A stimulated photon has the same frequency, direction, phase and polarisation as the one that caused it (Day 19 lesson PH-066, module on stimulated emission and coherence; Brijlal PDF pp. 620 to 621). The emitters are not independent: their fields add in a fixed phase relation. The book summarises this on PDF p. 649: a large number of identical photons are emitted in phase, so the light has a high degree of coherence.
2. **The resonator selects direction.** Two mirrors keep only light travelling along the axis (PDF p. 648), giving the narrow beam and a nearly plane wavefront, which is spatial coherence across the beam.
3. **The resonator selects frequency.** The standing waves of the cavity are spaced by c/(2nL) (Day 19 lesson PH-066); only the modes inside the gain line oscillate (Day 20 lesson PH-067, module on cavity modes). A few narrow modes give a bandwidth far narrower than the spontaneous line.

## Numbers (the book's figures converted; the conversion is the lesson's own)

- Sodium lamp: l_coh about 0.3 mm gives Delta nu about 10^12 Hz.
- Ordinary He-Ne laser: l_coh about 100 m gives Delta nu about 3 x 10^6 Hz (Chapter 22 figure; Chapter 16 says about 15 m, about 2 x 10^7 Hz).
- The ratio is about 3 x 10^5: a laser line is hundreds of thousands of times narrower.
- Cavity of length L = 30 cm: mode spacing c/(2L) = 5.0 x 10^8 Hz. The Doppler gain width of the neon line at 300 K is about 1.3 x 10^9 Hz (Day 20 lesson PH-067, module 8), so about three modes can oscillate (the 2018 mode count of PH-067). If all the gain width lased freely, c/Delta nu would be only 0.23 m; a long coherence length needs the laser to be forced onto a single narrow mode and kept stable.

## Single mode and multimode

Operating on several longitudinal modes gives more output power but a shorter coherence length: the light is a sum of fields of different frequencies, and the visibility in a Michelson interferometer then falls and returns as the path difference changes by multiples of the round trip 2L (standard knowledge; verify). The book remarks (PDF p. 638) that operating in multimode form provides considerably more power than single-mode operation. For spatial coherence the **transverse** mode matters: the lowest one, TEM00, has a smooth profile with one peak (PDF p. 638) and is the best case for focusing (module 8). Multimode transverse operation has several bright patches and a poorer focus (the same page; the focusing remark is standard knowledge; verify).

## A caution on the book's "< 10 Angstrom" monochromaticity

Brijlal (PDF p. 649) says laser light spans "a few angstroms (< 10 Angstrom)" against 100 to 1000 Angstrom for conventional lines. Your own numbers show a He-Ne line is about 0.02 Angstrom (the 2 x 10^-3 nm gain width) and a 3 MHz single mode about 4 x 10^-5 Angstrom. So "< 10 Angstrom" is a loose upper bound, not a typical width; use the numbers computed from Delta nu or l_coh when you need a value (PH-067 makes the same point).

## What this does not mean

Coherent does not mean "a single frequency". A laser line has a finite width Delta nu and a finite coherence length; PH-066 records the same caution.

### 7. Core: the divergence of a laser beam, near field and far field (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Estimate the divergence and beam radius of a laser from its aperture, wavelength and distance, state when the far-field rule applies and measure divergence from two spot sizes.

## What the book says

Brijlal (PDF p. 649, Section 22.16) states that a laser beam is nearly a bundle of parallel rays and that the small divergence that exists is due to diffraction at the exit mirror. A typical He-Ne divergence is 10^-3 rad, so the diameter grows by about 1 mm for every metre. The book measures it with two spot diameters d1 and d2 on a screen at distances l1 and l2: phi = (d2 - d1)/(l2 - l1) (Eq. 22.46). *Example:* d1 = 2 mm at 1 m and d2 = 6 mm at 3 m give phi = 4 mm/2 m = 2 x 10^-3 rad. This phi is the growth of the **diameter** per unit distance, a full-angle divergence.

## Derivation 3: far-field radius and intensity

**Assumptions.** A circular aperture of diameter D uniformly illuminated by a plane wave of wavelength lambda and power P, with the screen so far away that the pattern is the Fraunhofer (far-field) pattern; the first dark ring is taken as the beam edge.

1. From bridge B2 (Brijlal PDF p. 451; Day 17 lesson PH-057) the first dark ring lies at angle theta = 1.22 lambda/D.
2. At distance L the radius of the central disc is r(L) = L theta = 1.22 lambda L/D.
3. Its area is A = pi r^2 = pi (1.22 lambda L/D)^2.
4. If all the power were spread uniformly inside this disc the intensity would be I = P/A = P D^2/(pi (1.22)^2 lambda^2 L^2).

*Units:* theta is a number (radians); r is a length; I is power per area, W/m^2. The Airy disc actually carries 83.8 per cent of the power inside the first dark ring and is brightest at the centre (standard values; verify), so this I is an average over the disc, a convenient estimate.

## When does the far-field rule apply?

The diffraction pattern is already the far-field form at distances well beyond the **Fresnel distance** z_F = D^2/lambda (standard; verify): for D = 3 mm and lambda = 600 nm, z_F = 15 m; at 200 m, L/z_F = 13, comfortably far. For D = 1 mm and lambda = 632.8 nm, z_F = 1.6 m. At L much less than z_F the beam keeps roughly its aperture size.

## Numbers (recomputed)

D = 1 mm, lambda = 632.8 nm: theta = 7.7 x 10^-4 rad, and r = 7.7 mm at 10 m and 77 mm at 100 m (at 1 m, inside z_F = 1.6 m, the formula is not yet valid; the beam is still about the aperture size). The book's He-Ne value of 10^-3 rad is a full angle (1 mm of diameter per metre), that is a half-angle of 5 x 10^-4 rad; 1.22 lambda/D = 5 x 10^-4 gives an exit aperture of about 1.5 mm, which is consistent with its statement that diffraction at the exit mirror causes the spread.

## A larger beam spreads less

Since theta is proportional to 1/D, a lens pair that expands the beam to ten times the diameter reduces the divergence ten times, to 7.7 x 10^-5 rad for D = 10 mm (a beam expander; standard use). A laser aimed at a distant target is therefore sent out through a wide aperture.

## Gaussian beams (a note)

The beam of a laser in TEM00 mode has a bell-shaped (Gaussian) profile, not a uniform one. Its 1/e^2 divergence half-angle is lambda/(pi w0), with w0 the waist radius (standard; verify): for w0 = 0.5 mm and 632.8 nm, 4.0 x 10^-4 rad. The circular-aperture rule above is the "hard aperture" model that the exam questions use. If a question gives only an aperture width, state which model you use.

### 8. Core: focusing a laser beam, the spot size and the intensity P/lambda^2 (derivation 4)

*Objective:* Compute the diffraction-limited spot radius and intensity for a focused laser, explain the limit P/lambda^2 and say why a lamp cannot be focused as well.

## Derivation 4: the focused spot

**Assumptions.** A spatially coherent (TEM00-like) beam of diameter D and wavelength lambda falls on a lens of focal length f which it fills; the lens is free of aberrations (lens aberrations are Day 21 lesson PH-047); paraxial; the Airy model of module 7.

1. The lens turns the plane wave into a converging wave. Its focal-plane pattern is the Fraunhofer pattern of the aperture: a disc surrounded by rings.
2. From Brijlal Eq. 18.27 (PDF p. 451) the first dark ring in the focal plane is at the radius **r = 1.22 lambda f/D**.
3. If the power P is spread uniformly over the disc, the intensity at the focus is I = P/(pi r^2) = P D^2/(pi (1.22)^2 lambda^2 f^2).

*Units:* r in metres; I in W/m^2. *Limits:* a smaller f/D (a "faster" lens) gives a smaller spot. *Consistency:* the same 1.22 lambda/D as in module 7, applied at the focal plane instead of at a far distance.

## Numbers (recomputed)

For lambda = 632.8 nm and P = 1 mW:

| f/D | spot radius r | intensity I = P/(pi r^2) |
|---|---|---|
| 50 | 38.6 um | 2.1 x 10^5 W/m^2 |
| 20 | 15.4 um | 1.3 x 10^6 W/m^2 |
| 6.5 | 5.0 um | 1.3 x 10^7 W/m^2 |

The depth over which the focus stays sharp is of order lambda (f/D)^2 (standard; verify): about 27 um for f/D = 6.5 and 1.6 mm for f/D = 50. The book states that drilling can make holes as small as 10 um (PDF p. 650); a fast lens gives spots of a few micrometres, consistent with that.

## The limit P/lambda^2

Brijlal (PDF p. 649, Eq. 22.47) writes the intensity of a laser beam as I = (10/lambda)^2 P, and its Problem 6 (PDF p. 653) says a beam can be focused to an area of about lambda^2: for 1 mW of 6328 Angstrom light it prints I = 2.5 x 10^15 W/m^2. If a spot of area lambda^2 is meant, I = P/lambda^2 = 10^-3/(6.328 x 10^-7)^2 = **2.5 x 10^9 W/m^2**, six orders of magnitude below the printed number. The printed Eq. 22.47 does not reduce to P/lambda^2 with lambda in metres (it gives 2.5 x 10^11), and its units are unclear, so treat both the equation and the printed answer as a likely slip and check against the page image. In answers state the area you assume: a spot of radius about lambda has area about pi lambda^2 and a lens would need f/D near 1 to approach it.

## Why the limit

A converging beam can concentrate light only into a region about one wavelength across, because even a perfect lens spreads a point image by diffraction. So there is a ceiling intensity of order P/lambda^2, and real focused spots are larger by (r/lambda)^2.

## Why a laser can and a lamp cannot

The expression r = 1.22 lambda f/D holds for a wave that is coherent across the whole aperture D. A lamp is spatially incoherent: it has a coherence width d_c of order 0.06 mm for the Sun (module 5) and no lens, however good, can focus its light into a spot smaller than the **image** of the source. A laser is spatially coherent across its beam and is focused to the diffraction limit. The book (PDF p. 649) puts the point as: the intensity of a laser stays large because the beam is narrow, and a tungsten bulb would need about 4.6 x 10^6 K for the same intensity.

## Drilling and welding

Intensity in this range explains the book's applications (PDF p. 650): drilling by evaporation at the focus (pulsed ruby and neodymium lasers) and welding.

### 9. Master: coherence in use (holography, long-path interferometry) and the Doppler width as a coherence limit

*Objective:* State why holography needs a coherent source, relate the He-Ne Doppler width of the 2025 question to coherence length, and compare laser and ordinary light on coherence for the 2017 question.

## Holography needs coherence

Brijlal Chapter 23 (PDF pp. 655 and 659) says holography records the interference pattern between two beams of coherent light from the same source, a reference beam and an object beam, on a photographic plate, so that both amplitude and phase are stored; it required an intense coherent source and became practical only after the laser. Recording a hologram differs from photography in three ways: a laser illuminates the object, a second coherent beam (the reference) strikes the film and overlaps the object beam, and the film must resolve the fine pattern (PDF p. 659).

**The coherence conditions in numbers (the lesson's own analysis; verify).** The two beams reach the plate by different paths; the path difference must be less than l_coh or the fringes wash out (module 4). For an object 20 cm deep the extreme path differences can be near 40 cm, so l_coh should exceed about 0.4 m. A single-mode He-Ne laser with l_coh of tens of metres is ample; a multimode He-Ne with l_coh of about 0.3 m would be marginal (module 6). Spatial coherence matters because the object beam and the reference beam must each be coherent across the plate; a laser beam is (module 5).

## Interferometry at long path difference

Module 4 showed that a lamp gives fringes for 2d of about a millimetre to centimetre; a laser gives them over metres. This is why laser interferometers measure long distances; it follows directly from l_coh = lambda^2/Delta lambda.

## The 2025 question as a coherence statement

The Doppler full width of the He-Ne line with omega_0 = 3 x 10^15 s^-1 and T = 300 K, solved in Day 20 lesson PH-067 (module 8), is Delta omega_D = omega_0 sqrt(8 k T ln 2/(m c^2)) = 8.3 x 10^9 s^-1, recomputed here for neon-20: 8 k T ln 2 = 2.30 x 10^-20 J, m c^2 = 2.98 x 10^-9 J, ratio 7.7 x 10^-12, square root 2.77 x 10^-6, times 3 x 10^15 gives 8.3 x 10^9 s^-1. In ordinary frequency Delta nu_D = Delta omega_D/(2 pi) = 1.32 GHz. As a **coherence length**: c/Delta nu_D = 0.23 m. A lamp-like thermal line with this width would have l_coh of about 0.23 m (using the book's own convention l = c/Delta nu); a stabilised single-mode laser, with a line about 2 x 10^-3 of the Doppler width (3 MHz against 1.32 GHz), reaches 100 m. The Doppler width is thus the bandwidth the gas could radiate; the cavity narrows what comes out. The conversion is the lesson's own.

## 2026 Q3(c), 15 marks: intensity at 200 m

"The aperture width of a laser light source of wavelength 6000 Angstrom is 3 mm and its power is 20 mW. Calculate the light intensity at a distance of 200 m from the light source."

**Reading to state.** The stem gives no beam model. The standard one is diffraction at a circular aperture of diameter D = 3 mm, taking the "aperture width" as the diameter of the beam leaving the laser.

**Solution (recomputed in Python).**

1. lambda = 6.0 x 10^-7 m, D = 3.0 x 10^-3 m, P = 2.0 x 10^-2 W, L = 200 m.
2. Fresnel distance z_F = D^2/lambda = 15 m, so 200 m is far field (L/z_F = 13).
3. Half-angle to the first dark ring: theta = 1.22 lambda/D = 2.44 x 10^-4 rad.
4. Beam radius: r = L theta = 4.88 x 10^-2 m. Area A = pi r^2 = 7.48 x 10^-3 m^2.
5. Intensity averaged over the disc: **I = P/A = 2.7 W/m^2**.
6. Check of the units and size: W divided by m^2; at this distance the beam is 9.8 cm across, and 20 mW over 75 cm^2 is 0.27 mW/cm^2.

**Variants (state the one you use).** With the cruder estimate theta = lambda/D, r = 4.0 cm and I = 4.0 W/m^2. If the central Airy disc is regarded as carrying 83.8 per cent of the power, the mean intensity inside the first dark ring is 2.2 W/m^2, and the on-axis peak of the far-field pattern is P pi D^2/(4 lambda^2 L^2) = 9.8 W/m^2 (standard result; verify). If the 3 mm is the 1/e^2 diameter of a Gaussian beam (w0 = 1.5 mm), the beam radius at 200 m is 2.55 cm and the peak intensity is 2P/(pi w^2) = 20 W/m^2. Textbook "areal spread" reading: d theta = lambda/D = 2 x 10^-4 rad, areal spread (L d theta)^2 = 1.6 x 10^-3 m^2, I = 12.5 W/m^2 (common in engineering-physics texts; verify). The readings span 2.2 to 20 W/m^2; choose, state the model and keep the units.

### Notes: Definitions

- Coherence: a fixed (predictable) phase relation between the light at two points or two times; measured by fringe visibility V = (I_max - I_min)/(I_max + I_min).
- Wave train (wave packet): the finite burst of waves emitted by an atom in about 10^-8 s; a lamp emits a jumble of independent trains.
- Coherence time t_coh and coherence length l_coh = c t_coh: the time and distance over which the phase of the wave is predictable.
- Temporal (longitudinal) coherence: phase relation along the beam, measured by l_coh; related to the bandwidth. Spatial (lateral) coherence: phase relation across the wavefront; related to the size of the source.
- Coherence width: the separation d_c = lambda/theta_s of two points across the beam within which they are well correlated; theta_s is the angular size of the source.
- Degree of monochromaticity: xi = Delta nu/nu = Delta lambda/lambda = 1/N.
- Diffraction-limited beam: its divergence is set only by the aperture D, theta = 1.22 lambda/D for a circular aperture.

### Notes: Equations

- l = c Delta t; N = l/lambda; l_coh = c t_coh (Brijlal Eqs. 16.1, 16.2, 16.5).
- Delta nu = 1/t_coh = c/l_coh; l_coh = lambda^2/Delta lambda; N = lambda/Delta lambda (Eqs. 16.7, 16.11, 16.13).
- Michelson visibility, rectangular line: V = |sin(pi x/l_coh)/(pi x/l_coh)|, x = 2d (Day 18 lesson PH-051).
- Two-slit visibility, uniform source slit of width b at distance D: V = |sin(beta)/beta|, beta = pi b d/(lambda D); coherence width d_c = lambda D/b.
- Divergence phi = (d2 - d1)/(l2 - l1) (Eq. 22.46); circular aperture theta = 1.22 lambda/D; far-field radius r = 1.22 lambda L/D; Fresnel distance z_F = D^2/lambda (verify).
- Focused spot radius r = 1.22 lambda f/D; I = P/(pi r^2); ceiling I of order P/lambda^2.

### Core concepts

- A lamp emits independent finite wave trains; its coherence length is the length of a train.
- Bandwidth and coherence length are reciprocal: Delta nu = c/l_coh, l_coh = lambda^2/Delta lambda.
- Temporal coherence is about the bandwidth; spatial coherence is about the size of the source.
- A laser is coherent because stimulated emission clones the photons and the resonator selects direction and frequency.
- A beam of width D spreads by about 1.22 lambda/D and can be focused to a spot of radius 1.22 lambda f/D.

### Memory skeleton

- Wave trains: an atom emits for about 10⁻⁸ s, a 3 m train; random phase between trains, so independent lamps never interfere
- l_coh = c t_coh; Δν = 1/t_coh = c/l_coh; l_coh = λ²/Δλ; N = λ/Δλ
- Temporal coherence (along the beam, set by bandwidth): Michelson, fringes vanish at 2d = l_coh
- Spatial coherence (across the wavefront, set by source size): two slits behind a source slit; fringes fade as the slit widens
- Laser coherence: stimulated emission clones photons; the resonator selects direction and frequency
- Divergence: diffraction at the exit mirror; He-Ne about 10⁻³ rad, a full angle, φ = (d₂ − d₁)/(l₂ − l₁)
- Focusing: a coherent beam reaches r = 1.22λf/D; a lamp only the image of its source
