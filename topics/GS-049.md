### Bridge: Words you need before the INA story

Seven terms carry the whole topic.

- **Axis powers**: Germany, Italy and Japan, the enemies of Britain in the Second World War. Spectrum notes that Bose accepted help from them as a matter of strategy.
- **Prisoner of war (POW)**: a soldier captured by the enemy. The INA was first built from Indian soldiers of the British army captured by Japan in Malaya and Singapore.
- **Fauj**: army. *Azad Hind Fauz* (or Fauj) means the Army of Free India, the INA's other name.
- **Provisional government in exile**: a government that claims to rule a country but sits abroad. Bose's *Azad Hind* government was proclaimed in Singapore, not on Indian soil.
- **Supreme Commander**: the head of an army. Bose held this post of the INA from 25 August 1943 (Spectrum).
- **Court-martial**: a military court that tries soldiers. The INA prisoners were to be court-martialled for waging war against the King (the standard charge; verify).
- **Forward Bloc**: the party Bose founded in 1939 inside the Congress after his break with Gandhi (module 2).

Remember the main line: the INA fought and failed as an army, but as a *cause* it succeeded after 1945.

### 1. Orientation: the INA on one page

*Objective:* Hold the INA story in three phases and three questions before details.

## The story in a paragraph

The **Indian National Army (INA)**, or Azad Hind Fauz, was an army of Indian prisoners of war and civilians raised in South-East Asia with Japanese help during the Second World War. It had two phases: a first INA under **Mohan Singh** in 1942, and a second under **Subhas Chandra Bose**, who took over in 1943, formed the Azad Hind government in October that year and led the INA towards Imphal and Kohima in 1944. The army failed; Japan surrendered in August 1945. But when the INA prisoners were brought home to be court-martialled at the Red Fort, a powerful movement grew in their defence and became one of the forces that made British rule untenable.

### 2. Bose before the INA: career, views and the break with Gandhi

*Objective:* Describe Bose's career to 1939 and explain the Haripura, 1939 election and Tripuri sequence that led him out of the Congress presidency.

## Career to 1938

Spectrum describes **Subhas Chandra Bose** as intrepid, with a militant streak. He passed the Indian Civil Service examination with fourth position but resigned in **1921** to join the freedom struggle in the Congress. His political guru was **Chittaranjan Das**. Spectrum says he became mayor of Calcutta in 1923 (standard accounts give 1924 for his post as Chief Executive Officer of the Calcutta Corporation and 1930 for the mayoralty; verify). He was jailed many times. He worked for the youth and trade unions as president of the Bengal Provincial Congress Committee, opposed the Motilal Nehru Report's dominion status, announced an Independence League, welcomed the Lahore resolution for Poorna Swaraj, took part in the Salt Satyagraha (1930) and opposed the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931), especially because Bhagat Singh's death sentence was not negotiated.

**Haripura, February 1938.** He was unanimously elected president. He called the Congress provincial ministries rich in revolutionary potential, spoke of planned economic development (the National Planning Committee followed), and the session gave moral support to people's struggles in the princely states.

## The 1939 contest

In January 1939 Bose stood again. Gandhi was unhappy. Bose said he represented new ideas and programmes born of the sharpening anti-imperialist struggle. Patel, Rajendra Prasad, Kripalani and others replied that ideology was shaped in bodies such as the working committee and that the president was a symbol of unity; they backed **Pattabhi Sitaramayya**. Bose won **1,580 votes to 1,377**, with the support of the Congress Socialists and communists. Gandhi's remark: Pattabhi's defeat is my defeat. It became a Gandhi versus Bose issue.

## Tripuri, March 1939

The working committee is nominated by the president, so the election was a real choice of direction. Bose had accused the old leaders of readiness to compromise on federation, and they resigned from the committee. At **Tripuri** (near Jabalpur), Bose, ill, forecast an imperialist war in Europe and wanted a **six-month ultimatum** to Britain for independence, followed by mass civil disobedience if rejected. Gandhi felt neither the Congress nor the masses were ready, and was aware of communal discord and class strife. Govind Ballabh Pant's resolution reaffirmed faith in Gandhian policies and asked Bose to name the working committee in accordance with Gandhi's wishes; it passed without opposition from socialists or communists. Gandhi declined to impose a committee, saying Bose should choose it. Bose did not succeed in winning Gandhi's confidence and refused to nominate a committee; Gandhi wrote that their views were so opposed he saw no way to bridge them.

## Why Bose lost his base

Spectrum says Bose misjudged his support. Even the socialists and communists mostly did not want a split, since it would reduce the Left (not very consolidated) to a splinter group; they preferred a united Congress under Gandhi. Bose **resigned in April 1939**, and Rajendra Prasad became president.

## The Forward Bloc

In **May 1939** Bose and his followers formed the **Forward Bloc** as a party within the Congress (Spectrum names Makur, Unnao; the place is to be verified). After he called an all-India protest on 9 July against an AICC resolution, the Working Committee acted: in **August 1939** he was removed from the presidency of the Bengal Provincial Congress Committee and debarred from elective Congress office for three years.

The Gandhi-Bose comparison is built in module 8. The 1939 election also appears in GS-053 module 7.

### 3. From the Forward Bloc to the escape and Berlin, 1940 to 1942

*Objective:* Trace Bose from Ramgarh to his escape and his work in Germany.

## Ramgarh and the anti-war stand, 1940

In **March 1940** Bose convened an **Anti-Compromise Conference at Ramgarh**, a joint effort of the Forward Bloc and the Kisan Sabha. It resolved to launch a worldwide struggle on **6 April**, the first day of National Week, calling on people to give the imperialist war no help in men, money or materials, and to resist exploitation of Indian resources for the imperial cause. People took part enthusiastically.

## Arrest and escape

Bose was arrested in July 1940 when he tried a satyagraha against a proposed monument in Calcutta to Holwell. After a hunger strike he was released and placed under **house arrest in December 1940**. In **January 1941** it was reported that he had escaped. Spectrum says he reached Peshawar on 26 January 1941 under the assumed name Ziauddin, helped by Bhagat Ram, and later left India 'to supplement from outside the struggle going on at home'. Other books give a different sequence of dates in January and the route through Kabul (GS-053 module 8 follows that standard version); treat the exact dates as verify. NCERT Class VIII states the year, 1941.

## Russia, then Germany

Spectrum says he approached Russia for help, was disappointed when Russia joined the Allies in **June 1941**, and then went to **Germany**. Standard accounts give a different order: he left Kabul for Moscow on an Italian passport as 'Orlando Mazzotta' and reached Berlin in **early April 1941**, before Germany attacked the USSR on 22 June 1941. The German attack on Russia was a setback for his plans in Germany, not the reason he went there (verify). Spectrum says he met Hitler under the name Orlando Mazzotta; standard accounts use that as his travel alias of 1941 and date the meeting with Hitler to May 1942 (verify). With German help a **Freedom Army (Mukti Sena)** was formed of Indian prisoners of war captured by Germany and Italy, with Dresden as its office. The people of Germany began to call him **Netaji**. From the Free India Centre he gave the slogan **'Jai Hind'** (some books credit others with the slogan; verify). From **January 1942** he broadcast from Berlin radio, which enthused Indians.

## What this phase shows

- Bose believed Britain's war was India's opportunity. Spectrum says he openly criticised Britain for claiming to fight for the freedom of European nations under Nazi control while refusing India independence.
- He accepted help from the Axis powers as strategy. Spectrum records that his writings disapproved of Nazi racialism and the suppression of democracy, while he admired Nazi and Fascist discipline; supporters say his links to Germany and Japan were a revolutionary strategy, not ideological kinship, and that he opposed racial superiority and Fascist acceptance of capitalism (module 8 develops the debate).
- The Congress, by contrast, was moving towards Quit India (GS-048), a non-violent movement.

## Leaving Europe

Spectrum says that in early 1943 he left Germany and travelled by German and then Japanese submarines to reach Japan and then Singapore in July 1943, to take over the movement from Rashbehari Bose. The details (who gave which submarine, the transfer at sea) are standard knowledge and should be checked before use. The takeover belongs to module 5; first module 4 explains what he found waiting.

### 4. Rash Behari Bose, Mohan Singh and the first INA, 1942

*Objective:* Explain the origin of the INA, the role of Rash Behari Bose and the Indian Independence League, and why the first INA broke down.

## Rash Behari Bose in Japan

Rash Behari Bose (spelt Rashbehari in Spectrum) was a leading revolutionary: GS-039 module 4 covers his role in the Delhi bomb of 1912 and the Ghadr plans, and Spectrum calls him one of the leading figures of the Ghadr revolution. When the 1915 plans failed he escaped to Japan, where he later became a naturalised citizen. He worked to interest the Japanese in Indian independence, became active in Pan-Asian circles, founded the **Indian Club of Tokyo** and lectured on the evils of Western imperialism. Spectrum says he also **founded the Indian Independence League in 1942 in Tokyo**.

## Mohan Singh and the idea

The idea of an army of Indian prisoners of war was originally that of **Mohan Singh** (Captain Mohan Singh: rank standard; Spectrum says 'an Indian army officer'), who decided not to join the retreating British army in Malaya. He turned to the Japanese, who until then had encouraged Indian civilians to form anti-British organisations. After the **fall of Singapore** the Japanese handed over Indian POWs and several were ready to join. By the end of **1942**, Spectrum says, 40,000 men were ready to join. The INA was meant to go into action only on the invitation of the Indian National Congress and the people of India. Many read it as a check on Japanese misconduct towards Indians in South-East Asia and a bulwark against future Japanese occupation of India.

## Bangkok and the League

When the INA was formed by Mohan Singh in Singapore, Rash Behari Bose left Tokyo for South-East Asia. At a conference in **Bangkok**, under Japanese occupation, it was decided to place the INA under the **Indian Independence League** with Rash Behari Bose as chairman. (Standard sources date the Tokyo and Bangkok conferences to March and June 1942; verify.)

## The first division and the breakdown

The INA got a boost from the Quit India Movement (GS-048). In **September 1942** the first division was formed with **16,300 men** (Spectrum). Then serious differences arose between Mohan Singh and the Indian officers and the Japanese about the INA's role: the Japanese wanted a token force of **2,000**, Mohan Singh wanted a much larger army. Mohan Singh was **taken into custody by the Japanese**. This ends the first phase. (Standard sources date the dissolution to December 1942; verify.)

## Why it matters

- The first INA shows the structure: Indian officers, Japanese support, a political League above the army.
- The core problem was control. Who decides when and how the INA fights, and who commands it? The Japanese and the Indians answered differently.
- Rash Behari Bose's spadework, in Spectrum's words, is what Subhas Bose built on, so the second phase is a handover, not a fresh start.

Numbers are as Spectrum prints them; they are not independently verified here (flagged in the completeness table).

### 5. Bose takes command: the Azad Hind government, 1943

*Objective:* Describe the handover of 1943, the Provisional Government of Free India, the Rani of Jhansi Regiment and the character of the INA.

## Arrival and handover

Bose reached Tokyo (Spectrum says **June 1943**; standard accounts give May 1943, with the meetings with Tojo in June; verify) under the alias Abid Hussain and met the Japanese Prime Minister **Tojo**. He then went to Singapore. Rash Behari Bose transferred the leadership of the Indian Independence League and the INA to him in **July 1943**, and on **25 August 1943** Subhas Bose became **Supreme Commander** of the INA. Spectrum adds that Rash Behari Bose died on 21 January 1945, aged 58.

## The Provisional Government of Free India, 21 October 1943

At **Singapore**, Bose formed the Provisional Government of Azad Hind with colleagues such as **H.C. Chatterjee** (finance), **M.A. Aiyar** (broadcasting) and **Lakshmi Swaminathan** (women's department). He held the posts of head of state, prime minister, and minister for war and foreign affairs, and Supreme Commander. Spectrum notes that some scholars see an authoritarian streak here. The government **declared war on Britain and the United States** and was **recognised by the Axis powers**. Recruits were trained, funds collected, and the war cry was 'Chalo Delhi'. Spectrum says the slogan 'Give me blood, I will give you freedom' was given in Malaya; standard accounts place it at a rally in Burma (Rangoon) on 4 July 1944 (verify).

## Islands and symbols

On **6 November 1943** the Japanese handed the **Andaman and Nicobar islands** to the INA, renamed **Shahid Dweep** and **Swaraj Dweep**. (Spectrum describes this as a handover to the INA; the real administration is commonly debated, verify.) The INA's headquarters moved to **Rangoon** in **January 1944**.

## The Rani of Jhansi Regiment

Bose formed a **women's regiment in 1943**, named after the Rani of Jhansi and commanded by **Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan** (Sahgal after marriage: she married Prem Kumar Sahgal, spelt Sehgal in Spectrum's trial account, in 1947; standard, verify). Those less suited to combat served as nurses or in support roles; the majority were trained as soldiers, with the same treatment as men and no special privileges. Spectrum calls this a radical view in Bose's thinking, which held women to be equals of men and set up a women's sub-committee on planning in 1938.

## An army without caste or creed

Spectrum describes the INA as a mix of religions, races and castes with total social equality: common kitchen, common barracks, common celebration of all religious festivals. It was called Azad Hind Fauz, not by any Hindu name, and many non-Hindus were close to Bose. Bose, in Spectrum's account, held that the state should be neutral towards religion and that economic issues cut across communal lines.

## Gandhi, 6 July 1944

From Azad Hind Radio on 6 July 1944 Bose addressed Gandhi as **Father of the Nation** and asked his blessings for 'India's last war of independence'. Spectrum says he was the first to use the phrase (it also places a Rangoon broadcast of 1944 in its Gandhi-Bose discussion; verify the exact occasion). In the same speech he held that force was the only way to win freedom.

## The shape of the INA

- **Leaders**: Bose as Supreme Commander; officers such as Shah Nawaz.
- **Members**: former POWs, plus civilians recruited among Indians in South-East Asia (the civilian share is standard knowledge, verify).
- **Support**: Japanese arms and rations, though the relationship was strained (module 6).
- **Politics**: a government, a radio, a flag and a regiment of women, designed to make the INA a national army, not a Japanese auxiliary.

### 6. The campaign of 1944 and the end of the INA

*Objective:* Narrate the march to Imphal and Kohima, the withdrawal, the surrender and Bose's reported death with the right caveats.

## The march

The INA's headquarters moved to Rangoon in January 1944 and the soldiers were to march from there with the war cry 'Chalo Delhi'. One battalion commanded by **Shah Nawaz** was allowed to accompany the Japanese army to the Indo-Burma front and take part in the **Imphal campaign**. The INA crossed the Burma border and stood on Indian soil on **18 March 1944** (Spectrum). Units advanced up to Kohima and Imphal. On **14 April** Colonel Malik of the Bahadur Group hoisted the INA flag for the first time on the Indian mainland at **Moirang, Manipur**, where an INA memorial now stands. For three months the INA ran military administration there, until the Allied forces reclaimed the territory.

## Why it failed

The extract points to two reasons. First, the INA met the same fate as the Japanese: all brigades began their **withdrawal on 18 July 1944**, and the steady Japanese retreat quashed hope of liberating the nation; the retreat continued until mid-1945. Second, **discriminatory treatment by the Japanese**: Indians were denied rations and arms and made to do menial work, which disgusted and demoralised the INA units. Behind both was the problem from the first phase: the Japanese used the INA as a supporting force, while Bose meant it as a national army.

## Surrender and Bose's reported death

On **15 August 1945** Japan announced its surrender (the formal signing was on 2 September, standard) and with this the INA surrendered. Spectrum says Bose **reportedly** died mysteriously in an air crash at Taipei (Taiwan) on **18 August 1945**. The circumstances remain a matter of public controversy; treat the date and manner as reported and verify. When Gandhi heard of the reported death he called Bose's patriotism second to none (module 8). In an exam, write 'reportedly' and do not argue the mystery.

## Numbers and claims to keep cautious

- 40,000 men ready to join (end 1942) and 16,300 in the first division (September 1942) are Spectrum's figures for the first phase.
- The strength at the front in 1944, casualties and the number captured are not in the extracts; do not state them (verify).
- NCERT Class VIII says only that the INA tried to enter India through Imphal and Kohima in 1944 and the campaign failed.

## Military verdict

Militarily the campaign was small and failed. Its importance lay elsewhere: the INA had planted the flag on Indian soil, shown an army of Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs fighting together under an Indian command, and when the war ended its soldiers became the focus of a national cause. That story is the next two modules.

### 7. The Red Fort trials, the INA agitation and the naval revolt

*Objective:* Explain how the trials produced a mass movement, what the three upsurges were, how the RIN revolt ended and what concessions followed.

## The British decision

The INA prisoners were brought back to be court-martialled. The British first decided to hold public trials of several hundreds, dismiss them from service and detain without trial about **7,000**. Spectrum says they compounded the folly by holding the first trial at the **Red Fort, Delhi, in November 1945**, with a Hindu (**Prem Kumar Sehgal**), a Muslim (**Shah Nawaz Khan**) and a Sikh (**Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon**) in the dock together. (The verdicts and sentences are standard knowledge: all three were convicted and transported for life, and the sentences were then remitted; verify.)

## Congress and the defence

At the first post-war Congress session at Bombay in **September 1945** (Spectrum calls it a Congress session; it was a meeting of the AICC, verify) a strong resolution supported the INA cause. The defence was organised by **Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, Kailash Nath Katju, Jawaharlal Nehru and Asaf Ali**.

## A landmark agitation

Spectrum calls the INA agitation a landmark on many counts. Its pitch was unprecedented: editorials, pamphlets, graffiti, public meetings, **INA Week (5 to 11 November 1945)** and **INA Day (12 November 1945)**. The nerve centres were the big cities, but it reached Coorg, Baluchistan and Assam. People gave money from film stars to tongawallas; shops closed; students boycotted classes; peasants and women's bodies joined. Those who supported the cause beyond the Congress included the **Muslim League, Communist Party, Unionists, Akalis, Ahrars in Rawalpindi, Justice Party, Hindu Mahasabha, RSS and the Sikh League** (as Spectrum lists them). Even government employees, titled loyalists and servicemen gave sympathy and funds. The central theme became the question of Britain's right to decide a matter concerning Indians, which gave the issue an Indian versus British colour. Spectrum notes that the upsurges were short-lived and confined to a few cities, while the general INA agitation reached the remotest villages.

## Three upsurges, winter 1945 to 46

1. **21 November 1945, Calcutta**: a student procession against the trials; lathi-charge and firing, two deaths.
2. **11 February 1946, Calcutta**: protest against the seven-year sentence on INA officer **Rashid Ali**.
3. **18 February 1946, Bombay**: strike by Royal Indian Navy ratings.

Spectrum's three-stage pattern: a group defies authority and is repressed; city people join with strikes and hartals; other parts of the country express solidarity.

## The RIN revolt

On 18 February 1946 about **1,100 ratings of HMIS Talwar** struck against racial discrimination in pay, poor food, abuse by superiors, the arrest of a rating who had scrawled 'Quit India', the **INA trials** and the use of Indian troops in Indonesia. They hoisted the Congress tricolour, the League's crescent and the communist hammer and sickle together. Sympathetic strikes spread to military establishments from Karachi to Aden and to the Air Force. **Patel and Jinnah** persuaded the ratings to surrender on **23 February** with an assurance against victimisation. The Congress did not officially support it because of its tactics and timing; Gandhi said that if the men mutinied for India's freedom they were doubly wrong, and if they had grievances they should have awaited their leaders.

## Concessions

Spectrum lists the concessions: only INA men accused of murder or brutal treatment would be tried; sentences on the first batch remitted; Indian troops withdrawn from Indo-China and Indonesia; a parliamentary delegation and the **Cabinet Mission** decided on. Spectrum prints the years as 1 December 1946, January 1947, February 1947 and November 1946. These cannot be right: the concessions answered the upsurges of 1945-46, and the same list dates the Cabinet Mission decision to January 1946 (another page says February 1946). Standard accounts give 1 December 1945 for the limit on trials and January 1946 for both the remission and the delegation's visit. The withdrawal date is to verify. The Cabinet Mission is in GS-051 (Partition, Cabinet Mission and Independence Act; to come on Day 15, not yet written).

### 8. Master: Gandhi and Bose compared, and the evaluation

*Objective:* Compare Gandhi and Bose on method and ideas as Spectrum does, and give a balanced judgement of the INA's impact.

## Respect with disagreement

Spectrum stresses mutual respect: in 1942 Gandhi called Bose the 'Prince among the Patriots', and on the report of his death said his patriotism was second to none. At Tripuri Bose said he would yield to none in his respect for Gandhi. Both disliked communism, worked against untouchability and spoke for women's emancipation.

## The comparison table

| Theme | Gandhi | Bose |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Non-violence and satyagraha; masses unarmed | Force needed alongside civil disobedience; total revolution |
| Means and ends | Means must be as good as ends; would not ally with Nazis or Japan | Used any opportunity; accepted Axis help as strategy |
| Government | Village republics, decentralised, little State | Authoritarian State at first, then liberty and democracy; a planned socialist economy |
| Military | Minimal, for defence; war brutalises | Drawn to military discipline; women as soldiers |
| Economy | Trusteeship, small-scale, village industry | Industrialisation, state ownership of heavy industry, with cottage industry kept |
| Religion | Religion the basis of politics; all paths one | Spiritual but secular state; INA free of caste |
| Caste | Against untouchability, kept varna | Casteless, classless society |
| Women | Brought them into the struggle; complementary roles | Equal, trained as soldiers |
| Education | Vernacular, craft-centred (GS-035 module 7) | Higher, technical and scientific education for an industrial India; students trained abroad to a plan (Spectrum PDF 484) |

## Spectrum's cautions

- Bose called his political-economic goal *samyavada*, a synthesis of socialism and fascist efficiency (1930), but Spectrum says he was neither Nazi nor communist: he backed secularism, women's empowerment and liberal ideas.
- Spectrum quotes his 1944 view that economic reform on socialist lines needs an authoritarian State, and notes that he took all top posts in 1943; some scholars see an authoritarian streak.
- Bose, in the Tokyo speech, admitted that Gandhi's method had greatly helped rouse and unify the Indian people.

## Evaluation of the INA

Arguments for a large impact (Spectrum): the agitation for the INA's release was unprecedented in pitch; it united diverse groups and parties across the country; it questioned Britain's right to decide; government employees and soldiers sympathised; officials feared another Congress revolt, this time with INA men's military experience. The RIN revolt was seen as an event marking the end of British rule.

Arguments for limits (Spectrum): the INA was militarily defeated; the upsurges were violent, short-lived and in a few cities; communal unity was more organisational than among the people; the British repressive machine remained intact. Spectrum (PDF 513) lists why the government's attitude changed in 1945: a shift in global power, a sympathetic Labour government, socialist-radical governments in Europe, weary soldiers and a shattered economy, an anti-imperialist wave in South-East Asia, officials' fear of a 1942-type revolt joined by INA men, and inevitable elections. It concludes that the British would have had to retreat and Labour only quickened the process. Hence a balanced answer says the INA was **one** of several causes, with a powerful moral and political effect on the army, the services and public opinion rather than a military effect.

### 9. Exam: the two verified questions and answer frames

*Objective:* Use the lesson to analyse the 2021 and 2026 Prelims questions and plan Mains answers on the INA.

## Likely Mains questions

1. **The INA and the end of British rule.** Frame: origin (two phases), Bose's role, Azad Hind government, campaign, trials, agitation, concessions, assessment (module 8).
2. **Gandhi versus Bose.** Frame: respect, then method, means, State, economy, religion, women; end with complementary roles in the freedom struggle.
3. **Bose's break with the Congress.** Frame: 1938 to 1939 sequence, ideological gap, Forward Bloc.

## Prelims traps

- Two INA phases: do not give Bose the 1942 INA or Mohan Singh the 1943 government.
- Bose escaped in 1941 (the year is secure); the exact January date varies by book.
- 'Reportedly' for the air crash; no exact casualty or strength figures.
- The INA trials are post-war (November 1945), not wartime.
