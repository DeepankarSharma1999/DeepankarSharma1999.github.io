## GS-293: Communicable diseases and vaccines

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Science · General science / technology

*Goal:* Explain Communicable diseases and vaccines and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Pathogens and transmission routes

Typhoid fever is caused by the bacterium Salmonella typhi, which enters the small intestine through contaminated food and water and spreads via blood; sustained high fever is typical and the Widal test confirms it. Pneumonia, caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae, fills the alveoli with fluid and spreads through droplets or shared utensils. Rhinoviruses cause the common cold, spread by droplets and contaminated objects. Malaria is protozoan: Plasmodium sporozoites enter through the bite of an infected female Anopheles, multiply in liver cells and then burst red blood cells, releasing haemozoin that causes recurring chills and fever.

### Prevention depends on the route

For food- and water-borne infections such as typhoid, amoebiasis and ascariasis, safe disposal of waste, clean water reservoirs and hygienic catering are essential. For airborne diseases, close contact with patients and their belongings should be avoided. For vector-borne diseases like malaria and filariasis, the key is controlling mosquitoes and their breeding places: no stagnant water, cleaned coolers, bed nets, larva-eating fish such as Gambusia and insecticide spraying. Aedes-borne dengue and chikungunya make this urgent in India. Vaccination eradicated smallpox and has largely controlled polio, diphtheria, pneumonia and tetanus, while antibiotics treat many bacterial infections.

### How immunity and vaccines work

Active immunity arises when the body meets antigens, through infection or deliberate immunisation, and makes its own antibodies; it is slow to reach full effect. Passive immunity is given by ready-made antibodies, such as IgA-rich colostrum for newborns or maternal antibodies crossing the placenta. Vaccines introduce antigenic proteins or inactivated or weakened pathogens; they induce antibodies and memory B and T cells that respond quickly on later exposure. When an immediate response is needed, as in tetanus or snakebite, preformed antibodies or antitoxin are injected, which is passive immunisation. Recombinant DNA methods now produce antigens in microbes, for example the yeast-derived hepatitis B vaccine.
