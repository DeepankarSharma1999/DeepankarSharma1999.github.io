## PH-062: Optical activity

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### 1. Orientation: what optical activity is, what the earlier lessons already give, and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Say in one sentence what optical activity is, name the three earlier lessons this one builds on, and list the five things this lesson teaches.

## The meaning in one sentence

**Optical activity** is the power of certain substances to turn the plane of vibration of plane-polarised light about the direction in which the light travels, while the light stays plane polarised. Crystals such as quartz and cinnabar do it, and so do solutions of sugar and tartaric acid (Brijlal Section 20.27, PDF pp. 546 to 547). Light goes in vibrating along one line and comes out vibrating along another line, turned by an angle that grows with the thickness of the substance.

## Words to know before module 2

- **Plane (linearly) polarised light:** the electric vector E vibrates along one fixed line in the plane at right angles to the beam. The **plane of polarisation** is named by that line and the beam direction.
- **Polariser and analyser:** two polarising devices. The first makes light plane polarised; the second tests it. In the **crossed** position their transmission directions are at 90 degrees and no light gets through.
- **Circularly polarised light:** E has constant length and turns steadily, so its tip draws a circle. It can turn clockwise or anticlockwise.
- **Optic axis:** the one direction in a uniaxial crystal such as quartz or calcite along which the two rays of double refraction travel with the same speed.
- **Refractive index** is written μ in the book and in this lesson (μ = c / speed in the medium).

- Day 21 lesson PH-061 ('Double refraction and quarter-wave plate') and Day 17 lesson PH-207 explain the polariser, the analyser, the optic axis and the half-wave plate. They are used, not repeated.

### 2. Foundation: the crossed-polariser experiment, the two signs and what optical activity is not

*Objective:* Describe the experiment that detects optical rotation, say how the rotation angle is read off, and tell dextrorotatory from laevorotatory.

## The experiment (Brijlal Section 20.28, PDF p. 547)

Put a polariser P and an analyser A in the crossed position and view a lamp through them: the field is dark. Now slip in a quartz plate cut with its faces perpendicular to the optic axis, so that the light travels along the axis (normal incidence). The field **lights up**. Turn the analyser until the field is dark again. The angle you turned it through is the angle by which the plane of polarisation was rotated.

```
 lamp → [ P ] → [ quartz plate ] → [ A ] → eye
          |        optic axis along      |
          |        the beam              |
   P and A crossed, no plate:  DARK
   plate inserted:             BRIGHT   (plane has turned)
   turn A by θ:            DARK     (θ = rotation of the plane)
```

Two conclusions follow. The light that leaves the plate is **still plane polarised**, because a rotated analyser can extinguish it completely. And the plane has turned through an angle that you can measure directly with the graduated analyser.

## Looking toward the source: the sign convention

The turning has a sense, so we need a viewpoint. This lesson uses the same rule as PH-060: the observer looks **back toward the source**, so the beam comes toward the eye. Turning **clockwise** for that observer is **right-handed** or **dextrorotatory** (sign +); turning **anticlockwise** is **left-handed** or **laevorotatory** (sign -) (book, PDF p. 548: 'toward the right' and 'toward the left'). The extract does not spell out the viewpoint; the one given here is the standard one in physics texts (verify against your own notes).

Quartz occurs in both hands: right-handed crystals and left-handed crystals are mirror images of each other. The biquartz plate of module 8 uses one of each.

## Crystals and solutions

| | Crystal (quartz, cinnabar) | Solution (sugar, tartaric acid) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause, as the book puts it (PDF p. 546) | twisted arrangement of the atomic layers | structure and symmetry of the molecules |
| Law (module 3) | θ = α l | θ = s c l |
| Beam direction | along the optic axis (no double refraction there) | any; the liquid is isotropic |

## What optical activity is not

- **Not double refraction.** Light travelling along the optic axis of quartz is not split into an ordinary and an extraordinary ray; there is a single beam, still linear.
- **Not absorption.** The beam is not weakened by the turning itself.
- **Not the Faraday effect.** An ordinary glass has no natural rotation, but in a magnetic field parallel to the beam it rotates the plane (θ = V l H, book PDF p. 557). Optical activity needs no field; it is a property of the substance (module 6 compares them).

## A fast self-test

If the field is dark when A is at 0 degrees without the plate and dark when A is at 30 degrees clockwise (facing the source) with the plate in, the plate is dextrorotatory with a rotation of +30 degrees. If A has to be turned 30 degrees anticlockwise, the sign is - . The analyser turns in the same sense as the plane did.

### 3. Core: the laws of rotation and specific rotation, with units

*Objective:* State what the rotation depends on, write θ = α l and θ = s c l, define specific rotation with its units, and rearrange for concentration and for mixtures.

## What the rotation depends on (Brijlal Section 20.29, PDF p. 548)

The angle depends on four things: (a) the **thickness** of the substance, (b) its **density** (for a pure solid or liquid) or the **concentration** of the active substance (for a solution), (c) the **wavelength** of the light, and (d) the **temperature**. The first two are simple proportions; the last two make a quoted value meaningless unless wavelength and temperature are named.

## The two laws

For a crystal of thickness l:

$$ θ = α l $$

where α, the **rotational constant**, is the rotation per unit length (degrees per mm for quartz). For a solution of concentration c in a tube of length l:

$$ θ = s c l $$

where s is the **specific rotation**. Rotation is proportional to the path and to the amount of active substance in the path (book Eqs. 20.55 and 20.56).

## Specific rotation: the convention

The book defines the specific rotation at a given wavelength and temperature as the rotation produced by a column **one decimetre (10 cm) long** containing **1 g of active substance per cm³ of solution** (Eq. 20.57):

$$ [S] = θ / (l_dm c) = 10 θ / (l_cm c) $$

with θ in degrees, l_dm the length in decimetres, l_cm the length in centimetres and c in g per cm³. The units are therefore degrees per (decimetre x g/cm³), written as deg dm⁻¹ (g/cm³)⁻¹. The wavelength and temperature are attached to the symbol; the sodium D line (589 nm) is the usual choice (standard practice, verify). For a solid the book (PDF p. 548) also calls the rotation per 1 mm slab its specific rotation (rotatory power), so 'specific rotation of quartz = 21.71 deg/mm' means α.

## Three unit traps

1. **Length in decimetres.** A 20 cm tube is 2 dm, not 20.
2. **Concentration in g/cm³.** A solution with 50 g per litre has c = 0.05 g/cm³. 'A 10% solution' means 0.1 g/cm³ only if 'percent' means grams per 100 cm³ of solution.
3. **Sign.** Give θ its sign (+ for dextro, - for laevo) before combining rotations.

## Worked values (book problems, all recomputed in Python)

- **Specific rotation.** 20 cm of a 10% (0.1 g/cm³) sugar solution rotates 13.2 degrees: [S] = 13.2 / (2 × 0.1) = **66 deg dm⁻¹ (g/cm³)⁻¹** (book problem 5, answer 66 degrees, correct).
- **Concentration.** 25 cm tube, rotation 10 degrees, [S] = 60: c = 10 / (2.5 × 60) = **0.0667 g/cm³** (problem 6, correct).
- **Mass of solute.** 20 cm tube, rotation 11 degrees, [S] = 66: c = 11 / (2 × 66) = 0.0833 g/cm³; for 48 cm³ of solution the mass is 4.0 g (Example 20.19, correct).
- **Scaling.** 25 cm of 50 g/L gives 5 degrees. For 75 cm (three times the length) of 100 g/L (twice the concentration) the rotation is 5 × 3 × 2 = **30 degrees** (problem 21, correct).

## A mixture (Example 20.21 recomputed)

20 cm of solution 1 rotates +40 degrees (right) and 30 cm of solution 2 rotates -24 degrees (left). Mix them 1 : 2 by volume and put the mixture in a 30 cm tube. Each solution is diluted: solution 1 to one third, solution 2 to two thirds of its strength. Rotation per cm at full strength: +2.0 and -0.8 degrees per cm. In the mixture over 30 cm: +2.0 × 30 / 3 = +20 degrees and -0.8 × 30 × 2 / 3 = -16 degrees, total **+4 degrees (right)**.

### 4. Core derivation 1: Fresnel's explanation, θ = π (μ_L - μ_R) d / λ

*Objective:* Derive the rotation of the plane of polarisation from the two circular components, state the assumptions and the sign convention, and check the result.

## The idea (Brijlal Section 20.30, PDF pp. 548 to 549)

Fresnel proposed that plane-polarised light entering an active crystal along the optic axis splits into two equal circular vibrations turning in opposite senses. In calcite they travel at the same speed and the line is rebuilt unchanged. In quartz they travel at **different speeds**, one falls behind, and the line is rebuilt **turned**.

## Assumptions and sign convention

Monochromatic plane wave of angular frequency ω, normal incidence on a plate of thickness d cut perpendicular to the optic axis, no absorption. The beam travels toward the observer, who looks at the x-y plane (x right, y up), so 'clockwise' on the page is the right-handed (R) sense of PH-060. The incident vibration is along y with amplitude a. μ_R and μ_L are the indices for the clockwise (R) and anticlockwise (L) circles.

## Step 1: write the line as R + L

$$ R: (x, y) = (a/2)(sin ωt, cos ωt)    L: (x, y) = (a/2)(−sin ωt, cos ωt) $$

R moves right from the top (clockwise), L moves left (anticlockwise); the sum is (0, a cos ωt), the incident line.

## Step 2: what the crystal does

Each circle stays a circle but is delayed by the phase 2π μ d/λ (λ is the vacuum wavelength). With φ_R = 2π μ_R d/λ and φ_L = 2π μ_L d/λ, the emerging components are

$$ R: (a/2)(sin(ωt − φ_R), cos(ωt − φ_R))    L: (a/2)(−sin(ωt − φ_L), cos(ωt − φ_L)) $$

## Step 3: add them

Put u = ωt − (φ_R + φ_L)/2 and δ = φ_L − φ_R = 2π (μ_L − μ_R) d/λ. Then ωt − φ_R = u + δ/2 and ωt − φ_L = u − δ/2. Use sin P − sin Q = 2 cos((P + Q)/2) sin((P − Q)/2) and cos P + cos Q = 2 cos((P + Q)/2) cos((P − Q)/2):

$$ X = (a/2)[sin(u + δ/2) − sin(u − δ/2)] = a cos u sin(δ/2) $$

$$ Y = (a/2)[cos(u + δ/2) + cos(u − δ/2)] = a cos u cos(δ/2) $$

## Step 4: read the result

X and Y contain the same factor cos u, so they are **in phase**: the resultant is a **line**, and its direction satisfies tan ψ = X/Y = tan(δ/2). The plane has turned by

$$ ψ = δ/2 = π (μ_L − μ_R) d / λ $$

measured from +y toward +x, that is **clockwise** when μ_L > μ_R (the clockwise circle is the faster one). This is the right-handed (dextro) case; the book says the clockwise component in quartz is faster (PDF p. 549). The book prints the result with μ_R − μ_L in Eq. 20.58 and μ_L − μ_R in Eq. 20.63; the second agrees with its derivation (masterNotes).

```
 R at clockwise angle  u + δ/2 from +y      y   D = resultant line
 L at clockwise angle -u + δ/2 from +y      |  /
 mirror images in D, fixed at δ/2          |/ δ/2
 clockwise from +y: the sum stays on D  ---O---→ x
```

**Why half?** The resultant always points along the bisector of the two circle vectors, so delaying one circle moves the bisector by half the delay. 'Angle = δ' is a classic wrong answer.

## Checks

- **Dimensions:** d/λ and μ are pure numbers, so ψ is an angle.
- **Limits:** μ_L = μ_R (calcite along the axis) or d → 0 gives ψ = 0; ψ ∝ d is the law θ = αl with α = π (μ_L − μ_R)/λ.
- **Sign:** swap the indices (the mirror-image, left-handed crystal) and ψ changes sign.
- **Numerical test:** for δ = 0.7 rad, sampling the two circles gave X/Y = 0.3650 = tan 0.35, with X and Y in phase.

### 5. Core: from the index difference to the angle - the 2024 question and the book's examples recomputed

*Objective:* Convert units correctly, compute the rotation from d, λ and the index difference, name the sense, and spot the slips in the book's examples.

## The recipe

1. Convert **everything to metres** (or everything to the same unit).
2. Compute d/λ, a pure number.
3. Multiply by π and by the index difference: ψ = π Δμ d/λ, in radians.
4. Convert to degrees (multiply by 180/π).
5. Name the sense: clockwise (right-handed, +) if μ_L > μ_R.

## The verified 2024 question (Q3(a)(ii), 10 marks)

*Light is linearly polarised and travels along the optic axis of a quartz crystal 0.2 cm thick. The index difference for right and left circular beams is 7 × 10⁻⁵ and the wavelength is 0.5 μm. Calculate the angle of polarisation.* (After defining optical activity.)

**Solution as printed.** d = 0.2 cm = 2 × 10⁻³ m. λ = 0.5 μm = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m. Δμ = 7 × 10⁻⁵.

- d/λ = 2 × 10⁻³ / 5 × 10⁻⁷ = 4000.
- Δμ d/λ = 7 × 10⁻⁵ × 4000 = 0.28.
- ψ = π × 0.28 = 0.8796 rad = **50.4 degrees**.

**What the number means.** The phase difference between the circles is δ = 2ψ = 100.8 degrees = 1.759 rad; the plane turns by half of it. The thickness gives 25.2 degrees per mm, quartz-like: the book's problem 24 gives 21.71 degrees per mm for sodium light, which the rough 1/λ² rule of module 8 scales to about 30 at 500 nm. The question does not say which index is larger, so the **sense** is not determined by the data: if μ_L > μ_R the quartz is right-handed and the rotation is +50.4 degrees. State the magnitude and say this. The phrase 'angle of polarisation' (the book's own name for θ in Eq. 20.58, PDF p. 549) is read as the rotation of the plane of polarisation; the phase difference (100.8 degrees) is a different quantity.

## Real-world scale

An index difference of only 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ is enough: a few millimetres of quartz turn the plane by tens of degrees, because d/λ is of order 10³ to 10⁴ (4000 in the 2024 question). That is also why the effect is hard to see as a difference in speed: the two circles differ in speed by about 0.0045 per cent here (Δμ/μ = 7 × 10⁻⁵/1.55 ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻⁵, taking μ ≈ 1.55 from the book's quartz problems).

### 6. Core: why molecules rotate light, Fresnel's prism experiment, and optical activity against the Faraday effect

*Objective:* Explain the helix argument for two refractive indices, describe Fresnel's multi-prism proof that the two circles really exist, and contrast natural optical activity with the Faraday effect.

## The cause: chirality (Brijlal PDF p. 549)

The book's answer is that optically active molecules are **chiral**: they have a helical twist, and a helix can be left-handed or right-handed, each the mirror image of the other. A rotating wheel looks clockwise from one side and anticlockwise from the other, so its name depends on where you stand. A **helix is different**: turn a right-handed helix round and it is still right-handed. So a helix interacts differently with circular light of its own hand and of the opposite hand, and the two circles meet different refractive indices. The book adds that the cause lies at the molecular level and not in the way the molecules are ordered in space, so liquids and solutions can be active. Crystalline quartz is the other kind: there the helix is the arrangement of the lattice (book p. 546), so fused quartz is inactive (standard knowledge; verify).

## What follows (standard knowledge, verify)

- **Mirror-image molecules rotate oppositely.** A left-handed and a right-handed form of the same molecule have equal and opposite specific rotations. A mixture of equal amounts rotates by nothing. (The extract does not state this; it is standard chemistry.)
- **Direction of travel does not matter for the sign.** A right-handed screw looks right-handed from either end, so light sent back through the same substance by a mirror has its plane turned back to where it started, and the net rotation is zero. In the Faraday effect the sense of rotation follows the direction of the magnetic field, not the direction of travel, so a return trip **adds** the rotation. This is a standard contrast; the extract gives the Faraday formula but not this property.

## Fresnel's experiment: the two circles really exist (Section 20.31, PDF pp. 550 to 551)

Fresnel built a rectangular block from prisms of quartz, alternately right-handed and left-handed, with the optic axis parallel to the base of each prism. Plane-polarised light enters the first face normally, so both circles go straight in at different speeds. At the first oblique face the faster circle (R) of a right-handed prism enters a left-handed prism, where it becomes the **slower** one. The second prism is a denser medium for R and a rarer medium for L, so the R beam bends one way and the L beam the other way. At the next boundary the roles swap again and each beam bends further apart. The beams that leave are widely separated, and tests with a quarter-wave plate and an analyser show that they are circularly polarised in opposite senses. That is the confirmation of the hypothesis.

```
 right-quartz | left-quartz | right-quartz
 R fast  ----→ R slow  ---→  R fast   (R bends one way at the 1st boundary,
 L slow  ----→ L fast  ---→  L slow    L the other; the gap widens)
```

## Optical activity against the Faraday effect

| | Natural optical activity | Faraday effect (Section 20.37.2, PDF p. 557) |
|---|---|---|
| Needs a field? | No | Yes, a magnetic field parallel to the beam |
| Law | θ = αl or θ = scl | θ = V l H (V is the Verdet constant) |
| Cause | helical (chiral) structure | magnetic field acting on the medium |
| Return trip (verify) | undoes the rotation | doubles it |
| Typical size in the book | tens of degrees per mm in quartz | about 1 to 2 degrees for H of order 10⁶ A/m and l = 0.1 m |

The book also notes a practical Faraday problem: radio waves crossing the ionosphere have their plane turned by the ionised particles with the Earth's magnetic field, so satellites use circularly polarised antennas, which work whatever the rotation (PDF p. 557).

### 7. Core derivation 2: the half-shade polarimeter - why equal darkness is the sensitive setting

*Objective:* Describe the polarimeter, derive the brightness of the two halves of Laurent's field from Malus's law, and show why the equal-darkness setting is far more sensitive than the equal-brightness setting.

## The instrument (Brijlal Section 20.32, PDF pp. 551 to 552)

A **polarimeter** measures the rotation of a solution; for sugar it is called a **saccharimeter**. Along the beam: a monochromatic source, a lens making the beam parallel, a polariser N1, a **half-shade plate**, the glass tube G with the solution, an analyser N2 that turns about the tube axis with a graduated circle, and a telescope.

```
 S → lens → N1 → [half-shade] → [ tube G ] → N2 → telescope
                     (field split in two)          (circle scale)
```

**Method.** With the tube empty, set N2 for equal darkness and read the scale. Fill the tube; the two halves become unequal. Turn N2 until they are equal again and read again. The **difference of the two readings is the rotation**. Repeating with several concentrations and plotting θ against c gives a straight line whose slope is s l (book Fig. 20.70).

**The difficulty.** Judging complete darkness in a single field is hard; comparing two neighbouring patches is far easier for the eye (standard knowledge, verify), so the device makes the halves unequal except at the right setting.

## Laurent's half-shade plate (PDF p. 552)

A semicircular **half-wave plate** of quartz is cemented to a semicircular plate of glass of equal absorption, with the quartz optic axis along the dividing line AB. The polariser is set so that its vibration OP makes a small angle θ with AB.

- **Glass half:** the vibration stays along OP, at +θ from AB.
- **Quartz half:** a half-wave plate reflects the vibration in its optic axis, so it emerges along OQ at −θ from AB, turned by 2θ (the book says the plate turns the plane through 2θ).

## Derivation 2: the two intensities

**Assumptions.** Incident intensity I₀ on each half, small θ, an analyser whose transmission direction is at angle φ from AB (angles measured the same way as θ), Malus's law I = I₀ cos² (angle between vibration and analyser).

$$ I_glass = I₀ cos²(φ − θ)    I_quartz = I₀ cos²(φ + θ) $$

$$ I_glass − I_quartz = (I₀/2)[cos(2φ − 2θ) − cos(2φ + 2θ)] = I₀ sin 2φ sin 2θ $$

The halves are equal when sin 2φ = 0: **φ = 0** (analyser along AB, both halves I₀ cos²θ, bright) or **φ = 90°** (analyser along CD, both I₀ sin²θ, dark). The book gives both positions (PDF p. 552).

## Sensitivity

Turn the analyser away from the setting by a small angle ε (with ε much less than θ, and θ small). The relative difference (I_glass − I_quartz) divided by the mean intensity is

- near **φ = 90° + ε:** |ΔI|/I ≈ 4ε/θ (the numerator is I₀ sin 2ε sin 2θ ≈ 4εθ I₀ and the mean is I₀ sin²θ ≈ θ² I₀);
- near **φ = ε:** |ΔI|/I ≈ 4εθ (the mean is about I₀).

The ratio is 1/θ². For θ = 5° and ε = 0.1° (Python check): 8.0 per cent at the dark setting against 0.061 per cent at the bright setting, a ratio of 131. **So set the analyser for equal darkness.** The price is dimness: each half passes only I₀ sin²θ, about 0.76 per cent of I₀ at θ = 5°, so a bright source is needed.

## What the solution does

The solution turns OP and OQ rigidly by the same angle, so the equal-dark setting moves by exactly that angle: the difference of readings is the rotation.

## Limits and checks

- θ → 0: the two halves become identical for every φ (I₀ sin 2φ sin 2θ → 0) and the signal is lost; the 1/θ² gain holds only while ε ≪ θ.
- **Why one wavelength?** A quartz plate is a half-wave plate for one wavelength only, so Laurent's polarimeter needs monochromatic light (PDF p. 553).

### 8. Master: biquartz, the Lippich polarimeter, the 180-degree ambiguity and how rotation changes with wavelength

*Objective:* Describe the biquartz and Lippich arrangements, explain why a single reading fixes the rotation only up to 180 degrees, and use the approximate 1/λ² rule for rotary dispersion.

## The biquartz plate (Brijlal Section 20.33, PDF p. 553)

Instead of the half-shade, a **biquartz plate** is used in white light: two semicircular quartz plates, **each 3.75 mm thick**, one right-handed and one left-handed. The book says that yellow light is quenched, so both halves show the same 'tint of passage'. The setting used is the one where the two halves match.

**Why it works (the lesson's own argument; verify).** The right-handed half turns the plane of each colour by +α(λ)t and the left-handed half by −α(λ)t. With the analyser parallel to the polariser the two halves turn every colour by equal and opposite angles, so by symmetry they pass the same mixture of colours and match. The colour turned through exactly 90° (yellow-green for 3.75 mm) is quenched in both halves, leaving the purple tint of passage. Turn the analyser slightly and the quenched colour moves toward the red end in one half and toward the blue end in the other, so one half looks blue and the other red. With quartz at 21.71 degrees per mm for sodium light (book problem 24), 3.75 mm gives 81.4° for 589.3 nm. The thickness gives exactly 90° at α = 24 degrees per mm, which the 1/λ² rule below puts at about 560 nm: the yellow-green region, in line with the book's 'yellow'. The eye is very sensitive to this change of tint, which is why the setting is sharp in white light.

## The Lippich polarimeter (Section 20.34, PDF pp. 553 to 554)

Laurent's plate suits only the wavelength for which the half-wave plate was made. Lippich replaced it with a **second small polariser N3** covering half the field, its vibration plane inclined by a small angle θ to that of N1. It works for any wavelength because it uses no wave plate. The book says the halves are equally illuminated when N2 lies along the bisector of the two planes (PDF p. 553). **Check (verify):** Malus's law for a plain polariser over half the field gives cos²φ and cos²θ cos²(φ − θ), equal at φ = θ (bright) and at about 90° + θ/2 (dark; 92.5° for θ = 5°), so the book's bisector statement is loose. The improved **three-field** form (N3 and N4 each at a small angle to N1) removes the off-axis brightness defect; the procedure is as for Laurent's.

## The 180-degree ambiguity (standard knowledge; verify)

A plane turned by θ and a plane turned by θ − 180° are the same plane. A single reading therefore gives the rotation only modulo 180°: +10° (clockwise) cannot be told from −170° (anticlockwise). The remedy is to change the amount of active substance and watch the trend: halve the concentration (or the tube length) and +10° falls to +5°, while −170° would become −85°.

## Rotary dispersion: approximate 1/λ² (standard; verify)

The extract gives no law for the wavelength dependence. The standard approximate rule (Biot) is rotation per unit length ∝ 1/λ². Test on the book's own data:

## The verified 2024 question (analysis only; no official answer key is asserted)

**Q3(a)(ii), 10 marks:** What do you understand by optical activity? A linearly polarised beam travels along the optic axis of a quartz crystal 0.2 cm thick. The index difference for right and left circular beams is 7 × 10⁻⁵ and the wavelength is 0.5 μm. Calculate the angle of polarisation.

**Answer skeleton (about 12 minutes; a practice split of the 10 marks, not an official allotment: definition 3, formula and derivation line 3, numerical 3, sense and check 1):**

1. **Definition (about 3 lines).** Optical activity is the power of a substance to rotate the plane of polarised light about the direction of travel; examples; dextro and laevo; plane stays plane.
2. **Cause in two lines.** Linear light = R + L of equal size; in an active medium μ_R ≠ μ_L, so one circle lags; the resultant stays linear but turns by half the phase difference.
3. **Formula with the one-line derivation:** δ = 2π (μ_L − μ_R) d/λ, θ = δ/2 = π (μ_L − μ_R) d/λ (module 4).
4. **Numbers with units:** d = 2 × 10⁻³ m, λ = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, Δμ = 7 × 10⁻⁵: θ = π × 0.28 = 0.880 rad = **50.4°**.
5. **A closing line:** sense clockwise (dextro) if μ_L > μ_R; the phase difference is 100.8°, the plane turns by half; 25.2° per mm is quartz-like.

## Elimination for a true or false item

A claim that 'light splits into two beams on passing along the quartz axis' is false because along the optic axis there is no double refraction (module 2). A claim that 'the rotation is independent of wavelength' is false because rotation depends on wavelength (book PDF p. 548).

### Notes: Definitions

- Optical activity: the power of a substance (quartz, cinnabar, sugar or tartaric acid in solution) to rotate the plane of polarisation of plane-polarised light about the direction of travel; the light stays plane polarised (Brijlal Section 20.28).
- Dextrorotatory (right-handed, +): turns the plane clockwise for an observer looking toward the source. Laevorotatory (left-handed, -): anticlockwise. The viewpoint is the standard one (verify; the extract says 'toward the right' and 'toward the left').
- Rotational constant α: rotation per unit length of a crystal, θ = α l (Eq. 20.55). Quartz: 21.71 degrees per mm for sodium light (book problem 24).
- Specific rotation [S]: rotation produced by a column 1 dm long containing 1 g of active substance per cm³ of solution, at a stated wavelength and temperature (Eq. 20.57); units degrees per (dm × g/cm³).
- Polarimeter: instrument that measures the rotation of a solution; called a saccharimeter when used for sugar. Half-shade device: splits the field into two halves that match only at the correct analyser setting.
- Circular components: a line is the sum of a clockwise and an anticlockwise circle of equal amplitude; in an active medium they have indices μ_R and μ_L.
- Chirality: a helical (handed) structure that cannot be superposed on its mirror image; the cause of optical activity at the molecular level (book PDF p. 549).

### Notes: Equations to know

- θ = α l (crystal) and θ = s c l (solution); Eqs. 20.55 and 20.56.
- [S] = θ / (l_dm c) = 10 θ / (l_cm c), with c in g/cm³; Eq. 20.57.
- δ = 2π (μ_L − μ_R) d / λ (phase difference of the circles); θ = δ/2 = π (μ_L − μ_R) d / λ; Eqs. 20.62 and 20.63 (Eq. 20.58 prints the opposite order: see the slips).
- tan ψ = X/Y = tan(δ/2) from the sum of the two circles; the resultant is linear because X and Y are in phase.
- Half-shade: I_glass = I₀ cos²(φ − θ), I_quartz = I₀ cos²(φ + θ), difference I₀ sin 2φ sin 2θ; equal at φ = 0 (bright) and φ = 90° (dark).
- Sensitivity near the dark setting ≈ 4ε/θ, near the bright setting ≈ 4εθ; ratio 1/θ².
- Faraday effect (contrast): θ = V l H, V the Verdet constant (Eq. 20.67).
- Rough rotary dispersion (standard; verify): rotation per unit length ∝ 1/λ².

### Notes: Derivation summary: Fresnel's explanation (10 marks, practice target about 12 minutes)

- Assumptions: monochromatic plane wave, normal incidence on a plate of thickness d cut perpendicular to the optic axis, no absorption; beam toward the observer; clockwise on the page is right-handed.
- Incident line along y: R = (a/2)(sin ωt, cos ωt) plus L = (a/2)(−sin ωt, cos ωt).
- After d: R lags by φ_R = 2π μ_R d/λ and L by φ_L = 2π μ_L d/λ.
- Let u = ωt − (φ_R + φ_L)/2 and δ = φ_L − φ_R. Then X = a cos u sin(δ/2) and Y = a cos u cos(δ/2).
- X and Y are in phase, so the light is linear with tan ψ = tan(δ/2): ψ = π (μ_L − μ_R) d / λ, clockwise if μ_L > μ_R.
- Checks: pure number d/λ; zero for μ_L = μ_R and for d = 0; proportional to d; sign flips for the mirror-image crystal; numerical sum agreed (X/Y = 0.3650 = tan 0.35 for δ = 0.7).

### Notes: Derivation summary: the half-shade polarimeter (10 marks, practice target about 12 minutes)

- Glass half: vibration at +θ from AB. Quartz half-wave half: vibration reflected in the optic axis AB, so −θ.
- Analyser at φ from AB: I_glass = I₀ cos²(φ − θ), I_quartz = I₀ cos²(φ + θ); difference = I₀ sin 2φ sin 2θ.
- Equal at φ = 0 (each I₀ cos²θ) and φ = 90° (each I₀ sin²θ).
- Small ε off the dark setting the relative difference is 4ε/θ; off the bright setting it is 4εθ. For θ = 5°, ε = 0.1°: 8.0 per cent against 0.061 per cent (ratio 131 = 1/θ²).
- Rotation by the solution moves OP and OQ together, so the equal-dark reading moves by the rotation angle.

### Notes: The three polarimeter arrangements compared

- All three compare two neighbouring halves of the field; they differ in how the halves are made unequal.

### Notes: Optical activity against double refraction and the Faraday effect

- Three different ways in which light in a material is affected by polarisation.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Optical activity (one beam, plane turned) against double refraction (two beams, different polarisations).
- Phase difference δ between the circles against the rotation θ = δ/2 of the plane.
- Rotation per mm of a solid, α (which the book also calls the specific rotation of a solid), against [S] of a solution (per dm per g/cm³).
- Dextrorotatory (clockwise looking toward the source) against 'right circularly polarised' (a state of the light, PH-060); the faster circle in a dextro medium is the clockwise one.
- Natural rotation (no field, undone on return, verify) against Faraday rotation (needs a field, adds on return, verify).
- Equal-brightness setting against equal-darkness setting in the half-shade: the second is the sensitive one.

### Core concepts

- Optical activity turns the plane of polarisation of plane-polarised light about the beam direction; the light stays linear.
- Linear light is a clockwise plus an anticlockwise circle of equal size; in an active medium they travel with different indices, and the plane turns by half their phase difference.
- Rotation is proportional to thickness (crystal) or to concentration times length (solution); specific rotation is the rotation per dm per g/cm³.
- A half-shade plate turns a polarimeter reading into a comparison of two halves, and the equal-darkness setting is the sensitive one.

- Optical activity: the plane of polarisation turns about the beam and the light stays plane; quartz, cinnabar; sugar, tartaric acid
- Crossed P and A: dark; quartz plate cut perpendicular to the optic axis: bright; turn A back to dark: that angle is the rotation
