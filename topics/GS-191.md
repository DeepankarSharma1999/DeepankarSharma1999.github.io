## GS-191: Centre-state legislative relations

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · States, local governance and federalism

### Bridge: Law-making jurisdiction: which legislature may make which law

A **legislature** is a body that makes laws: **Parliament** (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) for the whole country, and a **State legislature** (the Legislative Assembly, and a Council in some States) for one State. **Jurisdiction** means the area or the kinds of matters over which a body has authority. For law-making, the Constitution answers two separate questions about every law.

First, the **territory** question: for which area may this body legislate? Parliament may legislate for the whole or any part of India; a State legislature for the whole or any part of its State. Second, the **subject** question: on which topic may this body legislate? The Constitution divides topics between the Centre and the States by three **Lists** (module 3). A law that fails either test is **ultra vires**, a Latin phrase meaning 'beyond the powers', and a court can strike it down (GS-186 module 7 on judicial review).

Think of two different dimensions of a postal address. A law can be perfectly sensible and still fail because it is addressed to the wrong body, in the wrong place, or on the wrong subject. Most of this lesson is about the second dimension: the subject, and what happens when two legislatures both have a claim on the same subject.

This bridge also warns you that legislative power is only one of three kinds of power divided between Centre and States. **Executive** power (running the law) and **financial** power (raising and sharing money) follow their own rules; GS-201 module 3 outlines them and GS-193 covers money.

### Bridge: List versus entry: how to read the Seventh Schedule

The **Seventh Schedule** is an appendix to the Constitution. (A **Schedule** is an appendix; an **Article** is a numbered rule in the main text; a **Part** is a group of Articles.) It contains three **Lists**. A **List** is a numbered list of topics; each topic on it is an **entry**. So 'List I, Entry 81' means the 81st topic on the Union List: inter-State migration and inter-State quarantine, in Laxmikanth's Appendix II (page 1375).

Entry numbers have gaps. When an entry is omitted (cancelled by a later Amendment) its number is not reused; when one is added it gets a letter, such as 2A, 17A or 92A. Laxmikanth's notes on page 369 explain that the Union List's last entry is still numbered 97 though the total is now 98, the State List's last is still 66 though only 59 remain, and the Concurrent List's last is numbered 47 though the total is 52 (see module 3). The printed extract of Appendix II also skips some entries (for example State List entries 4 to 6), so entry numbers quoted in this lesson that are not in the extract are marked as standard knowledge.

**How to read an entry.** An entry names a *field of law*, not a single statute. Entry 8 on the State List, 'intoxicating liquors', covers any law about liquor, past or future. Entries can overlap at the edges (module 3). Courts therefore decide which entry a law really belongs to using the doctrines of module 5.

A quick check on method: in an exam, never say 'police is a State subject' from memory alone if the question uses a different word, such as 'investigation' or 'intelligence'; search for the matching entry text, because the exam may be testing the neighbouring entry.

### Bridge: What 'repugnant' means: two laws that cannot both be obeyed

In everyday English 'repugnant' means 'disgusting'. In constitutional law it has a narrow technical meaning: two valid laws on the **same subject** are **repugnant** when they are so **inconsistent** that one cannot obey both at once. If they can be obeyed together, there is no repugnancy.

An invented illustration (not from the textbook). Suppose a Central law on a subject in the Concurrent List says an activity needs a licence, and a State law on the same subject says no licence is needed. A person cannot both hold and not hold a licence for the same act without breaking one law. That is repugnancy. By contrast, if the State law adds a small extra rule that the Central law does not forbid, one can obey both: no repugnancy.

Repugnancy arises when *both* legislatures were allowed to make the law, which happens in the **Concurrent List** (module 6). A State law that is repugnant to a Union law is not wiped from the statute book; the usual statement is that it is **void to the extent of the repugnancy**: the clashing part gives way and the rest may survive. That is why the phrase 'to the extent of' matters in answers.

A second technical idea you will meet is the **occupied field**: if Parliament has made a law that covers the whole subject exhaustively, a State's law on the same ground may be inconsistent even without a word-for-word clash (standard knowledge; verify with case law in module 6).

### 2. Territorial extent: where Parliament and a State legislature can make laws (Article 245)

*Objective:* State the territorial limits of Parliament and of a State legislature, the 'nexus' idea, and the three areas where Parliament's laws do not apply as they stand.

## The two basic rules (Laxmikanth, page 329)

- **Parliament** may make laws for the **whole or any part of the territory of India**. The territory includes the States, the Union Territories and any other area for the time being included in India.
- A **State legislature** may make laws for the **whole or any part of the State**.

## Extraterritorial laws: only Parliament

A law that applies *outside* the territory is **extraterritorial**. Only **Parliament** can make such laws, so Parliament's laws also bind Indian citizens and their property anywhere in the world. A State legislature's laws do not apply outside the State, **except where there is a sufficient nexus** between the State and the object of the law. *Nexus* means a real connection. The doctrine of **territorial nexus** is standard knowledge here: a State may legislate about something outside its borders only if the thing has a real and substantial link to the State; the extract does not name a case, so verify cases before quoting.

## Where Parliament's laws do not apply as they stand

Laxmikanth lists three situations in which Parliament's plenary (full) territorial power is restricted:

1. **The President's regulations for certain Union Territories.** The President can make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of listed Union Territories. A regulation has the **same force as an Act of Parliament** and may even repeal or amend an Act of Parliament for that territory. Laxmikanth names five territories: Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Daman and Diu, and Ladakh. **Flag:** Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu were merged into one Union Territory in 2020, and the list in Article 240 should be checked at legislative.gov.in before use. Article 240 also covers Puducherry when its Legislative Assembly is dissolved or suspended (standard knowledge; verify).
2. **Scheduled Areas.** The **Governor** may direct that an Act of Parliament does not apply to a **Scheduled Area** in the State, or applies with modifications (GS-198 modules 3 and 6 on the Fifth and Sixth Schedules).
3. **Tribal areas.** In Assam the **Governor**, and in Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram the **President**, may direct that an Act of Parliament does not apply to an autonomous district or applies with changes (same wording as GS-198 module 6, the Sixth Schedule lesson).

## One more territorial rule: areas outside any State

Parliament may legislate on **any matter, even a State List matter**, for any part of India that is **not in a State** (for example, Union Territories). That is why a Union Territory has no 'State List': Parliament holds both.

## What the rules do not say

They do not say that a State law can never have any effect outside the State; they say it is generally confined to the State *unless the nexus test is met*. Also, the territorial rule is only half the question: even a law that satisfies Article 245 must still fall within the subject lists of Article 246 (module 3).

### 3. Distribution of subjects: the three Lists, the entries and paired entries (Articles 246 and 246A)

*Objective:* Describe the three Lists, the counts, the notable entries, and how pairs of entries interlock, including the mining and prisons examples.

## The scheme (Laxmikanth, pages 330 to 331)

Article 246 divides subjects in the Seventh Schedule among three Lists.

| List | Who legislates | Entries now (originally) | Examples in the extract |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I: Union** | Parliament exclusively | 98 (97) | Defence, atomic energy, foreign affairs, currency, banking, insurance, inter-State trade, census, audit |
| **II: State** | State legislature, 'in normal circumstances' | 59 (66) | Public order, police, public health, agriculture, prisons, local government, fisheries, markets, theatres, gambling |
| **III: Concurrent** | Both Parliament and State legislatures | 52 (47) | Criminal law and procedure, civil procedure, marriage and divorce, population control, electricity, labour welfare, economic and social planning, drugs, newspapers |

**Why these counts differ from the last numbers:** on the Union List 97 numbered entries + 4 lettered additions (2A, 92A, 92B, 92C) - 3 omissions (33, 92, 92C) = 98 (Laxmikanth lists 92C only as omitted: source slip; verify the years: 2A by the 42nd Amendment 1976; 92A by the 6th 1956; 92B by the 46th 1982; 92C by the 88th 2003; 33 omitted by the 7th 1956; 92 and 92C omitted by the 101st 2016); seven State entries (11, 19, 20, 29, 36, 52 and 55) are omitted; five Concurrent entries (11A, 17A, 17B, 20A, 33A) were added (Laxmikanth's notes, page 369). The arithmetic for all three Lists: Union 97 + 4 - 3 = 98; State 66 - 7 = 59; Concurrent 47 + 5 = 52.

**The logic of the split.** Matters of national importance or needing nationwide uniformity go to the Union List; matters of regional or local importance, allowing diversity, to the State List; matters where uniformity is desirable but not essential to the Concurrent List. Concurrent permits 'diversity along with uniformity'.

## Notable entries (only numbers printed in Appendix II)

Union List: **8** Central Bureau of Intelligence and investigation; **42** inter-State trade and commerce; **54** regulation of mines and mineral development in the public interest; **69** Census; **76** audit of accounts of the Union and of the States; **81** inter-State migration and inter-State quarantine; **97** residuary. State List: **1** public order; **2** police; **14** agriculture; **18** land; **23** regulation of mines and mineral development; **26** trade and commerce within the State; **28** markets and fairs; **46** taxes on agricultural income. Concurrent List: **1** and **2** criminal law and procedure; **3** preventive detention for State security or public order; **4** removal of prisoners from one State to another; **25** education; **29** infectious or contagious diseases; **33** foodstuffs trade; **38** electricity. Prisons, local government and public health are State subjects according to the chapter text, but their entry numbers (4, 5 and 6, standard knowledge) are not printed in the extract.

## Paired entries: the lists interlock

1. **Mines:** Union 54 and State 23. In the full text, the State entry is 'subject to' the Union one (standard knowledge; the extract omits the 'subject to' words; verify). The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act 1957 is the Central law; standard knowledge: it lets the Centre notify 'minor minerals' and lets States make rules to grant leases for them (verify sections).
2. **Trade:** Union 41 and 42 (foreign and inter-State), State 26 (within the State), Concurrent 33 (foodstuffs).
3. **Police and investigation:** State 2, Union 8 and Union 80 (extending a State police force's powers outside that State).
4. **Prisons:** State subject; Concurrent 4 covers removal of prisoners between States.
5. **Education:** Concurrent 25; Union 63, 64 and 66 deal with institutions of national importance and standards.

## Article 246A and the GST

The 101st Amendment (2016) added Article 246A: both Parliament and State legislatures may legislate on goods and services tax; Parliament has **exclusive** power where supply is in the course of inter-State trade or commerce. See GS-193 module 5 and GS-194 module 8 for the money side.

### 4. Residuary power (Article 248), the 42nd Amendment shift and who changed the Lists

*Objective:* Say who holds residuary power and why, compare with other federations, and list the five subjects moved by the 42nd Amendment.

## Residuary power: what is left over

**Residuary** subjects are matters not named in *any* of the three Lists. **Article 248** gives the power to legislate on them to **Parliament**, and Union List **Entry 97** repeats it: 'any other matter not enumerated in List II or List III including any tax not mentioned in either of those lists'. The power includes **residuary taxation**. NCERT lists cyber laws as an example of a residuary subject (its chart, page 10).

## Comparison with other federations (Laxmikanth, pages 331 to 332)

| Country or Act | Enumerated powers | Residuary powers |
|---|---|---|
| USA | Only the federal government's powers are listed | States |
| Australia | Same pattern as the USA (single enumeration) | States |
| Canada | Double enumeration (federal and provincial) | Centre |
| Government of India Act 1935 | Three lists: federal, provincial, concurrent | Neither legislature: the **Governor-General** |
| India (1950) | Three lists | **Parliament** (like Canada) |

Compare with the commissions in GS-201 module 5: the Rajamannar Committee, the Anandpur Sahib Resolution and the West Bengal Memorandum asked for residuary powers to go to the States; the Sarkaria Commission recommended that **residuary taxation stay with Parliament** and other residuary powers go to the **Concurrent List** (Laxmikanth, pages 358 to 360).

## The 42nd Amendment, 1976: five subjects moved to the Concurrent List

| Subject | Concurrent List entry |
|---|---|
| Education | 25 |
| Forests | 17A |
| Weights and measures (except establishing standards, which stay on the Union List, Entry 50) | 33A |
| Protection of wild animals and birds | 17B |
| Administration of justice; constitution and organisation of all courts except the Supreme Court and High Courts | 11A |

The 42nd Amendment inserted five new Concurrent entries (11A, 17A, 17B, 20A, 33A) and widened Entry 25 (education). Four of the new entries plus Entry 25 are the five transfers above (State Entries 11, 19, 20 and 29 were omitted, and administration of justice was taken out of State Entry 3); Entry 20A (population control and family planning) is a new subject, not a transfer (standard knowledge; verify; keep this wording identical in GS-172 module 4 and GS-201).

Why this matters: on the Concurrent List the Union law prevails in a clash (module 6). Moving a subject from the State List to the Concurrent List therefore widens Parliament's reach without removing the States' right to legislate. (GS-172 module 4 lists this Amendment; this table is consistent with it.)

## Other changes to the Lists

The 101st Amendment (2016) added Article 246A (GST) and removed State Entries 52 and 55 (note 18, page 370). Laxmikanth's tally of originally 97, 66 and 47 entries tells you the Lists are not frozen: Parliament changes them by constitutional Amendment (Article 368, GS-172).

## An exam point

If a question says 'the Concurrent List gave Parliament the power to override the States' — remember what is true: the Union does not *remove* the State's power, it prevails *in conflict* (module 6).

### 5. Union supremacy and the interpretive doctrines: pith and substance, colourable legislation, harmonious construction

*Objective:* Explain the order of supremacy and the three doctrines courts use to resolve overlaps, and apply them in a short checklist.

## Express supremacy (Laxmikanth, page 332)

The Constitution expressly secures the **predominance of the Union List over the State List and the Concurrent List, and of the Concurrent List over the State List**. So: Union over State where entries overlap; Union over Concurrent; Concurrent over State. The Sarkaria Commission (quoted on pages 334 to 335) called the rule of federal supremacy a technique to avoid absurdity, resolve conflict and ensure harmony between Union and State laws; without it, a host of conflicting laws would stultify the two-tier system.

## Doctrines used by courts (standard knowledge; verify cases)

The entries overlap at the edges, so courts developed rules. The extract does not name them, so learn each with a flagged example.

1. **Pith and substance.** *Pith* is the core, *substance* the real nature. The court asks what the law is *really about*. If its true subject falls in the legislature's own List, the law is valid even if it incidentally touches a subject in another List. Commonly cited: the Bombay prohibition case, *State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara* (1951) (verify).
2. **Colourable legislation.** A legislature may not do indirectly what it cannot do directly. If a law pretends to be on a permitted subject but really deals with a forbidden one, it is a 'colourable' use of power and invalid. Commonly cited: *K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa* (1953) (verify).
3. **Harmonious construction.** Read the entries together so that each has meaning; avoid an interpretation that makes one entry useless. Only if the clash cannot be reconciled does the order of supremacy decide.

This is exactly the pairing in the 2019 Mains question: harmonious construction is the **first step**; the **principle of federal supremacy** is the **fallback**.

## A checklist for any competence puzzle

1. Find the **pith and substance** of the law.
2. Match it to an **entry**; remember that overlapping entries are read together (harmonious construction).
3. If impossible to reconcile, the **Union List prevails over the State List**, and the Concurrent List over the State List.
4. If both legislatures may legally act (Concurrent), check **repugnancy** and Article 254 (module 6).
5. Check whether an **extraordinary situation** lets Parliament act (modules 7 and 8).
6. Remember that a law within a legislature's competence can still fail for other reasons, such as violating a Fundamental Right (GS-186 module 7).

## Edge cases that test the rule

- A State law on markets that incidentally affects the movement of goods across State lines: pith and substance asks what the law is about; incidental effect is tolerated.
- A State law that is really a tax on something only Parliament may tax, labelled 'fee': colourable legislation.
- Two entries that can both apply: harmonise first, supremacy second.

Do not say 'the Union always wins'. The Union wins in a **true** clash, after harmonising and subject to the exception in Article 254(2).

### 6. Conflict on the Concurrent List: repugnancy and Article 254, with the 254(2) exception

*Objective:* State the Article 254 rule and its exception and test it against edge cases.

## The rule (Laxmikanth, page 332)

On a subject in the **Concurrent List**, if a Central law and a State law conflict, the **Central law prevails** over the State law (Article 254(1)). The State law is void *to the extent of the repugnancy* (bridge B3).

## The exception: Article 254(2)

If the **State law has been reserved for the consideration of the President and has received his assent**, then the State law **prevails in that State**. But the **Parliament remains competent to override it** by subsequently making a law on the *same matter*. So the State's win is local and not permanent.

**Why such an exception?** It lets a State, with Union approval, make a different rule for local conditions on a Concurrent subject. The assent is the Centre's consent to the difference.

## Edge cases (test the rule)

1. **State law first, no President's assent; Parliament passes a conflicting law later.** The Central law prevails to the extent of the conflict.
2. **State law reserved for the President and assented to by him, on a matter where a Central law already exists.** The State law prevails in that State over the earlier Central law. If Parliament *later* legislates on the same matter, the Central law can override it again.
3. **Both laws can be obeyed together.** No repugnancy; both stand.
4. **A conflict between a Union List law and a State List law.** Article 254 is not the tool here; the Union List prevails by the express supremacy of module 5 (Article 246), because a State has no power on a Union subject at all.

Test the rule with a trap: 'On a Concurrent subject the Central law always prevails.' It does not: it fails in edge case 2.

## Related Articles from the table

- **Article 251**: if a State law is inconsistent with a law made by Parliament under Article 249 or 250 (module 7), the parliamentary law prevails (Laxmikanth, pages 332 to 333).
- **Article 255**: requirements of a recommendation or previous sanction are matters of procedure only (the table gives only the title; standard knowledge: failure to obtain a required recommendation does not by itself invalidate an Act that has received the necessary assent; verify).

## The occupied-field and 'inconsistency' tests (standard knowledge; verify)

Court decisions in the repugnancy area ask three things: is there *direct inconsistency* (one cannot obey both); has Parliament intended to *cover the whole field* (the occupied field); and is the *same subject* covered. The extract does not name cases; verify authorities before using them in a Mains answer.

## Who executes a law on a Concurrent subject?

A law on a Concurrent List subject, even if made by Parliament, is executed by the **States**, unless the Constitution or Parliament directs otherwise (Laxmikanth, page 336); for example, the Essential Commodities Act places executive power in the Centre (note 5). The Sarkaria Commission recommended that the Centre should consult States before making a law on a Concurrent subject, and the Punchhi Commission asked for broad agreement before introducing such Bills (pages 361 and 364).

### 7. Parliament in the State field: five situations (Articles 249, 250, 252, 253 and 356)

*Objective:* Describe the five situations, who triggers each, how long it lasts and what happens to the State legislature, with one example each.

## The principle

The normal scheme of the Lists applies in **normal times**. In **abnormal** circumstances the Constitution lets **Parliament legislate on State List matters** (Laxmikanth, page 332). Memorise them with their triggers.

| # | Situation and Article | Who or what triggers it | How long | Position of the State legislature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rajya Sabha resolution (Article 249)** | The Rajya Sabha declares by **two-thirds of members present and voting** that it is necessary in the national interest for Parliament to legislate on a **State List matter or on goods and services tax** (the GST words were added to Articles 249 and 250 by the 101st Amendment, 2016; Laxmikanth notes 3a and 3b, pages 332-333) | The resolution lasts **one year**, renewable any number of times, **one year at a time**; laws cease six months after the resolution ceases | **Not restricted**; but if a State law conflicts with Parliament's law, the parliamentary law prevails (Article 251) |
| 2 | **National emergency (Article 250)** | A Proclamation of national emergency is in operation; Parliament may then legislate on a State List matter or on goods and services tax (101st Amendment, 2016) | Laws become inoperative **six months** after the emergency ceases | **Not restricted**; Parliament's law prevails if inconsistent |
| 3 | **State request (Article 252)** | Legislatures of **two or more States** pass resolutions | The law applies only to those States; other States may **adopt** it later by resolution; no time limit | State legislature **ceases to have power** on that matter; the law can be **amended or repealed only by Parliament** |
| 4 | **International agreements (Article 253)** | Parliament implements a treaty, agreement or convention | No time limit in the extract | Not restricted in the extract; Article 253 has no resolution requirement and no time limit, and is an independent power to implement treaties (standard knowledge; verify) |
| 5 | **President's Rule (Article 356)** | President's Rule imposed in a State | The law **continues after** President's Rule ends | The law can be **repealed, altered or re-enacted by the State legislature** |

## One example each (as Laxmikanth gives them)

- **Article 252:** the Prize Competition Act 1955; the Wild Life (Protection) Act 1972; the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974; the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act 1976; the Transplantation of Human Organs Act 1994. (The 42nd Amendment later moved wild animals and birds to the Concurrent List; the Act still shows the 252 route.)
- **Article 253:** the United Nations (Privileges and Immunities) Act 1947; the Geneva Convention Act 1960; the Anti-Hijacking Act 1982; laws on the environment and on TRIPS.
- **Article 249 (invented illustration, not historical):** the Rajya Sabha resolves by two-thirds present and voting that Parliament should legislate on a State List subject, such as markets, for a year.
- **Article 250 (illustration):** during a national emergency Parliament passes a law on supply and distribution of goods, a State subject.
- **Article 356:** while a State is under President's Rule, Parliament makes laws for that State on State List subjects. 'Article 356' is shorthand: the power is exercised under Article 356(1)(b) read with Article 357, under which Parliament legislates itself or delegates the power to the President (standard knowledge; verify). This is also why the Rajamannar Committee asked to omit Articles 356, 357 and 365 together.

## Arithmetic of the Rajya Sabha resolution

Two-thirds of members *present and voting* means at least 147 votes if 220 members are present and voting (two-thirds of 220 is 146.67, so 147 is the least whole number that reaches it); and exactly 130 if 195 are present and voting. Members absent or not voting are not counted. Note the difference from the majority needed to remove a Supreme Court judge, which also needs a majority of the total membership (GS-186 module 3).

## NCERT's wording

NCERT (page 13) says the Centre may legislate on State List matters 'if the move is ratified by the Rajya Sabha'. This is a loose summary: the Article requires a **Rajya Sabha resolution** with two-thirds support.

## Two traps

A single State cannot request Parliament to make a law on a State subject; at least two States must (page 338). And 'emergency' in Article 250 means a Proclamation of emergency in operation (national emergency, Article 352), not President's Rule.

### 8. Edge-case tests: the five situations on sixteen statements

*Objective:* Apply the five situations to three worked statements each and notice which neat rules have exceptions.

Each statement below is judged **true** or **false**, with the reason. Use module 7's table; this is practice in applying it.

## Article 249 (Rajya Sabha resolution)

1. *'A resolution needs two-thirds of the total membership of the Rajya Sabha.'* **False.** It needs two-thirds of members *present and voting*.
2. *'Once the resolution lapses, laws made under it continue for another six months.'* **True.** They cease six months after the resolution ceases (as the extract says).
3. *'During the resolution, State legislatures lose their power on that matter.'* **False.** Their power is not restricted; only on inconsistency does the parliamentary law prevail.

## Article 250 (national emergency)

4. *'Laws made under Article 250 cease six months after the emergency ceases.'* **True.**
5. *'A national emergency suspends State legislatures.'* **False.** State powers are not suspended; the State legislature may still make laws on the same matter and the parliamentary law prevails if they conflict (the extract also says State governments are brought under complete Central control during an emergency, but are not suspended).
6. *'The State legislature's law prevails in a clash with a law made under Article 250.'* **False.** The parliamentary law prevails (Article 251).

## Article 252 (State request)

7. *'Two or more State legislatures must pass resolutions.'* **True.** One State cannot ask.
8. *'A State that did not pass the first resolution can later adopt the law.'* **True.** By a resolution of its legislature.
9. *'The States that passed the resolution can amend the law.'* **False.** Only Parliament can amend or repeal it.

## Article 253 (treaties)

10. *'Parliament needs a Rajya Sabha resolution to legislate for a treaty on a State subject.'* **False.** Article 253 has no resolution requirement; it is an independent power to implement treaties (standard knowledge; verify).
11. *'The Geneva Convention Act 1960 is an example.'* **True.**
12. *'A law under Article 253 must be renewed every year.'* **False.** Article 253 has no time limit (standard knowledge; verify); the yearly renewal rule belongs to the Article 249 resolution.

## Article 356 (President's Rule; the power is under Article 356(1)(b) read with Article 357, verify)

13. *'Parliament can legislate on State List matters for a State under President's Rule.'* **True.**
14. *'Such a law ends the day the President's Rule ends.'* **False.** It continues until altered.
15. *'The revived State legislature can repeal or alter that law.'* **True.**

## Edge case added after the 101st Amendment

16. *'A Rajya Sabha resolution under Article 249 can enable Parliament to legislate on goods and services tax.'* **True.** Since the 101st Amendment (2016), Articles 249 and 250 speak of 'goods and services tax or a matter in the State List' (Laxmikanth notes 3a and 3b, pages 332-333; verify at legislative.gov.in). A student who thinks Article 249 covers State List matters only would wrongly reject this statement.

## The classifier traps

- 'Temporary against permanent' is a neat rule: 249 and 250 are time-limited, 252 and 356 laws continue, and 253 has no limit in the extract. But the neat rule has an exception: a 252 law continues yet only Parliament can change it, while a 356 law continues and the State legislature can change it.
- 'The State loses power' is true only in situation 3.
- 'The Rajya Sabha is involved' is true only in situation 1; the Rajya Sabha's resolution is about Article 249 only (it also features in Article 312 for All-India Services, outside this lesson).

### 9. Centre's control over State legislation and recent friction (farm laws, CBI, Governor)

*Objective:* List the Centre's controls over State Bills, summarise the commissions' views, and discuss the farm laws and CBI issues in a neutral frame.

## Three controls (Laxmikanth, page 334)

1. **Reservation by the Governor.** The Governor may reserve certain Bills passed by the State legislature for the President, and Laxmikanth says the President enjoys an **absolute veto** over them. The mechanics (Articles 200 and 201) and the arguments are in GS-189 module 4; in brief, reservation is compulsory for Bills that endanger the position of the High Court, and the Governor may reserve Bills that are against the Constitution, against the Directive Principles, against the larger interest of the country, of grave national importance, or dealing with compulsory acquisition of property (Laxmikanth's Governor chapter). A related provision, **Article 31A**, protects certain State land-reform and similar laws from challenge under Articles 14 and 19 only if they were reserved and assented to by the President (chapter 7, page 239).
2. **Previous sanction of the President.** Certain State List Bills can be introduced in the State legislature only with the President's **previous sanction**, for example Bills imposing restrictions on trade and commerce (standard knowledge: Article 304(b); verify).
3. **Financial emergency.** During a financial emergency the Centre can direct the States to reserve Money Bills and other financial Bills for the President.

## What the commissions said

- **Sarkaria (1988):** when the President withholds assent, give reasons to the State; consult States before legislating on a Concurrent subject; the Centre should be strong but not over-centralised ('blood pressure at the Centre and anaemia at the periphery').
- **Punchhi (2010):** get broad Union-State agreement before Concurrent List Bills; be 'extremely restrained' in asserting parliamentary supremacy in State matters; a six-month time limit for the President to decide a reserved State Bill and for the Governor to decide on assent or reservation.

## Friction case 1: the three farm Acts (2020 to 2021), a neutral frame

In 2020 Parliament passed three Acts on farm trade, contract farming and essential commodities (standard knowledge; verify). **Reported arguments (neutral):** supporters said the Acts rested on Concurrent List Entry 33 (trade and commerce in foodstuffs) and Union Entry 42 (inter-State trade). Critics said the heart of the matter lay in State List Entries 14 (agriculture), 26 (trade within the State) and 28 (markets and fairs). The Supreme Court stayed their implementation in January 2021 and the Acts were repealed later that year (verify); the competence question was therefore not settled on its merits. Use it to show how the *pith and substance* of a law can be disputed.

## Friction case 2: the CBI and State consent

The Central Bureau of Intelligence and investigation is Union List Entry 8, while the *police* are State List Entry 2 and Union Entry 80 deals with extending a State police force's powers outside the State. Under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act 1946 the CBI needs the consent of a State to investigate there (standard knowledge: section 6; verify); several States have reportedly withdrawn 'general consent' (verify current position). Courts, standard knowledge, can still direct CBI probes through the Supreme Court and High Courts; the 2021 Mains question asks you to state that the States' power to withhold consent is **not absolute**.

## Friction case 3: Governors and assent

See GS-189 modules 4 and 7. Developments after 2023 (Supreme Court rulings and a Presidential reference) are post-2024 sensitive: verify before using.

## A frame for 'Centre-State legislative friction'

Cause (centralising constitutional design, uniformity, national security); instrument (Concurrent List, 249 to 253, reservation); dispute (competence, consultation); resolution (courts, Inter-State Council, GS-194); reform (consultation, cost-sharing).

### 10. How UPSC tests this topic: 14 verified questions, patterns and a guided MCQ

## Pattern A: 'whose subject is it?' (Prelims, six questions)

2024 Q75 asks which statement about Union and State subjects is correct. Check each against Appendix II: inter-State trade and commerce is Union Entry 42; inter-State migration is Union Entry 81 (with inter-State quarantine); corporation tax is a Union tax (the chapter's note 12 on page 370 places Union tax entries at 82 to 91; standard knowledge: Entry 85). For 2023 statement II (standard knowledge; verify the sections): the Prisons Act 1894 is a pre-Constitution Central Act placing prison administration under the State (then Provincial) Government, for example an Inspector-General of Prisons under the State Government; the constitutional basis today is State Entry 4.

## Pattern B: doctrines and principles (Mains)

2019 Q4 (federal supremacy and harmonious construction) is module 5. 2020 Q3 and Q11 are 'federal character' questions: the Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 is a pre-Constitution law continued under Article 372, read with Concurrent Entry 29 (inter-State spread of infectious diseases) and State Entry 6 (public health); the Disaster Management Act 2005 is generally reported to rest on Concurrent Entry 23 (social security and social insurance) (standard knowledge; verify both); the farm Acts are module 9. 2017 Q100 asks which is not a feature of Indian federalism: India's federation was a constitutional design, not an agreement among federating units (GS-201 module 1; verify Ambedkar's formulation).

## Pattern C: Governor and reservation

2025 Q54 (two statements): whether the Constitution explicitly says the Governor acts in his own discretion in certain spheres (GS-189 module 6) and whether the President can reserve a State Bill of his own accord. ## Pattern D: contemporary federalism

### Notes: Interpretive doctrines (definitions)

- Pith and substance: the true nature of a law decides which List it belongs to; incidental trenching on another List does not invalidate it (standard knowledge; verify cases).
- Colourable legislation: a legislature cannot do indirectly what it cannot do directly; a law disguised as one on a permitted subject is invalid.
- Harmonious construction: read the entries together so that none is rendered meaningless; resort to supremacy only if irreconcilable.
- Federal supremacy: Union over State, and Concurrent over State, where laws truly conflict (Sarkaria: indispensable to the federal system).
- Territorial nexus: a State law reaches outside the State only with a real and sufficient connection to it.
- Repugnancy: inconsistency so that both laws cannot be obeyed; the State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy (Article 254(1)).

### Notes: Exceptions to neat rules

- 'Union law always prevails on the Concurrent List': not if the State law was reserved and assented to by the President, but Parliament can still override it later.
- 'State loses power when Parliament legislates on the State List': true only under Article 252; in 249 and 250 the State keeps power but Parliament prevails.
- 'Parliament's law ends with the emergency or President's Rule': a law under Article 250 ceases six months after the emergency; a law under Article 356 continues until altered.
- 'Residuary power goes to the Centre everywhere': in the USA and Australia it goes to the States; India follows Canada.
- 'State laws never apply outside the State': they may where a sufficient nexus exists.
- 'Parliament's laws apply to all areas': the President, and Governors, may restrict their application to certain Union Territories, Scheduled Areas and tribal areas.

### Notes: Commissions on legislative relations

- Rajamannar Committee (1969-71): transfer certain Union and Concurrent entries to the State List; residuary powers to States; omit Articles 356, 357 and 365.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) and West Bengal Memorandum (1977): confine the Centre to defence, foreign affairs, communications, currency (and economic coordination in the memorandum); residuary powers to States.
- Sarkaria (1983-88): 247 recommendations; residuary taxation with Parliament, other residuary powers to the Concurrent List; consult States before Concurrent List legislation; reasons when assent is withheld; Article 356 sparingly.
- Punchhi (2007-10): over 310 recommendations; broad agreement before Concurrent List Bills; restraint in parliamentary supremacy; six months for the President and the Governor on State Bills; localised emergency; cooperative federalism.

### Notes: Centre's control over State legislation

- Governor reserves certain Bills for the President; reservation is compulsory for a Bill that endangers the position of the High Court (GS-189 module 4).
- Previous sanction of the President for introducing certain State List Bills (example: restrictions on trade and commerce; standard knowledge Article 304(b), verify).
- Financial emergency (Article 360): the Centre may direct the reservation of Money Bills and other financial Bills for the President.
- Article 31A: State laws on land reform and similar matters are protected from challenge under Articles 14 and 19 only if reserved and assented to by the President.

### Notes: Flags: standard knowledge and slips to verify

### Practice

**Q.** Which Article gives residuary power to Parliament?

**A.** Article 248 (with Entry 97 of the Union List).

**Q.** How many entries does each List have now?

**A.** Union 98, State 59, Concurrent 52 (as Laxmikanth states).

**Q.** Which Amendment inserted Article 246A?

**A.** The 101st Amendment Act, 2016 (GST).

**Q.** Who alone can make extraterritorial laws?

**A.** Parliament.

**Q.** Explain why the Union List prevails over the State List but the State law can prevail over a Central law under Article 254(2).

**A.** Union List supremacy covers subjects the State may not legislate; 254(2) covers a Concurrent subject where the President's assent to a reserved State law makes it prevail in that State, subject to Parliament's later law on the same matter.

**Q.** Distinguish Article 249 from Article 252.

**A.** 249: a Rajya Sabha resolution by two-thirds present and voting, one year at a time, State legislature keeps power. 252: two or more State legislatures request; State loses power; only Parliament amends or repeals.

**Q.** What is colourable legislation, and how does it differ from pith and substance?

**A.** Colourable legislation disguises a forbidden subject as a permitted one; pith and substance looks at the true nature of the law and tolerates incidental effects.

**Q.** Why is repugnancy relevant only to the Concurrent List?

**A.** Only there may both legislatures validly legislate on the same subject; on the Union and State Lists only one has the power.

**Q.** Two States ask Parliament to make a law on a State List subject. What law results, to whom does it apply, and who can amend it?

**A.** A law under Article 252, applying to those States (others may adopt it), amendable or repealable only by Parliament.

**Q.** A State passes a law on a Concurrent subject that conflicts with a Central law and gets the President's assent. Two years later Parliament passes a new law on the same matter. Which prevails?

**A.** Initially the State law prevails in that State; the later parliamentary law can override it.

**Q.** During President's Rule in a State Parliament makes a law on a State List subject. The Rule is withdrawn. Is the law still in force?

**A.** Yes, until repealed, altered or re-enacted by the State legislature.
