### Bridge: Judicial and quasi-judicial: what is the difference?

Start with the verb **adjudicate**: to hear two sides, look at the evidence and the law, and give a binding decision. A **court** (Supreme Court, High Court, district court) adjudicates and belongs to the **judiciary**, one of the three organs of government. Its judges are appointed and protected under the Constitution.

A **quasi-judicial** body also adjudicates, but it is created by an ordinary law, sits outside the regular court hierarchy, and often has members who are not judges. 'Quasi' means 'as if' or 'resembling'. Examples that the syllabus clause GS II-9 hints at are tribunals and regulators when they decide cases: a service tribunal deciding a promotion dispute, or a regulator deciding whether a company broke the rules.

The practical tests are simple. Does it **hear both sides**? Does it follow **natural justice** (a fair hearing, no judge in his own cause, a reasoned decision)? Is its decision **binding** and open to **challenge in a higher court**? A quasi-judicial body says yes to these, but it may be more informal, quicker and staffed by experts. A **purely administrative** body (a ministry deciding where to build a road) makes policy and does not settle a dispute between two parties.

Why it matters: quasi-judicial bodies are the reason the judiciary insists on the right of appeal and on judicial review. Whatever they decide, a High Court can still examine whether they stayed within the law.

### Bridge: Adjudication versus mediation: who decides?

Every dispute-resolution method answers one question: **who decides the outcome?**

In **adjudication** a judge or tribunal decides and the parties must obey. It is the model of the ordinary court. The process is formal, one side usually wins, and it can be slow because each side may appeal.

In **negotiation** the parties talk directly and agree among themselves. In **mediation** a neutral person (the mediator) helps them talk and look for a solution, but the mediator does **not** decide; the settlement is the parties' own. **Conciliation** is close to mediation, but the conciliator may suggest terms. In **arbitration** the parties agree in advance to be bound by a private decision-maker (the arbitrator), who gives an **award** after hearing both sides; this is private adjudication by consent.

The family of methods other than a regular trial is called **alternative dispute resolution (ADR)**. The Law Commission, as Laxmikanth records, praised ADR because it is cheaper, quicker, less technical, private, and leaves no feeling of a winner and a loser, so relationships can survive.

A **Lok Adalat** is mainly conciliation: it helps the parties compromise. A court or a tribunal is adjudication. A **Permanent Lok Adalat** blends the two: it first tries for settlement, and if that fails it decides on merits (module 8).

### 1. Orientation: courts, tribunals and the four routes to justice

*Objective:* Name the four routes by which the system tries to deliver justice faster or closer to people, and place each topic of this lesson on one route.

A **dispute** is a disagreement about rights or duties that someone must settle: a clerk says his promotion was unfair, a tenant says the rent rise is illegal, a villager cannot afford a lawyer. GS-187 showed the ordinary route: a trial court, then appeals up the **court ladder** to the High Court and the Supreme Court. That ladder is trusted but can be slow, costly and technical. So the system has added other routes. This lesson maps them.

**Route 1: specialised forums (tribunals).** A **tribunal** is a body created by law to decide disputes in one field, such as government service, tax, company law or the environment. The idea is speed and expertise. The original Constitution had no provision for tribunals; the **42nd Amendment (1976)** added **Part XIV-A** with two Articles, **323A** and **323B** (modules 2 to 6).

**Route 2: settle instead of fight (alternative dispute resolution, ADR).** Mediation, conciliation, arbitration and **Lok Adalats** try to end a dispute by agreement or private decision rather than a full trial (module 8, and bridge module B2).

**Route 3: reach those who cannot reach the court.** **Legal aid** under **Article 39A**, the **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)**, **public interest litigation (PIL)** and **Gram Nyayalayas** aim at the poor and the distant (modules 7 and 8).

**Route 4: fix the system itself.** Pendency, **e-courts**, the **Law Commission** and the **2023 criminal laws** (module 9).

Two warnings for a beginner. First, 'tribunal' does not mean 'court of lower rank'. Tribunals sit **beside** the ladder, and their orders are still open to challenge before a High Court (module 5). Second, 'justice reform' is not one law; it is many separate steps, so a good answer groups them under these four routes.

Study order for the first pass: modules 1 to 5, 7 and 8 are core. Module 6 (the 2021 reform and later rulings), module 9 and module 10 are deeper layers that can wait for the revision cycle.

### 2. Part XIV-A: Articles 323A and 323B

*Objective:* State what Articles 323A and 323B allow, and tabulate their three differences.

**Part XIV-A** of the Constitution is titled 'Tribunals' and contains only two Articles. It was added by the **42nd Amendment Act, 1976**. Both Articles are **enabling**: they do not create any tribunal themselves; they authorise a legislature to pass a law that does so.

**Article 323A: administrative tribunals.** Parliament may by law provide for tribunals to adjudicate disputes about the **recruitment and conditions of service** of persons appointed to public services of the Centre, the States, local bodies, public corporations and other public authorities. The effect, in Laxmikanth's words, is to take **service matters** out of the civil courts and the High Courts and place them before an administrative tribunal. Under this Article Parliament passed the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** (module 3).

**Article 323B: tribunals for other matters.** Parliament **and** State legislatures (each for matters within its own legislative competence) may set up tribunals for eight subjects: **taxation; foreign exchange, import and export; industrial and labour matters; land reforms; ceiling on urban property; elections to Parliament and State legislatures; foodstuffs; and rent and tenancy rights**. The last item was added by the **75th Amendment Act, 1993**.

The three differences, as the source lists them:

| Point | Article 323A | Article 323B |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Public service matters only | Taxation, labour, land, elections, rent and other listed matters |
| Who can create | Parliament only | Parliament and State legislatures, within their competence |
| Structure | One tribunal for the Centre and one for each State or group of States; no hierarchy | A hierarchy of tribunals may be created |

**The excluded-courts clause.** As originally framed, each Article let the Act exclude the jurisdiction of courts, including the High Courts and (apart from an appeal route: the original clauses kept only the Supreme Court's Article 136 jurisdiction and excluded Articles 226/227 and 32; verify) the Supreme Court, over matters given to the tribunal. In **L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India (1997)** the Supreme Court declared those clauses, namely clause 2(d) of Article 323A and clause 3(d) of Article 323B, **unconstitutional**. So judicial remedies against tribunal orders are available again (module 5). *Verify the exact wording of the excluded clause in the official text; the sources read give only the clause numbers.*

**An important nuance.** Not every tribunal in India is created under Part XIV-A. Many are established by ordinary statutes passed under a legislature's general power (for example the tribunals in module 4), so check each body's own Act. Part XIV-A is the constitutional hook for the idea of tribunals, not a complete list.

### 3. The Administrative Tribunals Act 1985: CAT, State tribunals, joint tribunals

*Objective:* Describe the jurisdiction, composition, procedure and appeal route of the CAT, and state what is dated in the 2019 text.

The **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** authorises the Centre to set up **one Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)** and **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)**. It was meant to give public servants speedy and inexpensive justice.

**The CAT** was set up in **1985**, with its principal bench in **Delhi** and other benches in the States; Laxmikanth (2019) records 17 regular benches (15 at the seats of High Courts, plus Jaipur and Lucknow) with circuit sittings elsewhere. *The count is as printed in 2019; verify the present benches.*

*Jurisdiction.* The CAT has **original jurisdiction** (it hears the dispute first, not on appeal) over recruitment and all service matters of the **all-India services, Central civil services, civil posts under the Centre and civilian employees of the defence services**. It does **not** cover **members of the defence forces, officers and servants of the Supreme Court, and the secretarial staff of Parliament**.

*Composition.* A multi-member body of a chairman and members drawn from the **judicial and the administrative streams**, appointed by the President. The Vice-Chairman post was removed by the **2006 amendment**. The 2019 text gives a term of **five years or age 65 (chairman) / 62 (members)**, whichever is earlier, and says appointments follow a selection committee chaired by a sitting Supreme Court judge nominated by the Chief Justice of India, with the concurrence of the CJI and the approval of the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet. *These service conditions were reportedly changed by the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 (module 6); verify.*

*Procedure.* The CAT is **not bound by the Civil Procedure Code, 1908**; it follows **natural justice**, which keeps it flexible. The fee is **nominal** (₹50 in the source) and the applicant can appear **in person** or through a lawyer.

*Appeal.* Originally appeals went only to the Supreme Court. After **Chandra Kumar (1997)** they lie to the **division bench of the concerned High Court**, so a public servant cannot go straight to the Supreme Court.

**State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)** are set up by the Centre **on the request of a State government**. Laxmikanth (2019) says they were set up in nine States, three of which were later abolished (Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh), with Himachal re-establishing its tribunal and other States making requests. *This is a moving picture; verify.* Himachal's tribunal was reportedly abolished again in 2022 (verify). The chairman and members of a SAT are appointed by the **President after consulting the Governor**. A **joint administrative tribunal (JAT)** may serve two or more States, with appointments after consulting the Governors concerned.

**Reading the 2019 PYQ.** The 2019 Mains question says the CAT, set up for grievances of Central employees, now exercises powers 'as an independent judicial authority'. A good answer explains: original jurisdiction replacing the civil courts and High Courts for service cases; reasoned, binding orders; the practice (standard, not in the extract; verify) that it can punish for contempt and test the validity of rules; the Chandra Kumar bridge, which made it subject to High Court review. Do not say it is a High Court; say it is a **specialised substitute** with a **supervised** position.

### 4. Tribunals for other matters: a tour of the main bodies

*Objective:* Match each main tribunal to the kind of dispute it handles and note that most rest on their own Acts.

Beyond service matters, Parliament has created many specialised tribunals. Laxmikanth's chapter on tribunals covers Articles 323A and 323B and the CAT in detail but does not list the others, so the table below is **standard textbook knowledge outside the extract**. Treat names, parent laws and appeal routes as **'verify against the Act'** items; the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 and later changes have touched several of these bodies.

| Tribunal | Deals mainly with | Parent law (standard; verify) |
|---|---|---|
| Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) | Appeals against income-tax orders; a fact-finding appeal body | Income-tax law; set up in 1941 |
| Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT) | Appeals in customs, central excise and service tax | Customs law; earlier called CEGAT |
| National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) | Company law and corporate insolvency; NCLAT hears appeals | Companies Act 2013; Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 |
| Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) | Appeals against orders of the securities regulator | SEBI Act 1992 |
| Armed Forces Tribunal (AFT) | Service matters and court-martial appeals for the armed forces | Armed Forces Tribunal Act 2007 |
| Debts Recovery Tribunal (DRT) and Appellate Tribunal (DRAT) | Recovery of dues to banks and financial institutions | Act of 1993 |
| National Green Tribunal (NGT) | Environmental disputes and related civil cases | NGT Act 2010 |
| RERA Appellate Tribunals | Appeals in real-estate regulation | Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 |
| Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT) | Telecom and related disputes | TRAI Act 1997; TDSAT inserted by the TRAI (Amendment) Act 2000 (verify) |

**Why this table is a tool and not a list to cram.** UPSC rarely asks for a tribunal's founding year. It asks what a tribunal is **for**, why governments create them, and whether they work. For recall, group them: **tax** (ITAT, CESTAT), **business and finance** (NCLT, NCLAT, SAT, DRT), **people and rights** (NGT, AFT, RERA), **network industries** (TDSAT).

**Three points the table hides.**
1. Many of these bodies sit **outside Article 323B**; they are created by ordinary Acts, so describing every one of them as a '323B tribunal' is a mistake (module 2).
2. **Elections**, the item in Article 323B(2)(f), are in practice decided by the High Courts through election petitions; do not assume a separate election tribunal exists (see GS-187).
3. The **appeal route differs by body**: some appeal to the High Court, some directly to the Supreme Court under their own Act. The safe rule from Chandra Kumar is that tribunal orders are never wholly beyond the reach of constitutional courts.

**A second way to think.** Tribunals appear where a field is **technical** (company insolvency, environment, telecom), **high-volume** (tax, service matters, debt) or **needs speed** (loan recovery). A tribunal is justified when expertise and speed matter more than the formality of a full civil trial.

### 5. L. Chandra Kumar (1997) and the tribunalisation debate

*Objective:* State what L. Chandra Kumar decided, and list the arguments for and against moving disputes from courts to tribunals.

**The case.** In **L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India (1997)**, the Supreme Court examined the clauses of Articles 323A and 323B that excluded the High Courts and the Supreme Court from hearing matters given to tribunals. It held:

1. **Judicial review is part of the basic structure** of the Constitution (see GS-173 on the basic structure doctrine).
2. Clause **2(d) of Article 323A** and clause **3(d) of Article 323B** were therefore **unconstitutional**.
3. Tribunal orders are open to challenge before the **High Court**. For the CAT, appeals lie to the **division bench** of the concerned High Court, so a public servant must first go to the High Court and cannot approach the Supreme Court directly.

*Standard knowledge, not in the extract (verify wording):* the Court treated tribunals as **supplemental, not substitutes**, for the High Courts; a tribunal can hear constitutional challenges to laws except to the law that created it, but its decision is subject to the High Court's review.

**The word 'tribunalisation'.** It means the policy of moving whole classes of disputes from the ordinary courts to specialised tribunals. This is the heart of the Mains debate.

| Arguments for | Arguments against |
|---|---|
| **Speed**: a body with one subject can clear cases faster than a general civil court. | Delay can return: vacancies, inadequate staff and heavy lists. |
| **Expertise**: members with tax, company or environmental experience. | Quality may vary; reasoning may be thin if members lack judicial training. |
| **Lower cost and informality**: nominal fee, applicant may appear in person (the CAT). | Extra layer: an appeal to a High Court after the tribunal can lengthen the road. |
| **Relief for courts**: fewer cases in regular courts. | **Independence**: if the executive controls appointment, tenure and service conditions, the tribunal may lean toward the government, which is often a party. |
| **Flexible procedure** (not bound by the Civil Procedure Code, guided by natural justice). | **Weakening the High Courts**, which are the constitutional guardians of rights. |

**Why independence is the central worry.** The government is the biggest litigant in many tribunals (service matters, tax). If it also decides who sits, for how long and on what terms, litigants may doubt fairness. This links to the basic-structure argument: if tribunals replace courts in an important field, they must offer an **effective alternative** to judicial review, with independence in appointment, tenure and conditions. That is why the Supreme Court has repeatedly looked at the rules for tribunal members (module 6).

**A balanced conclusion pattern.** 'Tribunals are a useful institutional response to complexity and volume, but their effectiveness depends on independent appointments, secure tenure, adequate members and infrastructure, and preserved judicial review.' Keep that sentence ready for GS II answers.

### 6. The Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 and the courts' response (deeper layer)

*Objective:* Explain what 'rationalisation of tribunals' meant in 2021 and state the independence issues, flagging every point as 'verify'.

**Status warning.** This module deals with law after Laxmikanth's 2019 text. None of it is in the sources read in this session, so every statement is **reported or standard knowledge to be verified** against the Act and the judgments. Do not write a precise detail in an answer unless you have checked it.

**Background.** Over many years the number of tribunals grew, each with its own Act and its own rules for the qualifications, appointment and tenure of members. Critics found the arrangement fragmented and also found that the executive had a strong hand in appointments. The **Finance Act 2017** reportedly rationalised tribunals by merging some and authorised the Centre to make uniform rules on members' qualifications and service conditions. The Supreme Court, in cases including **Rojer Mathew (2019)** and the **Madras Bar Association** line of cases, examined those rules from the point of view of independence. *Verify names and years.*

**The 2021 Act.** A **Tribunals Reforms (Rationalisation and Conditions of Service) Ordinance** was issued in April 2021, followed by the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021**. The reported features:
- **Abolition of several appellate bodies**, with their work moved to existing courts, chiefly High Courts (reported examples: the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal and the Intellectual Property Appellate Board; verify the list).
- **Uniform service conditions** for members of many tribunals: a **four-year term** (reported), a minimum age of 50 for appointment (reported) and upper age limits of 70 for chairpersons and 67 for members (reported; as GS-187 also notes).
- A **search-cum-selection committee**, with the Central Government choosing from names it recommends.

**Why it was challenged.** Petitioners argued that short terms, a minimum age and a government role in selection undermine independence and separation of powers, and that Parliament was re-enacting provisions the Court had already criticised. In **July 2021** the Supreme Court, in the Madras Bar Association litigation, reportedly struck down some provisions of the Ordinance that preceded the Act (verify), and a later challenge to the Act itself reached the Court. A **November 2025** ruling (Madras Bar Association) is reported to have struck down parts of the 2021 Act (verify); *treat as reported and verify the date, the bench and exactly what was struck down.*

**Impact assessment (as the 2025 Mains asked).** A balanced answer can say, with a 'verify' habit:

| Possible gain | Possible concern |
|---|---|
| Fewer, simpler tribunals; uniform conditions | Independence, if the executive controls tenure and selection |
| Less duplication of institutions | Shifting work to High Courts that already carry heavy lists |
| Clear service rules for members | Tribunals still depend on vacancies being filled and on infrastructure |

**Exam use.** Keep two safe sentences: 'Rationalisation reduced the number of tribunals and standardised member conditions.' 'Its effect on justice depends on whether independence and capacity survive judicial scrutiny.'

### 7. Access to justice I: Article 39A, legal services authorities, NALSA and PIL

*Objective:* Describe the legal aid network, who is eligible, and how PIL widened access, with limits.

**Why legal aid.** A right to justice means little if a person cannot pay a lawyer or court fees. **Article 39A**, a Directive Principle added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**, directs the State to ensure that the legal system promotes justice on the basis of **equal opportunity** and to provide **free legal aid**. **Article 14** (equality before law) and **Article 22(1)** (the right to consult a lawyer on arrest) support it.

**The legal services network.** The **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (in force from **9 November 1995**) created a nationwide structure:

| Level | Body |
|---|---|
| National | **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)**: lays down policies and principles and frames schemes; monitors programmes |
| State | State Legal Services Authority |
| High Court and Supreme Court | High Court Legal Services Committee; Supreme Court Legal Services Committee |
| District and taluk | District Legal Services Authority; Taluk Legal Services Committee |

Their regular functions: give **free and competent legal services** to eligible persons, **organise Lok Adalats**, and run **legal awareness camps**, especially in rural areas.

**What 'free legal services' includes** (source list): payment of court fees and process fees; a lawyer in the proceedings; certified copies of orders; preparation of appeals and paper books including printing and translation.

**Who is eligible** (source list, as printed in 2019): women and children; SC/ST members; industrial workmen; victims of mass disaster, violence, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster; disabled persons; persons in custody; persons whose **annual income does not exceed ₹1 lakh** (₹1,25,000 for the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee); and victims of trafficking or begar. *The income limits are dated and may differ by State or later rule; verify the current figure before using it.* Notice that the list is by **category**, not only by income.

**Public interest litigation (PIL).** PIL is a court action brought **in the public interest**, not only for the petitioner's own right. In India it grew from the Supreme Court's activism in the early 1980s, with Justices **V.R. Krishna Iyer** and **P.N. Bhagwati** as pioneers. It works by relaxing **locus standi**, the traditional rule that only the person whose right is violated may go to court. Under PIL a public-spirited citizen or organisation with 'sufficient interest' may move the court for people too poor or ignorant to do so. NCERT's early examples: the **Hussainara Khatoon** case (1979), on under-trial prisoners held beyond the sentence they would have served, and **Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration** (1980), begun from a prisoner's letter.

*Limits.* The Supreme Court has set guidelines to stop abuse: verify the petitioner's credentials and the contents of the petition, insist on genuine public interest, impose costs on frivolous petitions. NCERT notes the costs of PIL: more load on courts and blurring of lines between the judiciary and the executive and legislature. Laxmikanth lists categories ordinarily entertained (bonded labour, neglected children, minimum wages, jail complaints, police inaction, atrocities on women, SC/ST atrocities, environment and others) and categories not entertained (landlord-tenant, service matters, admission to colleges). Cross-link GS-186 module 8 and GS-173 module 7.

### 8. Access to justice II: Lok Adalats, arbitration, mediation, Family Courts, Gram Nyayalayas, plea bargaining

*Objective:* Distinguish a Lok Adalat, a Permanent Lok Adalat, arbitration and mediation, and describe Family Courts, Gram Nyayalayas and plea bargaining.

**Lok Adalat** ('people's court'). A forum where cases pending in a court, or at the **pre-litigation** stage, are settled by compromise. The first post-Independence Lok Adalat camp was in **Gujarat in 1982**; the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** gave it statutory status. It is organised by legal services authorities, usually with a judicial officer as chairman and a lawyer and social worker as members.
- It may take civil matters and **compoundable criminal offences** (a compoundable offence is one the complainant may lawfully withdraw by compromise); examples in the source include family, bank recovery, labour, pension, consumer, electricity and municipal disputes.
- It has **no jurisdiction over non-compoundable offences**.
- A case reaches it if the parties agree, one party applies, or the court thinks it suitable; a pre-litigation matter is referred on a party's application.
- It has civil-court powers for summoning, evidence and documents; it fixes its own procedure.
- Its **award is deemed a decree of a civil court**, **final and binding**, with **no appeal**; no court fee is charged, and a fee already paid is refunded on settlement (source: P.T. Thomas v Thomas Job, 2005). The 'no appeal' rule closes the appeal route only: a writ under Articles 226/227 remains possible on narrow grounds such as fraud or lack of consent (verify).

**Permanent Lok Adalat** (2002 amendment). For **public utility services** (transport, postal and telephone, power and water supply, sanitation, hospitals, insurance). It has a chairman who is or was a district judge or higher and two members with utility-service experience; a party applies **before** going to court; and if settlement fails it **decides on merits**. The pecuniary limit printed in 2019 is ₹10 lakh (*dated; verify*); the limit was reportedly raised to Rs 1 crore in 2015 (verify). Its award is final and binding, though like a Lok Adalat award it can still be challenged by writ under Articles 226/227 on narrow grounds such as fraud or lack of consent (verify).

**Arbitration and mediation (standard knowledge; verify).** **Arbitration** is a private, consent-based process under the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**: the arbitrator hears both sides and gives a binding award, normally on civil and commercial matters. **Mediation** is facilitated settlement; the **Mediation Act, 2023** is reported to give it a legal framework. The 2024 Mains question asked you to distinguish Lok Adalats from arbitration tribunals and to say whether they hear civil and criminal cases. A safe reading: Lok Adalats help parties compromise and may hear civil cases and compoundable criminal cases; arbitration is an adjudicative award chosen by agreement, typically for civil and commercial disputes (verify any criminal dimension).

**Family Courts** (Act of **1984**). Meant to promote **conciliation** in marriage and family disputes. Subject matter: matrimonial relief, spouses' property, legitimacy, guardianship and custody, maintenance. States must set one up in every city or town with population above **one million**. The court first tries **reconciliation**; parties have no right to a lawyer, though the court may appoint an *amicus curiae*; there is **one appeal, to the High Court**.

**Gram Nyayalayas** (Act of **2008**, following the Law Commission's 114th Report). The court of a **Judicial Magistrate of the first class**, presided by a **Nyayadhikari** appointed by the State with the High Court; a **mobile court** with civil and criminal powers; summary procedure in criminal cases; conciliation first; in civil disputes not bound by the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (Section 30 of the 2008 Act; verify) but guided by natural justice, while criminal cases follow summary-trial procedure; appeals to the Sessions Court (criminal) or District Court (civil), each to be heard and disposed of within six months of filing; an accused may apply for **plea bargaining**. Problems recorded: States already had taluka courts, and police, the Bar and States were reluctant. Do not quote a count of functioning Gram Nyayalayas without a current source.

**Plea bargaining** (standard; verify scope): an accused pleads guilty in return for a lighter sentence; it was introduced into Indian criminal procedure by a 2005 amendment, for lesser offences only.

### 9. Fixing the system: pendency, e-courts, the Law Commission and criminal law reform

*Objective:* Explain the causes of delay qualitatively, name the main reform instruments, and state the 2023 criminal laws cautiously.

**Pendency, without numbers.** *Pendency* (arrears, backlog) means cases waiting to be decided. The sources read give **no case counts**, so this lesson uses none. Laxmikanth and NCERT both say that courts are overburdened, which is why ADR was promoted. Commonly cited causes (analysis for Mains) are: **vacancies and too few judges**; **procedural delay and repeated adjournments**; the **government as a frequent litigant**; weak **court infrastructure and staff**; appeals at every level; and the growth of litigation as rights awareness rises, helped by PIL. The effects: long undertrial detention (the Hussainara Khatoon story), denial of justice to the poor, loss of confidence in law. Reforms attack each cause: more courts and judges, tribunals, ADR, legal aid, technology.

**e-Courts (standard; verify).** The **eCourts Mission Mode Project**, run in phases, aims to computerise courts: case-status information, e-filing, e-payment of fees, video-conferencing and virtual hearings. Treat the phase details and any figure as 'verify'.

**The Law Commission.** An advisory body appointed by the Government to study and recommend law reform (a non-statutory executive body, not a constitutional body; the 23rd Law Commission was reported constituted in 2024; verify its present status and term). In the sources it matters because its reports lead to institutions: the **59th Report (1974)** on family disputes led to Family Courts; the **114th Report** on Gram Nyayalayas led to the 2008 Act; the **222nd Report (2009)** praised ADR as cheaper, quicker, free of technicalities, private and non-adversarial.

**Criminal law reform, 2023 (verify in effect).** Three Acts of 2023 replace the colonial-era codes:

| Old law | Replaced by |
|---|---|
| Indian Penal Code, 1860 | **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** |
| Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 | **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** |
| Indian Evidence Act, 1872 | **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023** |

They came into force on **1 July 2024** (verify any later amendment). Their link to justice reform: faster procedure and the use of electronic means. The 2026 Prelims question on **Zero FIR** shows the style: a Zero FIR lets a person register information at **any police station**, which then forwards it to the station with territorial jurisdiction (standard understanding; read BNSS carefully for the exact wording on e-information, preliminary enquiry and cognizable offences). The Gram Nyayalayas source still mentions the 1872 Evidence Act; after the BSA, check that wording.

**Mains caution.** Do not claim the new codes have 'cleared pendency'. Say they aim at modernisation and speed, and that their effect depends on implementation, training and infrastructure.

### 10. How UPSC tests this topic: PYQ patterns, 'Are tribunals effective?', and elimination

**Skeleton: 'Are tribunals effective?'** (15 marks, 250 words).
1. *Introduction (30 words)*: define a tribunal and its constitutional basis (Part XIV-A, 42nd Amendment).
2. *Where they help*: speed, expertise, informality, nominal fee (CAT), relief to courts.
3. *Where they fall short*: vacancies and infrastructure, executive influence over appointment and tenure, an extra layer of appeal, variable quality.
4. *Constitutional guardrail*: Chandra Kumar (1997); the 2021 reform debate flagged 'reported'.
5. *Way forward*: independent selection, secure tenure, filled vacancies, training, e-filing, periodic audit.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Tribunal: a body created by law to adjudicate disputes in a particular field; it sits outside the regular court ladder but is subject to High Court review after L. Chandra Kumar (1997).
- Administrative tribunal: a tribunal for recruitment and service-condition disputes of public servants, under Article 323A and the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985.
- Quasi-judicial body: one that decides disputes with a fair hearing and a binding order but is not a regular court.
- Tribunalisation: the policy of moving classes of disputes from ordinary courts to specialised tribunals.
- Alternative dispute resolution (ADR): methods other than a regular trial, namely negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration and Lok Adalats.
- Lok Adalat: people's court; a statutory forum for compromise of pending and pre-litigation disputes under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987.
- Compoundable offence: an offence that the complainant may lawfully settle by compromise; Lok Adalats may take these but not non-compoundable offences.
- Locus standi: the traditional rule that only the person whose right is violated may approach the court; relaxed in PIL.
- Pendency: the stock of cases awaiting decision; discussed here qualitatively because the sources give no counts.

### Notes: Articles and Acts at a glance

- Article 323A: administrative tribunals for public service matters; Parliament only; no hierarchy.
- Article 323B: tribunals for taxation, foreign exchange and trade, industrial and labour matters, land reforms, urban land ceiling, elections, foodstuffs, rent and tenancy; Parliament and State legislatures; hierarchy allowed.
- Part XIV-A: added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976; rent and tenancy item added by the 75th Amendment Act, 1993.
- Article 39A: free legal aid and equal opportunity (Directive Principle, added in 1976).
- Articles 14 and 22(1): equality before law and the right to consult a lawyer; they support legal aid.
- Article 40 (village panchayats) is treated in GS-195; Articles 233-237 on subordinate courts are in GS-187.
- Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 (CAT, SATs, JATs); Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (in force 9 November 1995; amended 2002 for Permanent Lok Adalats); Family Courts Act, 1984; Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008.
- 2023 criminal laws: BNS replaces the IPC, BNSS replaces the CrPC, BSA replaces the Evidence Act; in force reported 1 July 2024 (verify).

### Notes: Cause and effect: delay and reform

- Too few judges and vacancies -> slow disposal -> accumulating arrears -> pressure for more courts and tribunals.
- Formal, technical procedure -> high cost and delay for ordinary people -> ADR, Lok Adalats and simplified procedure.
- Poverty and distance -> poor cannot reach courts -> legal aid, PIL and Gram Nyayalayas.
- Executive as a major litigant and as appointing authority -> doubts on tribunal independence -> judicial scrutiny of tribunal rules.
- Colonial-era codes seen as dated -> 2023 criminal law overhaul aimed at modernisation, with outcomes depending on implementation.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Lok Adalat versus Permanent Lok Adalat: the first only helps compromise; the second may decide on merits for public utility services.
- Lok Adalat versus arbitration: compromise by agreement versus an award by a chosen arbitrator.
- Article 323A versus Article 323B: service matters, Parliament only, no hierarchy versus other matters, Parliament and States, hierarchy allowed.
- CAT versus High Court: the CAT is a specialised first forum for service matters; the High Court hears appeals and has writ jurisdiction.
- Gram Nyayalaya versus Nyaya Panchayat: a Gram Nyayalaya is a court of a first-class magistrate under the 2008 Act; Nyaya Panchayats are village bodies in some States that try petty cases.
- Legal aid versus PIL: legal aid pays for a person's own case; PIL is brought for a public cause or for people who cannot approach the court.
