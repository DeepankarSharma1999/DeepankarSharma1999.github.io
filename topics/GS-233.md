## GS-233: GST, direct and indirect taxes

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain GST, direct and indirect taxes and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Direct versus indirect taxes

Tax revenue is a revenue receipt that creates no liability for the government. NCERT separates direct taxes, on individual incomes and on company profits, from indirect taxes such as customs duties on goods crossing the border and the older excise on domestic production. Equity is pursued through progressive income tax, where the rate rises with income, while firms pay a proportional rate on profits. Under the old excise design, necessities were exempt or lightly taxed and luxuries, tobacco and petroleum heavily taxed. Wealth, gift and estate taxes earned so little that NCERT calls them paper taxes. The 2026 FRBM statement budgets direct taxes at about three-fifths (61.2 per cent) of Gross Tax Revenue in BE 2026-27, so income-based taxes now outweigh transaction-based ones in central collections.

### How GST removed cascading

Before GST, a good was taxed at each stage on its full value, including taxes already embedded in its inputs, and credit for earlier tax was limited. Tax therefore piled up on tax. GST levies tax at every stage of supply but lets the supplier set off the tax paid at the previous stage, so in effect only the value added is taxed and the final burden reaches the consumer in the state of consumption. The reform needed a constitutional change: Article 246A gave the Union and the States concurrent power over GST, followed by the CGST, SGST and UTGST Acts. NCERT credits GST with a common national market, standardised procedures, online compliance through a common portal and a wider tax base.

### What stays outside, and recent rationalisation

GST is not universal. Five petroleum products were kept out for the time being, liquor for human consumption stays under state VAT, and tobacco attracts both GST and central excise. The 2026 FRBM statement records a major rationalisation: on the GST Council's recommendation the earlier four-tier structure was compressed into an 18 per cent standard rate and a 5 per cent merit rate, with a 40 per cent demerit rate for a select few goods and services. The same statement lists faster, automated registration for low-risk applicants and risk-based provisional refunds for zero-rated supplies. For Mains, link simpler rates to compliance and consumption, as the statement does, while noting that petroleum and alcohol still sit outside the credit chain.
