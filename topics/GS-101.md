### Bridge: Bridge: reading a plan view, a side view and a cross-section

Geography sketches look at the land from three directions, and mixing them up is the commonest drawing mistake.

A **plan view** is the view from directly above, like a map. It shows positions across the ground, for example which way the horns of a dune point or how three cirques sit round a peak. A **side view (long section)** cuts the land lengthwise along the path of an agent and shows heights along that path, for example a glacier flowing from its snowfield down to its snout. A **cross-section** cuts across a valley and shows its shape, for example the narrow V of a river valley against the broad U of a glacial trough.

In every sketch draw an arrow for the direction of ice flow or wind, label each landform by name, and check that the arrow points the same way in the plan view and the section. The text figures in this lesson use plain characters; redraw them by hand in your own style, because drawing from memory is a retrieval exercise.

### 1. Orientation: two sculptors, one of ice and one of air

*Objective:* Say what a landform is, name the two agents of this lesson and the family each landform belongs to (erosional or depositional).

## Meaning

A **landform** is a shape on the Earth's surface: a hill, a valley, a ridge, a hollow. Rock is first loosened by weathering. An **agent** then picks it up and carries it (**erosion**) and later drops it when it slows down (**deposition**). Weathering, erosion and mass wasting are taught in Day 21 lesson GS-099 and rivers in Day 22 lesson GS-100; this lesson adds two more agents.

- **Glaciers** are moving ice. Ice is a solid, so it moves slowly, driven by gravity, but it is heavy and carries rock frozen into its base. NCERT (kegy206, PDF p8) says glaciers can damage even un-weathered rock and can reduce high mountains to low hills and plains.
- **Wind** is moving air. Air is light, so wind does serious work only where the ground is dry, bare and loose. Leong (PDF p99) says wind is not the most effective agent overall but is more efficient in arid than in humid regions, because little vegetation or moisture binds the surface.

The word **aeolian** (from the Greek wind-god Aeolus; standard usage, not defined in the extracts) means "made or carried by wind".

## The two families

Every landform in this lesson is either **erosional** (rock removed, a hollow or a bare ridge is left) or **depositional** (debris dropped, a mound, plain or ridge is built). Ask two questions of any landform: which agent made it, and did it take rock away or leave it?

## Why climate decides

Glaciers need snow to pile up faster than it melts, so they sit on high mountains and near the poles. Wind needs bare dry land, so deserts show wind's work best (Leong, PDF pp96-99). Modules 2 to 5 take ice, modules 6 to 9 take wind (and the water that also shapes deserts), and module 10 takes the two official Mains questions.

The types of ice and why ice is shrinking belong to Day 19 lesson GS-111, and the fjord as a coast type to Day 17 lesson GS-102 (module 5); here the fjord is explained as a glacial landform.

### 2. Foundation: how a glacier moves and does its work

*Objective:* Describe how a glacier forms and moves, name its three forms, and explain plucking and abrasion.

## From snow to a moving river of ice

Fresh snow is light and powdery. As it settles, the crystals are lost, air is squeezed out and it becomes **firn** (also called **neve**); more weight on top compacts it into ice (Leong, PDF p85). Under gravity the ice flows downslope along existing valleys: this is a **glacier**, a "river of ice". The ice-type vocabulary (ice sheet, ice cap, ice shelf, iceberg) and the gains and losses of a glacier are in Day 19 lesson GS-111.

NCERT (PDF p8) says glaciers move because of gravity, a few centimetres to a few metres a day. Leong gives about one metre a day for the Alps, and says movement is fastest in the middle, where there is least friction. A row of stakes planted straight across a glacier bends into a curve pointing downvalley as the centre outruns the sides (Leong Fig. 6.3).

## Three forms of glacier

| Form | What it is | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Valley (mountain, Alpine) glacier | Linear flow down a broad trough-like valley | NCERT p8; Leong p85 |
| Piedmont glacier | Ice spread over the plain at the foot of mountains, where valley glaciers meet; Malaspina Glacier, Alaska, is Leong's example | NCERT p8; Leong p86 |
| Continental glacier (ice sheet) | Vast sheet of ice over a land area | NCERT p8 |

NCERT groups glaciers as sheets (continental, or piedmont at a mountain foot) and linear valley flows.

## How ice does its work

Leong names two erosional processes:

- **Plucking**: the glacier freezes onto the joints and cracks of the bed rock, tears out blocks and drags them away.
- **Abrasion**: the rock fragments frozen into the ice scratch, scrape, polish and scour the floor and walls like sandpaper.

The dragged rock is the glacier's tool. Large angular fragments cut grooves called **striations**; the finest material polishes the surface and makes **rock flour** (Leong, PDF p88). Leong lists what controls the rate of erosion: speed of flow, steepness of the slope, weight of the glacier, temperature of the ice and the structure of the rock.

## Highland erosion, lowland deposition

Leong (PDF p86) states the pattern: glaciation generally gives **erosional** features in the highlands and **depositional** features on the lowlands, but the two are not exclusive, because one glacier erodes, carries and deposits along its whole course. NCERT (p8) adds the end state: as debris is removed and divides are lowered, the slope eases until the glacier stops moving, leaving low hills, outwash plains and depositional forms.

`HIGH, STEEP: plucking + abrasion -> hollows, ridges, U-valleys   |   LOW, GENTLE: ice melts -> till, moraines, outwash`

### 3. Core: erosion in the highlands - cirque, tarn, arete and horn

*Objective:* Explain how a cirque, tarn, arete and horn form and say which one needs three or more cirques.

## Cirque (corrie, cwm)

NCERT (PDF p8) calls the **cirque** the most common landform of glaciated mountains. It sits at the head of a glacial valley: a deep, wide basin with very steep concave walls at its head and sides. Leong (PDF pp87-88) explains the steps (Fig. 6.6):

1. Snow and firn collect in a hollow high on the mountain.
2. Freeze-thaw shatters the rock; plucking steepens the back wall.
3. The moving ice abrades the floor and over-deepens the hollow into a horseshoe-shaped basin with a rocky lip at the exit.
4. When the ice melts, debris is left on the lip, and water collects behind this barrier as a lake called a **tarn** (NCERT: cirque lake or tarn lake).

The names differ by place: **cirque** is French, **corrie** is Scottish and **cwm** is Welsh (Leong). NCERT notes that two or more cirques can lead one into another in a stepped sequence.

At the top of the glacier, where moving ice pulls away from the snowfield, a deep crack opens, the **bergschrund** (German) or rimaye (French). It forms in summer, when ice keeps moving out but no new snow replaces it. Further down, where the glacier bends or steepens, other cracks form, called **crevasses** (Leong, PDF p88).

## Arete

The divide between two neighbouring cirques is attacked from both sides. As the cirques cut back, the divide narrows to a knife-edge ridge. Leong calls it an **arete** (two corries cutting back until they nearly meet); NCERT calls the same thing a serrated or saw-toothed ridge with a very sharp crest and zig-zag outline.

## Horn (pyramidal peak)

Where cirques gather round one mountain, headward erosion carves the peak into a sharp pyramid. NCERT: if **three or more** radiating glaciers cut headward until their cirques meet, high, sharp, steep-sided peaks called **horns** form; it names the **Matterhorn** (called the highest peak of the Alps in NCERT's wording; verify, since Mont Blanc is the highest Alpine summit) and **Everest** as horns. Leong's name for the same landform is **pyramidal peak**, with the Matterhorn on the Swiss-Italian border as the example.

## Plan view

`  cirque A    arete    cirque B
        \   |   /
         [ HORN ]          arete = ridge between two cirques
        /    |    \        horn = peak with three or more cirques round it
  cirque C  arete  cirque D`

## Two exceptions

The roche moutonnee and crag and tail are erosional relics found mostly on lowlands (Leong: roches moutonnees occur in highlands too); they come with the depositional features in module 5.

### 4. Core: troughs, hanging valleys and fjords

*Objective:* Explain how a glacier makes a U-shaped trough with truncated spurs, hanging valleys and ribbon lakes, and how a fjord forms.

## The U-shaped trough

A river cuts a narrow V because it cuts mainly downward (Day 22 lesson GS-100). A glacier is a broad mass of ice, so it wears away the sides and the floor together. NCERT (PDF p9) describes the glaciated valley as trough-like and U-shaped, with a broad floor and steep, relatively smooth sides. Leong (PDF pp88-89) adds the detail:

- Fed by ice from several corries, the glacier **scratches and grinds** its bed and removes soil and loose debris.
- The ice does not wind around the **interlocking spurs** of a river valley; it blunts them into **truncated spurs**, whose faces NCERT compares to triangular facets.
- Over-deepened stretches fill with water as long narrow **ribbon (trough or finger) lakes**; Leong's examples are Loch Ness and Lake Ullswater in Britain.

| Feature | River valley | Glacial trough |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-section | V | U |
| Floor | Narrow | Broad, flat |
| Spurs | Interlocking | Truncated |
| Floor in long section | Smooth, graded | Rock basins, rock steps, lakes |

## Hanging valleys

The main valley holds a much larger glacier, so it is cut much deeper than the small tributary valleys. When the ice melts, the tributary valley ends high above the main floor and its stream plunges down as a waterfall (Leong, PDF p89). The drop can give a natural head of water for hydroelectric power.

## Rock basins and rock steps

Ice excavates unevenly. Soft or jointed rock gives **rock basins**, later filled by lakes. Where a tributary joins, the extra weight of ice cuts deeper and leaves a **rock step**, a stair of steps along the floor.

## Fjords

Both books define the fjord. NCERT (p9): very deep glacial troughs filled with sea water, forming shorelines in high latitudes. Leong (PDF pp90, 134-135): a submerged U-shaped glacial trough marking the path of a glacier that plunged from the highlands. Its features follow from the steps above:

1. A glacier cuts a deep U-trough down to, and below, sea level.
2. The ice melts (or the sea rises) and the sea floods the lower trough.
3. Steep walls rise straight from the water; tributaries (hanging valleys) join the main inlet at right angles.
4. The trough is deep far inland but has a shallow **threshold**, a rock ridge at the seaward end (sometimes only about 60 m deep), with islands called skerries off the coast.

Examples in Leong: Norway, Alaska, British Columbia, southern Chile and the South Island of New Zealand, all high-latitude coasts once glaciated. Leong gives the Sogne Fiord of Norway as 177 km long, 6 km wide and almost 1,220 m deep in mid-channel (as in the book; modern surveys give it as longer and deeper, about 205 km and about 1,300 m; verify). Few big ports stand on fjords, because the mountains behind limit access (Leong).

## Why fjords look picturesque (Mains 2023)

The extracts do not use the word, so this is reasoning from the features above (verify with a tourism source): steep walls rising from still deep water, waterfalls spilling from hanging valleys, islands and a mountain backdrop. Leong (PDF p94) also notes that glaciated mountains attract tourists.

Coast types are in Day 17 lesson GS-102 module 5.

### 5. Core: what glaciers deposit - till, moraines, erratics, eskers and drumlins

*Objective:* Name and distinguish the main depositional landforms of ice and the two erosional relics of lowland glaciation.

## Till and outwash

NCERT (PDF p9) separates two deposits. **Glacial till** is the unsorted mixture of coarse and fine debris dropped directly by melting ice; most fragments are angular. **Outwash** (glacio-fluvial) deposits are carried and dropped by melt-water streams, so they are roughly **stratified and sorted**, with somewhat rounded edges. Leong calls till *boulder clay*: spread in sheets rather than mounds, forming drift plains.

## Moraines

A **moraine** is a ridge or sheet of glacial debris. The kinds (NCERT pp9-10; Leong pp89-90):

| Moraine | Where it forms |
|---|---|
| Lateral | Along the sides of the valley, parallel to it; can join a terminal moraine in a horseshoe |
| Medial | In the centre, where the inside lateral moraines of two merging glaciers unite |
| Ground | Irregular sheet spread over the valley floor by a retreating glacier |
| Terminal (end) | At the snout, where the glacier stood; a pile left by the toe |
| Recessional | A series of end ridges left as the ice melted back by stages (Leong) |

## Erratics

An **erratic** is a boulder carried by ice and left stranded on rock of a different type (Leong, PDF p92). Because its rock differs from the local rock, it traces the source and direction of ice movement; a large one balanced where the ice dropped it is a *perched block*.

## Features of melt-water

- **Outwash plain**: broad flat fans of gravel, silt, sand and clay beyond the terminal moraine (NCERT p10). **Kames** are small rounded hillocks of sand and gravel, and a **kettle** is a hollow left where a buried block of ice melted, often a lake, giving 'knob and kettle' ground (Leong).
- **Esker**: a long, narrow, sinuous ridge that marks the bed of a former stream flowing under or through the ice (NCERT p10; Leong p93). Leong describes its material as stratified sand and gravel, while NCERT describes very coarse boulders and blocks with minor finer debris (verify). In an option, rely on the shape (long, sinuous ridge) and the origin (a stream under the ice). Leong gives heights from a few metres to 60 m and a 160 km Maine example.

## Drumlin

A **drumlin** is a smooth oval ridge of till whose long axis is parallel to the direction of ice movement (NCERT p11). Its **stoss end**, facing the oncoming ice, is blunter and steeper than the tapering tail, so it records the way the glacier moved. Leong's 'basket of eggs' groups are in County Down (Northern Ireland) and the Great Lakes plain. NCERT gives up to about 1 km long and 30 m high; Leong says a few metres up to 120 m, so treat sizes as book values.

## Erosional relics on lowlands (Leong, PDF pp91-92)

- **Roche moutonnee**: a resistant rock hummock smoothed by abrasion on its upstream side and roughened and steepened by plucking on its downstream side.
- **Crag and tail**: a hard rock crag with a steep upstream face that shields a gentler tail of softer rock and debris; Castle Rock, Edinburgh, is the classic case.

### 6. Core: where deserts lie, why rock breaks there, and the five desert surfaces

*Objective:* Locate the hot deserts, explain why bare desert rock breaks up, and name the five kinds of desert landscape.

## Where the deserts are

About a fifth of the world's land is desert: some rocky, some stony, the rest sandy; true deserts where nothing grows are rare (Leong, PDF p96). Almost all lie between **15 and 30 degrees** north and south of the equator, in the trade wind belt on the western parts of continents, where the trades blow off the land and, on some coasts, cold currents dry the air. These are the tropical **hot (trade wind) deserts**: Sahara, Arabian, Iranian, Thar, Kalahari, Namib, Atacama, Great Australian and those of south-west USA and northern Mexico. Deep inside the mid-latitude continents the Gobi and Turkestan deserts have extreme temperatures. The air-pressure reason is in Day 19 lesson GS-104, the climate in Day 18 lesson GS-110 (dry climates), and the cold-current reason in Day 23 lesson GS-108.

## Why bare rock breaks

With almost no plants, bare rock is heated hard by day and cools quickly at night. Leong says the outer skin expands and contracts faster than the inside, cracks and peels off in thin layers like an onion (**exfoliation**, PDF p98). NCERT treats exfoliation as a result rather than a process and links exfoliation domes to unloading (Day 21 lesson GS-099, module 4); in an answer write 'thermal stress, aided by moisture and salt, contributes to exfoliation' (verify). Water in cracks that freezes on a cold night expands (Leong says by 10 per cent of its volume) and prises fragments off. A little chemical weathering also works. The fragments become the "teeth" of wind erosion.

## Five desert landscapes (Leong, PDF pp97-98)

| Type | Surface | Example or local name |
|---|---|---|
| Hamada | Bare rock swept clean of sand and dust, smooth and polished | Hamada el Homra, Libya (almost 52,000 sq km) |
| Reg (serir in Libya and Egypt) | Sheets of angular pebbles and gravel the wind cannot lift | Camel herds graze; easier to cross than sand |
| Erg | Sand sea with dunes; ripples show wind direction | Calanscio Sand Sea, Libya; koum in Turkestan |
| Badlands | Hills cut into gullies and ravines by rare rainstorms | South Dakota; Painted Desert, Arizona |
| Mountain desert | Dissected highlands with wadis and frost-cut edges | Ahaggar and Tibesti, Sahara |

A key warning: **only the erg is sandy dune country**. Many deserts are rocky or stony, so "desert means sand" is a wrong shortcut. Leong adds that the Sahara was wetter about 2,000 years ago, so some features may be older than today's climate (verify).

## Two agents in a desert

NCERT (PDF p13) names wind as one of the two dominant agents in hot deserts; the other is water. Rain is scarce but falls torrentially, so sheet floods and sheet wash do most of the general lowering, while wind moves the fine material. Leong (PDF p97) stresses that even with extremely low rainfall, the intensity of rain makes running water a major agent of denudation. Module 8 takes water; modules 7 and 9 take wind.

### 7. Core: how wind moves sand and the landforms it erodes

*Objective:* Name the three modes of sand transport and three wind erosion processes, and match pedestal rocks, zeugen, yardangs and deflation hollows to their causes.

## Three ways wind carries sand (Leong, PDF p99)

1. **Suspension**: very fine particles, finer than about 0.2 mm, float in the air. They are carried far and cause little erosion, because the dust-laden wind flows round obstacles.
2. **Saltation**: grains hop. Turbulent air lifts a grain, it falls and bounces forward in the wind direction, and each landing can start another grain moving. Saltation rarely rises above **1.5 m**, so most sand-blasting happens low down.
3. **Surface creep**: grains too heavy to hop are pushed along the ground, often started by the impact of saltating grains.

How much moves depends on wind speed, moisture and sand supply. NCERT (PDF p14) adds that wind is a **good sorting agent**: different grain sizes drop at different speeds.

## Three erosion processes

| Process | What happens | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Deflation | Lifting and blowing away loose material, lowering the surface; helped by dry soil, little vegetation and medium to strong wind | Leong p99; NCERT p13 |
| Abrasion | Sand-blasting of rock by hurled grains; strongest near the base of rocks | Leong p100; NCERT p13 |
| Attrition (Leong) or impact (NCERT) | Grains knock against each other and wear away, becoming rounded 'millet seed' sand; NCERT's impact is the sheer blow of sand against rock | Leong p100; NCERT p13 |

The two books use different third terms; learn both.

## Landforms of wind erosion

- **Deflation hollows (basins)**: shallow depressions blown out where loose cover is removed. Leong calls them playas, pans or dayas (in this lesson 'playa' means the basin lake of module 8). If the floor sinks to the water table, moisture stops further lifting and plants grow: an **oasis**. Leong's Egyptian examples are the Qattara Depression and the Faiyum Depression (40 m below sea level). Leong prints Qattara as 135 m below sea level in one place and minus 133 m in another: check before quoting.
- **Rock pedestals and mushroom rocks**: abrasion cuts a rock mainly near its base, where most sand is carried. The result is a slender stalk under a broad rounded cap. NCERT (PDF p14) also lists table rocks, and describes blow-outs that deepen into caves.
- **Zeugen**: tabular blocks with a resistant layer lying over a soft layer. Weathering opens joints; abrasion eats the soft layer into furrows, leaving ridges that may overhang. Leong: they stand 3 to 30 m above the furrows.
- **Yardangs**: like zeugen but the hard and soft rock lie in **vertical bands aligned with the prevailing wind**, so the wind excavates long corridors between steep ridges. Atacama is a common site; Central Asia has the best ones, rising 8 to 15 m (Leong).
- **Ventifacts (dreikanter)**: pebbles faceted by sand-blasting; one with three wind-cut faces is a dreikanter. Many together form **desert pavement**, a smooth mosaic of pebbles.

## Zeugen against yardang

`ZEUGEN: horizontal layers, hard cap over soft base   |   YARDANG: vertical bands, long axis along the wind
 [=====]  [=====]  ridges                              |  >>wind>>  ||  ||  ||  ridges and corridors
 \soft furrows/                                        |  >>wind>>  ||  ||  ||`

### 8. Core: floods, fans and slopes - wadi, alluvial fan, pediment, playa and mesa

*Objective:* Explain the water-made landforms of deserts and arrange them in order from mountain front to basin floor.

## Flash floods

Desert storms last one to three hours. With sparse plants and dry soil, a great volume of rain runs straight off. Torrents called **flash floods** sweep gravel, sand and dust down the slopes and cut gullies and ravines, creating badland ground. If a flood carries much sediment it becomes a **mud flow** (Leong, PDF p104). NCERT (PDF p13) says the same work is done as **sheet floods**: rain falling on bare rock and washing the weathered debris away.

## Alluvial fans, bajada and wadi

When the torrent leaves the mountains onto a plain, the gradient drops suddenly, the flow loses carrying power and drops its load as a cone of alluvium with the **apex pointing uphill**: an **alluvial fan**. Neighbouring fans can merge into a **bajada** (alluvial plain). Leong cites the Basin and Range province of the USA and the Himalayan foot as examples.

A **wadi** is a large desert stream channel that starts in highlands and ends in a depression or at the coast. It flows only after heavy rain; the Nile survives because its source lies in humid country outside the desert (Leong, PDF pp104-105).

## Pediment, pediplain and inselberg (NCERT, PDF pp13-14)

A **pediment** is a gently sloping rocky floor at the foot of mountains, with or without a thin debris cover, cut by lateral erosion of streams and by sheet floods. Slopes above it retreat in parallel, stage by stage (**parallel retreat of slopes**, backwasting), so the pediment widens at the mountain's expense. The shrinking mountain finally leaves an isolated remnant, an **inselberg** (German for island mountain), and the whole high-relief area becomes a low featureless **pediplain**. Leong adds that inselbergs, often of granite and gneiss, have steep slopes (Ayers Rock, or Uluru, Australia, is his example; it is a sandstone inselberg, not granite; verify) and offers two explanations: slope retreat, or deep weathering and stripping of the weathered cover.

## Playa and alkali flat

Where hills enclose a basin, drainage runs to the centre and sediment builds a nearly level plain. After rain a shallow lake forms, a **playa** (also salina, salar, or shott in North Africa), and it evaporates quickly, leaving salts; a salt-covered playa is an **alkali flat** (NCERT p14; Leong p105).

## Mesa and butte

A **mesa** (Spanish: table) is a flat-topped landform capped by a resistant layer, with very steep sides; when the cap breaks up and the table shrinks, a smaller **butte** remains. Leong cites the Grand Canyon region of Arizona and Table Mountain, Cape Town.

## Profile from mountain to basin

| Zone | Process | Landform |
|---|---|---|
| Mountain front | Backwasting | Steep slope, cliff |
| Foot of mountain | Erosion | Pediment |
| Beyond | Deposition | Alluvial fans, bajada |
| Basin centre | Evaporation | Playa, alkali flat |

`MOUNTAIN | pediment (eroded rock floor) | bajada (fans of alluvium) | PLAYA   <- inselberg = lone remnant on the plain`

Do not mix pediment (erosional, rock) with bajada (depositional, alluvium).

### 9. Core: wind deposition - dunes, loess and ripples

*Objective:* Distinguish barchan, seif or longitudinal, transverse and parabolic dunes, explain loess and state the naming variants of NCERT and Leong.

## Why wind deposits

The wind sorts the load. Coarse sand stays in the desert as dunes; the finest dust travels as far as 3,700 km (Leong, PDF p102). NCERT (PDF p14): dunes need a sand source, fairly constant wind and an obstacle to start them.

## Dunes

A **dune** is a hill of sand built by wind. Active (live) dunes keep moving; inactive (fixed) dunes are held by plants (Leong). The main kinds:

| Dune | Shape and wind | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Barchan | Crescent; wings (horns) point **downwind**; gentle convex windward side, steep concave leeward slip face; forms where wind is steady and moderate over a uniform surface | NCERT p15; Leong p103 |
| Seif (Leong) / longitudinal | Long narrow ridge **parallel** to the prevailing wind, often over 100 km long and may exceed 60 m in height (Leong); winds sweep the corridors between ridges clear | Leong p103 |
| Longitudinal (NCERT) | Long, low ridges where sand supply is poor and wind direction constant | NCERT p15 |
| Transverse | Ridge **across** the wind; steady wind, elongated sand source at right angles | NCERT p15 |
| Parabolic | Forms on partly vegetated sand; a 'reversed barchan' with the arms pointing upwind | NCERT p15 |

**Naming variant.** NCERT describes a seif as barchan-like with only one wing, produced when wind conditions shift, and keeps longitudinal dunes separate. Leong equates seif with longitudinal dune, which is the usual modern usage (standard; verify). In an exam, read what the option describes (parallel to wind, long, narrow) rather than relying on the name alone.

**How a barchan moves.** Sand is driven up the windward side; at the crest it slips down the slip face, so the whole dune advances. Leong gives about 15 m a year for low dunes and about 8 m a year for dunes up to 30 m high. Advancing barchans can bury palm trees, houses and oases; sand-holding trees and grasses are planted to halt them.

## Loess

Fine dust blown beyond the desert margin settles as **loess**: a yellow, friable, porous, lime-rich and usually very fertile loam (Leong, PDF p103). Water sinks in so fast that the surface stays dry; streams cut deep valleys, and badlands can develop. The largest deposit is the loess plateau of the Hwang-Ho (Yellow River) basin in north-west China, about 650,000 sq km and 60 to 150 m deep, built from Gobi dust. The word comes from a village in Alsace, France. Leong says similar deposits in Germany, France and Belgium (called *limon*) and in the US Mid-West were blown from ice-sheet material, so loess links the two halves of this lesson: ice grinds rock to flour and wind spreads it.

**Ripples** are small wavy surfaces, a few centimetres high, on sand sheets and dune slopes (Leong).

## India link

The Indian desert north-west of the Aravali has longitudinal dunes and barchans and rainfall below 150 mm a year, as taught in Day 21 lesson GS-113 (the Indian Desert).


### 10. Exam: aeolian processes and desertification, fjords, and answer frames

*Objective:* Use the two official Mains questions to build answer frames and avoid the common Prelims traps in glacial and desert landforms.

## Official question 1 (Mains GS I 2026, Q6, 10 marks)

*Discuss the role of aeolian processes in desertification and land degradation.* A frame for about 150 words:

1. **Define**: aeolian processes are erosion, transport and deposition by wind; desertification is deterioration of a dry-land ecosystem until its biological productivity is destroyed (Leong, via Day 18 lesson GS-110); land degradation is the wider loss of soil and productivity. A formal UN definition exists (verify before quoting).
2. **Deflation** strips loose, dry, unprotected topsoil, especially where plants are removed: the Great Dust Bowl in the western USA, farmed bare and then blown away (Leong, PDF p100). Dust storms carry fines for thousands of kilometres.
3. **Dune advance**: barchans move about 8 to 15 m a year (Leong) and bury oases, farmland and houses; ergs are hard to cross.
4. **Deposition and abrasion**: sand blankets fertile soil; sand-blasting scours surfaces (abrasion is concentrated near ground level, so low crops are exposed; standard, verify).
5. **Balance**: wind is not the only cause. Overgrazing, clearing of cover and drought expose soil (Day 18 lesson GS-110), and loess shows wind can also build fertile land.
6. **Remedies**: sand-holding trees and grasses to halt dunes (Leong); wider shelter-belt and regional programmes are standard knowledge to be verified from a current source before naming schemes.

## Official question 2 (Mains GS I 2023, Q6, 10 marks)

*How are the fjords formed? Why do they constitute some of the most picturesque areas of the world?* Use module 4: **formation** in four steps (U-trough, flooding by sea, steep walls with hanging valleys, shallow threshold); **picturesque** features (sheer walls, waterfalls, deep calm water, islands, mountain backdrop; flagged as reasoning). Add a balancing line from Leong: few big ports are on fjords because the mountain barrier limits access.

### Notes: Human aspects of glaciated lands and deserts

- Glaciated hills: ice and glaciers stripped much topsoil in places such as Scandinavia, leaving thin, bare soils; summer pastures on benches and drifts support transhumance, moving cattle up in summer and down in winter (Leong pp93-94).
- Fertile and infertile: boulder clay plains (East Anglia, the Mid-West of the USA) and loess plains are among the most fertile; sandy outwash heaths (geest of northern Germany), marshy boulder clay in central Ireland and ice-scoured shields (Canadian and Baltic) are infertile (Leong p94).
- Obstacles and resources: erratics and perched blocks hinder farming; eskers and kames are quarried for sand and gravel; lakes in glaciated mountains serve as reservoirs; waterfalls from hanging valleys feed hydroelectric power in Scandinavia, Switzerland and Canada; glaciated mountains attract tourists (Leong p94).
- Deserts: low productivity supports few people; settlement clusters in oases, river valleys, deltas and coastal plains; crude oil, phosphates, salts and gold draw people; vast groundwater reserves are largely untapped (Leong p107).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Arete (ridge between two cirques) against horn (peak with three or more cirques).
- Drumlin (oval hill of till, aligned with flow) against esker (sinuous ridge laid by a stream under the ice; Leong: sorted sand and gravel) against kame (small mound of sand and gravel).
- Till (unsorted, dropped by ice) against outwash (sorted, laid by melt-water).
- Zeugen (horizontal strata) against yardang (vertical bands aligned with the wind).
- Pediment (erosional rock floor) against bajada (depositional alluvium); playa (the lake) against alkali flat (the salt crust).
- Hamada (bare rock) against reg or serir (stones) against erg (sand): only the erg has dunes.
- Roche moutonnee (smooth upstream face, plucked downstream face) against crag and tail (hard crag shelters a tail).
- Loess (fine wind-blown dust, fertile, beyond the desert) against dune sand (coarse, stays inside the desert).

### Notes: Definitions

- Plucking: a glacier freezes onto rock joints and tears out blocks (Leong p86).
- Abrasion: scraping and scouring by rock fragments carried in ice, or sand-blasting by wind-borne grains (Leong p86, p100).
- Deflation: lifting and blowing away of loose material, lowering the land surface (Leong p99).
- Attrition: wearing of wind-borne particles against one another into rounded grains (Leong p100).
- Saltation: hopping movement of sand grains in the wind, rarely above 1.5 m (Leong p99).
- Oasis: a vegetation patch where a deflation hollow has reached the water table (Leong p100).
- Wadi: a large desert stream channel that flows only after heavy rain (Leong p105).

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-NCERT-GLACIER-MOVEMENT (verified):** NCERT: glaciers are masses of ice moving as sheets over the land (a continental glacier, or a piedmont glacier where a vast sheet spreads over the plains at the foot of mountains) or as linear flows down mountain slopes in broad trough-like valleys (mountain and valley glaciers); the movement is slow, from a few centimetres to a few metres a day or even less or more, basically because of gravity.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GLACIER-EROSION (verified):** NCERT: erosion by glaciers is tremendous because of the friction caused by the sheer weight of the ice; the material plucked from the land (usually large angular blocks and fragments) is dragged along valley floors and sides and damages them through abrasion and plucking, so glaciers can reduce high mountains to low hills and plains, finally leaving low hills and vast outwash plains.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CIRQUE-TARN (verified):** NCERT: cirques are the most common landforms of glaciated mountains, found at the heads of glacial valleys; they are deep, long and wide troughs or basins with very steep concave or vertical walls at the head and sides, and the lake that often forms in a cirque after the glacier disappears is a cirque or tarn lake; two or more cirques can lead one into another in a stepped sequence.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-HORN-ARETE (verified):** NCERT: horns form through headward erosion of cirque walls, when three or more radiating glaciers cut headward until their cirques meet, leaving a high, sharp, steep-sided peak; the divides between cirque walls narrow with progressive erosion into serrated, saw-toothed ridges called aretes, with a very sharp crest and zig-zag outline.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GLACIAL-TROUGH (verified):** NCERT: glaciated valleys are trough-like and U-shaped, with broad floors and relatively smooth, steep sides; hanging valleys can lie at an elevation on one or both sides of the main glacial valley, the spurs of such valleys are often truncated into triangular facets, and very deep glacial troughs filled with sea water that make up shorelines in high latitudes are called fjords.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TILL-OUTWASH (verified):** NCERT: glacial till is the unassorted coarse and fine debris dropped by melting glaciers, most fragments being angular to sub-angular; glacio-fluvial outwash deposits, laid down by melt-water streams, are roughly stratified and assorted with somewhat rounded fragments, and outwash plains of gravel, silt, sand and clay are formed by broad flat alluvial fans joining at the foot of glacial mountains or beyond continental ice sheets.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-MORAINES (verified):** NCERT: moraines are long ridges of glacial till; terminal moraines are deposited at the end (toe) of the glacier, lateral moraines form along the sides parallel to the valley and may join a terminal moraine in a horse-shoe shaped ridge, ground moraines are irregular sheets of till left over valley floors by rapidly retreating valley glaciers, and the medial moraine lies in the centre of the valley flanked by lateral moraines.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-ESKER (verified):** NCERT: summer melt-water accumulates beneath a glacier and flows like a stream in a channel under the ice with ice forming its banks, and the material it deposits remains after the ice melts as a sinuous ridge called an esker.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-DRUMLIN (verified):** NCERT: drumlins are smooth oval ridge-like features composed mainly of glacial till with some gravel and sand, their long axes parallel to the direction of ice movement; the stoss end facing the glacier is blunter and steeper than the tail, so drumlins indicate the direction of glacier movement.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WIND-PROCESSES (verified):** NCERT: winds in deserts cause deflation (lifting and removal of dust and smaller particles from the surface), abrasion (sand and silt carried by wind act as tools that abrade the land surface) and impact (the sheer force of momentum when sand is blown against rock, similar to sand-blasting).
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-DESERT-SHEETFLOOD (verified):** NCERT: desert rocks devoid of vegetation decay fast under mechanical and chemical weathering caused by drastic diurnal temperature changes; rain is scarce but torrential, so debris is moved by rain wash as well as wind, the wind moves fine materials and general mass erosion is done mainly by sheet floods, and desert stream channels are broad, smooth, indefinite and flow only briefly after rain.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-PEDIMENT (verified):** NCERT: pediments are gently inclined rocky floors at the foot of mountains, with or without a thin debris cover, formed by lateral erosion by streams and sheet flooding; the steep slope retreats backwards (parallel retreat through backwasting), leaving an inselberg as a mountain remnant, and the high relief is finally reduced to low featureless plains called pediplains.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-PLAYA (verified):** NCERT: in desert basins with mountains around, drainage is towards the centre and deposition from the margins forms a nearly level plain, which becomes a shallow lake called a playa in times of sufficient water; the water is retained only briefly because of evaporation, playas often hold good deposits of salts, and a playa plain covered by salts is called an alkali flat.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-DEFLATION-MUSHROOM (verified):** NCERT: persistent one-directional wind blows out weathered mantle or bare soil to make shallow depressions called deflation hollows; impact and abrasion of wind-borne sand first make blow outs, some of which deepen into caves, and outcrops susceptible to deflation and abrasion are worn into mushroom rocks (slender stalk and broad rounded cap), table rocks or pedestal rocks.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WIND-SORTING (verified):** NCERT: wind is a good sorting agent; depending on wind velocity, grains of different sizes are moved along the floor by rolling or saltation and carried in suspension, and as the wind slows the grains settle according to size and critical velocity, so wind-deposited landforms show good sorting of grains.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-DUNE-TYPES (verified):** NCERT: barchans are crescent-shaped dunes with the wings pointing downwind, forming where wind direction is constant and moderate over a uniform surface; parabolic dunes form where sandy surfaces are partly covered with vegetation (reversed barchans); longitudinal dunes form when sand supply is poor and wind direction constant, and are long low ridges; transverse dunes lie perpendicular to the wind; NCERT describes the seif as barchan-like with only one wing, formed when wind conditions shift.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 6 Geomorphic Processes and Landforms: Landforms and their Evolution (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy206.pdf)

#### One glacier: erosion in the uplands, deposition in the lowlands (draft)

Steep slopes and heavy, fast ice make the uplands mainly erosional; where the ice thins and melts its carrying power falls and it drops its load, so the lowlands are mainly depositional. A few erosional relics (roche moutonnee, crag and tail) also occur on lowlands.

#### How wind carries its load: suspension, saltation, creep (draft)
