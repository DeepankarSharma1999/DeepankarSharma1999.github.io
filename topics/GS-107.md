### Bridge: Evaporation, condensation and the heat that clouds release

Water takes in heat when it evaporates and gives that heat back when the vapour condenses into cloud droplets. This stored heat is called **latent heat** ('hidden' heat, because it does not change the temperature while the water changes state). A warm sea evaporates a great deal of water into the air above it. When that moist air rises, cools and condenses, the heat comes out into the surrounding air, warms it, makes it lighter and lets it rise further.

Follow one parcel of air. 1. Warm sea air picks up vapour. 2. It is forced to rise (by convergence at a low). 3. As it rises it cools, and at the **saturation point** the vapour condenses into towering **cumulonimbus** clouds. 4. The latent heat released warms the air in the cloud, so it keeps rising. 5. Rising air lowers the pressure at the surface even more, drawing in more moist air from the sea. This loop is the engine of a tropical cyclone. NCERT (kegy106 p8) calls the storm 'a heat engine that is energised by the release of latent heat on account of the condensation of moisture'.

Two consequences follow. A cyclone needs a **continuous supply of warm, moist air**, so it needs a warm sea below it. When it moves over land the supply is cut off and the storm weakens (kegy209 p8). If you remember only one idea, remember this: **no warm sea, no tropical cyclone**.

### Bridge: Why the wind spirals round a low, and why not at the equator

Air moves from high to low pressure. Near a low-pressure centre, the pressure gradient force pushes air towards the centre. On a rotating earth the moving air is also deflected by the **Coriolis force**: to the **right** of its motion in the northern hemisphere and to the **left** in the southern (Day 19 lesson GS-104).

Take a northern-hemisphere low. Air coming from the south moves north and is deflected right, towards the east. Air coming from the east moves west and is deflected right, towards the north. Air from the north is deflected towards the west; air from the west towards the south. Follow the four deflections round: south side flows east, east side flows north, north side flows west, west side flows south. That is an **anticlockwise** whirl. In the southern hemisphere the deflection is to the left and the whirl is **clockwise**. This agrees with NCERT's Table 9.2: cyclone, low pressure, anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the south; anticyclone, high pressure, clockwise in the north and anticlockwise in the south (kegy209 p4).

Why not at the equator? NCERT says the Coriolis force is zero there, so the wind blows straight across the isobars and the low simply fills up instead of deepening (kegy209 p4). The size of the effect follows the sine of the latitude: the Coriolis parameter, 2 x 7.292e-5 x sin(latitude) per second, is 0 at the equator, about 1.27e-5 at 5 degrees N, 3.77e-5 at 15 degrees N and 4.99e-5 at 20 degrees N. So at 5 degrees it is only about 0.34 of its value at 15 degrees (sin 5 / sin 15 = 0.0872 / 0.2588 = 0.337). NCERT's sentence that Coriolis is 'directly proportional to the angle of latitude' is a simplification (verify). Either way the lesson is the same: too close to the equator, the spin is too weak.

### 1. Orientation: two spinning storms, two different engines

*Objective:* Say in one minute what a cyclone is, how a tropical cyclone differs in engine from a temperate one, and what this lesson owns.

## The one-line picture

A **cyclone** is a low-pressure system with winds circulating around its centre (NCERT kegy209 Table 9.2: anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern). The word covers two very different families.

1. **Tropical cyclone:** a violent storm that originates over warm tropical oceans and moves to the coast, bringing violent winds, very heavy rain and storm surges (NCERT kegy209 p8). Its engine is the **heat of a warm sea**, released as cloud forms. It has no fronts.
2. **Temperate (extra-tropical, mid-latitude) cyclone:** a system of the middle and high latitudes, beyond the tropics, that forms along the **polar front** and has a warm front and a cold front (NCERT kegy209 p7). Its engine is the **contrast between a warm and a cold air mass**. Leong calls the same system a **depression**.

## A warning about the word 'cyclone'

NCERT also uses 'cyclone' for the weak winter depressions that reach north-west India (kegy104 p7) and for the destructive storms of October-November on the east coast (kegy104 p11). The adjective, *temperate* or *tropical*, tells you which family is meant.

### 2. Foundation: the words - isobar, convergence, air mass and front

*Objective:* Define isobar, pressure gradient, convergence, air mass, front and the tropics, and say how a front forms and what the four types are.

## Pressure words

An **isobar** is a line joining places of equal pressure (sea-level pressure is drawn on weather maps; NCERT kegy209 p2). A **low-pressure system** is enclosed by one or more isobars with the lowest pressure in the centre; a **high** has the highest in the centre. The **pressure gradient** is the rate of change of pressure with distance; it is strong where isobars are close and weak where they are far apart (p3). Over a low, air **converges** (flows in) and rises; over a high it subsides and diverges (p4). Rising moist air cools, forms clouds and brings precipitation, so lows are cloudy and rainy places and highs are clear.

## Direction table (NCERT Table 9.2)

| System | Pressure | Northern hemisphere | Southern hemisphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclone | Low | Anticlockwise | Clockwise |
| Anticyclone | High | Clockwise | Anticlockwise |

## Air mass and source region

When air stays long over a homogeneous area (a vast ocean or plain), it acquires the area's temperature and humidity. A large body of air with little horizontal variation in temperature and moisture is an **air mass**; the area is its **source region** (NCERT p6). Five source regions give five types: maritime tropical (mT), continental tropical (cT), maritime polar (mP), continental polar (cP) and continental arctic (cA). Tropical air masses are warm and polar ones cold.

## Front

When two different air masses meet, the boundary zone is a **front**; the making of a front is **frontogenesis**. Four types (NCERT p6-7):

1. **Cold front:** cold air moves towards warm air.
2. **Warm front:** warm air moves towards cold air.
3. **Stationary front:** the front does not move.
4. **Occluded front:** an air mass is fully lifted above the land surface.

Fronts occur in the middle latitudes and are marked by steep gradients of temperature and pressure. They bring abrupt changes of temperature and make air rise to form clouds and rain. The **polar front** is where polar and sub-tropical air meet in middle latitudes; temperate cyclones form along it.

## Where are 'the tropics'?

The tropics lie between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, about 23.5 degrees N and S. NCERT says tropical cyclones lie between about 30 degrees N and 30 degrees S (kegy106 p8), a wider band, and that beyond the tropics lie the 'middle latitude or extra-tropical' systems (kegy209 p7). Leong's typhoons are placed mainly between about 6 and 20 degrees N and S (PDF p. 199).

## Two words for later

**Landfall** is the place where a tropical cyclone crosses the coast. **Storm surge** is the abnormal rise of sea level driven onto the coast (kegy106 p8-9; Day 21 lesson GS-120).

### 3. Core: the tropical cyclone - names, zones and conditions of formation

*Objective:* Name the tropical cyclone in each ocean, state the conditions that favour formation and give the reason for each, including why none forms near the equator.

## What it is and what it is called

Tropical cyclones are violent storms that originate over oceans in tropical areas and move to coastal areas, bringing large-scale destruction by violent winds, very heavy rainfall and storm surges (NCERT kegy209 p8). The name depends on the ocean: **cyclones** in the Indian Ocean, **hurricanes** in the Atlantic, **typhoons** in the western Pacific and South China Sea, and **willy-willies** in western Australia. Leong adds the tornado to this list, an older, loose classification; NCERT and modern usage treat the tornado as a small, short-lived local storm (module 8).

## The conditions (merging two books)

NCERT kegy209 p8 lists five conditions; NCERT kegy106 p8 lists four.

| Condition | Why it is needed |
|---|---|
| **Large sea surface with temperature higher than 27 degrees C** (kegy209) | Warm water supplies the vapour and so the latent heat. The temperature figure is given by kegy209 only. |
| **Presence of the Coriolis force** (both books) | It lets the inflowing air spin up and keeps the central low from being filled; so none forms between about 0 and 5 degrees of latitude (kegy106). |
| **Small variations in vertical wind speed** (kegy209); 'absence of strong vertical wind wedge' (kegy106) | Strong wind change with height would tear the tall column of cloud apart and disturb the vertical transport of latent heat. |
| **A pre-existing weak low-pressure area or low-level cyclonic circulation** (kegy209); unstable conditions through the troposphere that create local disturbances (kegy106) | A seed disturbance gives the air somewhere to converge. |
| **Upper divergence above the sea-level system** (kegy209) | Air that rises in the storm must be carried away aloft, or the surface low would fill. |
| **A large, continuous supply of warm, moist air** (kegy106) | Fuel for the latent-heat engine. |

NCERT adds that the energy comes from the condensation in the towering cumulonimbus clouds around the centre. With a continuous supply of moisture from the sea the storm is strengthened; on reaching land the supply is cut and the storm dissipates (kegy209 p8).

## Where on the globe

Tropical cyclones lie between about 30 degrees N and 30 degrees S (kegy106). Leong places typhoons mainly between about 6 and 20 degrees N and S and most frequent from July to October in the China Sea (PDF p. 199). Both fit the picture of a ring of warm sea beyond the equatorial strip where Coriolis is too weak. Cyclones that cross 20 degrees N generally recurve and are more destructive (kegy209).

## A worked check on Coriolis and the 0-5 degree gap

By bridge module GS-107-B2 the Coriolis parameter at 5 degrees N is about 0.34 of that at 15 degrees N, so the gap is a zone of weak spin-up, not a hard edge.

### 4. Core: inside a mature tropical cyclone - eye, eye wall, rain bands and the engine

*Objective:* Describe the structure of a mature tropical cyclone, explain where the strongest wind and heaviest rain occur and why the eye is calm, and state what powers the storm.

## Parts of a mature cyclone (NCERT kegy209 p8)

A mature tropical cyclone is marked by strong spirally circulating wind around a centre called the **eye**.

1. **Eye:** a region of **calm** with **subsiding air**. Leong (PDF p. 199) describes hurricanes as having calm, rainless centres where the pressure is lowest (an example value of about 965 mb is given).
2. **Eye wall:** the ring around the eye, where there is a strong spiralling **ascent** of air to great height, reaching the tropopause. The wind reaches its **maximum velocity** here, as high as **250 km per hour**, and **torrential rain** occurs. Leong adds that dense dark clouds gather and stormy weather lasts for several hours.
3. **Rain bands:** from the eye wall, bands of rain may radiate, and trains of cumulus and cumulonimbus clouds drift into the outer region.

So the heaviest rain and the strongest wind are in the **eye wall**, not in the eye. This is the trap the 2020 official item sets (module 10).

## Size and speed

NCERT gives the diameter of the circulating system as 150-250 km, and the diameter of the storm over the Bay of Bengal, Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean as 600-1,200 km; kegy106 p8 gives 500-1,000 km horizontally and 12-14 km vertically. The numbers describe different things (the circulating core against the whole storm) and Leong's 80-320 km for a typhoon is a third measure, so use only the ordering: a tropical cyclone is small against a temperate one yet very intense. The system moves slowly, about 300-500 km a day, which is 12.5-20.8 km per hour (300 / 24 and 500 / 24). NCERT's maximum of 250 km per hour equals about 69 metres per second or about 135 knots (250 / 1.852).

## The engine

The energy that intensifies the storm comes from the condensation in the towering cumulonimbus clouds around the centre (bridge GS-107-B1). Kegy106 calls the cyclone 'like a heat engine'. The moist air spirals in near the sea surface, rises in the eye wall and flows out at the top; the **upper divergence** carries it away so the surface low can keep deepening. Hence the storm needs the sea beneath it; over land it dies.

## Why is the eye calm, and is it warm?

NCERT says the air in the eye subsides. Sinking air is compressed and warms (the same adiabatic idea NCERT uses for warm dry winds on the leeward side of mountains, kegy209 p6), and sinking air clears clouds. The standard teaching is therefore that the eye is a clear, calm, **warmer** core than the surroundings (verify); the extract gives no temperature. The 2020 statement that the eye is about 10 degrees C cooler than the surroundings does not fit this reasoning (analysis only).

### 5. Core: life cycle, intensity classes, tracks and the Indian pattern

*Objective:* Describe how a tropical cyclone develops, moves and dies, give the Indian pattern from NCERT, and state the standard intensity classes (verify).

## Life cycle in NCERT's words

NCERT does not name numbered stages. Its sequence: tropical cyclones **originate and intensify** over warm tropical oceans from a weak low or low-level cyclonic circulation (kegy209 p8); a **mature** storm has an eye and eye wall; at **landfall**, where it crosses the coast, the moisture supply is cut and it **dissipates** (p8). Four stages are the usual summary: formation of a disturbance, intensification, maturity and decay (standard; verify).

## Intensity classes used by the India Meteorological Department (standard knowledge; verify)

The IMD classifies storms of the north Indian Ocean by maximum sustained wind. A tropical storm is called a **cyclonic storm** from about 34 knots (about 63 km per hour). The usual bands are shown below, converted at 1 knot = 1.852 km per hour.

| Class | Wind (knots) | About (km per hour) |
|---|---|---|
| Depression | 17-27 | 31-50 |
| Deep depression | 28-33 | 52-61 |
| Cyclonic storm | 34-47 | 63-87 |
| Severe cyclonic storm | 48-63 | 89-117 |
| Very severe cyclonic storm | 64-89 | 119-165 |
| Extremely severe cyclonic storm | 90-119 | 167-220 |
| Super cyclonic storm | 120 and above | 222 and above |

NCERT's 250 km per hour near the eye wall is about 135 knots, which falls in the top band. NCERT's average of 180 km per hour for storms hitting the coast (kegy106 p9) is about 97 knots, in the 'extremely severe' band. Class boundaries are standard knowledge and may be revised; check the current IMD scale.

## Tracks and steering

NCERT (kegy209 p8): tropical cyclones move from **east to west**; storms that cross **20 degrees N** generally **recurve** and are more destructive. The usual reason (standard; verify) is steering by the winds of the layer around them: the trade easterlies in low latitudes push the storm westwards, and after it moves poleward into the westerlies it curves towards the east. The rule is a general one; many Bay of Bengal storms move north-westwards and some recurve (standard; verify).

## The Indian pattern (NCERT)

- Cyclones form over the **Bay of Bengal** and the **Arabian Sea** (kegy106 p9). NCERT (kegy106 p9): most cyclones originate at 10-15 degrees N in the monsoon season; those of the Bay develop mostly in **October and November**, and by July the place of origin shifts to about 18 degrees N, west of 90 degrees E near the Sunderban delta. NCERT's printed latitude range for the Bay storms is garbled in the extract; do not quote it.
- Retreating-monsoon rain on the east coast of the southern Peninsula is associated with cyclonic depressions from the **Andaman Sea**; the thickly populated deltas of the **Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri** are their preferred targets, and a bulk of the Coromandel coast's rain comes from them (kegy104 p11).
- Arabian Sea storms are **less frequent** than those of the Bay (kegy104 p11).

## Lead time arithmetic

At 300-500 km per day a storm 900 km offshore reaches the coast in 900 / 500 = 1.8 days to 900 / 300 = 3 days, that is about 43 to 72 hours. This is why warning systems work in days, not hours (module 9).

### 6. Core: naming, and when two cyclones meet - the Fujiwhara effect

*Objective:* Explain how cyclones get names (verify), define the Fujiwhara effect and say how it can change a cyclone's movement and intensity.

## Why cyclones are named (standard knowledge; verify)

Meteorologists give each tropical storm a short name so that warnings about several storms in the same ocean are not confused. For the north Indian Ocean the India Meteorological Department (IMD) issues names from lists agreed by the member countries of a regional panel working under the World Meteorological Organization. The storms are named in the order of the list once they reach the cyclonic-storm class (module 5). The list, the number of members and the rules have been revised over time, so confirm them in a current source before quoting.

## The Fujiwhara effect (Mains 2026, 10 marks)

The staged extracts do not contain this idea. The account below is standard knowledge, flagged verify, and kept to the reasoning a Mains answer needs.

**What it is.** When two tropical cyclones come close enough to each other (the distance depends on their sizes; no figure is given here), the flow of each storm acts on the other, and the pair begin to **rotate around a common centre** instead of moving independently. The usual name honours the Japanese meteorologist Sakuhei Fujiwhara (verify). Each storm already spins anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, and the pair orbit in the same anticlockwise sense; in the southern hemisphere both turn clockwise.

**Effect on movement.**

1. **Orbiting:** the tracks bend; a storm may loop or turn sharply from the path forecast for it alone.
2. **Merger:** if the centres come very close, the smaller storm may be absorbed by the larger one.
3. **Capture or slingshot:** the stronger storm may pull a weaker one along a curved path, or fling it away.

**Effect on intensity.** The effect is not simple.

1. **Merger** may give one larger and sometimes stronger storm.
2. **Absorption** removes the weaker storm.
3. **Competition and shear:** each storm draws moist air from the same sea; the neighbour's circulation can disturb the other's vertical structure, which tends to weaken it (compare the 'small variation in vertical wind speed' condition in module 3).

**Why it matters.** It makes track forecasts harder, so warnings and evacuation plans are less certain.

## A figure worth drawing

Two small spirals side by side, each with an anticlockwise arrow, and a larger anticlockwise arrow around both labelled 'common centre'; below it a second frame with the smaller spiral drawn merging into the larger.

## How to write it

A 10-mark answer of about 150 words: definition (two sentences), effect on movement (orbit, merger, bending of tracks), effect on intensity (merger, weakening by shear, competition), one line on forecasting difficulty, and a statement that the effect is observed only when two storms are close. Do not invent named storms or distances.

### 7. Core: the temperate (extra-tropical) cyclone and its fronts

*Objective:* Describe how a temperate cyclone forms along the polar front, name its stages to occlusion, and give the weather sequence and the contrast with the tropical cyclone.

## Names and place

The systems of the middle and high latitudes, beyond the tropics, are the **middle-latitude or extra-tropical cyclones** (NCERT kegy209 p7). Leong calls them **cyclones or depressions, confined to temperate latitudes**, with the lowest pressure in the centre and close isobars (PDF p. 201). Leong notes that the temperate zones are dominated by the movement of cyclones and anticyclones, helped by the variable westerlies (PDF p. 196).

## Life cycle along the polar front (NCERT kegy209 p7)

1. **Stationary front.** Along the **polar front** warm air lies to the south and cold air to the north in the northern hemisphere.
2. **Wave.** When the pressure drops along the front, warm air moves northwards and cold air southwards, setting up an **anticlockwise** cyclonic circulation.
3. **Well-developed cyclone.** A **warm front** and a **cold front** form, with a pocket of warm air, the **warm sector**, wedged between the cold air ahead of the warm front and the cold air behind the cold front.
4. **Weather.** The warm air glides up over the cold air ahead of the warm front, and a sequence of clouds appears ahead of it with precipitation. The cold front approaches the warm air from behind and pushes it up, so **cumulus** clouds develop along it.
5. **Occlusion.** The cold front moves faster than the warm front and overtakes it; the warm air is lifted completely, the front is **occluded** and the cyclone **dissipates**.

## What it feels like (Leong p. 201)

The approach is marked by a fall in the barometer, a dull sky, oppressive air and strong winds. Rain or snow falls and the weather is generally bad. The precipitation arises from the convergence of warm tropical and cold polar air with fronts forming and condensation. Winds blow inwards, anticlockwise in the northern and clockwise in the southern hemisphere. The extent varies from about 240 to 3,200 km (Leong), which is far greater than a tropical cyclone; the systems may stay almost stationary or move several hundred kilometres a day. The opposite, an **anticyclone**, has high pressure in the centre, widely spaced isobars, light winds and fair weather (Leong p. 201).

## Direction of travel

Extra-tropical cyclones move from **west to east** (NCERT p8), carried by the westerlies. The tropical ones move east to west. A western disturbance reaching India is a northern-hemisphere example (Day 20 lesson GS-105).

## The southern hemisphere

Leong (p. 196) observes that in the southern hemisphere, with its large ocean expanse from 40 to 60 degrees S, the westerlies blow with greater force and regularity and bring heavy precipitation to western coasts; seafarers call them the Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties and Shrieking Sixties.

## Weather-map figure

Label cold sector, warm sector, direction of travel (eastward) and anticlockwise circulation arrows.

## A common confusion

The temperate cyclone is a *frontal* system; the tropical cyclone has no fronts and is fuelled by the sea (see the master notes).

### 8. Core: thunderstorms, tornadoes (twisters) and the Gulf of Mexico

*Objective:* Define a thunderstorm and a tornado, explain how a tornado differs from a cyclone, and give the reasoning for why most twisters occur around the Gulf of Mexico (verify).

## Thunderstorm (NCERT kegy209 p9)

NCERT calls thunderstorms and tornadoes 'other severe local storms', short in duration, over a small area but violent. A **thunderstorm** is a well-grown **cumulonimbus** cloud producing thunder and lightning, caused by intense convection on moist hot days. If the cloud reaches heights with sub-zero temperature, hail forms (a hailstorm); if moisture is insufficient, the storm can give a dust storm. It has an intense **updraft** of rising warm air that makes the cloud grow, followed by a **downdraft** that brings cool air and rain to the ground. Leong adds that the cloud top is about 6,000 m in temperate latitudes and up to 12,000 m in the tropics, and that lightning arises from a build-up of electrical energy in the cloud (PDF p. 187).

## Tornado (twister)

From severe thunderstorms, a **spiralling wind descends like the trunk of an elephant** with great force, with very **low pressure at the centre**, causing massive destruction on its way (NCERT kegy209 p9). Tornadoes generally occur in **middle latitudes**. Over the sea the same phenomenon is a **water spout**. Leong describes a tornado as a dark funnel cloud, typical of the USA and occurring mainly in the Mississippi Basin, most frequent in spring. Its pressure difference is so great that houses 'virtually explode'.

## Tornado versus cyclone (an old grouping to avoid)

Leong's passage groups tornadoes with tropical cyclones (an older, loose classification) and quotes a spiral speed of 800 km per hour; the extract's own funnel width is only 76-427 m. A tornado is a small, short-lived vortex that comes **down from a thunderstorm**, whereas a tropical cyclone is hundreds of kilometres wide, lasts days and is driven by the sea. Teach the NCERT placement among local storms and do not quote the 800 figure (verify). In India, local storms of this type are the Nor'westers or Kalbaisakhi of Bengal and Assam (kegy104 p9).

| Feature | Tornado | Tropical cyclone |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Narrow funnel, tens to hundreds of metres | Hundreds of kilometres |
| Duration | Short | Days |
| Parent | A severe thunderstorm | A low over a warm sea |
| Latitude | Mostly middle latitudes | Tropical oceans |
| Over sea | Waterspout | Cyclone |

## Why the Gulf of Mexico? (Mains 2024, 15 marks; standard knowledge, verify)

The extracts only say that tornadoes are typical of the USA and the Mississippi Basin. The reasoning for the Gulf of Mexico belt is standard knowledge to verify. Severe tornado-making thunderstorms need four ingredients: **moisture**, **instability**, a **lifting trigger** and **wind shear** (a change of wind with height).

1. **Moisture:** warm, humid air flows north from the **Gulf of Mexico**.
2. **Contrast:** it meets cooler, drier air from the north and drier air from the high plateaus and the Rockies to the west, so a sharp boundary forms in spring.
3. **Flat land:** the central plains have no east-west mountain barrier, so these air masses meet freely.
4. **Shear:** strong upper winds supply the shear that makes thunderstorms rotate.

The result is that the plains north of the Gulf receive repeated spring storms (Leong: most frequent in spring). Write the answer as ingredients and map-based position, and avoid unsupported counts.

## Sea surface temperature (SST) rise (Mains 2024, 10 marks)

**Sea surface temperature** is the temperature of the top layer of the sea. The sourced link is NCERT's first condition: a large sea surface warmer than 27 degrees C (kegy209 p8), and a continuous supply of warm, moist air that releases latent heat (kegy106). Reasoning from that, a rise in SST (standard; verify):

1. **Enlarges the zone and season** in which water exceeds the threshold, so cyclones can form over more area and for more months.
2. **Supplies more vapour** and so more latent heat; warmer air holds more moisture, so rainfall can be heavier.
3. **Favours rapid intensification** of storms that are already formed.
4. **Raises the surge hazard** when combined with sea-level rise.

A balanced answer adds that observed increases are mainly in the intensity of the strongest storms, that the number of storms and regional changes are less certain, and that SST is only one condition; vertical wind change, an existing disturbance and upper divergence still matter.

## Management (NCERT kegy106 p16; Day 21 lesson GS-120)

Cyclone shelters, embankments, dykes, reservoirs and afforestation to reduce wind speed are the steps listed; NCERT blames the growing losses in India, Bangladesh and Myanmar on the high vulnerability of the coastal population.

## Prelims traps

### Notes: Core terms

- Cyclone: a low-pressure system with winds circulating around the centre, anticlockwise in the northern and clockwise in the southern hemisphere (NCERT kegy209 Table 9.2).
- Tropical cyclone: a violent storm originating over warm tropical oceans, with violent winds, very heavy rain and storm surges (kegy209 p8).
- Extra-tropical (temperate, middle-latitude) cyclone: a frontal low of the middle and high latitudes formed along the polar front (kegy209 p7); Leong's 'depression'.
- Eye: the calm centre with subsiding air. Eye wall: the ring of strong ascent, maximum wind and torrential rain (kegy209 p8).
- Landfall: the place where a tropical cyclone crosses the coast. Storm surge: the abnormal rise of sea level driven onto the coast (kegy106 pp. 8-9).
- Air mass: a large body of air with little horizontal variation in temperature and moisture. Front: the boundary zone between two air masses (kegy209 p6).
- Latent heat: heat released when water vapour condenses; the fuel of a tropical cyclone (kegy106 p8).
- Fujiwhara effect: the mutual rotation and interaction of two close tropical cyclones (standard knowledge; verify).

### Notes: Tropical cyclone versus temperate cyclone

- Based on NCERT kegy209 pp. 7-8 and Leong pp. 199-201; the full NCERT comparison is also in Day 20 lesson GS-105. New rows added here come from other passages of the same extracts.

### Notes: Conditions for a tropical cyclone (merged lists)

- Large sea surface above 27 degrees C (kegy209) so that vapour and latent heat are plentiful.
- Coriolis force present (both) so that the inflow spins and the low is not filled; none forms between 0 and 5 degrees (kegy106).
- Small variation of wind speed with height (kegy209) / absence of strong vertical wind wedge (kegy106).
- A weak pre-existing low or low-level cyclonic circulation (kegy209) / unstable conditions through the troposphere (kegy106).
- Upper divergence above the system (kegy209).
- Large, continuous supply of warm moist air (kegy106).

### Notes: Life cycle of a temperate cyclone

- Stationary front along the polar front (warm air south, cold air north in the northern hemisphere).
- Pressure drops along the front; warm air moves north, cold air south; anticlockwise circulation starts.
- Warm and cold fronts develop; a warm sector lies between the cold air ahead of the warm front and behind the cold front.
- Warm air glides over cold air ahead of the warm front (cloud sequence, precipitation); cold front pushes warm air up (cumulus clouds).
- Cold front overtakes the warm front; the warm air is lifted completely; the front is occluded; the cyclone dissipates (kegy209 p7).

### Notes: IMD classes of storms (standard knowledge; verify)

- Depression 17-27 knots; deep depression 28-33; cyclonic storm 34-47; severe cyclonic storm 48-63; very severe cyclonic storm 64-89; extremely severe cyclonic storm 90-119; super cyclonic storm 120 knots and above.
- Conversions at 1 knot = 1.852 km per hour: 34 knots = 63; 64 knots = 118.5; 120 knots = 222.2 km per hour (computed; IMD's own published km per hour bands differ slightly, for example 62-88 for a cyclonic storm; verify).
- Boundaries may have been revised; check the current scale.

### Notes: Weather outside the cyclone family

- Thunderstorm: a cumulonimbus giving thunder and lightning from intense convection (kegy209 p9).
- Tornado: a spiralling wind descending like an elephant's trunk from a severe thunderstorm, very low pressure at centre; water spout over sea (kegy209 p9).
- Nor'wester (Kalbaisakhi, Bardoisila): evening thunderstorms of Bengal and Assam useful for tea, jute and rice (kegy104 p9).
- Western disturbance: a weak winter temperate cyclone from the Mediterranean reaching north-west India (kegy104 p7; Day 20 lesson GS-105).
- Retreating-monsoon depressions from the Andaman Sea: tropical, destructive, rainy for the Coromandel coast (kegy104 p11).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Eye versus eye wall: calm versus strongest wind and rain.
- Tropical cyclone versus tornado: hundreds of kilometres and days versus tens to hundreds of metres and minutes.
- Western disturbance versus October-November cyclone: temperate winter system versus tropical retreating-monsoon system.
- Cyclone versus anticyclone: low pressure with inflow and rising air versus high pressure with outflow and sinking air.
- Warm front versus cold front: warm air advancing versus cold air advancing.
- Cyclonic storm (the IMD class from 34 knots) versus cyclone (the general family).

### Notes: Source notes and slips

- kegy209 p4: Coriolis 'directly proportional to the angle of latitude'; the effect follows sin(latitude) (verify).
- kegy209 p8 sizes: 150-250 km (circulating system) against 600-1,200 km (storm); kegy106 p8: 500-1,000 km horizontally, 12-14 km vertically; Leong p. 199: 80-320 km for typhoons. Use ordering only.
- kegy106 p9: Bay of Bengal storm latitude range is garbled in the extract; not quoted. The same page puts most Indian cyclone origins at 10-15 degrees N in the monsoon season and Bay storms in October-November.
- Leong p. 199: tornadoes grouped with tropical cyclones (an older, loose classification, not a misprint) and quoted at 800 km per hour; NCERT's placement among local storms is taught.
- kegy209 p8: 'tropical cyclones move east to west' is a general statement; recurvature beyond 20 degrees N is also in the same passage.
- kegy209 p8: the extract has no definition of the warm core of the eye; the warm-core statement is standard (verify).

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-NCERT-WIND-FORCES (verified):** NCERT: near the surface wind responds to three forces: the pressure gradient force (strong where isobars are close), the frictional force (greatest at the surface, influence generally up to 1-3 km, minimal over the sea) and the Coriolis force (deflects wind to the right in the northern and the left in the southern hemisphere, more at high wind speed, maximum at the poles and absent at the equator).
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CYCLONE-DIRECTIONS (verified):** NCERT Table 9.2: around a low (cyclone) the wind is anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern; around a high (anticyclone) it is clockwise in the northern and anticlockwise in the southern hemisphere; over a low the air converges and rises, over a high it subsides and diverges at the surface.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WD-EFFECTS (verified):** NCERT: in north-western India weak temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean sea cause rainfall in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and western Uttar Pradesh; the amount is meagre but highly beneficial for rabi crops; the precipitation is snowfall in the lower Himalayas, and this snow sustains the flow of water in the Himalayan rivers during the summer months.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 4 Climate (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy104.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-EXTRATROPICAL (verified):** NCERT: extra-tropical cyclones are systems of the mid and high latitudes beyond the tropics that form along the polar front; they have a clear frontal system (warm and cold fronts) that tropical cyclones lack, cover a larger area, can originate over land and sea, have lower wind velocity and are less destructive than tropical cyclones, and move from west to east.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TROPICAL-CYCLONE (verified):** NCERT: tropical cyclones originate and intensify over warm tropical oceans (a sea surface temperature higher than 27 degrees C is one condition), originate only over the seas and dissipate on reaching land, move from east to west, and bring violent winds, very heavy rainfall and storm surges; a mature one has a calm eye with subsiding air, ringed by the eye wall where the wind reaches as high as 250 km per hour and torrential rain occurs.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-ANDAMAN-DEPRESSIONS (verified):** NCERT: in October-November the widespread rain of the eastern Peninsula is associated with cyclonic depressions that originate over the Andaman Sea and cross the eastern coast of the southern Peninsula; these tropical cyclones are very destructive, the thickly populated deltas of the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri are their preferred targets, and a bulk of the rainfall of the Coromandel coast is derived from them.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 4 Climate (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy104.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CYCLONE-SEASON (verified):** NCERT: tropical cyclones in India originate in two locations, the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea; most originate between 10 and 15 degrees north latitude during the monsoon season, but in the Bay of Bengal cyclones mostly develop in October and November.
  Source: [NCERT Class XI, India: Physical Environment, ch. 6 Natural Hazards and Disasters (kegy106), 2026-27 reprint](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy106.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CYCLONE-SURGE (verified):** NCERT: the energy of a tropical cyclone comes from the latent heat released by warm moist air, so its force decreases with distance from the sea; coastal areas are often struck by severe cyclonic storms with an average velocity of 180 km/h, causing an abnormal rise in sea level known as a storm surge that floods settlements and fields, damaging crops and structures.
  Source: [NCERT Class XI, India: Physical Environment, ch. 6 Natural Hazards and Disasters (kegy106), 2026-27 reprint](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy106.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CYCLONE-MGMT (verified):** NCERT: cyclones are more predictable in time and place than earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanic eruptions, and techniques to monitor their intensity and direction allow some management; cyclone shelters, embankments, dykes, reservoirs and afforestation to reduce wind speed help, but rising loss of life and property in India, Bangladesh and Myanmar is largely due to the high vulnerability of the coastal population.
  Source: [NCERT Class XI, India: Physical Environment, ch. 6 Natural Hazards and Disasters (kegy106), 2026-27 reprint](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy106.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-AIRMASS (verified):** NCERT: an air mass is a large body of air having little horizontal variation in temperature and moisture, acquired when air stays long over a homogeneous source region such as a vast ocean or plain; five source regions give maritime tropical (mT), continental tropical (cT), maritime polar (mP), continental polar (cP) and continental arctic (cA) air masses; tropical air masses are warm and polar ones cold.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-FRONTS (verified):** NCERT: when two different air masses meet, the boundary zone is a front; there are four types: cold front (cold air moves towards warm air), warm front (warm air moves towards cold air), stationary front and occluded front (an air mass fully lifted above the land surface); fronts occur in middle latitudes with steep gradients of temperature and pressure, bring abrupt changes in temperature and make air rise to form clouds and precipitation.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-NAMES (verified):** NCERT: tropical cyclones are violent storms that originate over oceans in tropical areas and move to coastal areas bringing violent winds, very heavy rainfall and storm surges; they are called cyclones in the Indian Ocean, hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the Western Pacific and South China Sea, and willy-willies in Western Australia.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-CONDITIONS-XI (verified):** NCERT lists the conditions favourable for the formation and intensification of tropical storms as (i) a large sea surface with temperature higher than 27 degrees C, (ii) presence of the Coriolis force, (iii) small variations in the vertical wind speed, (iv) a pre-existing weak low-pressure area or low-level cyclonic circulation and (v) upper divergence above the sea level system; the energy that intensifies the storm comes from condensation in the towering cumulonimbus clouds surrounding the centre.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-HEATENGINE (verified):** NCERT: tropical cyclones are intense low-pressure areas confined between 30 degrees N and 30 degrees S, around which high-velocity winds blow; a tropical cyclone or hurricane is like a heat engine energised by the release of latent heat from the condensation of moisture the wind gathers after moving over oceans and seas.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 6 Natural Hazards and Disasters (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy106.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-CONDITIONS-IND (verified):** NCERT (India: Physical Environment) gives the initial conditions for a tropical cyclone as a large and continuous supply of warm moist air to release latent heat, a strong Coriolis force that prevents filling of the low pressure at the centre (its absence near the equator prohibits formation between 0 and 5 degrees latitude), unstable conditions through the troposphere creating local disturbances, and absence of a strong vertical wind wedge, which disturbs the vertical transport of latent heat.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 6 Natural Hazards and Disasters (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy106.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CORIOLIS-EQUATOR (verified):** NCERT: the Coriolis force deflects wind to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, and acts perpendicular to the pressure gradient force; it is absent at the equator, where the wind blows perpendicular to the isobars and the low pressure gets filled instead of intensifying, which is why tropical cyclones are not formed near the equator.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-RAINBANDS-SPEED (verified):** NCERT: a mature tropical cyclone has strong spirally circulating wind around a central eye; from the eye wall rain bands may radiate and trains of cumulus and cumulonimbus clouds may drift into the outer region; the system moves slowly, about 300-500 km per day, creates storm surges that inundate coastal lowlands and peters out on land.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-LANDFALL-RECURVE (verified):** NCERT: the place where a tropical cyclone crosses the coast is its landfall; on reaching land the moisture supply is cut off and the storm dissipates; cyclones that cross 20 degrees N latitude generally recurve and are more destructive; tropical cyclones move from east to west.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-INDIA-JULY (verified):** NCERT: by July the place of origin of the Bay of Bengal storms shifts to around 18 degrees N latitude and west of 90 degrees E, near the Sunderban Delta.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 6 Natural Hazards and Disasters (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy106.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-TC-ARABIAN-LESS (verified):** NCERT: in the retreating-monsoon season a few cyclonic storms also strike the coast of West Bengal, Bangladesh and Myanmar, while such cyclonic storms are less frequent in the Arabian Sea; a bulk of the rainfall of the Coromandel coast is derived from these depressions and cyclones.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 4 Climate (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy104.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-EXTRA-LIFECYCLE (verified):** NCERT: extra-tropical cyclones form along the polar front, initially stationary with warm air from the south and cold air from the north in the northern hemisphere; when pressure drops along the front, warm air moves north and cold air south, setting up an anticlockwise circulation and a well-developed cyclone with a warm front, a cold front and a warm sector wedged between the forward and rear cold air; the cold front moves faster, overtakes the warm front, lifts the warm air completely and the front is occluded as the cyclone dissipates.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-THUNDER-TORNADO (verified):** NCERT: thunderstorms and tornadoes are severe local storms of short duration over a small area; a thunderstorm is a well-grown cumulonimbus cloud producing thunder and lightning, caused by intense convection on moist hot days, with an intense updraft followed by a downdraft; from severe thunderstorms a spiralling wind may descend like the trunk of an elephant with very low pressure at the centre, called a tornado, which generally occurs in middle latitudes; over the sea it is called a water spout.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-NORWESTER (verified):** NCERT: Nor'westers are dreaded evening thunderstorms in Bengal and Assam, known as Kalbaisakhi (a calamity of the month of Baisakh) and, in Assam, Bardoisila; these showers are useful for tea, jute and rice cultivation.
  Source: [NCERT, India: Physical Environment (Class XI), Chapter 4 Climate (2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy104.pdf)

### Last-week summary

- Names: cyclone (Indian Ocean), hurricane (Atlantic), typhoon (western Pacific, South China Sea), willy-willy (western Australia). Lie 30 N-30 S; none within 0-5 degrees (Coriolis too weak).
- Conditions (kegy209): large sea above 27 degrees C, Coriolis, small vertical wind change, pre-existing weak low, upper divergence. Engine: latent heat in cumulonimbus; land cuts moisture, storm dies.
- Eye calm with subsiding air; eye wall strong ascent, wind up to 250 km per hour, torrential rain. Moves east to west, 300-500 km a day; crossing 20 N, recurves and grows more destructive.
- Temperate: stationary polar front, wave with anticlockwise circulation, warm and cold fronts round a warm sector, faster cold front overtakes: occlusion. Warm front: gliding ascent, cloud sequence; cold front: cumulus.
- Tornado: elephant-trunk vortex from a severe thunderstorm, very low central pressure, middle latitudes; water spout over sea. Not a cyclone: narrow, short-lived, thunderstorm parent.
