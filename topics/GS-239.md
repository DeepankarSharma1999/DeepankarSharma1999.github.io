## GS-239: Savings, investment and capital formation

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Savings, investment and capital formation and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Stocks, flows and depreciation

Capital goods last across many production cycles, so the capital stock is measured at a point in time while additions to it are flows over a period. NCERT's tank analogy captures this: water in the tank is the stock, the tap's discharge per minute is the flow. The part of final output made up of capital goods is gross investment, but much of it only replaces machines and buildings that have worn out. Subtracting this depreciation gives net investment, the true addition to capital. Depreciation excludes sudden losses from disasters or accidents. For an economy as a whole, actual replacement spending is assumed to roughly match the depreciation recorded each year.

### Saving, investment and the trade-off over time

In any single year, output is either consumed or invested, so more capital goods means fewer consumer goods now. NCERT resolves the apparent paradox: more capital today equips workers with better tools tomorrow, raising total output and allowing more consumption later. Investment includes building up inventories, adding machinery and factory space, and adding housing; unplanned inventory accumulation occurs when sales fall short of expectations. Government behaviour matters for national saving. When the state runs a revenue deficit, it dissaves and draws on household and corporate savings to fund current spending, so borrowing finances consumption instead of assets, building debt and interest without adding capacity.

### India's investment rate now

The Economic Survey 2025-26 treats investment as a second anchor of growth alongside consumption. It estimates gross fixed capital formation at 30.0 per cent of GDP for FY26; in the first half of the year, GFCF grew 7.6 per cent and its share stood at 30.5 per cent, compared with a 28.6 per cent average before the pandemic. The Survey attributes this to sustained public capital expenditure and a revival of private investment announcements. Supporting signals include faster non-food bank credit, capital goods production and imports, and capacity utilisation above its long-run average. For answers, link this ratio to how savings are channelled and to whether public borrowing funds capital or consumption.
