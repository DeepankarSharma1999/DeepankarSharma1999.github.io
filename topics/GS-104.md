### Bridge: Naming wind directions, and left and right on a rotating earth

Wind directions have one rule that causes many errors: **a wind is named after the direction it blows FROM**. A north-easterly wind comes from the north-east and blows towards the south-west. A westerly wind comes from the west and blows towards the east. Say both ends in this lesson: 'north-easterly (from the north-east, towards the south-west)'.

The second habit is **right and left**. When the Coriolis force is said to deflect a wind 'to the right in the northern hemisphere', the right is judged by facing the way the air is travelling. Face north and your right hand points east; face south and your right hand points west. In the southern hemisphere the deflection is to the left: facing south, your left hand points east; facing north, your left hand points west.

Third, **clockwise and anticlockwise** are judged as seen from above, looking down on the surface, like a clock on a table.

Last, a **hemisphere** is half of the earth. The equator divides it into the northern and southern hemispheres. A latitude such as 30 degrees north (30 N) lies in the northern hemisphere. You will use all four habits in modules 5 to 8.

### 1. Orientation: from unequal heating to pressure to wind

*Objective:* Say in one minute how unequal heating of the earth produces pressure differences, wind and the planet-wide pattern of winds.

## The chain in five links

1. The sun heats the earth unequally (Day 18 lesson GS-103, section 'Core: latitudinal heat balance and the factors controlling temperature').
2. Air expands when heated and is compressed when cooled, so heated places have **lower pressure** near the surface and cooled places have **higher pressure** (NCERT kegy209 p1).
3. Air in horizontal motion is **wind**, and it moves from high pressure to low pressure.
4. The rotation of the earth bends the wind (the **Coriolis force**) and friction slows it near the ground.
5. Joined across the whole globe this makes a regular pattern: belts of high and low pressure and the **planetary winds** that blow between them.

NCERT adds that the wind redistributes heat and moisture across the planet, keeping the planet as a whole at a steady temperature, and that rising moist air cools to make clouds and rain.

## The whole lesson on one page

| Latitude | Pressure belt | Air movement | Surface wind that leaves or arrives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equator | Equatorial low | rises, surface winds converge | trades arrive |
| About 30 N and S | Subtropical high | sinks, surface winds diverge | trades and westerlies leave |
| About 60 N and S | Subpolar low | surface winds converge, air rises (Leong Fig. 14.28) | westerlies and polar easterlies arrive |
| Poles | Polar high | sinks | polar easterlies leave |

### 2. Foundation: what pressure is, how it is measured, and the words you need

*Objective:* Define atmospheric pressure, give its unit and sea-level average, and name the instruments and map terms used for it.

## Atmospheric pressure

The **weight of a column of air** standing on a unit area, from the mean sea level to the top of the atmosphere, is the atmospheric pressure (NCERT kegy209 p1). Because of gravity the air near the surface is denser, so pressure is highest at sea level. At sea level the average is **1,013.2 millibars** (NCERT; 1,013 in Leong). A **millibar (mb)** is the unit; Leong notes it was adopted by meteorological stations in 1914. Pressure is force per unit area; the millibar is a pressure unit (1 mb = 100 newtons per square metre, so 1,013.25 mb = 101,325 Pa). Leong p166 calls pressure a force: a slip.

## The two barometers

- **Mercury barometer.** A long glass tube sealed at the top, open at the bottom and stood in a bowl of mercury. The air pressing on the mercury surface balances the column in the tube. At sea level the column is **760 mm** high, which is **1,013 mb** (Leong p166). Mercury is used because it is the heaviest liquid known; a water barometer would need a column of about 10 m (Leong).
- **Aneroid barometer.** A sealed metal box with most air removed. The lid moves in or out as pressure rises or falls and a pointer shows the reading (Leong describes this; NCERT only names it). No liquid is used.

Leong credits the invention of the barometer to Galileo and his assistant Torricelli in 1643; the standard attribution is to Torricelli (verify).

## Map terms

- **Isobar**: a line joining places of equal pressure. To remove the effect of height, station pressure is first reduced to sea level, so places can be compared (NCERT p2).
- **Low-pressure system**: enclosed by one or more isobars with the lowest pressure at the centre. **High-pressure system**: the highest pressure at the centre.
- **Pressure gradient**: the rate of change of pressure with distance. It is strong where isobars are close together and weak where they are far apart (NCERT p3).
- **Convergence** and **divergence**: air coming together (usually rises) and spreading apart (usually sinks).
- **Wind**: air in horizontal motion.

In temperate latitudes the sea-level reading varies between about 960 and 1,040 mb (Leong p166).

### 3. Core: how pressure changes upward, with NCERT's table

*Objective:* Explain why pressure falls with height, use NCERT's table to compute the fall in each layer, and say why no strong upward wind results.

## Why pressure falls upward

As you climb there is **less air above you**, so the weight of the column is less. The air at height is also thinner (less dense), which is why one feels breathless on a mountain (NCERT p1). Leong gives two figures: pressure is only 572 mb at 4,500 m, and a sea-level reading is about halved at 5.6 km above sea level. Leong p194 says the pressure is negligible above 12,000 m; that is a slip, since NCERT's table gives 265 mb at 10 km and the standard atmosphere gives about 194 mb at 12 km, roughly a fifth of sea level (verify).

## NCERT's table (kegy209 Table 9.1)

| Level | Pressure (mb) | Temperature (degrees C) |
|---|---|---|
| Sea level | 1,013.25 | 15.2 |
| 1 km | 898.76 | 8.7 |
| 5 km | 540.48 | -17.3 |
| 10 km | 265.00 | -49.7 |

## What the table shows (own calculation)

Subtracting: the fall in the first kilometre is 1,013.25 - 898.76 = **114.5 mb**, which is about 1 mb for every 8.7 m. From 1 km to 5 km the fall is 358.3 mb over 4 km, about 89.6 mb per km. From 5 km to 10 km it is 275.5 mb over 5 km, about 55.1 mb per km. So **pressure falls fast near the ground and more slowly higher up**.

NCERT's rule of thumb, 'about 1 mb for each 10 m' in the lower atmosphere, is therefore approximate, and NCERT itself says the rate is not constant. Use it as the exercise does: if the surface reading is 1,000 mb, the reading at 1 km is about **900 mb** (1,000 - 1,000 m / 10 m per mb = 900).

At 5 km the pressure, 540.48 mb, is 53 per cent of the sea-level value, which matches Leong's 'about half at 5.6 km'. Interpolating on a logarithmic (exponential) curve gives about 576 mb at 4,500 m (a straight line would give about 585 mb), consistent with Leong's 572 mb. The temperature column falls by 6.5 degrees C per km (15.2 to 8.7, and so on), which matches the normal lapse rate in Day 18 lesson GS-103.

## Why the air does not rush upward

The **vertical pressure gradient force** is much larger than the horizontal one. But it is generally balanced by a nearly equal and opposite **gravitational force** (NCERT p1). Hence there are no strong upward winds from this cause. Winds are mainly horizontal.

## Temperature effect

A rise in temperature makes the air expand and become less dense, so pressure falls; a fall in temperature raises pressure (Leong p194). This is why a hot surface becomes a low and a cold one a high, which is the root of the belts in module 4.

### 4. Core: the four pressure belts and why they form

*Objective:* Name the four pressure belts with their latitudes, explain the cause of each and describe how they shift with the seasons.

## World distribution of sea-level pressure (NCERT p2; Leong p195)

Near the equator sea-level pressure is low: the **equatorial low**. Along 30 degrees north and south are the high-pressure areas called the **subtropical highs**. Further towards the poles along 60 degrees north and south are the low-pressure belts called the **subpolar lows**. Near the poles pressure is high: the **polar high**.

| Belt | Latitude | Air | Why it forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equatorial low (Leong: Doldrums) | Equator, within about 5 degrees N and S (Leong) | Rising; surface winds converge | Intense heating makes air expand, become lighter and rise; sailors found themselves becalmed |
| Subtropical high (Horse Latitudes) | About 30 N and S | Sinking; surface winds diverge | Air that rose at the equator moves poleward aloft, accumulates and part of it sinks; cooling of the air on reaching 30 degrees also helps it sink (NCERT p5) |
| Subpolar low (temperate low) | About 60 N and S | Surface winds converge and the air rises (Leong Fig. 14.28); cyclonic activity | Best developed over the oceans, where the summer-winter temperature difference is small |
| Polar high | The poles | Sinking | Permanently low temperature, dense cold air |

The northern polar high is complicated by the presence of much land, so summer and winter differ there; the southern belts, over water, are steadier (Leong).

## Thermal and dynamic causes in one idea

The equatorial low and the polar high are *thermal*: heat makes the low, cold the high. The subtropical high comes from air that rose at the equator and sinks around 30 degrees: accumulation of poleward-flowing air, plus cooling. NCERT's exercise asks for its reasons; say both.

## Belts are not permanent

NCERT p2: the belts oscillate with the apparent movement of the sun, moving **northwards in the northern summer and southwards in the northern winter**. Leong adds numbers: in June, when the sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, all the belts shift about 5 to 10 degrees north of their average position; in December, when the sun is over the Tropic of Capricorn, they swing 5 to 10 degrees south. Over the oceans the doldrum belt moves only about 5 degrees north in July (Leong p197); over the heated continents the low moves much farther: Leong's West African discontinuity reaches 22-25 N and the Asian low merges with the equatorial low (module 8; later lesson GS-106 for the Indian monsoon trough).

Seasonal land effects: in July intense heating of Asia lowers pressure and links with the equatorial low; in January a very intense high develops over Mongolia (Leong pp197-198). This is the root of the monsoon, treated in a later lesson (GS-106).

### 5. Core: the three forces on wind, and Coriolis in each hemisphere

*Objective:* State the pressure gradient, frictional and Coriolis forces with their properties, and give the Coriolis deflection direction in each hemisphere.

## The three forces (NCERT kegy209 pp3-4)

Horizontal winds near the surface respond to **three forces**: the pressure gradient force, the frictional force and the Coriolis force. Gravity also acts downward but does not move air sideways.

1. **Pressure gradient force.** The pressure difference produces a force; the rate of change of pressure with distance is the pressure gradient. It acts at right angles to the isobars, from high to low pressure. The closer the isobars, the stronger the force and the faster the wind.
2. **Frictional force.** It affects the speed of the wind. It is greatest at the surface and its influence generally extends up to 1 to 3 km. Over the sea surface the friction is minimal.
3. **Coriolis force.** Because the earth rotates about its axis, it affects the direction of the wind. It is named after the French scientist G.-G. Coriolis; NCERT prints 1844, standard histories give his 1835 paper (he died in 1843; verify).

## The properties of the Coriolis force (NCERT, plus one standard row marked verify)

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| Direction in the northern hemisphere | Deflects the wind to the **right** of its direction of travel |
| Direction in the southern hemisphere | Deflects the wind to the **left** |
| Effect of wind speed | The deflection is **more when the wind velocity is high** |
| Effect of latitude | **Zero at the equator, maximum at the poles** |
| Direction relative to the wind | Acts at right angles to the wind (standard; verify); in the balanced case it points opposite to the pressure gradient force (module 6) |

Right and left are judged by facing the way the air is travelling (bridge GS-104-B1). Leong states the same as **Ferrel's law of deflection** and adds that the effect grows steadily from the equator to the poles.

## Check, not slip

NCERT writes that the Coriolis force is 'directly proportional to the angle of latitude'. The standard relation is to the **sine** of the latitude (verify): at 30 degrees it is 0.5 of the polar value, while a linear rule would give 0.33. Use NCERT's two clear statements for examination answers: absent at the equator, maximum at the poles.

$$ Coriolis force per unit mass = 2 Ω v sin φ $$

(Standard; verify. Ω = the earth's angular speed, v = wind speed, φ = latitude.) The force grows with v, is zero at the equator and is largest at the poles; statement 1 of the 2024 item concerns this force.

## At the equator, and the forces together

At the equator the Coriolis force is zero, so wind blows straight across the isobars; a low there fills instead of intensifying, so tropical cyclones do not form there (NCERT; pointer to GS-107). Elsewhere a pressure gradient starts the air moving, the Coriolis force bends it more as it speeds up, and friction near the ground weakens the bend, so surface winds cut across isobars while upper winds do not.

## The 2024 Prelims item

Statement 1: 'It increases with increase in wind velocity.' NCERT says the deflection is more when the wind velocity is high, so the statement is consistent. Statement 2: 'It is maximum at the poles and is absent at the equator.' NCERT says exactly this. By analysis both statements are correct (the 'both' option); not an official key.

### 6. Core: balanced winds, the geostrophic wind, and highs and lows

*Objective:* Explain how the pressure gradient and Coriolis forces balance to give a geostrophic wind, and state the circulation around highs and lows in each hemisphere.

## The balance (NCERT kegy209 p4)

The velocity and direction of the wind are the net result of the wind-generating forces. Winds in the upper atmosphere, **2 to 3 km above the surface**, are free from the frictional effect of the surface and are controlled mainly by the **pressure gradient** and the **Coriolis** forces.

When the isobars are straight and there is no friction, the pressure gradient force is **balanced** by the Coriolis force, and the resultant wind blows **parallel to the isobars**. This is the **geostrophic wind**. NCERT writes that the Coriolis force acts perpendicular to the pressure gradient force. That is true only when air first starts moving straight across the isobars. The Coriolis force is always at right angles to the wind. In the geostrophic balance it points exactly opposite to the pressure gradient force, equal in size, and the wind, at right angles to both, blows along the isobars.

Step by step: (1) air starts across the isobars from high to low; (2) the Coriolis force bends it to the right (northern hemisphere); (3) as it speeds up the bend grows until the Coriolis force points exactly against the pressure gradient force; (4) the two cancel and the air travels along the isobar.

Buys Ballot's rule (standard knowledge, verify): in the northern hemisphere the wind keeps low pressure on its left and high on its right; in the southern hemisphere the reverse.

## What friction changes

Near the surface (up to 1 to 3 km) friction slows the wind, so the Coriolis force is weaker and the surface wind crosses the isobars at an angle towards the low pressure. Higher up it flows more nearly parallel to the isobars.

## Highs and lows (NCERT Table 9.2)

| System | Pressure | Northern hemisphere (looking down) | Southern hemisphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclone (a low) | Low | **Anticlockwise** | **Clockwise** |
| Anticyclone (a high) | High | **Clockwise** | **Anticlockwise** |

The wind circulation around a low is called **cyclonic circulation**; around a high, **anticyclonic**. Both directions reverse across the equator because the Coriolis deflection reverses.

Vertically, over a low the air converges and rises; over a high the air subsides from above and diverges at the surface. Eddies, convection, mountains and fronts also lift air, which clouds and rain need.

### 7. Core: the planetary winds, with direction in each hemisphere

*Objective:* Derive and state, from where to where, the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies in each hemisphere, with their main climatic effects.

## The method: travel, then bend

Planetary winds blow from the high-pressure belts to the low-pressure belts (Leong p195). Without rotation they would go straight. Because of the Coriolis force they bend (Ferrel's law). Use the same method for every wind: **write the direction of travel, bend it to the right (northern hemisphere) or the left (southern hemisphere), then name the wind by where it comes FROM** (bridge GS-104-B1).

## 1. Trade winds: subtropical high towards the equatorial low

- **Northern hemisphere:** the air travels towards the south (from about 30 N to the equator). Bending to the right, it turns towards the west. Result: wind blowing from the **north-east** towards the south-west: the **north-east trades**.
- **Southern hemisphere:** the air travels towards the north (from about 30 S). Bending to the left it also turns towards the west. Result: wind blowing from the **south-east** towards the north-west: the **south-east trades**.

Both are 'easterlies' (they come from the east). NCERT's exercise asks why the tropical winds are north-easterly: the answer is the right-hand Coriolis deflection.

Leong: the trades are the most regular planetary winds, blowing with great force in a constant direction (hence the name, from early traders). Blowing from cooler subtropics to the warmer tropics they hold much moisture and bring heavy rain to the east coasts of continents in the tropics. They are off-shore on west coasts, which are arid: the 'trade-wind hot deserts' such as the Sahara, Kalahari, Atacama and Great Australian deserts.

## 2. Westerlies: subtropical high towards the subpolar low

- **Northern hemisphere:** the air travels towards the north (30 N to 60 N). Bending to the right it turns towards the east. Result: wind from the **south-west** towards the north-east: **south-westerlies**.
- **Southern hemisphere:** the air travels towards the south. Bending to the left, it turns towards the east. Result: wind from the **north-west** towards the south-east: **north-westerlies**.

Leong: they are more variable in the north (cyclones and anticyclones dominate) and, over the wide southern ocean from 40 S to 60 S, stronger and more regular: the Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties and Stormy Sixties. They bring rain to the west coasts of continents. Some western coasts of the temperate zone, such as California, Iberia, central Chile, the south-western tip of South Africa (the Cape; Leong writes southern Africa) and south-western Australia, get the westerlies only in winter because the belts shift (module 8).

## 3. Polar easterlies: polar high towards the subpolar low

- **Northern hemisphere:** the air travels towards the south and bends right (west): wind from the **north-east**.
- **Southern hemisphere:** the air travels towards the north and bends left (west): wind from the **south-east**.

They are extremely cold winds from the tundra and ice-cap regions, and are more regular in the south than in the north (Leong).

## Summary table

| Wind | Northern hemisphere: blows FROM | Southern hemisphere: blows FROM |
|---|---|---|
| Trade winds (30 degrees to equator) | North-east | South-east |
| Westerlies (30 to 60 degrees) | South-west | North-west |
| Polar easterlies (poles to 60 degrees) | North-east | South-east |

In the northern hemisphere the trades and the polar easterlies are both north-easterly, and in the southern hemisphere both are south-easterly; they are told apart by latitude, not by compass direction.

### 8. Core: the three circulation cells and the Inter Tropical Convergence Zone

*Objective:* Describe the Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells as NCERT presents them, locate the ITCZ and state how its seasonal shift matters.

## What decides the planetary wind pattern (NCERT kegy209 p4)

Five factors: (i) latitudinal variation of atmospheric heating; (ii) emergence of pressure belts; (iii) migration of the belts following the apparent path of the sun; (iv) distribution of continents and oceans; (v) rotation of the earth. The pattern of planetary winds is called the **general circulation of the atmosphere**. Heat transfer from lower to higher latitudes maintains it (ENSO link: later lesson GS-106).

## The ITCZ (NCERT p5)

At the **Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** air rises because of convection caused by high insolation, and low pressure forms. The tropical winds converge on it. The easterlies from either side of the equator meet there. NCERT notes the zone oscillates with the apparent movement of the sun: in the northern winter it moves south and in the northern summer it moves north. (Leong's West African Intertropical Discontinuity, where dry and moist air masses meet, moves with the sun: about 22-25 N in July or August, about 4-6 N in January.)

## The three cells (NCERT p5)

A **cell** is a circulation that goes from the surface upwards and back.

1. **Hadley cell (tropics).** Converged air rises at the ITCZ to the top of the troposphere, up to an altitude of about 14 km (NCERT's figure; Day 18 lesson GS-103 gives the equatorial tropopause as about 18 km; check), and moves towards the poles. This causes accumulation of air at about 30 N and S. Part of it sinks and forms the subtropical high; the cooling of the air at 30 degrees is another reason. Near the ground the air flows towards the equator as the **easterlies** (the trades).
2. **Ferrel cell (middle latitudes).** NCERT words this loosely. Standard reading (verify): air sinks at about 30, flows poleward at the surface as the **westerlies**, rises at about 60 and returns equatorward aloft. At 60 degrees the westerlies and polar easterlies converge and the air RISES (Leong Fig. 14.28); at 30 degrees and at the poles it SINKS.
3. **Polar cell (polar latitudes).** Cold dense air subsides near the poles and blows towards the middle latitudes as the **polar easterlies**.

By the belt latitudes the cells lie roughly over 0 to 30, 30 to 60 and 60 to 90 degrees in each hemisphere (derived; verify).

## Summary table

| Cell | Latitude band (derived) | Surface wind | Air at the ground | Air aloft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hadley | 0 to 30 | Trade easterlies | Moves towards the equator | Moves towards the poles |
| Ferrel | 30 to 60 | Westerlies | Moves poleward | Moves towards the equator (standard; verify) |
| Polar | 60 to 90 | Polar easterlies | Moves towards the middle latitudes | Moves towards the pole (standard; verify) |

## The seasonal shift, in one chain

The overhead sun moves between the Tropics. The belts follow it by about 5 to 10 degrees (Leong). Therefore (i) the ITCZ is north of the equator in the northern summer and south of it in the northern winter, (ii) a Mediterranean-type coast at 30-40 degrees comes under the westerlies and gets rain in its winter, and under the subtropical high and is dry in its summer (Leong p196: California, Iberia, central Chile, the south-western Cape of South Africa, which Leong calls southern Africa, and south-western Australia), and (iii) the monsoon reversal in Asia follows from the seasonal shift (a later lesson, GS-106).

### 9. Master: local winds, and Foehn, Chinook and Loo compared

*Objective:* Explain land and sea breezes, mountain and valley winds and katabatic wind, and compare Foehn, Chinook and Loo by region, nature and climatic impact.

## Why local winds exist

Differences in heating and cooling of surfaces, in daily or yearly cycles, produce local winds (NCERT p5): wind blows from the cooler, higher-pressure place to the warmer, lower-pressure place.

## Land and sea breezes (NCERT p6; Leong p197)

- **Day:** land heats faster than sea; air over land rises, pressure there is low; the sea is cooler with higher pressure. The pressure gradient is from sea to land, so the **sea breeze** blows **from the sea to the land**.
- **Night:** the land loses heat faster and is cooler than the sea; the pressure gradient is from land to sea, and the **land breeze** blows **from the land to the sea**.

Leong adds that the sea breeze blows at about 8 to 32 km per hour, is stronger in the tropics, and normally reaches no more than about 24 km from the coast. Leong calls these breezes monsoons on a smaller scale: the same differential heating, daily rather than seasonal.

## Mountain and valley winds and katabatic wind (NCERT p6)

- **By day** slopes heat up, air moves upslope, and air from the valley blows up to fill the gap: the **valley breeze**.
- **At night** the slopes cool and dense air descends into the valley: the **mountain wind**.
- **Katabatic wind:** the cool air of high plateaus and ice fields draining into the valley.

## Warm leeward winds

NCERT notes another type of warm wind on the leeward side of mountain ranges: moisture condenses and precipitates as the air crosses; the dry air that descends the leeward slope is warmed by the **adiabatic** process (compression as it sinks) and may melt snow quickly.

## Foehn, Chinook, Loo

The full grid (region, season, nature, temperature effect, impact) is in the master notes. In short: the Foehn (northern Alps, spring) and the Chinook ('snow-eater', eastern Rockies, winter) are dry warm downslope winds that melt snow; the Loo (verify) is a hot dry summer westerly of the north-western plains of India and Pakistan.

Do not call the Loo a foehn-type wind unless your source says so; the extracts give no Loo text.

## The traps list

- **'From' versus 'towards'.** A north-easterly blows from the north-east.
- **Hemisphere swap.** Coriolis right in the north, left in the south; cyclonic circulation is anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the south.
- **Same direction, different belts.** North-east trades and north-east polar easterlies share a compass direction in the northern hemisphere.
- **Cells.** Hadley, Ferrel and polar; the Ferrel cell has westerlies at the surface.
- **Pressure with height.** It falls, fast near the ground; 1 mb per 10 m is only approximate.

### Notes: Core terms

- Atmospheric pressure: the weight of a column of air on a unit area from mean sea level to the top of the atmosphere; unit the millibar; sea-level average 1,013.2 mb (NCERT kegy209 p1).
- Isobar: a line joining places of equal pressure, drawn after reducing station readings to sea level (NCERT p2).
- Pressure gradient: the rate of change of pressure with distance; strong where isobars are close (NCERT p3).
- Coriolis force: the effect of the earth's rotation on the direction of wind; deflects to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern; zero at the equator, maximum at the poles (NCERT p3-4).
- Geostrophic wind: wind that blows parallel to straight isobars when the pressure gradient force is balanced by the Coriolis force, above the frictional layer (NCERT p4).
- Cyclone: a low-pressure system with cyclonic circulation; anticyclone: a high-pressure system with anticyclonic circulation (NCERT p4).
- Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ): the low-pressure zone near the equator where the winds of the two hemispheres converge and air rises (NCERT p5).
- General circulation: the pattern of planetary winds (NCERT p4).

- NCERT kegy209 p2 and Leong pp194-195. The belt names in brackets are Leong's.

### Notes: Planetary winds: where each blows FROM

- Derived from NCERT p3-5 and Leong pp195-196 by travel then Coriolis bend (module 7). 'From' = the direction the wind comes from.

### Notes: Numbers to remember

- Sea-level average: 1,013.2 mb (NCERT); 760 mm of mercury (Leong).

### Notes: Local winds compared

- Foehn and Chinook from Leong p198-199; Loo is standard knowledge (verify).
- Foehn (Leong): valleys of the northern Alps, especially Switzerland; spring; rain or snow falls on the windward slope and the descending air is warmed by compression; may raise temperature 8 to 17 degrees C within an hour; melts snow, may cause avalanches.
- Chinook (Leong): eastern slopes of the Rockies in the USA and Canada; winter; the name means 'snow-eater'; reported rise of 19 degrees C in 15 minutes; melts snow, thaws pastures, hastens growth of crops and fruits, makes winters milder when frequent.
- Loo (standard knowledge; verify): plains of north and north-west India and Pakistan; summer, especially May and June; hot, dry, dust-laden westerly wind heated over the land, not a leeward slope wind; heat-wave conditions, heat stress, dryness.

### Notes: Worked example: reading a pressure statement

- Statement: 'Pressure at 5 km is about half of that at sea level.' Check: 540.48 / 1,013.25 = 0.533, so 53 per cent. Reasonably half; Leong says about half at 5.6 km. Correct as an approximation.
- Statement: 'In the lower atmosphere pressure falls by about 1 mb for every 10 m.' Check: first kilometre 114.5 mb, about 1 mb per 8.7 m. NCERT's rule of thumb; approximate.
- Statement: 'The Coriolis force is zero at 30 degrees.' Check: it is zero only at the equator. Wrong.
- Statement: 'The north-east trades blow from the south-west.' Check: they blow from the north-east towards the south-west. Wrong; the name gives the origin.

### Notes: Easily confused pairs

- Wind named by 'from' versus 'towards': a westerly comes from the west.
- Cyclone versus anticyclone: low-pressure versus high-pressure circulation.
- Anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere versus clockwise in the southern for a low.
- Geostrophic wind (parallel to isobars, no friction) versus surface wind (crosses isobars toward the low).
- Doldrums (equatorial low) versus Horse Latitudes (subtropical high).
- Sea breeze (sea to land, day) versus land breeze (land to sea, night).
- Valley breeze (day, up the slope) versus mountain wind (night, down the slope).
- Katabatic wind (cold drainage) versus Foehn or Chinook (warm and dry descent).

### Notes: Source notes and slips

- NCERT kegy209 p4: the Coriolis force is 'directly proportional to the angle of latitude'; the standard relation is the sine of latitude (check). NCERT's two clear statements (absent at the equator, maximum at the poles) are taught.
- NCERT p4: Coriolis 'acts perpendicular to the pressure gradient force'; strictly the Coriolis force is always at right angles to the wind and, in geostrophic balance, is equal and opposite to the pressure gradient force; the two are perpendicular only as air first starts moving down the gradient.
- NCERT p1: 'about 1 mb for each 10 m' versus its own Table 9.1 (114.5 mb in the first kilometre).
- NCERT p5: Hadley cell air rises 'up to an altitude of 14 km'; the tropopause over the equator is about 18 km in Day 18 lesson GS-103; check.
- Leong p166: barometer credited to Galileo and Torricelli in 1643; standard attribution is to Torricelli (verify).
- Cell latitude limits and the Buys Ballot rule are standard knowledge or derived (verify).
- Leong p166: 'pressure is a force'; it is force per unit area, and 1 mb = 100 N per square metre (slip).
- Leong p194: pressure 'negligible' above 12,000 m; NCERT's table gives 265 mb at 10 km and the standard atmosphere about 194 mb at 12 km (slip; verify).
- NCERT p3: Coriolis analysis 'in 1844'; standard histories give 1835 (he died in 1843) (verify).
- NCERT's Ferrel-cell sentence is loosely worded; standard reading: sinks at about 30, rises at about 60, equatorward flow aloft (verify).

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-NCERT-TROPOSPHERE (verified):** NCERT: the troposphere's average height is 13 km, about 8 km near the poles and about 18 km at the equator, thickest at the equator because strong convectional currents carry heat to great heights; temperature falls 1 degree C for every 165 m, and this layer holds dust and water vapour and all changes in weather.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Composition and Structure of Atmosphere (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy207.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-PRESSURE-TABLE91 (verified):** NCERT Table 9.1 (standard atmosphere): pressure and temperature are 1,013.25 mb and 15.2 C at sea level, 898.76 mb and 8.7 C at 1 km, 540.48 mb and -17.3 C at 5 km, and 265.00 mb and -49.7 C at 10 km; in the lower atmosphere pressure falls about 1 mb for each 10 m rise, though not always at the same rate.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-PRESSURE-BELTS (verified):** NCERT: sea-level pressure is low near the equator (equatorial low), high along 30 N and 30 S (subtropical highs), low along 60 N and 60 S (subpolar lows) and high near the poles (polar high); these belts oscillate with the apparent movement of the sun, moving south in the northern winter and north in the summer.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-WIND-FORCES (verified):** NCERT: near the surface wind responds to three forces: the pressure gradient force (strong where isobars are close), the frictional force (greatest at the surface, influence generally up to 1-3 km, minimal over the sea) and the Coriolis force (deflects wind to the right in the northern and the left in the southern hemisphere, more at high wind speed, maximum at the poles and absent at the equator).
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-GEOSTROPHIC (verified):** NCERT: winds 2-3 km above the surface are free from surface friction and are controlled mainly by the pressure gradient and Coriolis forces; when isobars are straight and there is no friction, the two forces balance and the resultant wind, the geostrophic wind, blows parallel to the isobars.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-CYCLONE-DIRECTIONS (verified):** NCERT Table 9.2: around a low (cyclone) the wind is anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern; around a high (anticyclone) it is clockwise in the northern and anticlockwise in the southern hemisphere; over a low the air converges and rises, over a high it subsides and diverges at the surface.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-HADLEY-FERREL-POLAR (verified):** NCERT: at the ITCZ air rises by convection to the top of the troposphere (up to about 14 km) and moves poleward, accumulating near 30 N and S where part sinks to form the subtropical high; the surface return flow to the equator is the easterlies (Hadley cell), the mid-latitude surface winds are the westerlies (Ferrel cell), and cold air subsiding at the poles blows out as the polar easterlies (polar cell).
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-SEA-LAND-BREEZE (verified):** NCERT: by day land heats faster than sea, so pressure is low over land and high over sea and the sea breeze blows from sea to land; at night land cools faster, the pressure gradient is from land to sea and a land breeze results.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
- **F-NCERT-MOUNTAIN-VALLEY (verified):** NCERT: by day heated slopes send air upslope and the valley breeze blows up the valley; at night cooled slopes send dense air down into the valley as the mountain wind; cool air draining from high plateaus and ice fields into valleys is the katabatic wind; a warm dry wind occurs on the leeward side of mountain ranges after moist air has lost its moisture crossing them.
  Source: [NCERT, Fundamentals of Physical Geography (Class XI), Chapter 9 Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems (reprint 2026-27)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kegy209.pdf)
