## GS-082: Classical Indian dance traditions

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Culture · Indian art, literature and architecture

*Goal:* Explain Classical Indian dance traditions and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Roots and building blocks

Evidence for Indian dance runs from Bhimbetka's painted dancers and the bronze dancing girl of the Indus Valley to carvings at Bharhut, Sanchi and Amaravati, Ajanta's painted mudras, and medieval temple sculpture at Bhubaneswar, Khajuraho, Mount Abu and southern shrines. Texts include the Natyashastra and Abhinaya Darpana, with origins ascribed to Shiva as Nataraja. The working elements are hand gestures, eye and head movement, body shapes, music and literary themes, plus costume and make-up that signal region. NCERT places classical dance beside tribal, folk and modern creative dance, the last shaped by artists such as Uday Shankar who blended classical and folk vocabularies.

### Eight forms, eight histories

Kathak travelled from wandering story-tellers to temples, Mughal and Rajput courts and the modern stage. Bharatanatyam's name is glossed from bhava, raga, tala and natyam. Odissi took its present shape roughly sixty years ago, drawing poses from Odisha's temple walls. Kathakali, dating to about the seventeenth century, is a Kerala 'story-play' of epic themes in heavy make-up, once male-only. Mohiniattam embodies lasya and shringara. Kuchipudi, named after a village near Vijayawada, adapted dance-dramas of Brahmin Bhagavatulus influenced by court dancers. Manipuri blends sankirtana and rasalila, with the pung drum dance a highlight. Sattriya carries Assamese monastic Vaishnavism into dance-drama on Krishna and other avatars.

### Patronage, decline and recognition

Kathak shows how classical status was made rather than inherited. Kathaks were originally a caste of temple story-tellers; with the bhakti movement, Radha-Krishna rasalila mixed folk dance with their gestures. Mughal courts gave Kathak its present features, and two traditions emerged, at Jaipur and at Lucknow, the latter flourishing under Wajid Ali Shah, last Nawab of Awadh. Footwork, costume and storytelling were emphasised as it spread across north India. Most British administrators frowned on it, yet courtesans kept it alive until it was recognised as classical after independence. NCERT asks whether 'classical' means religious theme, long training or fixed rules, and warns against treating folk forms as inferior.

### Worked example

Q: 'Classical dances of India are products of history, not timeless traditions.' Evaluate. Frame: common grammar from Natyashastra and sculpture; different institutional homes — temple (Bharatanatyam, Odissi), monastery (Sattriya), court (Kathak, Mohiniattam), Brahmin dance-drama (Kuchipudi); colonial disfavour and twentieth-century revival (Vallathol, recognition after 1947); NCERT's caution on the 'classical' label. Conclude that continuity and reinvention coexist.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which dance uses the tribhangi posture inspired by temple sculpture?
  - **a:** Odissi.
- - **q:** Under which ruler did the Lucknow tradition of Kathak become a major art form?
  - **a:** Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh.
- - **q:** What are Ankiya Nat?
  - **a:** One-act Sattriya plays combining ballad, dance and drama, staged in namghars of Assam's sattras.

### Common trap

Quoting a fixed count of classical dances from memory. NCERT's Class VII chapter speaks of Kathak as one of six classical forms recognised after independence and lists six, while the Class XI text lists eight; a question's statement must be checked against the specific list it cites.
