## GS-052: Women, peasants, Dalits and regional contributions

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Caste vocabulary you need before reading this topic

Indian society has long been divided into hereditary groups. **Varna** is the old four-fold scheme (priests, rulers and warriors, traders, and labourers); **jati** (often translated as caste or sub-caste) is the much larger set of hereditary communities that people actually lived in. Spectrum says caste decided who could get education or own land, what work a person did, whom one could eat with or marry, and even which wells and temples could be used. People outside this scheme, or at its very bottom, suffered **untouchability**: they were treated as ritually polluting and faced humiliating restrictions.

Different names were used for these communities at different times, and you will meet all of them in sources and questions:

- **Depressed Classes**: the official British-era term. It appears in the Communal Award, the Poona Pact and the Round Table Conferences.
- **Harijan** ('children of God'): the name Gandhi popularised. Spectrum records that Ambedkar rejected it as a clever scheme, so it is a politically contested word.
- **Scheduled Castes**: the later official term (NCERT says the Depressed Classes were later known as Scheduled Castes). It is the term used in the Constitution era.
- **Dalit** ('crushed' in Marathi): Spectrum says many believe Jyotirao Phule introduced the word; NCERT says that from about the 1930s the 'untouchables' began to call themselves dalit.

**Writing rule for your answers:** use 'Dalits' or 'Scheduled Castes' in your own sentences, and use 'Depressed Classes' or 'Harijan' only when describing the historical document, institution or campaign (for example 'the Harijan Sevak Sangh'). The word 'untouchables' is used in the sources as a description of a social fact that the movements fought against.

### Bridge: Franchise, electorates and reserved seats in plain words

The Poona Pact makes no sense unless you understand how Indians voted under British rule. Four terms are enough.

- **Franchise** is the right to vote. Under British rule it was restricted: only people who met conditions (such as property or tax) could vote, so most Indians had no vote at all. NCERT notes that the 1937 elections were held on a restricted franchise.
- **Legislative councils** were the provincial and central law-making bodies. Seats in them were filled by election (and some nomination); who could stand and who could vote was the whole political argument.
- **Separate electorate**: seats set aside for a community, and *only voters of that community* choose the person for those seats. Spectrum lists the groups that the Communal Award gave this to: Muslims, Europeans, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, depressed classes, women (Spectrum's wording; verify) and Marathas for 7 seats in Bombay.
- **Joint (general) electorate with reserved seats**: the seats are reserved for a group, so every candidate must belong to it, but *all voters of the constituency* vote. NCERT says this is how the Poona Pact worked: the depressed classes got reserved seats, but the members were voted in by the general electorate.

Spectrum also mentions a **double vote** in the Communal Award: one vote for the separate electorate and one for the general electorate.

**Why the difference mattered.** In a separate electorate the community chooses its own representative. In a joint electorate with reserved seats the community's seats are safe but the winning candidate can be shaped by the votes of the majority. Gandhi feared the first would freeze untouchability; Ambedkar feared the second would produce representatives who depended on caste-Hindu votes. Keep this one distinction in your head throughout module 6.

### 1. Four threads of the national movement and how to read them

*Objective:* Name the four threads of this topic (women, peasants, Dalits, regions) and use one four-question frame (GORL) to study each of them.

## What this topic is about

The national movement is often taught as a story of big leaders and big events: the Salt March, the Round Table Conferences, Quit India. This topic asks a different question: **who else was in the movement, why did they join, and what did they get or not get?** Four groups are studied: **women**, **peasants**, **Dalits** (then called the Depressed Classes) and **regions** (areas whose local leaders and grievances shaped the movement).

It is tested as short factual questions (who founded what, which Act did what) and as 15-mark 'Discuss' questions on how the movement widened its social base.

## The GORL frame

For every thread, ask four questions.

1. **G: Grievance.** What was wrong in this group's life? (Peasants: rent, revenue, moneylenders. Women: seclusion, child marriage, denial of education. Dalits: untouchability and exclusion.)
2. **O: Organisation and leaders.** Who spoke for them, through which societies, newspapers, sabhas or movements?
3. **R: Relation to Congress and Gandhi.** Alliance, use, or tension? Many of the best exam points sit here: Dalit leaders clashed with Gandhi over electorates, while peasants often acted in Gandhi's name in ways he did not approve.
4. **L: Limits and legacy.** What did the group gain, and what stayed unsolved? NCERT, for example, says increased public roles for women did not necessarily mean a radical change in how their position was seen.

## Five anchor dates

**1829** (sati banned), **1873** (Satyashodhak Samaj), **1930** (Civil Disobedience Movement), **1932** (Communal Award, fast, Poona Pact) and **1936** (All India Kisan Sabha).

## How this lesson links to others

Peasant and tribal narratives (Indigo, Pabna, Deccan, Eka, Bardoli and so on) belong to **GS-031**, and the region-by-region story of civil disobedience belongs to **GS-045** (module 'The spread of the movement: region by region'). This lesson links to them and adds the angle they lack: how peasants, women, Dalits and regions fitted with the Congress. The Round Table Conferences are **GS-046**, social and religious reform **GS-034** and Quit India **GS-048**; those come later.

## A reading habit

Spectrum and NCERT sometimes differ on dates or names. Where that happens this lesson flags it, and in a few cases says which version to learn; verify flagged items before relying on them.


### 2. Women in the reform era: laws, schools and the Rukhmabai case

*Objective:* Describe the condition of women in the nineteenth century, name the main laws (including what the 1891 Act really did) and educational steps, and explain why the Rukhmabai case mattered.

## The starting condition

Spectrum describes a society that treated women as inferior adjuncts to men: purdah (seclusion), early marriage, a ban on widow remarriage, **sati** (the burning of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre), female infanticide and polygamy, and generally no education. Hindu women could not inherit property or end an undesirable marriage; Muslim women inherited half as much as men.

Reformers appealed to **individualism and equality**, said true religion did not sanction women's inferior status, and argued that a country with ignorant women could not progress because the home gives a person's first education. Notice the limit: even this argument valued women mainly through the family.

## The laws, in brief

Six measures, in order (the full table is in the master notes): the Bengal infanticide regulations (1795 and 1804); the sati ban, Regulation XVII of 1829, under Bentinck; the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856, pushed mainly by Vidyasagar; the Native (Civil) Marriage Act, 1872; the Age of Consent Act, 1891; and the Child Marriage Restraint (Sarda) Act, passed 1929 and in force 1930, which set the minimum marriage age at **18 for boys and 14 for girls** (never the reverse).

**The 1891 Act, carefully.** It followed B.M. Malabari's efforts and the Rukhmabai controversy. Learn it as: the Act raised the age of consent for sexual intercourse, for married and unmarried girls alike, from 10 to 12, by amending the rape provision of the Indian Penal Code; it did not prohibit marriage below 12 (standard knowledge; verify). **Flag:** Spectrum says in one place that it 'forbade the marriage of girls below the age of 12' and in another that it regulated 'the age of consent'; treat the 'forbade marriage' wording as a source slip. The 2020 Prelims question uses the phrase 'age of consent'.

## Schools and education

Missionaries came first, with the Calcutta Female Juvenile Society (1819). The Bethune School (1849) followed, and Vidyasagar was linked with 35 girls' schools in Bengal. Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule opened a girls' school at Bhidewada, Pune, in 1848; Spectrum says Savitribai could be called the first female school teacher of modern India, and that the effort was indigenous, led by a non-Brahmin and unaided by the authorities. Spectrum concludes that private enterprise, more than government, drove women's education (Wood's Despatch 1854 and Karve's university 1916 are in the master notes).

## The Rukhmabai case (1884)

Rukhmabai was married at 11 but continued her education with her stepfather's support. When her husband Dadaji Bhikaji demanded, after about 12 years, that she live with him, she refused and he petitioned the Bombay High Court for **restitution of conjugal rights** (a court order directing a spouse to return to the marital home). She argued that the marriage took place before she had 'arrived at years of discretion'. Justice Pinhey dismissed the petition (1885, standard knowledge; verify), but on appeal in 1887 the bench of Chief Justice Sargent and Justice Farran ordered her to live with her husband; she said she would accept punishment rather than do so. A Rukhmabai Defence Committee was formed, and Spectrum says the case pushed reformers to get the Age of Consent Act passed.

She qualified as a doctor in 1894 at the London School of Medicine for Women (standard; verify) and became one of the first Indian women to qualify and practise as a doctor. **Flag:** Spectrum calls her the first; other accounts name Kadambini Ganguly and Anandibai Joshi as earlier, so say 'one of the first'. She later became Chief Medical Officer in Surat (Spectrum).


### 3. Women's organisations and women in the freedom struggle

*Objective:* Name the main women's organisations and founders, describe women's roles in the Civil Disobedience, revolutionary and Quit India phases, and state the limits of that participation.

## Organisations

Founders and dates are in the master-notes table. Four to remember: Ramabai Ranade's Ladies Social Conference (1904); Sarla Devi Chaudhurani's **Bharat Stree Mahamandal** (Allahabad, 1910), which Spectrum calls the first major women's organisation set up by a woman; Pandita Ramabai Saraswati's Arya Mahila Samaj; and Margaret Cousins's **All India Women's Conference** (1927, Pune), perhaps the first egalitarian women's organisation (Spectrum), which backed the Sarda Act and the Hindu Women's Right to Property Act (1937).

## Moving into politics

Spectrum says the Swadeshi, anti-partition and Home Rule movements were a liberating experience for home-centred women. After 1918 women faced lathis and bullets, went to jail, joined trade union and kisan movements, voted and sat in legislatures. **Sarojini Naidu** became President of the Indian National Congress in 1925 (NCERT says the first Indian woman to hold the post) and later Governor of the United Provinces (1947-49, per Spectrum).

## The Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-34)

NCERT's Themes in Indian History says the Salt March was the first nationalist activity in which women took part in large numbers. Spectrum says Gandhi specially asked women to play a leading part, and they became a familiar sight picketing liquor shops, opium dens and shops selling foreign cloth. It calls the movement the most liberating experience for Indian women. NCERT's example is Ambabai of Karnataka, widowed at sixteen, who picketed in Udipi, was arrested more than once and called those days the happiest of her life. At **Dharasana** (21 May 1930) Sarojini Naidu, Imam Sahib and Manilal Gandhi led the raid on the salt works, which met a brutal lathi-charge. Repression was particularly harsh on women (Spectrum).

**Flag: who persuaded Gandhi?** The two NCERT books differ. Class VIII (Our Pasts III) says Sarojini Naidu persuaded Gandhi, who was initially opposed, to let women join the Salt Satyagraha; Class XII (Themes in Indian History III) says Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay persuaded him not to restrict the protests to men. Do not state either as settled fact; say 'women leaders such as Sarojini Naidu and Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay argued for women's participation'. **Settled common ground:** Gandhi was initially reluctant (NCERT Class VIII); women then joined in large numbers (both NCERT books); and the 78 Dandi marchers were men, members of the Sabarmati Ashram (Spectrum).

## Revolutionary women

In the Surya Sen (Chittagong) phase young women gave shelter, carried messages and fought with guns (Spectrum). It names **Pritilata Waddedar**, who died conducting a raid; **Kalpana Dutt**, tried with Surya Sen and given a life sentence; **Santi Ghosh and Suniti Chaudhuri** (Spectrum's spelling of the second name is 'Chandheri'), schoolgirls of Comilla who shot the district magistrate in December 1931; and **Bina Das**, who fired at the Governor at her convocation in February 1932.

## Quit India

The extracts record only women's processions (a Bombay photograph in NCERT Class XII), not named Quit India women leaders; the names are listed as a gap, not taught from memory.

## The limits

NCERT Class X says the women in civil disobedience were mostly from high-caste urban families and rich peasant households, that Gandhi believed women's duty was to look after home and hearth, and that the Congress was long reluctant to give women positions of authority, wanting mainly their symbolic presence. A 'role of women' answer therefore has two halves: a real, large entry into public life, and unequal terms.

### 4. Peasants and workers: what to add to the movement stories

*Objective:* Explain how peasant movements connected with Congress, caste and women, correct the Bardoli date, and place workers' organisations beside them, with the GS-031 narratives recapped and linked rather than repeated.

## Division of work with GS-031

The movement narratives are in **GS-031**: Indigo, Pabna and the Deccan riots to 1885, then the Kisan Sabhas, Awadh, Eka and Bardoli (module 'Core: peasants and nationalism, 1917 to 1928'), then the All India Kisan Sabha, the Congress ministries, Tebhaga and Telangana (module 'Core: Kisan Sabhas, Congress ministries, Tebhaga and Telangana, 1930s to 1948').

**Recap.** Before 1918 peasant revolts were local, wholly economic and aimed at planters, zamindars and moneylenders. After 1918 they linked with the Congress through Kisan Sabhas, no-rent campaigns and the AIKS (Lucknow, 1936); office-bearers and 1937-39 stay in GS-031. Here we keep three angles for 'role of peasants' answers.

## Angle 1: acting in Gandhi's name

NCERT says peasants followed Gandhi for reasons different from those of the industrialists who also supported him. In Awadh in 1921 the movement turned to attacks on talukdars' houses and looting of bazaars, and local leaders said Gandhi had told peasants not to pay taxes and that land would be redistributed. Congress leaders disliked these forms: peasants joined *on their own terms*.

## Angle 2: rich and poor peasants

NCERT Class X separates two groups. Rich peasants (Patidars of Gujarat, Jats of Uttar Pradesh) grew commercial crops, were hit by falling Depression prices and joined civil disobedience against high revenue; many refused to rejoin in 1932 because rates had not been revised in 1931. Poorer tenants wanted unpaid rent remitted, joined movements led by Socialists and Communists, and found the Congress unwilling to back 'no rent' campaigns.

## Angle 3: caste and gender inside peasant movements

The Eka Movement (late 1921, Hardoi, Bahraich and Sitapur) drew its grassroots leadership from **Madari Pasi** and other low-caste leaders along with many small zamindars. It began under Congress and Khilafat leaders and then moved out of nationalist control under Madari Pasi (standard knowledge; verify). In Bardoli the women gave Vallabhbhai Patel the title 'Sardar' and special emphasis was placed on mobilising women; Patel, a Congress leader, led it from the start. Tebhaga's storm centre in north Bengal was among the Rajbanshis, a low caste of tribal origin; in Telangana Spectrum records improved conditions for women in guerrilla villages.

**Bardoli: learn 1928.** The revenue enhancement (initially proposed at about 30 per cent) dates from 1926-27. The Bardoli Satyagraha itself, under Vallabhbhai Patel in Surat district, ran from February to August 1928, as NCERT Class X and XII say. **Flag:** Spectrum prints 'February 1926' for Patel's call; treat that as a slip, since the same Spectrum passage goes on to 'August 1928'.

## Workers: the neighbouring movement

Prelims has used Spectrum's working-class chapter. The **All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC)** was founded on 31 October 1920 with Lala Lajpat Rai as first president. The **Trade Union Act, 1926** gave trade unions legal recognition. The **Trade Disputes Act, 1929** made Courts of Inquiry and Consultation Boards compulsory, made strikes in public utilities illegal without a month's notice and forbade coercive or purely political union activity and sympathetic strikes; the Meerut arrests came in March 1929.

Three more facts (the last two from outside the extracts; verify):

- **Lokhande:** N.M. Lokhande edited the newspaper Deenbandhu (from about 1880, per Spectrum) and founded the Bombay Mill Hands Association (1890 in standard accounts; Spectrum gives 1880, so verify).
- **Factories Act, 1881:** it mainly regulated child labour (minimum age 7, limited hours for children aged 7-12, fencing of dangerous machinery). It did not fix wages or allow trade unions; legal recognition of unions came only with the 1926 Act.
- **Hind Mazdoor Sabha:** formed in December 1948 by socialist trade unionists, including Ashok Mehta, Basawon Singh, R.S. Ruikar, T.S. Ramanujam and G.G. Mehta.


### 5. Dalit and non-Brahmin movements: Phule to the south

*Objective:* Explain the factors that weakened caste discrimination and describe the work of Phule, Savitribai, Walangkar, Bansod, Shinde and the non-Brahmin and temple-entry movements of the south (SNDP, Justice Party, Vaikom, Guruvayur).

## Four forces against caste discrimination

Spectrum names four. **British rule**, perhaps unintentionally, weakened caste: private land, commerce, towns and railways gave mobility, equality before law arrived, caste panchayats lost judicial functions, and services and education opened to all. **Social reform movements** (Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samaj, Ramakrishna Mission, Theosophists) spread education among untouchables and sought temple and tank access, though many defended varna. The **national movement** opposed caste privilege; after 1937 Congress governments gave free education to Harijans in some provinces, and Travancore, Indore and Devas opened state temples by proclamation. Finally, **stirrings among the lower castes themselves** produced independent movements. Spectrum concludes that the struggle could not fully succeed under a foreign government (standard knowledge: Article 17 abolishes untouchability; verify against the polity lesson).

## Jyotirao Phule and Savitribai Phule (Maharashtra)

Phule (1827-90), born in the mali (gardener) community, organised a powerful movement against upper-caste and brahminical domination. He founded the **Satyashodhak Samaj** (Truth Seekers' Society) in 1873, with leadership from backward classes, aimed at social service and education for women and lower castes. His books **Gulamgiri** and **Sarvajanik Satyadharma** inspired later movements; he promoted a marriage ceremony without a priest; and 'many believe' (Spectrum) he introduced the word 'dalit'. He received the title Mahatma in 1888 from Vithalrao Krishnaji Vandekar. Savitribai, married to him at nine and taught by him, became a teacher and headmistress, taught Mang and Mahar children, started the Mahila Seva Mandal, and with him set up in 1863 a home to prevent infanticide (Balhatya Pratibandhak Griha). She led the Samaj after his death and died in 1897 of plague caught while nursing a patient. **Flag:** Phule's *Shetkaryacha Asud* (on cultivators) is not in the extracts; verify before using it.

## Other Dalit pioneers

**Gopal Baba Walangkar** (about 1840-1900), a Mahar army veteran, founded the Anarya Dosh-Parihar Mandali, petitioned against the stopping of Mahar recruitment to the army, and published Vital Vidhvansak from 1888. **Kisan Faguji Bansod** (1879-1946) set up the Chokhamela girls' school at Nagpur and a press (1910). **Vitthal Ramji Shinde** (1873-1944), of the Prarthana Samaj, opened a night school for untouchable children (Pune, 1905), founded the Depressed Classes Mission (Bombay, 1906), gave evidence to the Southborough Committee (1919) and was jailed in civil disobedience. Phule, Walangkar and Bansod held that the untouchables were the original inhabitants and caste the creation of Aryan invaders; this is the movements' own theory, which historians debate (verify).

## South India

Spectrum says the non-Brahmin movement, encouraged by figures such as the Maharaja of Kolhapur, spread south in the first decade of the twentieth century and was joined by Kammas, Reddis, Vellalas and Muslims. Bhaskarrao Jadhav, a Maharashtrian non-Brahmin (Satyashodhak) leader, is among the names Spectrum lists for the 1920s (see master notes for Javalkar). The main southern strands, each with one hook:

- **SNDP Yogam (1903):** Sri Narayana Guru, with Kumaran Asan and Dr Palpu; slogan 'one religion, one caste, one God for mankind'. **Vokkaliga Sangha:** 1905.
- **Justice movement:** C.N. Mudaliar, T.M. Nair and P. Tyagaraja; Madras Presidency Association (1917).
- **Self-Respect Movement:** E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker ('Periyar'), 1925 in Spectrum's list.
- **Vaikom Satyagraha (1924):** for access to roads near the temple, K.P. Kesava leading; jathas came from Punjab and Madurai, and Gandhi toured in support.
- **Guruvayur (1931):** Kelappan, Subramaniyam Tirumambu, P. Krishna Pillai and A.K. Gopalan.
- **Travancore Temple Entry Proclamation:** 12 November 1936. Spectrum also lists Madras in 1938 among temple-entry steps (verify the detail).

Spectrum cautions that these movements could be divisive and conservative as well as radical.


### 6. Ambedkar, the Communal Award and the Poona Pact

*Objective:* Trace Ambedkar's early movement, the positions of Gandhi and Ambedkar at the Round Table Conferences, and the sequence and terms of the Communal Award and Poona Pact, with the weaknesses of each source.

## Ambedkar's movement before 1930

Spectrum records: **Mooknayak** (Leader of the Voiceless), 1920; the **Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha**, 1924, motto 'educate, agitate and organise'; Bahishkrit Bharat, 1927; and in March 1927 the **Mahad Satyagraha**, when about 2,500 untouchables marched to the Chawdar tank, a public water source they could not use, and he drank from it. In December 1927 the Manusmriti was burnt there. Working with the Simon Commission, he argued in October 1928 for universal adult franchise, called the depressed classes a distinct minority needing protection, and wanted reserved seats (and a separate electorate if universal franchise were refused). NCERT Class X says that in 1930 he organised the Depressed Classes Association.

## The Round Table Conferences

At the first conference (November 1930 to January 1931) Ambedkar and Rettamalai Srinivasan represented the depressed classes. At the second (7 September to 1 December 1931) Gandhi was the Congress's sole representative; he said the untouchables were Hindus and not a minority and opposed separate electorates, while a 'Minorities' Pact' of Muslims, depressed classes, Christians and Anglo-Indians pressed their demands. NCERT says Ambedkar argued that Gandhi and the Congress did not really represent the lowest castes. Gandhi said separate electorates would perpetuate the stigma; Ambedkar said so handicapped a community needed political power to protect itself.

## The Communal Award (16 August 1932)

Announced by Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald, based on the Indian Franchise (Lothian) Committee, it gave separate electorates to Muslims, Europeans, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and the depressed classes (for 20 years, with a double vote; see bridge B2), and also to Marathas for 7 seats in Bombay. Spectrum's list adds women to the separate electorates and says the award reserved three per cent of seats for women in all provinces except the NWFP. **Flag:** that is Spectrum's wording; standard accounts describe reserved women's seats filled through the communal electorates, not a distinct women's electorate (verify).

## Gandhi's fast and the Poona Pact

Gandhi saw the award as an attack on unity and as harmful to the depressed classes, since separate electorates would keep the untouchables untouchable. He demanded joint electorates, with no objection to more reserved seats, and began an indefinite fast in Yerwada jail on **20 September 1932** (jail: standard knowledge; verify). Ambedkar, M.C. Rajah and Madan Mohan Malaviya (who signed for the caste Hindus; verify) produced the **Poona Pact**, signed by Ambedkar on **24 September 1932**. It abandoned separate electorates but raised the reserved seats, and the government accepted it as an amendment to the Communal Award.

**Learn the numbers as Spectrum gives them:** reserved seats rose from 71 to 147 in the provincial legislatures, plus 18 per cent of the central legislature. **Flag:** Spectrum's Award passage prints 78 reserved seats, and some accounts give 148 (verify), so do not rest an answer on one figure.

## Judging the Pact

NCERT: it gave the depressed classes reserved seats voted in by the general electorate, but the Dalit movement stayed apprehensive of the Congress-led movement. Spectrum's criticism: the Pact made the depressed classes dependent on caste Hindu organisations, weakened their leadership because candidates chosen by caste Hindus could defeat true representatives, and denied Dalits a distinct existence. Ambedkar continued to denounce it until 1947, the Scheduled Castes Federation later asked again for separate electorates, and the struggle led to special representation in the Government of India Act, 1935.


### 7. Gandhi's anti-untouchability campaign and the Gandhi-Ambedkar debate

*Objective:* Describe Gandhi's Harijan campaign, its achievements and limits, and compare the Gandhi and Ambedkar positions on caste, democracy and religion.

## The campaign (1932-34)

After the Poona Pact Gandhi turned to a campaign against untouchability. From jail he set up the **All India Anti-Untouchability League** (September 1932) and the weekly **Harijan** (January 1933 in Spectrum; the usual date is 11 February 1933, verify). After release in August 1933 he made a **Harijan tour** (November 1933 to July 1934, 20,000 km), collecting money for the **Harijan Sevak Sangh**, and fasted twice (8 May and 16 August 1934) to show how serious the issue was (Spectrum). **Flag:** Spectrum elsewhere says Gandhi founded the 'All India Harijan Sangh' in 1932; the safest exam wording is 'Anti-Untouchability League (1932), later the Harijan Sevak Sangh'.

His themes were: an indictment of Hindu society; total eradication of untouchability, symbolised by opening temples; penance by caste Hindus; and the Shastras do not sanction untouchability and, if they did, should be ignored. He separated removing untouchability from inter-dining, inter-caste marriage and abolishing caste itself, since untouchability came from ideas of high and low. Orthodox Hindus (sanatanis) were to be won by persuasion, not compulsion, and Harijans were urged to reform themselves (education, cleanliness, giving up liquor). Orthodox groups disrupted his meetings and the government defeated the Temple Entry Bill in August 1934. Gandhi repeatedly described the campaign as not a political movement but one to purify Hinduism; Spectrum adds that it carried nationalism to Harijans, mostly agricultural labourers, and increased their participation in the national and peasant movements.

## Limits

NCERT says Dalit participation in civil disobedience was limited, particularly in the Maharashtra and Nagpur region where their organisation was strong, because many Dalit leaders wanted a political solution: reserved seats and separate electorates. Spectrum adds that little emphasis went to the economic grievances of landless and agricultural labourers, who were mostly untouchables. Ambedkar denounced the name 'Harijan' and left the Harijan Sevak Sangh, claiming that for Gandhi the removal of untouchability was only a platform.

## Gandhi and Ambedkar side by side

The full Spectrum comparison is in the master notes; three differences matter most. **Caste:** Ambedkar wanted the annihilation of caste; Gandhi distinguished untouchability from the caste (varna) system and held that varnashram, purged of high-low distinctions, could be complementary. **Politics and village:** Ambedkar preferred a parliamentary system and was sceptical of 'Gramraj'; Gandhi had little respect for parliamentary government and valued the village. **Religion:** Ambedkar held that Hindu scriptures were the source of untouchability and, in the later phase of his life (Spectrum), left Hinduism; Gandhi held that caste degeneration had nothing to do with true religion. Spectrum also notes similarities: both rejected violent overthrow, valued education, and used symbolic acts (burning foreign cloth and the Manusmriti, the pinch of salt and the drop of Mahad water).

**Caution about interpretation:** these comparisons are Spectrum's summary of scholarly views, and one claim, that Gandhi believed freedom must be wrested while Ambedkar expected it to be bestowed, is an interpretive generalisation. In your answer, phrase these as 'differed in emphasis' rather than as absolute opposites.

### 8. Regional contributions: a tour of the Civil Disobedience Movement and beyond

*Objective:* Place women, tribal and later-phase regional leaders (Saikiani, Gaidinliu, Satara, Paparandi, Gudem) in the story, and use the GS-045 region-by-region table for the rest of the Civil Disobedience Movement.

## Why regions matter

NCERT (Class XII) says the Congress was organised on linguistic regions, not British provincial boundaries, and that Praja Mandals took the message into princely states. Spectrum shows one programme taking different forms by local economy; for each region learn **leader, method and local twist**.

## Recap: the region-by-region table lives in GS-045

The full table (Tamil Nadu, Malabar, Andhra, Orissa, Bengal, Bihar, the North-West Frontier, Gujarat, Sholapur and others) is in **GS-045**, module 'The spread of the movement: region by region'. Practice items P6 and M3 here draw on it. This module adds only the women, tribal and later-phase angles.

## Women and tribal leaders in the regional story

- **Assam, Chandraprabha Saikiani:** led a student strike against the Cunningham Circular (May 1930) and incited Kachari villages to break forest laws, though the Assam Congress denied it.
- **Manipur and Nagaland, Rani Gaidinliu:** joined her cousin Haipou Jadonang's movement at 13 (Spectrum's wording: she raised the banner of revolt at 13). After Jadonang was hanged in 1931 she led the revolt herself (verify). She was captured in October 1932, sentenced to life imprisonment, and released from Tura jail on the orders of the Interim Government (formed 1946). Standard accounts give 1947 for the release (verify). Spectrum does not itself say she was released in 1946.

## Beyond civil disobedience

- **Satara and Medinipur:** NCERT Class XII says 'independent' governments were proclaimed in several districts in the Quit India phase. Satara's *prati sarkar* (1943), with volunteer corps and people's courts, was dominated by kunbi peasants, supported by Dalits, and ran until 1946.
- **Orissa, August 1942:** NCERT Class VIII records police firing at Paparandi in Nabarangpur (25 August 1942) with nineteen deaths, and the hanging of the tribal leader Lakhan Nayak.
- **Andhra tribal revolt:** NCERT Class X records the Gudem Hills guerrilla movement under Alluri Sitarama Raju, which ended with his arrest in May 1924.
- **Telangana and Tebhaga:** Spectrum notes Telangana in Hyderabad state as the biggest peasant guerrilla war in modern Indian history (3,000 villages and 3 million people), and Tebhaga in Bengal (September 1946). Details belong to GS-031.

**Method for answers.** Choose three regions with *different* features (a coastal salt march, a peasant no-tax area, a frontier or tribal area) rather than a list of eight names; that shows the examiner you understand diversity.


### 9. Examination analysis: how Prelims and Mains test this topic

*Objective:* Recognise the question patterns and traps for women, peasants, Dalits and regions, and plan a 15-mark answer with the GORL frame.

## What the verified questions show

Eleven verified questions are mapped here (notes in the examination section). Three are 15-mark Mains questions: 2025 on Phule's writings and subaltern classes; 2019 on the many voices of the Gandhian phase; 2020 on ideological strands and the social base since the 1920s. The Prelims items are mostly 'who or what': Trade Disputes Act (2017), Lokhande and the Factories Act (2017), Hind Mazdoor Sabha (2018), leader-organisation match (2019), Rakhmabai (2020), Madanapalle (2021), Self-Respect Movement (2025), Eka versus Bardoli (2026).

## Four recurring traps

1. **Right movement, wrong leader or place.** Make a one-line identity card for each: Eka = Hardoi, Bahraich, Sitapur, rent, Madari Pasi; Bardoli = Surat district, revenue enhancement, Vallabhbhai Patel, 1928.
2. **Mixed-up organisations.** Founders of women's organisations and Dalit societies are favourite items; the master-note tables help.
3. **Labour, not only land.** Several questions come from the working-class chapter (Trade Disputes Act 1929, Lokhande, Factories Act 1881, Hind Mazdoor Sabha); the facts are in module 4.
4. **Source disagreements.** Where Spectrum and NCERT differ (module flags), expect the examiner to follow the more standard account; do not assert a contested detail unless a statement forces you to.

## Prelims method

For two-mark questions with negative marking (one-third of the marks, about 0.67), eliminate options using the identity cards. A blind four-way guess has expected value 0.25 x 2 - 0.75 x 0.67 = 0; with two options eliminated it is about +0.67, so a reasoned guess is then worth taking.

## Mains method (15 marks, about 250 words)

Use GORL as a skeleton. For a 2019-style question: an introduction naming the Gandhian phase as the time the movement widened; four body paragraphs (women, peasants and workers, Dalits, regions and tribals), each with grievance, a named organisation or leader, relation to Congress and one limit; and a conclusion linking the wider social base with unfinished social questions (Poona Pact, women's authority in Congress). *Discuss* and *elaborate* ask for a developed account with evidence, not a list.

### Notes: Key terms

- Purdah: seclusion of women, one of the practices reformers attacked.
- Sati: the burning of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre; made illegal in Bengal by Regulation XVII of 1829.
- Restitution of conjugal rights: a court order directing a spouse to return to the marital home; this was the relief sought by Dadaji Bhikaji in the Rukhmabai case (1884).
- Depressed Classes: the British-era official term for the communities now called Scheduled Castes or Dalits.
- Separate electorate: seats chosen only by voters of the community concerned; joint electorate with reserved seats: seats reserved for a group but chosen by all voters.
- Double vote: the Communal Award provision of one vote in the separate electorate and one in the general electorate for the depressed classes (per Spectrum).
- Prati sarkar: 'parallel government', the name of the Satara government of 1943.
- Bedakhali: eviction of tenants from land; one of the Awadh grievances. Nazrana: renewal fee. Begar, hari and vethi: forms of unpaid forced labour.
- Tebhaga: the demand that sharecroppers keep two-thirds of the crop instead of half.
- Satyashodhak Samaj: Phule's Truth Seekers' Society (1873).

### Notes: Cause-effect chains

- Falling prices in the Depression, then no cash income for rich peasants, then no-revenue campaigns in civil disobedience, then disappointment in 1931 and non-participation in 1932.
- Communal Award gives separate electorates, then Gandhi's fast, then compromise in the Poona Pact, then reserved seats in a joint electorate, then lasting Dalit criticism that representatives depended on caste Hindus.
- Gandhi's call for women in picketing, then large-scale entry into public life, then NCERT's limit: little change in how women's position was imagined and few posts of authority.

### Core concepts

- GORL: Grievance, Organisation and leaders, Relation to Congress and Gandhi, Limits and legacy.
- Women's entry into public life was large in the Salt March and civil disobedience but on unequal terms.
- Peasant movements moved from local, economic and limited to linked with Congress after 1918.
- Dalit politics demanded political protection (reserved seats, separate electorates); Gandhi demanded joint electorates and social reform.
- Regions shaped the movement: the same programme took different forms in each region.
