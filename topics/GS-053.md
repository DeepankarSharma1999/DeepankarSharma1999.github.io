### Bridge: Reading a chronology: years, phases and anchor dates

Years in this topic are written in the Common Era (CE), the ordinary calendar: 1885 means 1,885 years after the start of that calendar. Years from 1801 to 1900 belong to the **19th century** and years from 1901 to 2000 to the **20th century**. So 1885 is 19th-century and 1905 is 20th-century.

A **phase** is a block of years with one dominant style of politics. Historians draw the boundaries differently, and that is acceptable as long as you can justify yours.

To order events quickly, do not memorise a hundred dates. Memorise a few **anchor dates** and hang other events on them. This lesson uses 1885, 1905, 1919, 1930, 1942 and 1947. Any event can then be placed by asking: is it before or after each anchor, and by how many years? For example, the Lucknow Pact (1916) sits between 1905 and 1919, and nearer 1919.

**Worked example.** Put these in order: Dandi March, Rowlatt Act, Surat split. Surat is 1907, the Rowlatt Act 1919, Dandi 1930. Between anchors 1905 and 1919 comes Surat; between 1919 and 1930 comes Rowlatt; Dandi is the 1930 anchor itself.

### Bridge: Councils, Acts and electorates in one minute

Under British rule India was governed by a **Viceroy** (Governor-General) in Calcutta (Delhi from 1911) and a **Governor** in each province. Alongside them sat **legislative councils**: bodies that discuss and vote on laws and, as the years passed, the budget. At first almost all members were nominated by the government; later some were elected.

An **Act** is a law passed by the British Parliament. The Indian Councils Acts (1861, 1892, 1909) and the Government of India Acts (1919, 1935) each changed how large and how powerful these councils were. Nationalists judged every Act by two tests: how many members are elected, and can the councils control the money (the budget)?

The **franchise** is the right to vote. Early on only a tiny, property-owning minority could vote. An **electorate** is the group of voters for a seat. In a **separate electorate** only members of one community vote for that community's seats. In a **joint (general) electorate** everyone votes together, sometimes with some seats **reserved** for a community. Much of the dispute among Indian groups was about which system to choose.

### 1. Orientation: the whole movement on one page

*Objective:* Hold the five phases of the movement and the three questions (base, goal, method) in mind before any detail.

## Why a synthesis lesson
This lesson steps back from the Day 9 stories and asks what UPSC keeps asking: **what did different groups want, and how did they differ about the way to get it?**

## The five phases
1. **1858/1885 to 1905: the Moderate phase.** Educated professionals petition and argue. The Indian National Congress meets for the first time in Bombay in December 1885.
2. **1905 to 1918: militant nationalism.** The Partition of Bengal brings boycott and swaraj. The Congress splits at Surat (1907). Revolutionaries act in secret. Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow session (1916) bring the camps together.
3. **1919 to 1934: the Gandhian mass phase.** Rowlatt satyagraha, Non-Cooperation (1920-22), the quieter 1920s, then Civil Disobedience (1930-34).
4. **1935 to 1939: politics through the ballot.** The Government of India Act, 1935, the 1937 elections, Congress ministries and open Left-Right quarrels.
5. **1939 to 1947: war and endgame.** Quit India (1942), the rise of the Muslim League, and the transfer of power with Partition on 15 August 1947.

## Two habits for this topic
**Habit 1: date first, then idea.** Anchor every event to a pillar year (bridge module B1).

**Habit 2: ask three questions of every group.** Who is it for (social base)? What does it want (goal)? How will it get it (method)? Nearly every ideological difference below is a difference in one of these three.

### 2. Foundation: vocabulary and the three questions

*Objective:* Define the working terms once, so that later modules can use them without confusion.

## Terms used throughout
- **Nationalism**: the belief that people of a territory form one political community. Spectrum treats Indian nationalism as the product of several factors and the Congress as its main organisation (module 3).
- **Moderate**: a Congress leader of about 1885-1905 who favoured gradual, legal reform and trusted British fairness.
- **Extremist** (also Radical or militant nationalist): a critic of the Moderates who wanted swaraj and pressure through boycott. "Extremist" was a label of the time; "militant" is more neutral.
- **Revolutionary**: someone who planned or used armed action, alone or in secret groups.
- **Swaraj**: self-rule. Its meaning changed: self-government like the colonies (Congress, 1906), complete independence (Aurobindo; Lahore, 1929).
- **Dominion status**: self-government inside the British Empire, as in Canada or Australia.
- **Constitutional / extra-constitutional**: inside the law and the councils, versus outside them (boycott, civil disobedience).
- **Satyagraha**: Gandhi's method of resisting wrong through truth and non-violence, accepting suffering without hatred.
- **Non-cooperation** is withdrawing help (schools, courts, councils, cloth); **civil disobedience** is deliberately breaking a specific law.
- **Communalism**: politics that treats a religion as a single political interest set against others. Spectrum applies the word to Hindu and Muslim organisations alike; it is a contested term.
- **Left and Right in the Congress**: labels Spectrum uses for the 1930s. The Left (Nehru, Bose, socialists, communists) stressed class demands and continuous struggle; the more conservative wing favoured working through existing structures when mass struggle was not possible. Gandhi fits neither label neatly.
- **S-T-S**: struggle, truce, struggle, the rhythm favoured by Gandhi and many Congress leaders.

## The three questions
Take any group and ask three things.
1. **Base**: whose interests does it speak for?
2. **Goal**: what does it ask for (reform, self-government, independence, a social order)?
3. **Method**: petition, boycott, mass non-violence, councils, arms?

**Guided example.** Moderates: base, educated professionals and landed upper middle classes; goal, a larger share in governance and economic justice; method, petitions and resolutions. Now the Extremists: base, wider middle and lower middle classes; goal, swaraj; method, boycott and passive resistance. Same three headings, different answers: that is the whole of "ideological difference".

## Not every difference is ideological
Some splits were about timing, a personality or a presidency. At Surat (1907), for instance, a dispute over the venue and the president sat on top of a real quarrel about how far to extend boycott. Separate the two.

### 3. Phase 1, 1858 to 1905: roots, the Congress and the Moderates

*Objective:* Explain why the Congress appeared in 1885, what the Moderates wanted and did, and why their base stayed narrow.

## Roots
Spectrum explains Indian nationalism as a mix of factors: the world spread of ideas of nationhood, Indian reform thinking, British modernisation, and a strong reaction to British policy. Colonial rule also unified the country administratively and economically and created a new educated middle class, which later supplied the Congress with its leaders. Racial arrogance mattered too: the maximum age for the Indian Civil Service examination was cut from 21 to 19 in 1876 under Lytton, on the Secretary of State's order (standard knowledge, verify), the Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act followed in 1878, and the Ilbert Bill row (1883) showed that fair play stopped where Europeans' interests began.

## Associations before the Congress
Regional bodies already existed: the British Indian Association (1851), the East India Association founded by Dadabhai Naoroji in London (1866), the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, the Indian Association of Calcutta (1876, Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose), the Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884) and the Bombay Presidency Association (1885). The Calcutta association's all-India conference of December 1883 was a forerunner of the Congress, which it later merged into (1886).

## 1885
A.O. Hume, a retired British civil servant, helped bring leaders together, and the first Congress met in Bombay in December 1885 with 72 delegates under W.C. Bonnerjee. Whether Hume meant it as a 'safety valve' is a debate (module 9).

## The Moderate method and programme
The Moderates believed the British were basically just but ill-informed, so public opinion, resolutions and petitions ('prayer and petition') would win gradual concessions. They also lobbied in Britain: a British committee of the Congress was set up in 1889. Their programme had four strands:
1. **Economic critique** of colonial rule: the drain of wealth (Naoroji, R.C. Dutt, Wacha), with demands for lower land revenue, abolition of salt tax and protection of industry.
2. **Council reform**: first a larger and more elected membership, then control of the budget. The Indian Councils Act, 1892 raised the number of non-official members and allowed budget discussion and questions, but officials kept their majority and the budget could not be voted on.
3. **Administrative reform**: Indianisation of services, separation of judiciary from executive, curbs on costly foreign wars.
4. **Civil rights**: a free press and freedom of association, shown in the protests over Tilak's arrest in 1897.
By 1904-06 Naoroji, Gokhale and Tilak were all asking for self-government on the colonial model.

## Base and limits
The Moderates did not trust the masses to enter politics before they were 'welded into a nation', so their base stayed urban and elite. The NCERT Class VIII text stresses that they still spoke in the name of all Indians. The government distrusted them anyway, calling the Congress a 'factory of sedition' (Dufferin).

## A Muslim current
Syed Ahmed Khan's Aligarh movement promoted modern education and loyalty, and he opposed the Congress. Deoband welcomed the Congress and issued a fatwa in 1888 against Syed Ahmed's organisations.

### 4. Phase 2, 1905 to 1918: militants, revolutionaries and Home Rule

*Objective:* Contrast Moderates, Extremists and revolutionaries on base, goal and method, and show how the war brought the camps together.

## Why militancy grew
Spectrum gives several causes: British refusal to concede demands (the 1892 Act disappointed, the Natu brothers were deported and Tilak jailed in 1897), the famines of 1896-1900, Curzon's measures (Official Secrets Act and Universities Act in 1904, Partition of Bengal in 1905), the shock of Japan's defeat of Russia in 1905, and a feeling that petitions were 'political mendicancy'.

## The Extremist programme
Its leaders were Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai (with Aurobindo Ghosh in Bengal). Its tenets: swaraj as the goal, direct action, faith in the masses and readiness for sacrifice. In Calcutta (December 1906, Naoroji presiding) the Congress adopted swaraj 'like the United Kingdom or the colonies', but left the word undefined, so each camp read it differently. Tilak meant some form of self-government; Aurobindo meant independence. Boycott and swadeshi now became the test (GS-038 has the campaign).

**Surat, December 1907.** The Extremists wanted boycott spread to schools, courts and councils all over India. The Moderates would not go beyond Bengal and certainly not to boycott of councils, and they hoped for council reforms. Quarrels over the venue and the president made the break inevitable. The Moderates kept the Congress and reaffirmed self-government within the empire by constitutional means.

**Limits of Extremism.** Spectrum notes a social conservatism: Tilak's Ganapati and Shivaji festivals, anti-cow-killing campaigns, and opposition to the Age of Consent Bill (on the ground that reform should not come from an alien government). It also credits them with raising patriotism to 'service and sacrifice'.

**Government strategy.** Repression of Extremists, concessions to Moderates, then more repression: the Morley-Minto Act of 1909 introduced separate electorates for Muslims. The Muslim League was formed at Dacca in December 1906 (Spectrum prints 1905 in one passage).

## Revolutionaries
They believed that individual heroic action (assassinating officials, 'swadeshi' robberies, and in the war military plots) would end fear of rulers and rouse the people. Spectrum criticises the Extremist leaders for failing to separate mass revolution from individual violence. The groups are the subject of GS-039 (Day 10, same day).

## The war and Home Rule
Moderates backed the war as a duty; Extremists (Tilak, released June 1914) backed it hoping for reward; revolutionaries sought to exploit it. Gandhi returned in January 1915 and kept away from the Home Rule agitation in wartime (Spectrum). Tilak's League (April 1916, Maharashtra, Karnataka and nearby provinces) and Annie Besant's (September 1916, rest of India) demanded self-government by year-round work, not annual sessions. Moderates joined after Besant's arrest (June 1917).

## Lucknow and the August Declaration
At Lucknow (December 1916), the Extremists returned to the Congress, and the Congress and the League made joint demands. The Congress accepted separate electorates, a move Spectrum calls a landmark in the two-nation idea but also an effort to allay minority fears. On 20 August 1917 Montagu announced 'responsible government' as the goal, with no timetable.

### 5. Phase 3, 1919 to 1934: the Gandhian mass phase

*Objective:* Explain what was new in Gandhian politics and how Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience differed from earlier methods.

## What was new
Gandhi arrived from South Africa in January 1915. At the Banaras University opening in 1916 he reminded the privileged audience of the labouring poor (NCERT). After Champaran, Ahmedabad and Kheda (1917-18) he called the nationwide Rowlatt satyagraha (March-April 1919). The government answered with repression and, at Amritsar on 13 April 1919, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre. Spectrum counts it the first mass strike; the NCERT books note that it was strongest in towns.

His distinct features, set against the earlier camps:
1. **Method**: satyagraha, firm, truthful, non-violent and fearless, resting on withdrawal of cooperation and a readiness to suffer, without hatred of the opponent. Means and ends could not be separated.
2. **Base**: peasants, workers, artisans, women and students joined alongside the middle class and business, though each read swaraj in its own way.
3. **Programme**: alongside agitation, a constructive programme of khadi, national education, temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity and removal of untouchability.
4. **Class**: the Congress opposed class war and sought class unity, yet peasants sometimes took Gandhi's name for their own anti-landlord actions (NCERT).

## Non-Cooperation and Khilafat, 1920-22
Three demands: a fair settlement for Turkey (Khilafat), redress of the Punjab wrongs, and swaraj. The Congress was not united at first (Tilak was uneasy about a religious alliance; C.R. Das had doubts over a council boycott), but a special session at Calcutta (September 1920) approved and the Nagpur session (December 1920) changed the Congress goal from self-government by constitutional means to swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means. Nagpur also created the Working Committee and linguistic provincial committees, and cut the membership fee.

The movement saw student exodus, lawyers giving up practice, bonfires of foreign cloth, Hindu-Muslim cooperation on a scale not seen again, and local struggles (Awadh, Eka, Mappila, Sikh reform). After Chauri Chaura (5 February 1922) Gandhi withdrew it. Subhas Bose called the order of retreat a 'national calamity'; a Marxist reading holds the leadership pulled back when the masses were gaining the initiative; Gandhi feared that violence would hand the state an excuse for force.

## Civil Disobedience, 1930-34
After Simon and the Nehru Report (module 6), the Lahore Congress (December 1929) declared purna swaraj. The campaign, opened with the Dandi March (12 March to 6 April 1930), differed from Non-Cooperation: the goal was independence, and breaking laws from the start; fewer lawyers and students but more peasants and business; Muslim participation far lower; three times as many imprisoned (Spectrum, full list in GS-045). After the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 March 1931) and the inconclusive Second Round Table Conference (September to December 1931), the movement resumed in 1932 and was withdrawn in April 1934. Round Table Conferences and Pact: GS-046 (modules 3 to 8).

## Gandhi against the earlier tradition
Moderates petitioned and Extremists boycotted, but both worked with the middle class. Gandhi turned the petition into mass non-violent defiance. Note that the goal stayed 'swaraj', not independence, until Lahore.

### 6. The 1920s: Swarajists, the Left, Simon and the road to Lahore

*Objective:* Explain how Congress politics split into several strands after 1922 and how those strands met again over Simon and Purna Swaraj.

## Swarajists and No-Changers
After Gandhi's arrest (March 1922) the movement was demoralised. One group (C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Ajmal Khan) wanted to enter the councils to 'end or mend' them by obstruction; the **No-Changers** (C. Rajagopalachari, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, M.A. Ansari) wanted constructive work and continued boycott. At Gaya (December 1922) the council-entry proposal lost, Das and Motilal formed the Swarajya Party, and a compromise at Delhi (September 1923) let them contest elections within the Congress. They won 42 of 141 elected seats in the Assembly elections of November 1923, and defeated the Public Safety Bill in 1928. They weakened after Das died (1925), communal riots and a split between those willing to take office (Responsivists: Lajpat Rai, Malaviya, Kelkar) and the rest. The party walked out of the councils in 1926 and finally in 1930.

## New currents
- **Marxism and socialism.** Nehru and Bose led a Congress Left, critical of both Swarajists and No-Changers, demanding purna swaraj and social justice. The Communist Party was formalised at Kanpur in 1925 (Spectrum also gives a Tashkent beginning, 1920); NCERT names the CPI and the RSS as mid-1920s bodies.
- **Labour and peasants.** AITUC began in 1920 with Lajpat Rai as president; the Trade Union Act of 1926 recognised unions. Kisan sabhas, Bardoli (a revenue dispute from 1926; the satyagraha ran February to August 1928) and the UP movements are in GS-031.
- **Caste and regional parties.** The Justice Party, the Self-Respect movement (1925), the Unionists in Punjab; GS-052 covers Dalit streams.
- **Revolutionary turn to socialism.** The HRA became the HSRA (Delhi, 1928); Bhagat Singh's ideas moved toward mass politics, secularism and a socialist order. See GS-039.

## Simon, the Nehru Report and the communal question
The all-white Simon Commission (announced November 1927) was boycotted by the Congress and many groups; the Justice Party and Punjab Unionists did not. Its arrival and the road to Calcutta 1928 are in GS-045 module 2 (and GS-044, to come on Day 15, not yet written). Ambedkar told the Commission (October 1928) that the depressed classes should be treated as a distinct minority (GS-052 modules 5 to 7). The Nehru Report (August 1928) proposed dominion status, joint electorates with reserved seats where Muslims were in a minority, and 19 fundamental rights; Hindu Mahasabha and League positions clashed (master-notes table) and Jinnah's 14 points followed (March 1929). The young Left rejected dominion status.

## Purna Swaraj
Calcutta (December 1928) gave the government a year to accept dominion status; Lahore (December 1929, Nehru presiding) made complete independence the goal (GS-045 module 3).

### 7. Phase 4, 1935 to 1939: after Civil Disobedience, Left against Right

*Objective:* Explain the post-1934 debates about council work and office, the response to the 1935 Act and the Gandhi-Bose clash of 1939.

## The first debate, 1934-35
With mass struggle suspended, Spectrum records three views. (1) Constructive work. (2) Council entry (Ansari, Asaf Ali, Bhulabhai Desai, Satyamurthi, B.C. Roy) to keep morale up. (3) The Left, led by Nehru, which held that the situation was still revolutionary and wanted continuous mass struggle with the class demands of peasants and workers. Nehru criticised the **struggle-truce-struggle (S-T-S)** pattern and proposed 'struggle-victory'. Gandhi conciliated the council group by allowing a Parliamentary Board (AICC, Patna, May 1934); he resigned from Congress membership in October 1934. The Congress took 45 of 75 Indian seats in the Assembly elections of November 1934. The Congress Socialist Party was formed in 1934 (standard knowledge, verify):

## The Government of India Act, 1935
The Act proposed an All-India Federation, never formed because its conditions about the princely states were not met, and gave the provinces autonomy, with wider franchise (about 14 per cent of British Indian population) and Governor's reserve powers. The Act received royal assent on 2 August 1935 and provincial autonomy began on 1 April 1937 (both verify; Spectrum prints July and August). Full treatment: GS-047. The Congress rejected it and demanded a constituent assembly elected on adult franchise; the Hindu Mahasabha and the Liberals were for working it (Spectrum).

## The second debate, 1937: office
Nehru, Bose and the socialists and communists opposed taking office: it would be power without responsibility and would blunt the revolutionary edge. Their alternative: enter and obstruct, as the Swarajists had once done. Supporters of office replied that it was tactics, not socialism, in a period without mass struggle. Gandhi first opposed, then agreed to a trial. Congress won 716 of 1,161 seats it contested in the 1937 elections, formed ministries in several provinces (seven at first, eight later; see the differences table), and ruled for 28 months until October 1939.

**Record.** Civil liberties were widened and prisoners released. Land, debt and tenancy reforms helped mainly occupancy tenants, not sub-tenants or labourers. Labour policy preferred conciliation, and the Bombay Industrial Disputes Act (1938; Spectrum calls it the 'Bombay Traders Disputes Act', verify) looked anti-labour to critics. The League's Pirpur Committee (1938) accused the Congress of bias, and NCERT says the League was angered when a joint government in the United Provinces was refused in 1937.

## Gandhi and Bose, 1939
Bose was president at Haripura (February 1938) and stood again in January 1939 against Gandhi's choice Pattabhi Sitaramayya, winning 1,580 to 1,377. At Tripuri (March 1939) he sought a six-month ultimatum to Britain; Gandhi felt neither Congress nor the masses were ready. The Left did not back Bose enough to split the Congress. He resigned in April and formed the Forward Bloc in May 1939. Spectrum sums up the difference: Gandhi held ahimsa and the unity of means and ends, Bose accepted force and help from any quarter against Britain, yet both thought socialism the way forward, in different forms.

### 8. Phase 5, 1939 to 1947: war, Quit India, the League and the endgame

*Objective:* Place Quit India, the INA and the communal struggle on one timeline and explain the divergence between Congress and League.

## War and the first offers
The ministries resigned in October 1939 when negotiations on India's part in the war broke down. At Ramgarh (March 1940, Azad presiding) the Congress said nothing short of complete independence would do and left the form and timing of struggle to Gandhi. In the same month the League passed its Lahore resolution calling for grouping of Muslim-majority areas in the north-west and east into independent states with autonomous and sovereign units; its text did not mention Pakistan (NCERT). The August Offer of 1940 promised dominion status as the objective, a constituent assembly after the war and a minority veto; the Congress rejected it ('dead as a doornail', Nehru) and the League welcomed the veto. The Cripps Mission (March 1942) failed to give the Congress real control of defence.

## Quit India
The Working Committee passed the resolution at Wardha on 14 July 1942 and the AICC ratified it at Gowalia Tank, Bombay, on 8 August. Gandhi's 'Do or Die' call followed, and the top leaders were arrested on 9 August. With them gone, younger workers and socialists carried on with sabotage, strikes and parallel governments (Satara, Medinipur). The Communists, who took up the 'people's war' line after the German attack on the USSR in June 1941 (standard knowledge, verify), dissociated themselves from Quit India.

## The Indian National Army
Bose left India in January 1941, went via Kabul to Germany and in 1943 by submarine to South-East Asia (standard knowledge, verify). He took over the Indian Independence League from Rashbehari Bose in July 1943 and became Supreme Commander of the INA on 25 August (Spectrum). The INA entered Indian soil through Imphal and Kohima in 1944 but was defeated. The INA trials after 1945 drew wide public support (NCERT).

## Endgame
Gandhi was released on health grounds on 6 May 1944 (standard knowledge, verify) and later that year met Jinnah; the C.R. proposal failed because Jinnah wanted the two-nation theory accepted first. At the Simla Conference (Wavell, June 1945) the Congress objected to being called a caste Hindu body and the League insisted on all Muslim nominees; the breakdown gave the League a virtual veto. The 1945-46 elections confirmed the polarisation: the Congress swept 'General' seats, the League won the Muslim seats. The Cabinet Mission of 1946 could not bring them to a common plan; after Direct Action Day (16 August 1946) riots spread from Calcutta. Mountbatten's appointment as Viceroy was announced on 20 February 1947 and he took office on 24 March (NCERT lehs303 gives only February; verify), and power was transferred on 15 August 1947. Partition in depth: GS-051 (Day 15, not yet written).

## Two ideas of the nation
The Congress, including Azad, held to one Indian nation of many communities. The League increasingly argued that Muslims were a separate nation; NCERT links this to fear of permanent minority status after the 1937 elections.

### 9. Debates among historians, and how to use them

*Objective:* State the main schools of interpretation and the evidence Spectrum and NCERT give for each, without over-claiming.

## Why this matters
'To what extent' questions invite evaluation; historians' views sharpen it. Use named schools only where you are sure of the claim (master-notes table).

## Debate 1: why was the Congress founded?
- **Safety valve**: Hume founded it to release discontent peacefully. Spectrum says Lajpat Rai believed this, and links the Marxist 'conspiracy' view (R.P. Dutt) to the same notion.
- **Lightning conductor**: Bipin Chandra, quoted by Spectrum, sees the early leaders using Hume as a shield to bring together nationalist forces which would have been refused permission had they acted alone. Spectrum's summary credits this to Gokhale; its text credits Bipin Chandra (a slip to flag).

## Debate 2: were the Moderates failures or foundation-layers?
Extremists called their methods 'political mendicancy'; Lajpat Rai thought the early Congress opportunist. Others, quoted by Spectrum, call it the 'seed time' (Bipin Chandra) or the brick and mortar beneath the building (Pattabhi Sitaramayya). The balanced reading: they built an economic critique and a national platform but not a mass base.

## Debate 3: what drove nationalism?
Spectrum rejects a pure 'response to the colonial stimulus' reading and prefers a mix of factors, in which the contradiction between colonial and Indian interests is central (this is the nationalist reading, associated with Bipin Chandra). The **Cambridge school** (Anil Seal and colleagues; standard knowledge, verify) read politics as competition among local elites for office and patronage rather than as a clash of ideals. The **Marxist** reading stresses class: Spectrum quotes a Marxist view that the leadership pulled the movement back from revolution after 1922 'within safe channels'. The **Subaltern** school (Ranajit Guha and others; NCERT cites Shahid Amin in Subaltern Studies III) studies peasants and others acting by their own understanding, as NCERT's accounts of 'Gandhi Raj' show.

## Debate 4: Gandhi's strategy
Retreat or tactic? Spectrum argues that the 1931 pact was no retreat because mass movements are short-lived and capacity for sacrifice is limited; critics such as the young Left and Subhas Bose saw loss of momentum. Nehru's later reconciliation with S-T-S after the 1937 victory is part of the same debate.

## Using the schools in an answer
Name the view and its claim, give one event as evidence (Chauri Chaura for the Marxist reading), add one limit, and conclude with a balanced judgment.
Say 'some historians' unless you are sure of the name.

### 10. Examination application: frames for the 10 verified questions

*Objective:* Convert the topic into answer plans for chronology, statement and evaluative Mains questions.

## What the 10 questions test
Four are Prelims (a chronology, a statement set, a pairs question and a Forward Bloc question) and six are Mains (two on Moderates, two on Gandhi, one on the ideological strands of the 1920s, one on the many voices of the Gandhian phase). Notes for each are in the PYQ notes; the patterns are below.

## Prelims methods
1. **Chronology**: place each event against the anchors (B1). If two events are inside the same decade, use the causal link (Chauri Chaura before Gaya; Gaya before Delhi compromise).
2. **Statement sets**: verify the verb. 'Instrumental in', 'did not support' and 'declared illegal' are claims about a person or a body at a precise time; check the actor and the date, then eliminate.
3. **Pairs**: match person with organisation and period. A wrong pair usually swaps two real organisations.
4. **Strategy**: with two options eliminated, the guess is favourable. A wrong answer costs one-third of the question's marks (2 marks lose 0.67). A blind guess among four has expected value 0 (2 x 1/4 minus 0.67 x 3/4). With one option eliminated it is about +0.22, and with two eliminated about +0.67 (2 x 1/2 minus 0.67 x 1/2).

## Mains frames
**Frame A, comparison** (Moderates versus Extremists versus Gandhi): intro defines the three questions; body one paragraph per group on base, goal and method; limits; conclusion says the strands shared the anti-colonial aim but differed on mass involvement.

**Frame B, evaluation** ('to what extent', 'why did X fail'): intro states the claim; give three reasons with evidence; add what X achieved; conclude with a proportion ('largely', 'partly').

**Frame C, ideas of a person** (Gandhi's thoughts today; Gandhi and Tagore): state the ideas as three or four principles; link each to an example from the movement; then a present-day relevance (current events are dynamic; verify).

**Frame D, social base**: structure by group (peasants, workers, women, Dalits, business, Muslims, regions) and note who was left out.

## Length and time
A 10-mark answer is about 150 words and a 15-mark answer about 250 words. As a practice target (not an official allotment), spend about 7 minutes and 11 minutes. Open with a one-line definition, keep three or four dated points, and end with a judgment.

## Common errors
Chronology instead of analysis; 'Extremists wanted violence' (most did not); treating 'Congress' as one voice; quoting a historian you cannot place.
