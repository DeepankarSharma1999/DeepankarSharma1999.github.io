### Bridge: Executive, legislature and judiciary on one page

A government has three jobs. **Making** rules is the **legislature** (in India, Parliament: the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha and the President together). **Carrying out** the rules and running the administration is the **executive**. **Deciding disputes** and checking whether rules agree with the Constitution is the **judiciary**. These three are called the three **organs** of government. NCERT notes that the Constitution wants them to work in coordination and keep a balance among themselves.

| Organ | Main job | Key office-holders in India | Where the President touches it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legislature | Make laws, control money, question the executive | Parliament (President + two Houses) | Summons and prorogues, gives assent, issues ordinances |
| Executive | Implement laws and run the administration | President (head of State), PM and council of ministers (real executive), civil servants | Formal head; all executive action is in his name |
| Judiciary | Settle disputes, protect rights, review laws | Supreme Court, High Courts, subordinate courts | Appoints judges; may seek the Supreme Court's advice; pardons |

In a **presidential** system (the USA) the three organs are kept apart and the President is both head of State and head of government. In a **parliamentary** system (India) the executive is drawn from the legislature and stays in office only while the Lok Sabha supports it, so the organs overlap and cooperate (Laxmikanth contrasts cooperation and coordination in the parliamentary system with separation of powers in the presidential system). That overlap is why the Indian President, though he is a part of Parliament, is only the nominal head of the executive.

Keep this table in mind: each time this lesson says 'power', ask which organ it belongs to.

### Bridge: Electoral college and single transferable vote with a small count

An **electoral college** is a fixed group of people chosen by law to elect an office-holder on behalf of everyone else. For the Indian President the group is made of elected legislators, not of ordinary voters.

In the ordinary 'first past the post' count, the person with the most votes wins even if that is less than half. The system used for the President is different. Each voter ranks the candidates 1, 2, 3 and so on. This is a **preferential** ballot. The vote can be **transferred**: if your first choice is knocked out, your ballot is moved to your next choice. A winner must reach a **quota**, which for one post is just over half the valid votes.

**Small count.** Eleven voters, three candidates (Asha, Bela, Chand). First preferences: Asha 5, Bela 4, Chand 2. The quota is eleven divided by (1 + 1), which is 5.5, rounded down to 5, plus one: **6**. Nobody has 6 after the first count (under first-past-the-post Asha would have won with 5 of 11, less than half). Chand has the fewest, so Chand is eliminated and his two ballots are moved to their second preferences. Suppose both rank Bela second. Bela now has 4 + 2 = 6, reaches the quota and is elected with a true majority of the valid votes.

Laxmikanth records that the Constituent Assembly itself said 'proportional representation' is a misnomer for a one-seat election, which is better called a preferential or alternative vote system. Module 4 applies this logic to the real President's election, where each voter's ballot carries a different weight.

### 1. Orientation: the Union executive and where the President sits

*Objective:* Name the five parts of the Union executive and explain why India chose a parliamentary executive with an indirectly elected, nominal head of State.

## The Union executive

Articles 52 to 78 in Part V of the Constitution deal with the **Union executive**. Laxmikanth says it consists of the **President**, the **Vice-President**, the **Prime Minister**, the **council of ministers** and the **Attorney General of India**. The President is the head of the Indian State, the first citizen, and the symbol of the unity, integrity and solidarity of the nation.

NCERT gives the everyday meaning of 'executive': the branch of government responsible for implementing the laws and policies adopted by the legislature, which also takes part in framing policy. NCERT separates the **political executive** (the heads of government and ministers who carry overall responsibility for policy) from the **permanent executive** (the civil servants responsible for day-to-day administration).

## Head of State versus head of government

The **head of State** is the formal, ceremonial top of the country. The **head of government** runs the administration. In a presidential system (the USA, Brazil) one person holds both. In a parliamentary system (India, the UK, Germany, Japan) they are different: the President or monarch is the **nominal** head and the Prime Minister with the cabinet is the **real** executive. Laxmikanth lists the 'presence of nominal and real executives' first among the features of India's parliamentary form of government.

## Why this design?

NCERT gives the Constitution-makers' reasoning. India had experienced running a parliamentary system under the Acts of 1919 and 1935, and had seen that the legislature could control such an executive. The presidential alternative concentrates power in one person and risks a personality cult. So the Constitution adopted the parliamentary executive at the Centre and in the States: a President who is the formal Head of State, with the real power in the Prime Minister and the council of ministers.

A council of ministers can fall at any time, so the country needs a head of State with a fixed term who can appoint the Prime Minister and represent the whole nation (NCERT).

### 2. Qualifications, nomination, oath and conditions of office

*Objective:* State the four qualifications, the nomination and deposit rules, the oath, and the conditions and immunities of the office of President.

## Who can be a candidate

A person must (1) be a **citizen of India**; (2) have completed **35 years of age**; (3) be qualified for election as a member of the **Lok Sabha**; and (4) not hold an **office of profit** under the Union government, a State government, a local authority or any other public authority. An office of profit means a post that brings financial gain from government. Laxmikanth adds that a sitting President or Vice-President, a Governor, and a Union or State minister are **not** deemed to hold an office of profit, so they can stand.

Nomination must be **subscribed by at least 50 electors as proposers and 50 as seconders**, and the candidate deposits **₹15,000** in the Reserve Bank of India. The deposit is forfeited if the candidate fails to get **one-sixth of the votes polled**. Before 1997 the numbers were ten proposers and ten seconders and a deposit of ₹2,500; the increase in 1997 was meant to discourage non-serious candidates. (Trap: these are rules of the 1952 Act as amended in 1997, not the Constitution's own text.)

## Oath

Before entering office the President makes an oath or affirmation, administered by the **Chief Justice of India** (or, in his absence, the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court available). He swears to faithfully execute the office, to preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law, and to devote himself to the service and well-being of the people of India. Any person acting as President or discharging the President's functions takes the same oath.

## Conditions of office

- He must **not be a member** of Parliament or a State legislature; if such a person is elected, the seat is deemed vacated on the day he enters office.
- He holds no other office of profit.
- He uses his official residence (Rashtrapati Bhavan) without rent.
- His emoluments, allowances and privileges are fixed by Parliament and **cannot be reduced during his term**. Laxmikanth records an increase to ₹5 lakh per month by the Finance Act, 2018 (verify the present figure; later changes are not in the extract).

## Immunities

Under **Article 361** (verify) he has personal immunity from legal liability for his **official acts**. During his term he is immune from **criminal proceedings**, even for personal acts, and cannot be arrested or imprisoned. **Civil proceedings** for personal acts can be started only after **two months' notice**.

Articles: 58 qualifications, 59 conditions, 60 oath (full list in the master notes).

### 3. The election I: who votes and how much a vote is worth

*Objective:* List the members of the electoral college and compute the value of an MLA's vote and an MP's vote in a worked numerical example.

## The electoral college

The President is elected **indirectly**, by an electoral college of (1) the **elected** members of both Houses of Parliament; (2) the **elected** members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States; and (3) the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of Delhi and Puducherry (added by the 70th Amendment of 1992, in effect from 1 June 1995).

Who does **not** vote: nominated members of Parliament, nominated members of State assemblies, members of State Legislative Councils (elected or nominated), and nominated members of the Puducherry Assembly (Delhi's Assembly has no nominated members; only Puducherry does; verify). Laxmikanth adds that when an assembly is dissolved, its members cease to be qualified to vote, even if fresh elections have not yet been held.

## Two aims: uniformity and parity

States differ in size. The Constitution wants **uniformity** in the scale of representation of the States, and **parity** between the States taken together and the Union. Votes are therefore given different values.

## The two formulas

(The PDF prints these as images; the formulas below follow its text and the standard rule, and the rounding rules in Article 55(2) are standard knowledge to verify.)

$$ Value of one MLA's vote = Population of the State ÷ (Number of elected MLAs × 1000) $$

$$ Value of one MP's vote = Total value of all MLAs' votes of all States ÷ Total number of elected MPs $$

Population means the **1971 census** figure, frozen by the 84th Amendment (2001) until the first census taken after 2026 has been published. The rounding rule is in Article 55(2) itself (verify), and the two formulas round differently. **MLA vote:** divide the population by (elected MLAs x 1000); if the remainder left after dividing by 1000 is **500 or more**, add one to the quotient. **MP vote:** divide the total value of all MLAs' votes by the number of elected MPs; a fraction **exceeding one-half** counts as one and any other fraction is disregarded. (The toy example below uses round numbers, so no rounding arises.)

## Worked example (toy Union, checked by Python)

Take a toy Union with two States only. **State A**: population 6,00,00,000 and 200 elected MLAs. Value per MLA = 60,000,000 ÷ (200 × 1000) = 300. **State B**: population 2,00,00,000 and 100 elected MLAs. Value per MLA = 20,000,000 ÷ (100 × 1000) = 200.

Total value of State A's MLAs = 200 × 300 = 60,000. State B's = 100 × 200 = 20,000. Total of both = **80,000**. Suppose Parliament has **64** elected members. Value of one MP's vote = 80,000 ÷ 64 = **1,250**. Total of the MPs' votes = 64 × 1,250 = 80,000, equal to the States' total: this is the **parity**. The whole college holds 80,000 + 80,000 = **1,60,000** votes.

## Four facts the examiner likes

1. The value of an MLA's vote **varies from State to State**.
2. The value of a vote **falls** as the number of elected seats rises (seats are in the denominator), and rises with population.
3. Every MP, Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha, has the **same** vote value.
4. More people per elected seat means a heavier vote. Actual State-wise values need the 1971 census figures (not in the extract; verify before quoting any value).

### 4. The election II: quota, count, disputes and why indirect

*Objective:* Apply the quota formula and the transfer of votes to a three-candidate count, and state who decides disputes and why the makers chose indirect election.

## System and ballot

The election is held by **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote**, by **secret ballot**. Each member of the electoral college gets one ballot paper and marks preferences 1, 2, 3 ... against the candidates. The system ensures that the winner is returned by an absolute majority of votes (see bridge B2).

## The quota

A candidate must secure a fixed **quota**: the total valid votes polled divided by (number of seats + 1), plus one. Here only one seat is filled:

$$ Quota = (Total valid votes ÷ 2) + 1 $$

Take the quotient as a whole number (rounding down) and add one. In the toy Union: 1,60,000 ÷ 2 = 80,000; +1 = **80,001**.

## The count, step by step

1. Count the **first preferences**. If someone has the quota, he is elected. Otherwise
2. Cancel the candidate with the **fewest** first-preference votes and transfer his ballots to the **next preference** on each ballot.
3. Repeat until a candidate reaches the quota.

**Worked count (toy Union, all votes cast).** Three candidates: P gets 66,000, Q 60,000, R 34,000 (sum 1,60,000). Quota is 80,001, so no one is elected. R is eliminated. Of R's 34,000 vote-value, 20,000 shows P as second preference, 10,000 shows Q, and 4,000 shows no further preference (exhausted). Now P = 86,000, Q = 70,000, exhausted 4,000 (total 1,60,000 again). P has passed 80,001 and is elected. Note that the quota stays fixed throughout; it is calculated once from the valid votes polled.

## Disputes and defects

All doubts and disputes about the election are decided by the **Supreme Court**, whose decision is final (Article 71). An election **cannot be challenged** on the ground that the electoral college was incomplete, that is, that some seats were vacant. If the Supreme Court declares the election void, acts done by the President **before** the declaration remain valid. Combine this with the dissolved-assembly rule of module 3: an assembly being dissolved does not postpone the election.

## Why indirect?

The makers reasoned that (a) indirect election suits a parliamentary system, where it would be anomalous to give a nominal head a direct popular mandate; (b) nationwide direct election is costly for a symbolic office. Election by MPs alone was rejected because one dominant party would choose its own man; adding State legislators makes the President a representative of the Union and the States equally. Members also noted that 'proportional representation' and 'single transferable vote' are loose names for a one-seat election.

### 5. Term, re-election, impeachment (Article 61) and vacancy

*Objective:* State the term and re-election rule, run through the impeachment procedure with the numbers, and apply the vacancy rules including who acts as President.

## Term

The President holds office for **five years** from the date he enters office. He can resign any time by writing to the **Vice-President**. He can be removed before the end of the term by **impeachment**. He continues in office beyond five years **until his successor takes charge**, and is **eligible for re-election any number of times**. Only Dr. Rajendra Prasad has served two terms (in the USA the limit is two).

## Impeachment (Article 61)

The **only ground** is 'violation of the Constitution', a phrase the Constitution does not define. Procedure:

1. Charges can be started in **either House** of Parliament.
2. The charges must be **signed by at least one-fourth of the members** of the House that frames them, and **14 days' notice** must be given to the President.
3. The House must pass the impeachment resolution by a majority of **two-thirds of its total membership** (not of members present and voting).
4. The resolution goes to the **other House**, which investigates the charges. The President has the right to **appear and be represented**.
5. If the other House also sustains the charges by two-thirds of its **total membership**, the President stands removed from the date the resolution is so passed.

Impeachment is thus a **quasi-judicial** procedure in Parliament. Two traps from Laxmikanth: **nominated** members of either House **can** take part in impeachment though they do not vote in the election; **elected State legislators** do **not** take part in impeachment though they vote in the election. No President has been impeached so far (as Laxmikanth records; verify the current position).

## Vacancy (Article 62 and related rules)

A vacancy can arise by (1) expiry of the term, (2) resignation, (3) removal by impeachment, (4) death, or (5) otherwise, for example disqualification or the election being declared void.

- **Term expiring:** the election must be held **before** the term ends. If the election is delayed, the outgoing President continues until his successor takes charge, so there is no gap (an 'interregnum'); the Vice-President does not act.
- **Any other vacancy:** the election must be held **within six months**, and the new President serves a **full five years**.
- **Who acts meanwhile:** the **Vice-President** acts as President until a new one is elected; he also discharges the functions when the President is absent or ill. If the office of Vice-President is also vacant, the **Chief Justice of India** (or the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court available) acts. The acting person has all the powers and immunities of the President.

Two Presidents have died in office (Zakir Hussain, 1969; Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed, 1977); in 1969 Chief Justice M. Hidayatullah briefly acted after V. V. Giri resigned to contest. **2022 and later (verify):** the 2022 election chose Droupadi Murmu; the next election falls due in 2027 (by the usual five-year cycle); a 2025 vacancy in the Vice-President's office and its election should be checked from current sources.

### 6. Powers of the President in seven heads

*Objective:* List the President's executive, legislative, financial, judicial, diplomatic, military and emergency powers with the main Articles, and note which are exercised on advice.

Laxmikanth groups the powers under seven heads. All these are exercised in practice on the advice of the council of ministers (module 9).

## 1. Executive

- All executive action of the Government of India is formally taken in his **name** (Article 77).
- He appoints the **Prime Minister** and other ministers (they hold office during his pleasure) and the **Attorney General**.
- He appoints the CAG, Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners, the Chairman and members of the UPSC, Governors, and members of the Finance Commission.
- He can seek information from the Prime Minister on administration and legislative proposals (Article 78) and can ask that a decision taken by one minister be put before the council.
- He administers Union Territories through administrators and can declare Scheduled Areas.

## 2. Legislative

He is an integral part of Parliament. He **summons and prorogues** Parliament and **dissolves** the Lok Sabha (Article 85), summons a joint sitting, addresses Parliament at the first session after each general election and the first of each year, and sends messages. He nominates **12** members to the Rajya Sabha (literature, science, art, social service). Laxmikanth also lists two Anglo-Indian nominees to the Lok Sabha: that provision was ended by the 104th Amendment (2019; verify). He decides disqualification of members in consultation with the Election Commission (Article 103: the EC's opinion binds the President; verify). His prior recommendation is needed to introduce certain Bills (expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, new States or boundary changes). He has the **assent, veto** and **ordinance** powers (module 7), and he makes regulations for Union Territories such as Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep and Ladakh (and Puducherry when its Assembly is suspended or dissolved).

## 3. Financial

Money Bills can be introduced only with his recommendation; he causes the **annual financial statement** (Union Budget) to be laid (Article 112); no demand for a grant can be made without his recommendation; he can make advances from the **Contingency Fund**; and he constitutes the **Finance Commission** every five years. (See GS-180.)

## 4. Judicial

He appoints the Chief Justice and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, can seek the Supreme Court's **advice** on law or fact (Article 143; not binding; see GS-186), and has the **pardoning power** (Article 72; module 8).

## 5. Diplomatic

Treaties and agreements are negotiated on his behalf and are subject to Parliament's approval as Laxmikanth records; he represents India in international affairs and sends and receives ambassadors and high commissioners.

## 6. Military

He is the **supreme commander** of the defence forces, appoints the chiefs of the Army, Navy and Air Force, and (as the source says) can declare war or conclude peace subject to Parliament's approval.

## 7. Emergency

Three: **National Emergency** (Article 352), **President's Rule** (Articles 356 and 365) and **Financial Emergency** (Article 360). The details belong to the emergency topic; here only learn the Articles.

### 7. The veto powers and the ordinance power (Article 123)

*Objective:* Distinguish absolute, suspensive and pocket veto with examples, and state the four limits and the life of an ordinance under Article 123.

## Four veto types, three in India

When Parliament passes a Bill, the President has three choices under **Article 111** (assent, withhold assent, return the Bill if it is not a Money Bill). Laxmikanth classifies executive vetoes as absolute, qualified, suspensive and pocket. India has **absolute, suspensive and pocket**; the **qualified** veto (the legislature overrides it by a higher majority) belongs to the US President.

| Veto | What happens | Example (Laxmikanth) | Money Bill? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absolute | Assent withheld; the Bill ends | 1954, PEPSU Appropriation Bill (Prasad); 1991, MPs' Salary Amendment Bill (Venkataraman) | Can be withheld |
| Suspensive | Bill returned; if re-passed (with or without amendments) by an **ordinary** majority, assent is **obligatory** | Used on ordinary Bills | Cannot be returned |
| Pocket | President takes no action; the Constitution sets no time limit | 1986, Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill (Zail Singh) | Not relevant, normally assented |

Absolute veto is usually seen on **private members' Bills** and when a new cabinet advises against assent to a Bill passed under its predecessor. The Indian pocket is called **bigger** than the American because the US President must return a Bill within 10 days. **Constitutional amendment Bills**: the President cannot veto; assent is obligatory after the 24th Amendment (1971). Veto over State Bills (Articles 200, 201) is in the master notes.

**Update to verify:** in 2025 the Supreme Court dealt with whether time limits can be read into the Governor's and the President's assent powers and a presidential reference followed. Reported details to verify: *State of Tamil Nadu v Governor* (8 April 2025) on Governors' delay, and the Court's opinion on the Article 143 reference (20 November 2025), reported to hold that courts cannot fix judicial timelines and that there is no 'deemed assent'. Check the outcome from a current source before stating a rule.

## Ordinance-making power (Article 123)

While Parliament is **not in session**, the President may promulgate an **ordinance** with the same force as an Act. It is a temporary law. Four limits (Laxmikanth):

1. Only when **both Houses are not in session or either one is not**; an ordinance made while both are in session is void.
2. Only when the President is satisfied that **immediate action is necessary**. In Cooper (1970) the Supreme Court said the satisfaction can be questioned for malafide; the 38th Amendment (1975) made it final, and the 44th Amendment (1978) deleted that, so it is justiciable on the ground of malafide.
3. **Co-extensive** with Parliament's law-making power except for duration: only on subjects Parliament can legislate on; it **cannot abridge Fundamental Rights**, and cannot amend the Constitution.
4. It must be **laid before both Houses** on reassembly and ceases to operate **six weeks** after reassembly (or earlier if both Houses disapprove). Maximum life is **six months plus six weeks**. Acts completed under it stay valid.

It **can** be retrospective, can amend or repeal an Act or another ordinance, and can amend a tax law. The President acts only on the **advice** of the council of ministers, and may withdraw it any time. D. C. Wadhwa (1987) held that repeated re-promulgation without placing it before the legislature violates the Constitution. Krishna Kumar Singh (2017) is in GS-179.

### 8. The pardoning power (Article 72)

*Objective:* Define the five forms of clemency, state where the President's power applies, and list the principles laid down by the Supreme Court.

## What Article 72 covers

The President can grant pardons to persons tried and convicted in all cases where the punishment or sentence is (1) for an offence against a **Union law**; (2) by a **court martial**; or (3) a **sentence of death**. The power is **executive**, independent of the judiciary, but the President does **not** sit as a court of appeal. Its twin aims are to leave the door open for correcting judicial errors and to give relief from a sentence the President thinks unduly harsh.

## The five forms (learn with one example each)

- **Pardon**: removes the sentence and the conviction and absolves the convict from all sentences, punishments and disqualifications.
- **Commutation**: a lighter form of punishment in place of a heavier one (death to rigorous imprisonment, rigorous to simple).
- **Remission**: reduces the period of the sentence without changing its character (two years rigorous to one year rigorous).
- **Respite**: a lesser sentence due to a special fact such as physical disability or pregnancy.
- **Reprieve**: a temporary **stay** of execution, especially of death, to give time to seek pardon or commutation.

## The Governor (Article 161)

The Governor has a similar power for offences against **State law**, with two differences: he **cannot pardon court-martial sentences** and he **cannot pardon a death sentence** (even under State law). But he can suspend, remit or commute a death sentence: the power over those three is concurrent.

## Principles laid down by the Supreme Court (Laxmikanth)

1. The petitioner has no right to an oral hearing.
2. The President may re-examine the evidence and take a view different from the court.
3. The power is exercised on the **advice of the Union cabinet**.
4. The President need not give reasons.
5. Relief may be given from a sentence thought unduly harsh or from an evident mistake.
6. The Supreme Court need not lay down guidelines.
7. Judicial review is available only where the decision is **arbitrary, irrational, mala fide or discriminatory** (limited review).
8. If a mercy petition was rejected earlier, a second petition cannot obtain a stay.

### 9. Constitutional position, discretion and the comparison with the US President

*Objective:* Explain Article 74 with the 42nd and 44th Amendments, list the situations of "situational discretion", and compare the Indian and US Presidents.

## The rule: aid and advice

India has a parliamentary system, so the President is a **nominal executive**; the real executive is the council of ministers headed by the Prime Minister. Dr. Ambedkar (quoted by Laxmikanth) said the President is head of the State but not of the Executive, a symbol and a ceremonial device by which the nation's decisions are made known, and generally bound by the ministers' advice. Three Articles:

- **Article 53**: executive power of the Union is vested in the President, exercised directly or through subordinate officers.
- **Article 74**: a council of ministers with the Prime Minister at the head shall **aid and advise** the President, who **shall act in accordance with such advice**.
- **Article 75**: the council is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**, the foundation of the parliamentary system.

**42nd Amendment (1976):** made the President **bound** by the advice (there was no such specific provision in the original Article 74). **44th Amendment (1978):** he may require the council to **reconsider** its advice once, but must then act on the reconsidered advice. Example: in 1997 President K. R. Narayanan returned a cabinet recommendation of President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh and it was dropped; in 1998 he returned one on Bihar, which the cabinet repeated in 1999 (Laxmikanth).

## Discretion: none constitutional, some situational

Laxmikanth says the President has **no constitutional discretion** but some **situational discretion**: (i) appointing the Prime Minister when no party has a clear majority or the sitting PM dies and there is no obvious successor; (ii) dismissing the council of ministers when it cannot prove confidence in the Lok Sabha; (iii) dissolving the Lok Sabha if the council has lost its majority. NCERT adds the return of advice, the delay on Bills (informal pocket veto) and the choice of PM in a hung House (Narayanan, 1998). Discretion widens when governments are unstable.

## Indian versus US President

| Point | India | USA |
|---|---|---|
| System | Parliamentary; President nominal head of State | Presidential; head of State and of government |
| Real executive | PM and council of ministers, responsible to Lok Sabha | The President himself; cabinet advises |
| Election | Indirect, electoral college, STV | Indirect through an electoral college chosen by popular vote in States (verify) |
| Re-election | Any number of times | Not more than twice (Laxmikanth) |
| Veto | Absolute, suspensive (ordinary majority), pocket; no time limit | Qualified veto overridden by a higher majority; must return a Bill within 10 days (Laxmikanth) |
| Removal | Impeachment by two-thirds of total membership of each House | Impeachment by the House, trial in the Senate (verify) |
| Dismissal of ministers | Cannot dismiss while they command the majority | Can dismiss any secretary at any time (Ambedkar) |
| Law without the legislature | Ordinance (Article 123) | Executive orders; no ordinance power in the Indian sense (verify) |

### Notes: Process: the impeachment and the election count in one view

- Impeachment: notice by one-fourth of members -> 14 days -> resolution by two-thirds of total membership -> second House investigates (the President may appear) -> two-thirds of total membership -> removal from the date of passing.
- Election: nomination (50 + 50, deposit ₹15,000) -> voting by secret ballot with preferences -> value of votes -> quota (valid votes / 2, plus one) -> first-preference count -> eliminate lowest and transfer -> repeat -> winner with quota -> disputes to the Supreme Court.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Election of the President (elected members only) versus impeachment (all members of Parliament, including nominated, but no State legislators).
- Vice-President acting as President (vacancy; only until a new election; no gap when the term simply expires) versus the outgoing President continuing in office.
- President's ordinance power (Article 123) versus Governor's (Article 213, outside the extract).
