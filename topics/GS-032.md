## GS-032: Revolt of 1857: causes, centres, consequences

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Vocabulary: sepoy, mutiny, revolt, taluqdar, paramountcy

## Words you will meet

- **Sepoy**: from the Indian word *sipahi*, a soldier; here an Indian soldier of the Company's army (NCERT).
- **Mutiny**: when soldiers as a group disobey their officers (NCERT). A mutiny is a military event.
- **Revolt, rebellion, uprising**: a wider rising that includes civilians. Whether 1857 was a mutiny or something larger is the central debate of module 8.
- **Zamindar and taluqdar**: a zamindar collects revenue from peasants and pays the state; a taluqdar is a large landholder, a term used for the landed magnates of Awadh.
- **Doctrine of Lapse**: Dalhousie's policy that a state whose ruler died without a male heir passed to the Company (NCERT).
- **Paramountcy**: the claim that the Company's authority was supreme over Indian states (NCERT).
- **Crown, Secretary of State, Viceroy**: the British monarch; the Cabinet minister responsible for India after 1858; and the Governor-General's new title as the Crown's personal representative (NCERT).
- **Cantonment**: a military station where troops were quartered (general knowledge).

Keep this list open while you read modules 3 to 9.

### 1. Orientation: the revolt in one page

*Objective:* State what happened in 1857, name the four questions the topic asks and give five dated anchors.

## The event in one paragraph

On 10 May 1857 Indian soldiers at Meerut freed imprisoned comrades, killed officers and rode to Delhi, where they proclaimed the aged Bahadur Shah Zafar as their leader. Within weeks regiments from the Punjab to Bihar mutinied and civilians rose in Awadh, Rohilkhand, the Doab, Bundelkhand and parts of central India. The Company needed about two years to regain control (by the end of 1859) and then Company rule itself was abolished and the Crown took over.

## Four questions, four layers

1. **Why** did it happen? Political, economic, social-religious, administrative and military causes, with the greased cartridge as a spark (modules 2 to 4).
2. **How** did it spread and who led it? (modules 5 and 6)
3. **Why did it fail**, and how was it put down? (module 7)
4. **What was it, and what followed?** The debate over its character, and the changes of 1858 onwards (modules 8 and 9).

## What to call it

The names carry arguments. British writers such as Seeley called it a 'sepoy mutiny'; Savarkar called it the first war of Indian independence; NCERT calls it a mutiny that became a popular uprising. This lesson uses the neutral label *Revolt of 1857* and treats the naming as an interpretive question, not a fact to memorise.

## Five anchors

- 8 April 1857: Mangal Pande hanged at Barrackpore.
- 10 May 1857: the Meerut outbreak.
- 20 September 1857: the British retake Delhi.
- 2 August 1858: the Act for the Better Government of India.
- 1 November 1858: the Queen's Proclamation.

### 2. Foundation: the sepoy army and the countryside before 1857

*Objective:* Explain who the sepoys were, why their service conditions clashed with their beliefs and how earlier mutinies prepared 1857.

## A peasant army

The Mughal army relied on cavalry and local armed peasants. In the eighteenth century successor states such as Awadh and Banaras began recruiting peasants as trained soldiers, and the Company copied the method for its **sepoy army**. As warfare changed after the 1820s the infantry became central. In the early nineteenth century the British developed a uniform military culture with European-style drill and discipline, which, NCERT says, often ignored caste and community feelings. Spectrum's phrase for the sepoy is a 'peasant in uniform': he belonged to the countryside and shared its grievances. NCERT adds that the anger of peasants quickly spread to sepoys whose families lived in the villages.

## Service conditions against belief

- To the religious Hindu of the time, crossing the sea meant loss of caste. In 1824 sepoys were ordered to go to Burma by sea; NCERT says they refused the sea route though they would go by land and were severely punished. In 1856 Canning's **General Service Enlistment Act** required all new recruits to the Bengal army to serve wherever the government needed them.
- Pay and allowances were poor compared with British soldiers; the foreign-service allowance (bhatta) was refused for service in Sindh and the Punjab.
- Racial discrimination in promotion and privileges; restrictions on caste and sectarian marks; rumours of proselytising chaplains.
- The annexation of Awadh, home of many sepoys, angered them further.

## Earlier mutinies

Spectrum lists sporadic military revolts before 1857: Bengal (1764), **Vellore (1806)**, the **47th Native Infantry (1824)**, the Grenadier Company in Assam (1825), Sholapur (1838) and mutinies of 1844, 1849, 1850 and 1852. Vellore began when a leather cockade replaced the turban. None spread beyond its locality and all were crushed, often with executions and disbanded regiments. Spectrum's separate chapter prints the Barrackpore date as 1824 (and the Assam revolt as 1825); the chapter on 1857 prints 1825 for Barrackpore, which is a slip. NCERT dates the refusal to go to Burma to 1824.

For the tribal and peasant risings that make up the wider pre-1857 background, see GS-031; for the political context see GS-024 module 9 (and its chain to the 63 per cent of territory and 78 per cent of population that NCERT says the Company ruled directly by 1857).


### 3. Core: political and economic causes

*Objective:* Explain how annexation, the treatment of rulers and Awadh, and the colonial economy created discontent.

## Political causes

Spectrum says the Company's policies of 'effective control', subsidiary alliance and the Doctrine of Lapse, with broken pledges, lowered its prestige and created suspicion among almost all rulers. NCERT adds the theme that since the mid-eighteenth century nawabs and rajas had lost power in stages: Residents in their courts, disbanded armies, lost revenue and territory.

- **Doctrine of Lapse**: Satara (1848), Sambalpur (1850), Udaipur (1852, a small state in present Chhattisgarh; see GS-024), Nagpur (1853) and Jhansi (1854). Rani Lakshmibai's adopted son was refused recognition; Nana Saheb, adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II, was refused his father's pension.
- **Awadh**: put under a subsidiary alliance in 1801 and annexed in 1856, Dalhousie declaring the territory misgoverned (NCERT). Spectrum says 21,000 taluqdars lost their estates and were left without income; they used the sepoy revolt to try to regain them. Standard accounts, not in the extracts (verify), add that the 1856 summary revenue settlement treated the taluqdars as outsiders with no right in the land, they were disarmed and their forts pulled down, and the Company preferred to take revenue directly from the peasants.
- **The Mughal house**: the Mughal name was removed from the Company's coins. In 1849 Dalhousie announced that the family would leave the Red Fort after Bahadur Shah's death, and in 1856 Canning decided that Bahadur Shah would be the last king; later heirs would be only princes (NCERT). Spectrum adds that after Prince Faqiruddin's death in 1856 the next heir was told to give up the title and palaces.

## Economic causes

- **Peasants**: heavy revenue and rigid collection pushed many into debt; moneylenders and traders emerged as the new landlords, evicting defaulters. NCERT and Spectrum agree on this; GS-031 module 2 explains the mechanism.
- **Artisans**: annexation cut off patronage from native rulers and nobles, and policy favoured British goods. Spectrum argues that one-way free trade, with high duties on Indian goods and low duties on British ones, ended Indian textile exports by the mid-nineteenth century and was not matched by modern industry. Treat this as Spectrum's argument; the economic-drain debate is taught later (GS-029 land revenue on Day 14 and GS-030 economic drain and deindustrialisation on Day 15, not yet written).
- **Zamindars**: frequent use of the legal order *quo warranto* forfeited their land rights.
- **Result**: ruined crafts increased pressure on agriculture, and Spectrum describes a lopsided, pauperising development.

## What these causes share

Each cause hit a different group: rulers lost power, taluqdars and zamindars lost status and land, peasants and artisans lost income, and sepoys came from the same countryside. A cause hits a person; a revolt needs a shared sense of a common enemy. NCERT notes that such a situation develops only when people organise, communicate and gain confidence; module 5 shows how Bahadur Shah's blessing gave that confidence.


### 4. Core: social-religious, administrative and military causes, and the spark

*Objective:* Show how religious fears, corruption and military grievances combined, and explain why the greased cartridge was a spark, not the cause.

## Social-religious causes

NCERT says the British believed Indian society needed reform, passed laws against sati and for widow remarriage and promoted English education. After 1830 missionaries were allowed to function freely and to own land, and in 1850 a law allowed converts to inherit ancestral property. Many Indians began to feel that their religion and social customs were under attack. Spectrum adds the taxing of mosque and temple lands and the racial arrogance of British administrators. Spectrum names the property law the 'Religious Disabilities Act, 1856'; NCERT dates it 1850, and standard accounts call it the Caste Disabilities Removal Act of 1850 (verify), so teach 1850 and treat Spectrum's year as a slip.

## Administrative causes and outside events

Spectrum stresses corruption among police, petty officials and lower courts and the alien character of rule, a 'foreign and absentee sovereignty'. It also argues that British setbacks in the First Afghan War (1838-42), the Punjab Wars (1845-49) and the Crimean War (1854-56) suggested that the British could be defeated.

## The sepoys' immediate grievances

The recruiting rules of module 2, the refusal of foreign-service allowance in Sindh and the Punjab, racial discrimination and the annexation of Awadh formed the military cause. The sepoys also shared the countryside's social, religious and economic grievances.

## The spark: the greased cartridge

The new **Enfield rifle** came with cartridges whose paper wrapper had to be bitten before loading. Reports spread that the grease contained beef and pig fat: the cow is sacred to Hindus and the pig is taboo for Muslims. Spectrum adds a rumour of bone dust mixed in flour (atta). The army administration did nothing to allay the fears, so the sepoys felt their religion was in grave danger.

NCERT includes two voices that show how the fear travelled. Subedar Sitaram Pande, who helped the British, wrote that the seizure of Oudh had filled the sepoys with distrust, and that when men sent to learn the new drill reported the cartridge rumour, 'interested parties' told them the English meant to turn all into Christians. Vishnubhatt Godse's travel memoir records sepoys telling travellers of a plan, circulated from Meerut, for a war of religion.

## Spark and fuel

Spectrum's judgement is the one to remember: the greased cartridge did not create a new cause of discontent but supplied the *occasion* for the simmering discontent to come into the open. Without the fuel of modules 2 and 3 a rumour would have passed off; Pande's colonel expected exactly that. The distinction is the core of a good causes answer.


### 5. Core: from Barrackpore to Delhi and the spread

*Objective:* Narrate the outbreak from February to May 1857 and explain why Bahadur Shah's proclamation changed the revolt.

## The road to Meerut

Even before Meerut there were rumblings. In February 1857 the 19th Native Infantry at Berhampore refused the new rifle and was disbanded in March. At Barrackpore a young sepoy of the 34th Native Infantry, **Mangal Pande**, attacked his officers on 29 March 1857 (standard, verify; the attack and the hanging are two dates) and fired at the sergeant major; he was overpowered and hanged on 8 April and his regiment was disbanded in May. On 3 May the 7th Awadh Regiment defied its officers and met the same fate. At Meerut on 24 April 90 men of the 3rd Native Cavalry refused the greased cartridges; on 9 May 85 of them were dismissed, sentenced to ten years' imprisonment and put in fetters.

## 10 May 1857

The next day the soldiers at Meerut released their comrades, killed officers, set fire to British property and rode through the night to Delhi, some 58 km away. Delhi's regiments joined them, killed European officers (including the political agent Simon Fraser) and seized the city; Lieutenant Willoughby resisted at the magazine but was overcome. The rebels forced their way into the palace and proclaimed Bahadur Shah Zafar their leader, though NCERT says the emperor was unwilling at first and Spectrum speaks of vacillation.

## Why that proclamation mattered

- It gave the rising a political centre. Spectrum says it transformed a mutiny of soldiers into a revolutionary war and that Indian chiefs who joined hastened to proclaim loyalty to the Mughal emperor.
- NCERT explains the logic: many chiefs controlled territory on behalf of the Mughals, so they felt that if the Mughal emperor could rule again, they too could rule their own lands under Mughal authority.
- Bahadur Shah wrote to all chiefs and rulers to form a confederacy of Indian states against the British.

## Spread

After the fall of Delhi, NCERT says there was almost a week without a rising because news travelled slowly; then regiment after regiment mutinied and made for nodal points such as Delhi, Kanpur and Lucknow, and townspeople and villagers rose around local leaders. Spectrum lists Awadh, Rohilkhand, the Doab, Bundelkhand, central India, large parts of Bihar and east Punjab as shaking off British authority.

## The civilian rising

The rising was accompanied by a rebellion of the civil population, especially in the north-western provinces and Awadh. Peasants and petty zamindars attacked the moneylenders and landlords who had displaced them, burned debt records and attacked law courts, tehsil offices, revenue records and police stations. By one estimate Spectrum quotes, over 1,00,000 of about 1,50,000 men who died fighting the British in Awadh were civilians. Spectrum's chapter on resistance adds that peasant participation was active only in some areas, mainly western Uttar Pradesh, and often alongside local feudal leaders.


### 6. Core: centres and leaders

*Objective:* Match each storm centre with its leader, the local reason for revolt and the British opponent.

Each centre had a leader with a local motive; the table in the master notes holds the full roll of names, opponents and fates. This module shows the pattern.

## Delhi

Nominal leadership belonged to Bahadur Shah, but real command lay with a court of soldiers headed by **General Bakht Khan**, who had led the Bareilly troops to Delhi. The court had ten members, six from the army and four from civil departments, and ran the state in the emperor's name. Spectrum calls the old, weak emperor perhaps the weakest link in the leadership, damaging the revolt at its nerve centre.

## Kanpur and Lucknow

At Kanpur the natural choice was **Nana Saheb**, refused his title and pension, who expelled the English, proclaimed himself Peshwa and acknowledged Bahadur Shah as emperor. General Wheeler surrendered on 27 June 1857. At Lucknow the revolt broke out on 4 June 1857; **Begum Hazrat Mahal** took the reins, her son **Birjis Qadr** was proclaimed nawab and offices were shared equally by Hindus and Muslims. The British Residency was besieged and Henry Lawrence killed; Spectrum says Lucknow was finally recovered in March 1858 with guerrilla activity continuing until September.

## Other centres

- **Bareilly**: Khan Bahadur, a descendant of Rohilkhand's rulers, raised an army of 40,000.
- **Bihar**: **Kunwar Singh**, a zamindar of Jagdishpur about 80 in 1857 (born c.1777, verify), joined the sepoys at Arrah after losing his estates.
- **Faizabad and Lucknow**: **Maulvi Ahmadullah**, a native of Madras, a respected leader in Awadh.
- **Jhansi and Gwalior**: **Rani Laxmibai**, whose adopted son was refused succession, driven out of Jhansi and then joined by **Tantia Tope**; they were hailed at Gwalior but the Scindia sided with the English.
- **Baghpat**: **Shah Mal** organised the headmen of 84 villages, cut bridges and roads that symbolised British rule, and was killed in July 1857.
- **Ramgarh (Mandla)**: NCERT names Rani Avantibai Lodhi, who raised an army of four thousand.

## The pattern

Most leaders were dispossessed rulers, landholders or soldiers with personal grievances. Spectrum later uses exactly this to argue that the revolt lacked a common programme (module 8), while NCERT stresses the many new leaders and the nodal centres around which rebels gathered. For each leader, learn the grievance and the place before you learn the fate.


### 7. Core: suppression and why the revolt failed

*Objective:* Narrate how the British regained control and explain the reasons for failure with their limits.

## Suppression

The Company brought reinforcements from England and passed new laws that let rebels be convicted easily (NCERT). The British retook **Delhi on 20 September 1857** after bitter fighting; the siege leader John Nicholson was mortally wounded. Bahadur Shah was captured and the princes were shot (NCERT's illustration names Captain Hodson; Spectrum spells the name 'Hudson', which looks like a slip). The emperor was exiled to Rangoon in October 1858 and died in November 1862. Sir Colin Campbell took Kanpur on 6 December 1857; Lucknow was recovered in March 1858; the Rani of Jhansi died in battle in June 1858 and Gwalior was retaken in June 1858; Tantia Tope was captured and executed in April 1859. By the end of 1859 British authority was fully re-established.

The British also used inducements. They announced rewards for loyal landholders and promised that rebels who had not killed Europeans would be safe if they submitted (NCERT). Spectrum says even the Awadh taluqdars backed off once promises of land restitution were spelt out. Defeats of rebel forces also led to desertions, NCERT adds.

## Why it failed

Spectrum gives these reasons, which you should treat as families.

1. **Limited reach.** The east, south and west were largely unaffected, probably because earlier risings there had been crushed. By one estimate not more than a quarter of the area and a tenth of the population were affected.
2. **Support from key groups.** Big zamindars acted as 'break-waters'; moneylenders and merchants favoured British protection; educated Indians saw the revolt as backward-looking; Scindia, Holkar, Patiala and other Sikh chiefs, Sindh rulers and Kashmir stayed out or helped the British.
3. **Poor arms and equipment.** Swords and spears against the Enfield rifle; the telegraph kept the commander-in-chief informed.
4. **No coordination.** The rebel leaders were no match in generalship for Lawrence, Nicholson, Outram or Havelock.
5. **No unified ideology.** The rebels lacked a clear programme or alternative order, and modern nationalism did not yet exist.

## What failure does not mean

Spectrum insists that Hindu-Muslim cooperation held throughout: rebels acknowledged a Muslim emperor, cow slaughter was banned wherever the rebels succeeded, and both communities were well represented in leadership. Maulana Azad, cited there, saw unity and loyalty to the Mughal crown as the two clear facts. So disunity between communities is not among the reasons for failure. NCERT adds a human cost: hundreds of sepoys, rebels, nawabs and rajas were tried and hanged.


### 8. Core: the character of the revolt, mutiny or war of independence?

*Objective:* Compare the main historians' views and build a balanced position on whether 1857 was a mutiny, a feudal uprising or a first war of independence.

## The views in Spectrum

Spectrum states that views differ and gives these positions; learn each with its label.

- **Mere sepoy mutiny**: Seeley (a selfish, unpatriotic mutiny with no native leadership or popular support) and Lawrence.
- **A military outbreak exploited by princes and landlords**: K. Datta, who says it was never all-Indian in character, localised, restricted and poorly organised.
- **A planned war of national independence**: V.D. Savarkar, whose book of that title (early twentieth century) called it the first war of Indian independence inspired by the ideal of self-rule.
- **From religion to independence**: S.N. Sen, for whom it began as a fight for religion and ended as a war of independence.
- **Neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence**: R.C. Majumdar, citing the large unaffected areas and the sections that did not take part.
- **Feudal elements**: Nehru saw an essentially feudal uprising with some nationalist elements; M.N. Roy saw feudalism's last stand against commercial capitalism; R.P. Dutt acknowledged peasant opposition to foreign domination but also a defence of the old feudal order; the Marxist formula called it a soldier-peasant democratic combine against foreign and feudal bondage, which Spectrum questions because the leaders came from feudal backgrounds.
- **Between the extremes**: S.B. Chaudhuri saw the first combined attempt of many classes to challenge a foreign power; Tara Chand argued that it was not inspired by nationalism, since the leaders and followers moved on personal loyalties; Spear saw passions centred on grievances; Wolpert called it far more than a mutiny and much less than a first war of independence.

Spectrum dismisses two older readings: a religious war of fanatics against Christians (Rees) and a conflict of civilisation against barbarism (Holmes).

## Testing the claims

Evidence for a *national* character: Bahadur Shah as a common symbol; Hindu-Muslim cooperation; widespread civilian participation; the same enemy in view. Evidence against: limited area; many rulers and classes stayed loyal; each leader had a personal cause; no common programme or concept of nationhood. Sen's point is that national revolutions are the work of minorities with or without mass support.

Spectrum's own conclusion is that it was not quite a first war of independence but had seeds of nationalism and anti-imperialism. For 'first', note that some historians say earlier uprisings were equally serious (Spectrum ch7; for the earlier risings themselves see GS-031 modules 3-5). For 'war of independence', note that the concept of a common nationhood was not inherent to the revolt.

## How to take a position

Define the claim ('national', 'first', 'war'), set evidence for and against, name two historians on each side, and end with a qualified judgement such as 'a revolt with seeds of nationalism but not yet a national movement'. Spectrum's summary notes that the revolt later helped the sense of belonging to one country. The Subaltern and other later schools are not in the extracts and are not taught here.


### 9. Core: consequences and changes in British policy

*Objective:* Describe the Act of 1858, the Queen's Proclamation, army reorganisation and the wider shift in policy and attitudes.

## Government: the Crown takes over

Before the revolt was fully suppressed, Parliament passed an Act for the Better Government of India (**2 August 1858**). It declared Queen Victoria sovereign of British India, abolished Company rule and created a Secretary of State for India, a Cabinet member, advised by a council (NCERT calls it the India Council). Lord Canning announced the change at an Allahabad durbar in the **Queen's Proclamation of 1 November 1858**. Spectrum says the proclamation gave the Governor-General the additional title of Viceroy, while NCERT links the title to the Act; teach 'Viceroy' as the Crown's personal representative from 1858 and note the sources differ on the instrument.

## The promises

According to Spectrum and NCERT: no more annexations and respect for the dignity and rights of princes, including the right to pass kingdoms to adopted sons; princes accepted the Crown's paramountcy; freedom of religion; equal and impartial protection of law and equal opportunity in service irrespective of race or creed; and respect for old rights, customs and practices. The reality: the Indian Civil Service Act of 1861 followed, but Spectrum says the examination rules kept the higher services a close preserve of the British.

## The army

The army that had been at the forefront was thoroughly reorganised under the idea of 'division and counterpoise'. The number of Indian soldiers was drastically reduced and European soldiers increased; units were created on caste, community and region; recruits came from the 'martial races' of the Punjab, Nepal and the north-west frontier who had stayed loyal, and NCERT names Gurkhas, Sikhs and Pathans in place of recruits from Awadh, Bihar, central and south India. Efforts were made to keep the army away from civilians. The Army Amalgamation Scheme of 1861 moved European troops of the Company to the Crown; most Indian artillery was abolished; higher posts were reserved for Europeans. The 'White Mutiny' (Spectrum, undated; commonly placed in 1859, verify) was unrest among Company European troops who resented transfer to the Crown, the loss of batta and the process.

## Wider policy and attitudes

- Spectrum says the earlier reforming zeal faded into a 'conservative brand of liberalism' that avoided interference with Indian society, and that divide and rule became systematic.
- NCERT says landlords and zamindars were protected in their rights, and that the property of many Muslims was confiscated and Muslims were treated with suspicion because the British blamed them; Spectrum's chapter on resistance adds that land was restored to Awadh's taluqdars, peasants' occupancy interests were neglected and extra cesses were imposed on peasants in some regions.
- Racial hatred and suspicion were, Spectrum says, the worst legacy, and the educated middle class's frustration fed modern nationalism.

## Significance

For the British the revolt exposed defects in the army and administration which they corrected. For Indians it brought genuine grievances into the open, showed that primitive arms could not match modern weapons and, Spectrum argues, turned the educated towards orderly rather than violent methods while leaving traditions of local resistance.


### 10. Exam: PYQs, answer frames and the Awadh facts

*Objective:* Know how the two PYQs map onto the lesson and use ready frames for Prelims statements and Mains answers.

## The two PYQs and what they test

The 2019 Mains question asks whether 1857 was the culmination of the recurrent local rebellions of the previous hundred years. The 2026 Prelims question tests British policy in Awadh after the 1856 annexation. Their analysis is in the PYQ notes; here is how to prepare.

## A frame for the 'culmination' question

Recap: continuity in grievances and the sepoy mutinies; discontinuity in localism against 1857's reach and Delhi's symbolic centre; verdict, a culmination of grievances, not of one tradition. Full frame: GS-031 module 9.

## The sepoy-mutiny chain (new from module 2)

Bengal 1764; Vellore 1806 (leather cockade replacing the turban); the 47th Native Infantry 1824 (the sea voyage to Burma); the Assam Grenadier Company 1825; Sholapur 1838; units in 1844, 1849, 1850 and 1852. Spectrum says none spread beyond its locality and all were crushed, often with executions and disbanded regiments. Use the chain as army-specific continuity: religion and status grievances recurred, but earlier mutinies stayed local, while 1857 joined sepoys, princes, taluqdars and peasants.

## Awadh checklist for statement questions

- The annexation (1856) is justified by 'misgovernment' (NCERT); a subsidiary alliance came first (1801).
- Spectrum: 21,000 taluqdars lose estates; later, after the revolt, land is restored to taluqdars.
- Facts outside the extracts (verify): the 1856 summary settlement assumed the taluqdars had no secure right and were outsiders; the taluqdars were disarmed and forts demolished; the Company preferred direct dealing with peasants. A statement saying they were dispossessed *but allowed to keep arms and forts* conflicts with the disarmament reported in standard accounts.

## Mains frames

- *Causes (15 marks):* four layers of fuel, then the spark; mention that no single cause is enough.
- *Character (15 marks):* define, evidence for and against, two historians each side, qualified judgement.
- *Consequences (10 or 15 marks):* governance, princes, army, services, social and economic policy, and the sting of racial attitudes.

## Dates drill

Drill the eight dates: 8 April, 10 May, 4 June (Lucknow), 27 June (Kanpur), 20 September 1857 (Delhi), 2 August and 1 November 1858, and April 1859 (Tantia Tope). Know that the Company's rule ended in 1858 even though Spectrum's chapter on the English company gives 1873 in one place (GS-025); that year refers to the Act dissolving the Company as a body (effective 1874, verify).

## Common traps

Calling the cartridge the cause; naming Kunwar Singh at Kanpur; treating Awadh as a case of lapse; and writing 'Hindu-Muslim disunity' as a reason for failure.

- Government: Company rule abolished; Crown sovereign; Secretary of State with council; Viceroy.

### Notes: Why the revolt failed: cause and effect

- Limited area and classes, so the British could concentrate forces and recover Delhi, Kanpur and Lucknow in turn.
- Loyal princes and landlords, so supplies, intelligence and Sikh and Gurkha troops reached the British.
- Weak arms and the telegraph, so superior British communication and firepower.
- No central command or programme, so each leader fought alone and was defeated separately.

### Notes: Frequently confused

- Mutiny (soldiers) against revolt (also civilians).
- Act of 1858 (structure of government) against Queen's Proclamation (promises).
- Governor-General against Viceroy; Company against Crown.
- Doctrine of Lapse (Jhansi, Nagpur, Satara) against annexation for misgovernment (Awadh).
- Nana Saheb (Kanpur) against Kunwar Singh (Bihar) against Khan Bahadur (Bareilly).
- Spark (cartridge) against causes (political, economic and others).

### Core concepts

- The Revolt of 1857 combined sepoy grievances with the wider political, economic, social-religious and administrative discontent of many groups.
- The greased cartridge was the occasion, not the cause.
- Bahadur Shah Zafar's proclamation at Delhi gave the rising a political centre; leaders and motives were local.
- It failed through limited spread, loyal groups, weak arms, no coordination or ideology.
- After 1858 the Crown ruled, the army was reorganised and policy turned conservative; its character remains debated.
