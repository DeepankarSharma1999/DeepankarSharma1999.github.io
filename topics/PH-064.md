## PH-064: Pulse and material dispersion in fibres

Physics Paper I · Physics · Polarisation, fibres, lasers

### Bridge: Snell's law, total internal reflection, critical angle and the small-difference expansion

**Recap (taught in PH-042 modules 5 to 7).** Snell's law is n1 sin(i) = n2 sin(r), angles measured from the **normal**. Going from a denser to a rarer medium, the critical angle is **sin(phi_c) = n2/n1**, and beyond it there is total internal reflection (TIR). Example: glass n1 = 1.5 to air gives phi_c = 41.8 degrees. See PH-042 module 7 for the derivation and the picture.

**New for fibres: two angle conventions.** In a fibre we also use the angle theta that a ray makes with the **axis**. The interface is parallel to the axis, so theta = 90 degrees minus the angle from the normal, and sin(theta) = cos(phi). Always check which angle a question gives (Brij Lal, PDF pp. 676 to 677).

**Small-difference habit.** If two indices are nearly equal, define Delta = (n1 - n2)/n1, a small number (0.01 to 0.05). Then n2 = n1(1 - Delta) and 1/(1 - Delta) = 1 + Delta + Delta^2 + ..., which is about 1 + Delta. Check: Delta = 0.0467 gives 1/(1 - Delta) = 1.0490 against the approximation 1.0467.

### Bridge: Units and scale: ns, ps, nm, km, bit rate and why dispersion is 'per km'

Fibre pulses are billionths of a second and fibres are kilometres long, so the prefixes matter.

- 1 nanosecond (ns) = 10^-9 s and 1 picosecond (ps) = 10^-12 s, so 1 ns = 1000 ps.
- 1 nanometre (nm) = 10^-9 m and 1 micrometre (micron, um) = 10^-6 m = 1000 nm. Light for fibres is 850, 1310 or 1550 nm (0.85, 1.31, 1.55 um; the extract lists 850, 1300 and 1550 nm).
- 1 kilometre (km) = 10^3 m.

**Dispersion per length.** A pulse spreads more the farther it travels, so the spread is quoted per unit length. Pulse dispersion is in **ns/km** (time per distance). Material dispersion is quoted as D in **ps/(nm km)**: picoseconds of spreading per nanometre of source spectral width per kilometre of fibre. Converting: 1 ps/(nm km) = 10^-12 s/(10^-9 m x 10^3 m) = 10^-6 s/m^2. So D = 18.23 ps/(nm km) is 1.823e-5 s/m^2. Total spread = D x (spectral width in nm) x (length in km).

**Bit rate and pulse spacing.** A digital message is a train of pulses, a light pulse for 1 and no light for 0. If one bit lasts T seconds, the bit rate is B = 1/T bits per second. If the pulses broaden to a width comparable to T they overlap and the 1s and 0s blur (the extract calls this inter-symbol interference, PDF p. 700). So dispersion limits B. For example T = 1 ns means B = 1 Gb/s.

**Check the scale.** A pulse of 1 ns broadened by 100 ps is a 10 per cent change; broadened by 5 ns it is destroyed.

### 1. Orientation: a light pipe, a train of pulses and why the pulses smear

*Objective:* Describe an optical fibre, define pulse dispersion, intermodal dispersion and material dispersion in plain words, and state what the lesson must deliver.

## The object
An **optical fibre** is a very thin cylinder of transparent glass or plastic that guides light along its length by total internal reflection (Brij Lal, PDF p. 673). It has a **core** (the light-carrying centre, typically 8.5 to 62.5 micrometres across in the extract), a **cladding** around it (about 125 micrometres across) with a slightly **lower** refractive index, and a protective coating. Light entering one end follows a zigzag path, striking the core-cladding boundary at angles larger than the critical angle, and emerges at the far end even if the fibre is bent.

## The message
Digital information is sent as pulses of light: a pulse is a 1, no pulse is a 0. The faster you want to send bits, the closer together the pulses must be. But as a pulse travels it **broadens** (the extract calls this distortion or **pulse dispersion**, PDF p. 695). After many kilometres two neighbouring pulses may overlap so that the receiver cannot tell them apart. This is the main limit on how many bits per second a fibre can carry over a distance.

## Why do pulses spread? Three mechanisms (extract PDF p. 696)
1. **Intermodal dispersion.** Light enters the fibre as a cone of rays; different rays travel different path lengths, so they arrive at different times. This is what the examiner calls **pulse dispersion** for a step-index fibre.
2. **Material dispersion.** Glass refracts different wavelengths with different indices. A real pulse contains a band of wavelengths, and each colour travels at its own speed. This is **material (chromatic) dispersion** and depends on the **spectral width** of the source.
3. **Waveguide dispersion.** Even within one mode, the geometry of the guide makes the speed depend on wavelength. It is small in multimode fibre but matters in single-mode fibre.

### 2. Foundation: guiding, critical propagation angle, acceptance angle and numerical aperture (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive the critical propagation angle, the acceptance angle and the numerical aperture NA = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) of a step-index fibre.

## Setting and assumptions
A step-index fibre: core of constant index n1, cladding of constant index n2 < n1, straight, light launched from a medium of index n0 (n0 = 1 for air) at the flat end face. We use the **ray picture** and look at **meridional rays**, those that cross the axis (Brij Lal, PDF p. 684). Angles: theta_i is the angle of the incident ray with the axis in the launching medium, theta_r the refracted angle with the axis inside the core, phi the angle of incidence at the core-cladding boundary (measured from the normal to that boundary).

## Two conditions for guiding (PDF p. 678)
1. n1 must be slightly greater than n2.
2. phi must exceed the critical angle phi_c at the boundary, where sin(phi_c) = n2/n1 (bridge B1).

## Critical propagation angle
A ray inside the core at angle theta_r to the axis meets the boundary with phi = 90 degrees - theta_r. TIR needs phi at least phi_c, so theta_r must be at most

$$ theta_c = 90 degrees - phi_c $$

This theta_c is the **critical propagation angle** (extract PDF p. 678). Equivalently cos(theta_c) = sin(phi_c) = n2/n1.

## Derivation 1: acceptance angle
1. At the end face, Snell's law: n0 sin(theta_i) = n1 sin(theta_r).
2. Geometry: theta_r = 90 degrees - phi, so sin(theta_r) = cos(phi).
3. The ray is guided only if phi is at least phi_c, so cos(phi) is at most cos(phi_c). The limiting ray has phi = phi_c.
4. cos(phi_c) = sqrt(1 - sin^2(phi_c)) = sqrt(1 - n2^2/n1^2) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2)/n1.
5. So the largest launch angle theta_0 satisfies n0 sin(theta_0) = n1 cos(phi_c) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2).
6. With air, n0 = 1:

$$ sin(theta_0) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) = NA $$

7. theta_0 is the **acceptance angle**; rays inside the cone of full angle 2 theta_0 (the acceptance cone) are trapped (PDF p. 680). The **numerical aperture** NA is defined as sin(theta_0) (PDF p. 681); for a launching medium of index n0 the product n0 sin(theta_0) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) is the quantity that depends only on the fibre.

*Dimension check:* indices are dimensionless, so NA is a pure number. *Limit:* n2 = n1 gives NA = 0 (nothing guided). If n1^2 - n2^2 exceeds 1 the sine would exceed 1, meaning every ray from air is accepted (theta_0 = 90 degrees).

## In terms of Delta
n1^2 - n2^2 = (n1 - n2)(n1 + n2) = n1 Delta x n1(2 - Delta), which is about 2 n1^2 Delta for small Delta, so **NA is about n1 sqrt(2 Delta)** (the extract's form, PDF p. 681). It depends only on the indices, not on the fibre's thickness.

## Numbers (recomputed)
- n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43: phi_c = 72.43 degrees, theta_c = 17.57 degrees, NA = sqrt(2.25 - 2.0449) = 0.4529, theta_0 = 26.93 degrees. (Verified PYQ 2023 Q4(a) uses these indices.)
- Extract Example 24.2: n1 = 1.6, n2 = 1.3: phi_c = 54.3 degrees, theta_0 = 68.9 degrees, cone angle 137.7 degrees (NA = sqrt(1.6^2 - 1.3^2) = sqrt(0.87) = 0.933). The extract prints 60.5 and 121 because it takes arcsin(0.87), dropping the square root.
- Extract Example 24.4 style: n1 = 1.546, n2 = 1.378 gives NA = 0.70 (recomputed).

## Trap
The **critical angle** phi_c (from the normal, about 72 degrees here) and the **critical propagation angle** theta_c (from the axis, about 17.6 degrees) are complements. Read the question: 2023 Q4(a)(i) asks for the 'critical propagation angle'.

### 3. Core: modes, the V-number and the fibre types

*Objective:* Define a mode, compute the V-number and the approximate number of modes, state the single-mode condition V < 2.405, and compare step-index, graded-index and single-mode fibres.

## Why only some ray directions are allowed
A ray that is totally reflected still has to remain in step with itself: a zigzag wave interferes constructively with itself only if its total phase change per two reflections is a whole multiple of 2 pi. Only particular angles satisfy this. Each allowed path is a **mode** (extract PDF pp. 683 and 689 to 690). The mode along the axis is the lowest-order (axial) mode; modes whose rays make larger angles with the axis, close to theta_c, are **higher-order** modes. The number of modes grows with NA, Delta and core diameter, and falls with wavelength.

## The V-number (normalised frequency)
The quantity that controls the mode count is

$$ V = (2 pi a / lambda) NA = (pi d / lambda) sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) $$

with a the core radius, d = 2a the diameter and lambda the free-space wavelength. The extract defines it (PDF p. 690) and states two results.
- For a step-index fibre the number of modes is approximately **V^2/2** (printed on PDF p. 691, Eqs. 24.31, 24.32). For a graded-index parabolic fibre it is about V^2/4, which agrees with the extract's remark that a graded-index fibre carries about half as many modes (PDF p. 700).
- The fibre is **single-mode** if **V < 2.405** (the first zero of the Bessel function J0, 2.405; PDF p. 691). The wavelength at which this happens is the cutoff wavelength.

## Numbers (recomputed)
| Fibre | n1 | n2 or Delta | core | lambda | V | modes ~ V^2/2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extract Ex. 24.10 | 1.52 | Delta = 0.0007 | d = 29 um | 1.3 um | 3.99 (extract 4.05) | 8 |
| Extract Problem 6 | 1.54 | n2 = 1.50 | d = 50 um | 1.3 um | 42.14 | 888 |
| Single-mode design | 1.48 | n2 = 1.478 | a = 4.08 um | 0.82 um | 2.405 | 1 |

Extract Problem 16 asks the core radius for single-mode operation at 820 nm with n1 = 1.480, n2 = 1.478: a = 2.405 lambda/(2 pi NA) = 4.08 micrometres (recomputed). To get a single-mode fibre one reduces the core diameter and/or Delta (extract PDF p. 691).

## Fibre types (extract PDF pp. 685 to 688)
| Type | Index profile | Core | Dispersion | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-mode step-index | step | 8 to 12 um | no intermodal; small material and waveguide | laser diode |
| Multimode step-index | step | 50 to 100 um | large intermodal | LED or laser |
| Graded-index multimode | smooth fall from centre, parabolic profile favoured | 50 to 100 um | intermodal much reduced | LED or laser |

In the graded-index fibre the index is highest on the axis and decreases to the cladding value at the boundary. Rays are bent back towards the axis by continuous refraction, so their paths are curved (extract PDF p. 688).

## Rays: meridional and skew
Meridional rays cross the axis; skew rays spiral without crossing it (PDF p. 684). The simplest dispersion calculation (module 4) uses meridional rays only.

## Slip in the extract to avoid
On PDF p. 697 the extract writes that 'lower-order modes travel a greater distance' and arrive first. This is reversed. Higher-order modes, at angles near theta_c, travel the longer paths and arrive later, as the extract itself says on PDF p. 683.

### 4. Core: intermodal (pulse) dispersion in a step-index fibre, bit-rate limit and graded-index cure (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive delta t = L n1 (n1 - n2)/(c n2), convert it to a bit-rate limit, and explain with numbers how a graded-index fibre reduces it.

## Setting and assumptions
A step-index multimode fibre of length L, core index n1, cladding index n2. All rays are meridional and launched together by a short pulse. Light in the core travels at v = c/n1. Material and waveguide dispersion are ignored here (modules 5 and 6). Each ray makes a fixed angle theta with the axis and reflects totally.

## Derivation 2: the delay between the fastest and slowest ray
1. A ray at angle theta advances L along the axis while travelling a zigzag path s = L/cos(theta).
2. Time taken: t(theta) = s/v = L n1/(c cos(theta)). It depends on theta: steeper rays take longer.
3. **Fastest ray**: the axial ray, theta = 0, t_min = L n1/c.
4. **Slowest guided ray**: theta = theta_c, with cos(theta_c) = n2/n1 (module 2). Then t_max = L n1/(c n2/n1) = L n1^2/(c n2).
5. Delay between the two:

$$ delta t = t_max - t_min = (L n1/c)(n1/n2 - 1) = L n1 (n1 - n2) / (c n2) $$

6. With Delta = (n1 - n2)/n1, (n1 - n2)/n2 = Delta/(1 - Delta), so delta t = (L n1/c) Delta/(1 - Delta), which is about **L n1 Delta / c** for small Delta.
7. Since NA^2 is about 2 n1^2 Delta, delta t is about **L NA^2/(2 c n1)** (proportional to NA^2, PDF p. 698).

*Checks:* L n1/c is a time; n2 = n1 or a single mode gives delta t = 0.

## From delay to bit rate
Intermodal dispersion does not depend on the source's spectral width, and a smaller NA means fewer modes and less spreading (PDF pp. 697 to 698). Pulses spaced T apart stay separate if T is at least 2 delta t, so

$$ B_max is about 1/(2 delta t) $$

(a common convention; other books use 1/delta t; the extract uses the bandwidth-distance product in MHz km, PDF p. 700; verify).

## Numbers (recomputed)
- n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43: delta t = 1.5 x 0.07 x 1000/(3e8 x 1.43) = 2.448e-7 s = 244.8 ns for 1 km. B_max = 1/(2 x 244.8 ns) = 2.04 Mb/s, a bandwidth-distance product of about 2 MHz km.
- Over 50 km with linear scaling: 12.24 microseconds (verified PYQ 2023). Real fibres with mode mixing scale more weakly than L (verify), but the examination follows the extract and multiplies by L.

## Graded-index cure: derivation for a parabolic profile
Take n^2(r) = n1^2 (1 - 2 Delta' r^2/a^2) with Delta' = (n1^2 - n2^2)/(2 n1^2) (about Delta for small differences, but it makes the edge index exactly n2), so the index falls smoothly from the axis. A meridional ray turns at radius r0; beta = n(r) cos(theta(r)) is constant along it (Snell's law in layers).
1. Time per axial length: dt/dz = n/(c cos theta) = n^2/(c beta).
2. Differentiating (dr/dz)^2 = (n^2 - beta^2)/beta^2 gives r'' = (1/(2 beta^2)) d(n^2)/dr = -(2 Delta' n1^2/(beta^2 a^2)) r: simple harmonic motion in z, so the average of r^2 is r0^2/2.
3. Averaged n^2 is n1^2 (1 - x) with x = Delta' r0^2/a^2; at the turning point beta^2 = n1^2 (1 - 2x). So t/L = (n1/c)(1 - x)/sqrt(1 - 2x) = (n1/c)(1 + x^2/2 + ...): the first-order term cancels.
4. Axial ray x = 0; marginal ray x = Delta' (beta = n2): exact **delta t = L (n1 - n2)^2/(2 c n2)** = 5.71 ns/km; the small-Delta form n1 L Delta^2/(2c) = 5.44 ns; 2/Delta = 43 times below the step-index 244.8 ns.
5. Quoted, not derived here: a profile exponent slightly below 2 (alpha = 2 - 2 Delta) leaves about n1 L Delta^2/(8c) = 1.36 ns (exact 1.43 ns; derived in Day 18 lesson PH-208).
The extract (PDF pp. 688, 700) gives only the qualitative reason: longer paths run where n is smaller and light is faster.

### 5. Core: material dispersion, the D parameter and the spread formula (derivation 3)

*Objective:* Derive the material-dispersion spread delta tau = |D| L delta lambda with D = -(lambda/c) d^2n/dlambda^2 from the group delay, and compute D and the spread for silica.

## Setting and assumptions
A pulse is a **wave packet**, a band of wavelengths around a central wavelength lambda with spectral width delta lambda (extract PDF p. 279 to 283; Day 14 lesson PH-040 modules 6 and 7 and Day 15 lesson PH-041). In glass each wavelength has its own refractive index n(lambda) (Cauchy, Sellmeier; extract PDF pp. 317 to 319). We consider one mode, a straight fibre of length L, and **material dispersion only**: the wavelength dependence of the glass itself. We use the vacuum wavelength lambda and neglect the guiding geometry (that is waveguide dispersion, module 6).

## Derivation 3
1. A pulse (a packet) travels at the **group velocity** v_g (PH-041 module 3 and 4). Light in a medium of index n has group index **n_g = n - lambda dn/dlambda** and v_g = c/n_g (PH-041 module 5, derived there).
2. Time for each spectral component to cross length L (the **group delay**): t_g = L/v_g = L n_g/c.
3. A source of spectral width delta lambda contains components of different lambda, so their delays differ by

$$ delta tau = (d t_g / d lambda) delta lambda = (L/c)(d n_g / d lambda) delta lambda $$

4. Differentiate n_g = n - lambda dn/dlambda with respect to lambda (product rule): d n_g/d lambda = dn/dlambda - dn/dlambda - lambda d^2n/dlambda^2 = - lambda d^2n/dlambda^2. The first-derivative terms cancel; only the **curvature** of n(lambda) matters.
5. Hence

$$ delta tau = - (L delta lambda lambda / c) d^2n/dlambda^2 = D L delta lambda,   D = -(lambda/c) d^2n/dlambda^2 $$

D is the **material dispersion coefficient** (the extract's D_m). The size of the spread is |D| L delta lambda (extract PDF p. 699: reduce it by narrower sources or longer wavelengths).

*Dimension check:* d^2n/dlambda^2 has units 1/m^2 and lambda/c has units m/(m/s) = s, so D is in **s/m^2**, which is **ps/(nm km)** when 1 ps/(nm km) = 1e-6 s/m^2 (bridge B2). Then D L delta lambda has units s/m^2 x m x m = s ✓.

*Sign:* D < 0 means longer wavelengths arrive earlier (the extract's statement that short wavelengths travel slower, PDF p. 698); D > 0 means longer wavelengths arrive later.

## Cauchy illustration (not silica)
Take the Cauchy form n = A + B/lambda^2 used in PH-041 module 5. Then d^2n/dlambda^2 = 6B/lambda^4 and **D = -6B/(c lambda^3)**, negative at all wavelengths. With the illustrative B = 0.0050 um^2 and lambda = 0.85 um, D = -163 ps/(nm km). This shows the sign and the steep fall with wavelength (D proportional to 1/lambda^3), but a one-term Cauchy form can never produce D = 0 and it overestimates the real value (see the next table).

## Real silica (Malitson Sellmeier fit, recomputed with sympy; standard; verify)
The full table is in the master notes. D = -96.8 ps/(nm km) at 0.82 um, -84.0 at 0.85, -39.9 at 1.00, +2.6 at 1.30 and +21.9 at 1.55 um.

D falls to zero at **1.273 um**, which is why the extract says low intramodal dispersion occurs near 1300 nm (PDF p. 694). Example (recomputed): an LED at 850 nm with 38 nm width gives |D| delta lambda = 84.0 x 38 = 3.19 ns/km, close to the extract's 'about 3 ns/km' for an LED at 820 nm (the same fibre at 820 nm gives 3.7 ns/km). The extract's other example (a 1140 nm laser with 3 nm width giving 0.3 ns/km) is not consistent with silica's D = -15.6 ps/(nm km) at 1140 nm, which gives 0.047 ns/km; treat it as an order-of-magnitude illustration only.

## Trap
In the extract and in many questions 'normal' and 'anomalous' refer to whether n falls or rises with lambda. Silica at 1550 nm has dn/dlambda < 0 (normal in that sense) but D > 0 (anomalous group-velocity dispersion). Always state which meaning you use (module 7).

### 6. Core: waveguide dispersion, total dispersion, bandwidth and the numbers that decide a link

*Objective:* Combine the three dispersions with the root-sum-of-squares rule, relate the total to the bit rate, and compare step-index, graded-index and single-mode fibres numerically.

## Waveguide dispersion
Even for one mode the speed of the wave depends on the ratio of core size to wavelength, because the mode's effective index and its confinement vary with wavelength (extract PDF p. 699). The result is an extra wavelength-dependent delay, written D_w and added to D_m. It is small in multimode fibre but important in single-mode fibre, where D = D_m + D_w (standard; verify). In standard single-mode fibre D_w is negative at 1.3 to 1.6 um, which moves the zero of the total D from 1.27 um to about 1.31 um and lowers D at 1550 nm to about 17 ps/(nm km) (typical value; verify). A **dispersion-shifted** fibre uses a refractive-index profile that moves the zero to 1550 nm.

## What exists in which fibre (extract PDF pp. 700 to 701)
| Fibre | Intermodal | Material | Waveguide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multimode step-index | large | present | small |
| Graded-index | small (not exactly zero) | present | small |
| Single-mode | none | present | present |

The extract says intermodal dispersion in a graded-index fibre is 'zero'; strictly it is only greatly reduced (module 4).

## Total dispersion (extract PDF p. 700)
When several independent mechanisms broaden a pulse, the widths combine as a **root sum of squares**:

$$ delta t_total = sqrt( delta t_modal^2 + delta t_material^2 + delta t_waveguide^2 ) $$

This is the usual rule for independent, roughly Gaussian contributions (standard; verify). For the PYQ 2021 numbers: modal 64.17 ns and material 36.46 ps give sqrt(64.17^2 ns^2 + 0.03646^2 ns^2) = 64.17 ns: the material term is smaller by a factor of about 1760 and changes nothing. A simple sum would give 64.20 ns. **The largest term wins** when terms differ by more than a factor of 3 or so.

## From dispersion to bandwidth (extract PDF p. 700)
If a pulse of width tau is spread to about delta t_total, neighbouring pulses must be at least about 2 delta t_total apart, so the maximum rate is B = 1/(2 delta t_total). The figure of merit is the **bandwidth-distance product** (units MHz km), because the usable bandwidth falls as the length grows (linear-scaling assumption). The extract also says the single-mode fibre carries 'up to 50 times more distance' than multimode (PDF p. 701) and that graded-index fibre has better bandwidth than step-index.

## A comparison (recomputed)
| Case | Spread | B = 1/(2 spread) |
|---|---|---|
| Multimode step-index, n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.43, 1 km | 244.8 ns | 2.04 Mb/s |
| Same, graded-index (optimum alpha profile, Delta = 0.0467; formula L n1 Delta^2/(8c)), 1 km | 1.36 ns | 0.37 Gb/s |
| Single-mode at 1550 nm, D = 17 ps/(nm km), delta lambda = 0.1 nm, 100 km | 170 ps | 2.94 Gb/s |
| Single-mode at 1550 nm, same, delta lambda = 1 nm, 100 km | 1700 ps | 0.29 Gb/s |

The comparison shows why long-distance links use single-mode fibre and narrow-line lasers at 1310 to 1550 nm: the modal term is gone and material dispersion is minimised by a small spectral width and an operating point near the zero of D.

## Other limits
Attenuation (loss of power in dB/km) is a separate limit that sets how far the signal travels before it needs a repeater; the extract treats it on PDF pp. 692 to 694. The lesson here is about the **time** spreading only.

## Traps
- Adding all three dispersions linearly when the question says 'total dispersion' (the extract uses the root sum of squares).
- Multiplying per-km values by L without converting units (ps versus ns).
- Using the spectral width in micrometres with D in ps/(nm km).

### 7. Master: dispersion as the second derivative of the dispersion relation (link to PH-041)

*Objective:* Relate D to the group-velocity dispersion beta2 = d^2k/domega^2, connect it to the packet-spreading formula of PH-041 module 8, and estimate when a transform-limited pulse is destroyed.

## The same mathematics, space replaced by time
Day 15 lesson PH-041 module 8 showed that a Gaussian packet in a dispersive medium spreads according to the second derivative of omega with respect to k: sigma_x(t) = sigma_x0 sqrt(1 + (omega'' t/(2 sigma_x0^2))^2), with omega'' = d^2omega/dk^2. In a fibre we observe the pulse at a fixed place and watch how its duration grows with distance z, so the roles of space and time swap. Define

$$ beta2 = d^2k / d omega^2 = - omega'' / v_g^3 $$

(from dk/domega = 1/omega' and the quotient rule; v_g = omega'). beta2 is called the **group-velocity dispersion (GVD)** parameter, in s^2/m (ps^2/km in practice).

## Link between D and beta2
The group delay per length is beta1 = dk/domega = 1/v_g = n_g/c. D is its derivative with respect to wavelength: D = d beta1/d lambda = beta2 (d omega/d lambda) = - (2 pi c/lambda^2) beta2. So

$$ D = - (2 pi c / lambda^2) beta2,   beta2 = - D lambda^2 /(2 pi c) $$

For D = 17 ps/(nm km) at 1550 nm: beta2 = -21.7 ps^2/km (recomputed). Negative beta2 (D > 0) is called anomalous GVD; positive beta2 (D < 0), as in silica below 1.27 um, is normal GVD (the sign convention used in fibre optics; verify).

## Broadening of a Gaussian pulse
By the correspondence x -> t, t -> z and omega'' -> beta2, an unchirped Gaussian pulse of rms duration sigma_0 (intensity) widens to

$$ sigma(z) = sigma_0 sqrt( 1 + ( beta2 z / (2 sigma_0^2) )^2 ) $$

(consistent with the standard dispersion-length form; verify). It is the Fourier-limited case: the narrower the pulse, the broader its spectrum (Day 14 lesson PH-040 module 7, sigma_t sigma_omega = 1/2) and the faster it spreads.

## Numbers (recomputed)
Take 1550 nm, D = 17 ps/(nm km), beta2 = -21.7 ps^2/km, and 100 km.
- sigma_0 = 10 ps: sigma_omega = 1/(2 sigma_0) = 5.0e10 rad/s, the rms wavelength width is lambda^2 sigma_omega/(2 pi c) = 0.0638 nm, and D L delta lambda = 17 x 100 x 0.0638 = 108.4 ps. The formula gives sigma(100 km) = 10 sqrt(1 + (21.7 x 100/(2 x 100))^2) = 10 sqrt(1 + 117.7) = 109 ps: **the pulse is stretched eleven-fold**. For 10 km: 14.7 ps.
- sigma_0 = 100 ps: beta2 z/(2 sigma_0^2) = 0.1085, so sigma(100 km) = 100.6 ps: negligible broadening. A slower bit rate survives much farther.
The two routes (D L delta lambda and the GVD formula) agree here because D L delta lambda is the large-distance limit of the GVD formula.

## Why a narrow laser is not always enough
A source narrower than the pulse's own Fourier width does not help: a short pulse necessarily has a wide spectrum (the extract's bandwidth relation delta nu about 1/tau for a wave train, PDF pp. 279 to 281). Hence the dispersion limit has two parts: the source linewidth and the unavoidable Fourier bandwidth of the pulse.

## Important distinctions
- Group velocity v_g (PH-041) tells where the pulse centre goes; GVD tells how its width changes.
- n_g > n for glass in the normal range, so the pulse is slower than the phase: c/n_g.
- 'Normal' in the Cauchy sense (n falls with lambda, extract PDF p. 318) and 'normal GVD' (beta2 > 0) are different statements. Silica is normal in the first sense throughout its transparency window but its GVD changes sign at 1.27 um.

### Notes: Definitions

- Optical fibre: a cylindrical dielectric waveguide (core, cladding, coating) that guides light by total internal reflection; n1 (core) is slightly greater than n2 (cladding).
- Critical angle at the core-cladding boundary: sin(phi_c) = n2/n1 (measured from the normal). Critical propagation angle: theta_c = 90 degrees - phi_c (measured from the axis).
- Acceptance angle theta_0: the largest launch angle for guidance; acceptance cone has full angle 2 theta_0. Numerical aperture NA = n0 sin(theta_0) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2), about n1 sqrt(2 Delta).
- Fractional index change Delta = (n1 - n2)/n1, with Delta much less than 1.
- Mode: an allowed ray path or field pattern that interferes constructively with itself; V = (2 pi a/lambda) NA; single-mode if V < 2.405.
- Pulse (intermodal) dispersion: spread of arrival times of different modes; material (chromatic) dispersion: spread due to n(lambda) over the source's spectral width; waveguide dispersion: due to the guide geometry; total by root sum of squares.

### Notes: Equations to know

- sin(theta_0) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) in air; NA = n1 sqrt(2 Delta) approx.
- Intermodal delay: delta t = L n1 (n1 - n2)/(c n2) = (L n1/c) Delta/(1 - Delta), about L n1 Delta/c, about L NA^2/(2 c n1).
- Graded-index residual delay: parabolic profile exact L (n1 - n2)^2/(2 c n2), small-Delta form L n1 Delta^2/(2c) (derived in module 4, with n^2 = n1^2 (1 - 2 Delta' r^2/a^2), Delta' = (n1^2 - n2^2)/(2 n1^2)); alpha = 2 - 2 Delta profile about L n1 Delta^2/(8c) (exact 1.43 ns in the example; derived in PH-208).
- Material dispersion: n_g = n - lambda dn/dlambda; D = -(lambda/c) d^2n/dlambda^2; spread = |D| L delta lambda.
- Units: 1 ps/(nm km) = 1e-6 s/m^2.
- Total: delta t_total = sqrt(sum of squares); bit-rate estimate B = 1/(2 delta t).
- GVD: beta2 = -D lambda^2/(2 pi c); Gaussian pulse sigma(z) = sigma0 sqrt(1 + (beta2 z/(2 sigma0^2))^2).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: intermodal delay

- Path of a ray at angle theta: L/cos(theta); speed c/n1; t(theta) = L n1/(c cos theta).
- Axial ray: t_min = L n1/c. Steepest guided ray: cos(theta_c) = n2/n1, t_max = L n1^2/(c n2).
- delta t = t_max - t_min = L n1 (n1 - n2)/(c n2), about L n1 Delta/c.
- Bit rate B about 1/(2 delta t); graded index equalises the optical paths (parabolic: delta t = L n1 Delta^2/(2c) from the averaged-n^2 argument).

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: material dispersion

- Group delay t_g = L n_g/c with n_g = n - lambda dn/dlambda (PH-041).
- d n_g/d lambda = - lambda d^2n/dlambda^2.
- delta tau = (L/c)(d n_g/d lambda) delta lambda = D L delta lambda with D = -(lambda/c) d^2n/dlambda^2.
- Units: D in s/m^2 = 1e6 ps/(nm km).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Critical angle (from the normal at the core-cladding boundary) versus critical propagation angle (from the axis): complements.
- Pulse (intermodal) dispersion versus material dispersion: different causes, different dependence (modal on NA and L, material on delta lambda and L).
- Acceptance angle versus acceptance cone angle: cone full angle is 2 theta_0 (the extract's Problem 8 value 33.96 degrees is the full angle).
- Normal dispersion in the Cauchy sense (n falls with lambda) versus normal GVD (beta2 > 0): silica is normal in the first sense throughout its transparency window but its GVD changes sign near 1.27 um.

### Core concepts

- Total internal reflection guides light in a core of higher index; only rays inside the acceptance cone are guided.
- Different modes (ray paths) take different times, giving pulse (intermodal) dispersion in multimode fibres.
- Material dispersion comes from the wavelength dependence of n(lambda) over the source's spectral width.
- Graded-index and single-mode fibres reduce or remove intermodal dispersion; single-mode still has material and waveguide dispersion.

### Memory skeleton

- Guiding: n1 > n2 and TIR at the core-cladding boundary; critical angle φc (from normal) versus critical propagation angle θc = 90° − φc (from axis)
- Acceptance angle: end-face Snell plus sin θr = cos φ ⇒ sin θ0 = √(n1² − n2²) = NA ≈ n1√(2Δ)
- Modes and V = (2πa/λ) NA; single-mode, multimode step-index, graded-index
- Intermodal delay: t(θ) = L n1/(c cos θ) ⇒ δt = L n1(n1 − n2)/(c n2); graded index equalises paths
- Material dispersion: t_g = L n_g/c ⇒ Δτ = |D| L δλ, D = −(λ/c) d²n/dλ²
- Total dispersion: root sum of squares; single-mode keeps material and waveguide dispersion
