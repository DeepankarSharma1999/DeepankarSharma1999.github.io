### Bridge: Rural against urban: the vocabulary

**Local government** means elected bodies that look after local affairs close to the people. India separates two systems. The **rural** system is **Panchayati Raj** (73rd Amendment, Part IX). The **urban** system is made up of **municipalities**, also called **Nagarpalikas** (74th Amendment, Part IX-A). A **ward** is a territorial constituency inside a town or city from which one **councillor** is elected. A **panchayat** is a rural body; a **municipality** is an urban body.

How does the State decide what is urban? NCERT says the Census of India calls an area urban if it has a population of at least 5,000, at least 75 per cent of male workers in non-farm jobs, and a density of at least 400 persons per square kilometre. These three tests identify a **census town**. The Census also counts as urban every **statutory town**: any place with a municipality, corporation, cantonment board or notified area committee, whatever its population (standard knowledge; verify). The Constitution itself leaves the classification of a municipal area to the Governor, who looks at population, density, revenue, non-farm employment and economic importance.

A **Schedule** is a list at the end of the Constitution. The Eleventh Schedule lists subjects for panchayats; the **Twelfth** lists subjects for municipalities. 'Local government' is a **State List** subject, so States make the detailed laws.

### Bridge: Municipality, corporation, council: the vocabulary

The 74th Amendment names three kinds of municipality: a **Nagar Panchayat** for an area changing from rural to urban (a *transitional area*), a **Municipal Council** for a smaller urban area and a **Municipal Corporation** for a larger urban area. A **municipality** in everyday speech can mean any of these, but in the textbook's list of eight types 'municipality' means a town body that is not a corporation.

Heads and officers have different names. A **corporation** has a **Mayor** (elected, mostly for a one-year renewable term, a formal head) and a **Municipal Commissioner** (an appointed official, generally an IAS officer, who is the chief executive). A **municipality** has a **President or Chairman** (an elected politician with real executive powers) and a **Chief Executive Officer / Chief Municipal Officer** appointed by the State. A **councillor** is an elected member of the council.

States use different names: Nagar Nigam in Uttar Pradesh, Municipal Boards in Rajasthan, Town Panchayats in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. Exam questions use the constitutional names, so learn those first and treat the State names as examples.

### 1. Orientation: what urban local government is, and how it differs from the village system

*Objective:* Define urban local government, state who creates it, and name the three constitutional types and the eight types in the textbook list.

## The idea

A **city or town** also needs a government close to its people: someone to run water supply, roads, drains, street lights, waste collection and fire services. **Urban local government** means the governance of an urban area by the people through their **elected representatives**. Laxmikanth adds that its jurisdiction is limited to a specific urban area **demarcated by the State government**.

Two basic facts:

- 'Local government' is a subject in the **State List** of the Seventh Schedule (Laxmikanth, note 1). Each State therefore makes its own municipal laws. The 74th Amendment then added **common constitutional rules** that every State must follow.
- At the Centre, three ministries deal with the subject: the **Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs**, the **Ministry of Defence** (for cantonment boards) and the **Ministry of Home Affairs** (for Union Territories).

## Rural against urban

India has two parallel systems. **Rural** local government is **Panchayati Raj** (73rd Amendment, 1992; GS-195). **Urban** local government is the system of **municipalities** (*Nagarpalikas*), created by the **74th Amendment Act, 1992**. NCERT says the 74th is in many ways a repetition of the 73rd, applied to urban areas: direct elections, reservations, transfer of subjects, State Election Commission and State Finance Commission all apply. The differences (types instead of tiers, Wards Committees instead of a Gram Sabha, the Twelfth Schedule instead of the Eleventh, and the Metropolitan Planning Committee) are the exam points; module 9 and the master notes set them side by side.

## What is 'urban'? (NCERT, citing the Census)

A place is a **census town** if it has all three (this is the census-town test): a **population of at least 5,000**; **at least 75 per cent of male workers** in non-agricultural jobs; and a **density of at least 400 persons per square kilometre**. In addition, every **statutory town** (a place with a municipality, corporation, cantonment board or notified area committee) is urban whatever its size (standard knowledge; verify). NCERT adds that about 31 per cent of India's population lived in urban areas at the 2011 Census (as printed; verify later figures).

## Eight types in the textbook, three in the Constitution

Laxmikanth lists **eight** types of urban local government that exist in India: municipal corporation, municipality, notified area committee, town area committee, cantonment board, township, port trust and special purpose agency. The **74th Amendment** requires only **three** kinds of municipality: *Nagar Panchayat*, *Municipal Council* and *Municipal Corporation*. The others are not municipalities under Part IX-A; they are created by other laws or by a department (module 7).

## Simple illustration

Imagine a street in a mid-sized town where garbage is not collected. Who is responsible? A councillor elected from the ward; the municipality's council and its officials; the State government that made the municipal law. If the town is a **military cantonment**, a board under the Defence Ministry's control is responsible. If the area is a **company township**, the company's own administrator. This chain of answers is the heart of the topic.

### 2. Evolution of urban bodies: from Madras 1688 to the 74th Amendment

*Objective:* Place eight events on a timeline from 1688 to 1993 and explain why urban bodies needed constitutional status.

## Under British rule (Laxmikanth's list)

Urban local government in its modern form began under British rule. Learn it as a ladder of dates.

- **1688**: the first **municipal corporation** in India was set up at **Madras**. (Some books cite 1687, the year of the charter; the source prints 1688. Verify before using either year as a fact.)
- **1726**: municipal corporations at **Bombay and Calcutta**.
- **1870**: **Lord Mayo's Resolution** on financial decentralisation envisaged local self-government institutions.
- **1882**: **Lord Ripon's Resolution**, hailed as the **'Magna Carta' of local self-government**; he is called the **father of local self-government** in India. NCERT adds that elected local boards began after 1882 on his initiative.
- **1907-09**: the **Royal Commission on Decentralisation**, chairman **Hobhouse**, appointed in 1907 and reporting in 1909.
- **1919**: under the dyarchy scheme of the Government of India Act, local self-government became a **transferred subject** under an Indian minister.
- **1924**: the **Cantonments Act**.
- **1935**: under provincial autonomy, local self-government became a **provincial subject**.

(*Dyarchy* means dual government: some subjects were 'transferred' to Indian ministers and others 'reserved' to British officials. See GS-158.)

## After independence: committees and commissions

Laxmikanth's Table 39.1 lists nine bodies appointed by the Centre between 1949 and 1988 (full table in the master notes). Four are the most useful for the exam: the **Local Finance Enquiry Committee (Wattal, 1949-51)**, the **Rural-Urban Relationship Committee (A. P. Jain, 1963-66)**, the **Committee of Ministers on Augmentation of Financial Resources of Urban Local Bodies (Rafiq Zakaria, 1963)** and the **National Commission on Urbanisation (C. M. Correa, 1985-88)**.

## The road to constitutional status

Why change the Constitution? NCERT's account of the village side is also true of towns: the subject belonged to the States, only the **Directive Principles** mentioned local self-government (Article 40, not enforceable by courts), and States often postponed elections, dissolved elected bodies and handed administration to officials. Constitutional status was meant to make regular elections and basic powers a **legal obligation** on the States.

- **August 1989**: the Rajiv Gandhi government introduced the **65th Amendment Bill** (the *Nagarpalika Bill*) in the Lok Sabha. It passed the Lok Sabha but was **defeated in the Rajya Sabha in October 1989** and lapsed.
- **September 1990**: the National Front (V. P. Singh) government introduced a **revised Bill**, which lapsed when the Lok Sabha was dissolved.
- **September 1991**: the **Narasimha Rao** government introduced a **modified Municipalities Bill**, which became the **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992**. Both Houses passed it in December 1992; after ratification by the required State legislatures it received the President's assent in April 1993 (Laxmikanth's note 2). It **came into force on 1 June 1993**.

## Link to the village story

The 73rd Amendment, in force on 24 April 1993, went the same route and about five weeks earlier. NCERT notes that both were introduced in 1989 and passed in 1992; Part IX is the village part and **Part IX-A** the town part. The village-side committees (Balwant Rai Mehta and others) are in GS-195 and are not repeated here.

### 3. The 74th Amendment: Part IX-A, Twelfth Schedule and the three types of municipality

*Objective:* State what the Act added to the Constitution, why it is 'justiciable' in effect, and how the Governor decides between a Nagar Panchayat, a Municipal Council and a Municipal Corporation.

## What the Act added

The **74th Amendment Act, 1992** added a new **Part IX-A**, titled **'The Municipalities'**, containing **Articles 243-P to 243-ZG**, and a new **Twelfth Schedule** of **eighteen** functional items for municipalities. The Twelfth Schedule is linked to **Article 243-W**.

It gave municipalities **constitutional status** and brought them within the **justiciable** part of the Constitution. In practice this means the States are under a **constitutional obligation** to adopt the municipal system in accordance with the Act. A State that does not hold elections, or does not set up a State Finance Commission, is breaking the Constitution, which a court can enforce. The Act aimed to revitalise urban governments so that they work as units of local government.

States were given **one year** from the commencement date, **1 June 1993**, to bring their laws into line. Existing municipalities continued until their terms ended unless the State legislature dissolved them sooner (Article 243-ZF).

## Three types of municipality

The Act provides, for **every State**, for:

1. a **Nagar Panchayat** (or a name chosen by the State) for a **transitional area**, that is, an area changing from rural to urban;
2. a **Municipal Council** for a **smaller urban area**;
3. a **Municipal Corporation** for a **larger urban area**.

**Exception**: if an industrial establishment provides the municipal services in an area, the Governor may declare it an **industrial township**; in that case a municipality may not be constituted.

## Who decides, and on what test?

The **Governor** specifies which area is transitional, smaller or larger. The Constitution gives **no population figure**. He must keep in view: (a) population; (b) **density** of population; (c) **revenue** generated for local administration; (d) percentage of **employment in non-agricultural activities**; (e) **economic importance**; and (f) any other factor he deems fit. So a Nagar Panchayat is not defined by a number in the Constitution; the State fixes it. This is a typical exam trap: be wary of options that quote a single population figure as 'the constitutional rule'.

## Metropolitan area (definition)

For the Metropolitan Planning Committee (module 6), Laxmikanth's note defines a **metropolitan area** as an area with a population of **ten lakh or more**, in one or more districts, consisting of two or more municipalities or panchayats or other contiguous areas.

## Articles in order (Table 39.3)

Learn them as a story: define, constitute, compose, wards, reserve, last (duration), disqualify, power, tax, finance, audit, elect, UT, exempt, district plan, metropolitan plan, continue, courts stay out.

### 4. Composition, Wards Committees, reservation, term and elections

*Objective:* Apply the composition, reservation, term, disqualification and election rules of Articles 243R to 243ZA.

## Composition (Article 243R)

All members of a municipality are **elected directly** by the people. The municipal area is divided into territorial constituencies called **wards**. The State legislature decides the manner of election of the **chairperson**. It *may* also provide for representation of persons who have special knowledge of municipal administration (without a vote), Members of Parliament and State legislators whose constituencies include the municipal area, Rajya Sabha and Legislative Council members registered as electors in the area, and chairpersons of committees other than Wards Committees.

## Wards Committees (Article 243S)

In a municipality with a **population of three lakh or more**, a **Wards Committee** (one or more wards) must be constituted. The State decides its composition and area. The State may also create other committees; their chairpersons can be made members of the municipality. This is the urban counterpart of the Gram Sabha, but it is a *committee of the council's ward*, not an assembly of voters.

## Reservation (Article 243T)

- **SC and ST**: seats reserved in **proportion to their population** in the municipal area.
- **Women**: **not less than one-third** of the total seats, **including** seats reserved for SC and ST women.
- **Chairperson offices**: the offices of chairpersons **shall be reserved** for SC, ST and women, in such manner as the State legislature provides by law (Article 243T(4); verify). The panchayat parallel, Article 243D(4), reads the same and then adds proportionality for SC/ST and one-third for women (GS-195). Laxmikanth's wording suggests a weaker rule on the urban side; that looks like a paraphrase slip.
- **Backward classes**: the State *may* provide reservation of seats or offices; it is not compulsory.
- SC/ST reservation ceases when the period in **Article 334** ends. Laxmikanth prints 'presently seventy years, that is, till 2020'. This is dated: the period was reported extended to 2030 by the 104th Amendment (2019); verify.

## Term (Article 243U)

Five years, but a municipality can be dissolved earlier. Elections must be completed **before the five years expire** or, after dissolution, **within six months**. If the remainder of the term would be **less than six months**, no election is needed. A body elected after dissolution serves **only for the remainder** of the term. Two protections: the municipality must be given a **reasonable opportunity of being heard** before dissolution, and no amendment of any law can dissolve a municipality before its five years end.

**Worked example.** A municipality is dissolved at the end of year 4. The remainder is one year, over six months, so elections must be held within six months, and the new body serves only until the end of year 5. If it is dissolved after 4 years and 8 months, the remainder is 4 months, under six, so no election is needed.

## Disqualification (Article 243V)

A person is disqualified if disqualified under any law for the time being in force for State legislature elections or under a State law. But nobody is disqualified for being **below 25** if he has reached **21**. Questions go to an authority the State legislature decides.

## State Election Commission (Article 243ZA)

Superintendence, direction and control of the electoral rolls and all municipal elections lie with the **State Election Commission**, the same one that conducts panchayat elections. NCERT stresses that it is an independent officer and **not under the Election Commission of India**.

## Courts (Article 243ZG)

Laws on delimitation of constituencies and allotment of seats cannot be questioned in any court; an election can be challenged only by an **election petition** as the State law provides.

### 5. Powers, the Twelfth Schedule, finance, audit and exempted areas

*Objective:* List the eighteen Twelfth Schedule functions in groups, and explain who funds municipalities and who reviews their finances.

## Powers and functions (Article 243W)

The State legislature *may* give municipalities the powers and authority they need to function as **institutions of self-government**. This can include devolution of responsibility for **plans for economic development and social justice** and for implementing schemes, including the **eighteen** matters of the **Twelfth Schedule**. The word 'may' matters: the transfer depends on State law (compare the 29 subjects of the Eleventh Schedule, GS-195).

## The Twelfth Schedule: eighteen items in five groups

1. **Planning and land**: urban planning including town planning; regulation of land use and building construction; planning for economic and social development.
2. **Infrastructure**: roads and bridges; water supply for domestic, industrial and commercial use; public amenities such as street lighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences.
3. **Health and safety**: public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid waste management; fire services; vital statistics including registration of births and deaths; regulation of slaughter houses and tanneries; burials, burial grounds, cremations and cremation grounds; cattle pounds and prevention of cruelty to animals (the Twelfth Schedule, item 15, reads 'Cattle pounds; prevention of cruelty to animals'; Laxmikanth prints 'ponds', a source slip).
4. **Environment and recreation**: urban forestry, protection of the environment and ecological aspects; parks, gardens and playgrounds; promotion of cultural, educational and aesthetic aspects.
5. **Social welfare**: safeguarding the interests of weaker sections, including the handicapped and mentally retarded (these are the Constitution's own dated words; current usage is persons with disabilities); slum improvement and upgradation; urban poverty alleviation.

That counts to eighteen when you remember: the first group has three, the second three, the third six, the fourth three and the fifth three.

## Money

The State legislature *may*: (a) authorise a municipality to **levy, collect and appropriate** taxes, duties, tolls and fees (Article 243X); (b) **assign** State taxes; (c) give **grants-in-aid** from the State's Consolidated Fund; (d) create **funds** for municipal money. All four are permissions, not orders.

## Finance Commission (Article 243Y)

The commission that is set up for panchayats also reviews municipal finances **every five years** and recommends to the Governor: the principles for sharing the net proceeds of State taxes between the State and the municipalities and among municipalities; which taxes may be assigned to them; grants-in-aid; measures to improve their finances; and anything else referred. The Governor lays the recommendations with an **action-taken report** before the State legislature. The Union Finance Commission also suggests measures to augment a State's Consolidated Fund to support municipal resources, on the basis of the State commission's recommendations (see GS-193).

## Audit (Article 243Z)

The State legislature *may* make provisions on municipal accounts and their audit.

## Union Territories and exempted areas

- The Part applies to **Union Territories**, but the President may direct that it applies with exceptions and modifications (Article 243ZB).
- It does **not apply** to **Scheduled Areas and tribal areas** and does not affect the powers of the **Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council** in West Bengal; Parliament may extend it with exceptions (Article 243ZC). Laxmikanth's note, 'as of 2019', says ten States have Scheduled Areas and ten tribal districts exist in four States. These counts are dated; verify.
- Existing State laws continued for one year from 1 June 1993 (Article 243ZF).

### 6. Planning committees: District Planning Committee and Metropolitan Planning Committee; compulsory against voluntary

*Objective:* Compare Article 243ZD with Article 243ZE and classify the 74th Amendment's provisions into those States must adopt and those they may adopt.

## District Planning Committee (Article 243ZD)

Every State must constitute at the district level a **District Planning Committee (DPC)**. Its job: to **consolidate the plans** prepared by the panchayats and the municipalities in the district and to prepare a **draft development plan for the district as a whole**. The State legislature decides the composition, the manner of election of members and of the chairperson, and its functions. The Act fixes one rule: **not less than four-fifths** of the members must be elected by the elected members of the **district panchayat and the municipalities** from among themselves, in proportion to the **rural-urban population ratio** of the district. The chairperson forwards the plan to the State government.

In preparing the plan, the DPC must have regard to matters of common interest between panchayats and municipalities (spatial planning, sharing of water and other physical and natural resources, integrated infrastructure development and environmental conservation) and to the available resources, and must consult institutions the Governor specifies.

## Metropolitan Planning Committee (Article 243ZE)

Every **metropolitan area** (population of ten lakh or more) must have a **Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC)** to prepare a draft development plan. The State legislature decides composition, representation of the Central and State governments and other bodies, functions, and the manner of election. The Act fixes **not less than two-thirds** of the members to be elected by the elected members of the **municipalities** and the **chairpersons of panchayats** in the metropolitan area from among themselves, in proportion to the **population ratio** of municipalities and panchayats. The MPC must have regard to the plans of municipalities and panchayats, coordinated spatial planning, sharing of water and natural resources, integrated infrastructure and environmental conservation, the overall objectives and priorities of the Union and State governments, and expected investments by Central and State agencies.

The DPC against MPC comparison table (area, plans consolidated, elected share, elected by) is in the master notes under 'DPC against MPC'.

## Compulsory (mandatory) against voluntary (discretionary)

The source does not print a separate list for the municipalities, but its wording does: where it says 'shall' the State must adopt the provision; where it says 'may' the State has a choice. Derived from the source (check the Article text before quoting):

**Compulsory**: three types of municipality; direct election of all members; Wards Committees in municipalities of three lakh or more; SC and ST seat reservation; at least one-third seats for women; reservation of chairperson offices for SC, ST and women (manner by State law, Article 243T(4); verify); five-year term and election within six months of dissolution; the age and disqualification rules; a State Election Commission; a State Finance Commission; a District Planning Committee and a Metropolitan Planning Committee.

**Voluntary**: representation of experts, legislators and committee chairpersons in the municipality; the manner of electing the chairperson; reservation for backward classes; devolution of powers and the eighteen functions; powers to tax, assign State taxes, give grants and create funds; audit rules.

As in the village side, **most functional and financial provisions are voluntary**, while elections, reservation and the commissions are compulsory. That is the root of the 'funds, functions, functionaries' problem in module 9.

## The thread to remember

DPC and MPC are the **only bodies in the Constitution that connect villages and towns in one plan**. They matter for the 2024 Mains question about merging rural and urban local bodies (module 10).

### 7. Eight types of urban government

*Objective:* Describe the eight types of urban local body, say who creates each and whether it is elected, and recall the structure of a corporation and a municipality.

## The list

Laxmikanth names eight types: **municipal corporation, municipality, notified area committee, town area committee, cantonment board, township, port trust and special purpose agency**. The first two are the municipalities of the 74th Amendment (with Nagar Panchayats as a third type). The comparison table is in the master notes.

## 1. Municipal corporation

For **big cities** (the source names Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Hyderabad and Bangalore). Established in States by **State legislature Acts** and in Union Territories by **Acts of Parliament**; there may be one common Act per State or one for each corporation. It has **three authorities**:

- The **Council**: the deliberative and legislative wing, with directly elected **councillors** plus a few nominated persons with municipal knowledge. Seat reservation for SC, ST and women follows the 74th Amendment. The Council is headed by a **Mayor**, assisted by a Deputy Mayor; in a majority of States the Mayor is elected for a **one-year renewable term**. Laxmikanth calls him basically an **ornamental figure** and formal head whose main job is presiding over the Council.
- **Standing committees** (public works, education, health, taxation, finance) that take decisions in their areas, since the Council is too large to do so.
- The **Municipal Commissioner**: chief executive authority, who implements decisions of the Council and the committees; **appointed by the State government**, generally an **IAS** officer.

## 2. Municipality

For **towns and smaller cities**; also State Acts (or Parliament's for Union Territories); known as municipal council, committee, board, borough or city municipality. Three authorities: the **Council** (directly elected councillors), **standing committees** and the **chief executive officer / chief municipal officer**, appointed by the State government. Its head is a **President / Chairman** with a vice-president or vice-chairman. Unlike the Mayor, he plays a **significant role and is the pivot** of administration, with executive powers.

## 3. Notified Area Committee

For a fast-developing industrial town or a town not yet qualifying for a municipality. Created by **notification in the government gazette**; only the Municipal Act provisions notified for it apply. Its powers are almost equal to a municipality's, but it is **entirely nominated**: all members and the chairman are nominated by the State government. So it is **neither elected nor statutory**.

## 4. Town Area Committee

For a **small town**; a **semi-municipal** body with a limited number of civic functions (drainage, roads, street lighting, conservancy). Created by a **State legislature Act**; may be wholly elected, wholly nominated or partly each. A. P. Jain's committee (1963-66) recommended merging small town area committees with Panchayati Raj institutions.

## 5. Cantonment Board

For the **civilian population in a cantonment** (area where troops are permanently stationed). Created under the **Cantonments Act, 2006** of the **Central government**, which repealed the 1924 Act. It works under the **Defence Ministry**. Unlike the other bodies, it is created **and** administered by the Centre. It is partly elected and partly nominated; the military officer commanding the station is the ex-officio president. The executive officer is appointed by the President of India from a central cadre. Laxmikanth prints 62 boards in 2019 (dated; verify: the civil areas of some cantonments have since been excised and merged with municipalities).

## 6. Township

Established by **large public enterprises** for staff in housing colonies near the plant. A **town administrator** appointed by the enterprise; **no elected members**; an extension of the enterprise's bureaucracy.

## 7. Port Trust

In port areas such as Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai; created by an **Act of Parliament**; elected and nominated members; the chairman is an official; manages the port and provides civic amenities. Dated (verify): the Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 converted the major port trusts into port authorities.

## 8. Special purpose agency

Function-based, not area-based: town improvement trusts, urban development authorities, water and sewerage boards, housing boards, pollution control boards, electricity boards and city transport boards. Statutory bodies or departments; they are **autonomous** and **not subordinate** to municipal bodies.

### 8. Revenue, personnel, the Central Council of Local Government and central schemes

*Objective:* Name the five sources of municipal revenue, the three personnel systems and the Central Council of Local Government, and state what is known and what must be verified about urban missions.

## Five sources of municipal revenue

1. **Tax revenue**: property tax (the most important), entertainment tax, taxes on advertisements, professional tax, water tax, tax on animals, lighting tax, pilgrim tax, market tax, bridge tolls, **octroi** and various cesses (library, education, beggary). **Octroi** (a tax on the entry of goods into a local area for consumption, use or sale) has been abolished in most States.
2. **Non-tax revenue**: rent on municipal property, fees and fines, royalty, profits and dividends, interest, user charges (water, sanitation, sewerage) and miscellaneous receipts.
3. **Grants** from the Central and State governments for development programmes, infrastructure schemes and reforms.
4. **Devolution**: transfers from the State government on the recommendation of the **State Finance Commission**.
5. **Loans** from the State government and financial institutions for capital spending; borrowing from financial institutions needs **State approval**.

## Municipal personnel: three systems

- **Separate Personnel System**: each local body recruits and controls its own staff; not transferable; the most widely prevalent system; supports local autonomy.
- **Unified Personnel System**: State-wide cadres controlled by the State government, transferable between bodies; Laxmikanth names (2019) Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh.
- **Integrated Personnel System**: municipal staff are members of the State services, transferable between bodies and State departments; named: Odisha, Bihar, Karnataka, Punjab and Haryana.

National training institutions in the extract include the All-India Institute of Local Self-Government (Mumbai, 1927; a private registered society), the Centre for Urban and Environmental Studies (New Delhi, 1967, after the Nur-ud-din Ahmed Committee), the National Institute of Urban Affairs (1976) and the Human Settlement Management Institute (1985).

## Central Council of Local Government

Set up in **1954** under **Article 263** (the same Article that allows the President to create an inter-State council; see GS-194) by an order of the President. Originally the Central Council of Local **Self**-Government; 'self' was dropped in the 1980s. Until 1958 it handled both rural and urban bodies; since then **urban only**. It is **advisory**, with the Union Minister in charge of urban affairs as chairman and State local-government ministers as members. Functions: considering policy, proposing legislation, examining Centre-State cooperation, drawing up a common programme, recommending Central financial assistance and reviewing work done with that assistance.

## Central missions: verify before using

The extracts do not describe urban missions, so every statement here is standard knowledge to be checked from current government sources: **Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (JNNURM)**, launched in the mid-2000s; **AMRUT**, **Smart Cities Mission** and **Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban)**, launched in the mid-2010s; and later housing and urban-transport missions. Do not quote budgets, targets or numbers of cities in an answer unless you have checked them for the year concerned. For Mains, the useful point is structural: these missions are **Centre-driven and project-based**, so cities depend on tied grants and special agencies rather than on their own revenue and elected councils (an analytical claim to be supported by current evidence).

Arunachal Pradesh is shown as having no urban local bodies; Meghalaya and Mizoram show only municipalities. Treat these as dated.

### 9. Problems, reforms and the 73rd against 74th comparison

*Objective:* Explain, with source-based reasons, why urban local bodies remain weak despite constitutional status, and state five contrasts with the village system.

## Why urban bodies stay weak (analysis from the sources)

Neither Laxmikanth's chapter nor NCERT gives a single list of 'challenges'; the following are drawn from facts they report. Anything beyond them is marked 'verify'.

- **Functions**: the Twelfth Schedule transfer depends on State law (module 5). NCERT's general finding: local governments enjoy limited autonomy, many States have not transferred most subjects, and the exercise of electing so many representatives becomes somewhat symbolic.
- **Funds**: the State legislature *may* assign taxes and grants; octroi has been abolished in most States; property tax is the main tax; borrowing needs State approval; many municipalities depend on grants. NCERT: local bodies have very little funds of their own and local governments continue to be agencies implementing the schemes of the Central and State governments. (Its revenue figures are for **rural** bodies and are not used here.)
- **Functionaries**: the Commissioner or CEO is appointed by the **State**, generally from the IAS; personnel systems differ; training institutions are few.
- **Weak political head**: in a corporation the Mayor is an **ornamental figure**, usually serving a one-year term, while real executive power sits with the State-appointed Commissioner.
- **Parallel agencies**: special purpose agencies (development authorities, water boards, housing boards) are **autonomous and not subordinate** to the municipality, so city functions are split.
- **Delayed elections and dissolutions**: NCERT describes this as a problem before 1993 and the Constitution now bars it; whether it still happens must be checked from a current source (verify).

## Reforms commonly suggested (verify with the Second Administrative Reforms Commission and recent reports)

Activity mapping that gives each function to one level; untied funds and stronger property-tax systems; empowered, directly elected mayors with longer terms (a recommendation to be checked); real Wards Committees and citizen participation; merging overlapping agencies; transparent audit; capacity building for municipal staff; integration with the DPC and MPC.

## The 73rd and 74th compared

The full 73rd against 74th table (Part and Articles, Schedules, dates, structure, the grassroots unit, chairperson reservation, the planning bodies, tribal areas and common features) is in the master notes. The three differences to remember: types instead of tiers, the Wards Committee instead of the Gram Sabha, and the Twelfth Schedule with the Metropolitan Planning Committee on the urban side.

## Balanced position for Mains

The 74th Amendment brought regular elections, women's and SC/ST representation, State commissions and planning bodies. But power, money and staff remain with the State and with agencies outside the municipality, and the compulsory provisions concern structure while the voluntary ones concern substance.

### 10. How UPSC tests this topic: PYQ patterns and answer frameworks

*Objective:* Recognise that all five verified PYQs are Mains questions on effectiveness, functionality and finances, and build a skeleton answer for each pattern.

## The pattern

All five verified PYQs for this topic are **Mains GS II** questions (2017, 2020, 2022, 2023, 2024). None is a pure fact question, though Prelims regularly asks the facts (Part IX-A, Twelfth Schedule, Wards Committees, types of bodies). So you need two skills: the facts of modules 3 to 7, and the argument of modules 8 and 9.

## Pattern 1: 'effective instrument of governance?' (2017, 10 marks)

'The local self-government system in India has not proved to be effective instrument of governance.' Critically examine the statement and give your views to improve the situation. **Skeleton**: introduction (constitutional status 1992-93); gains (regular elections, reservation, SEC and SFC, planning committees); shortfalls (functions, funds, functionaries; mayor and commissioner; parallel agencies); causes (voluntary provisions, State reluctance); reforms; conclusion.

## Pattern 2: 'Functions, Functionaries and Funds' to 'Functionality' (2020, 15 marks)

The question reframes the three Fs as a fourth: whether bodies can **function** in practice. Use the same skeleton but add examples of capacity: skills and staff, accountability to citizens, digital and e-governance tools (verify), property-tax systems, and coordination among agencies.

## Pattern 3: 'Decentralisation changed the governance landscape?' (2022, 10 marks)

'To what extent' invites a balanced answer: representation changed (women and SC/ST in local bodies; NCERT says the social profile has altered), planning (DPC/MPC), but the real transfer of power is limited.

## Pattern 4: 'States reluctant to empower urban local bodies' (2023, 10 marks)

'Comment' means state a view with reasons. Reasons (verify with sources): the **voluntary** nature of Articles 243W and 243X; State control over staff and finance; the competing State agencies; political fear of losing control over cities. Counterpoints: some States have devolved more; Central missions condition reforms.

## Pattern 5: 'Merging rural and urban local bodies' (2024, 10 marks)

'Analyse the role of local bodies in providing good governance at local level and bring out the pros and cons merging [sic] the rural local bodies with the urban local bodies.' **Pros** (analytical): rural-urban continuum and peri-urban areas, integrated planning (DPC and MPC already join the two), economies of scale, uniform services. **Cons**: different function lists (29 against 18), different needs and institutions (Gram Sabha against Wards Committees), the village side's PESA and Scheduled Area protections, risk to rural identity and Gram Sabha participation, financing gaps. Historical link: the A. P. Jain committee (1963-66) recommended merging small town area committees with panchayats. The Census definition (module 1) shows that the urban-rural line is statistical.

**Critically examine** (2017): weigh both sides. **Highlight** (2020): bring out the main challenges. **To what extent** (2022): reach a graded view. **Comment** (2023): give an opinion with reasons. **Analyse** (2024): break into parts and show relationships.

### Notes: Definitions

- Urban local government: governance of a State-demarcated urban area by elected representatives of its people.
- Nagar Panchayat: municipality for a transitional area; Municipal Council: smaller urban area; Municipal Corporation: larger urban area.
- Ward: territorial constituency of a municipality; Wards Committee: committee of one or more wards, compulsory where population is three lakh or more.
- Metropolitan area: population of ten lakh or more, in one or more districts, with two or more municipalities, panchayats or contiguous areas (Laxmikanth, note 5).
- Industrial township: area where an industrial establishment provides services; a municipality may not be constituted there.
- Octroi: tax on entry of goods into a local area; abolished in most States.

### Notes: Part IX-A: Articles 243P to 243ZG

- 243P definitions; 243Q constitution; 243R composition; 243S Wards Committees; 243T reservation of seats; 243U duration; 243V disqualifications.

- 243ZB Union Territories; 243ZC areas where the Part does not apply; 243ZD District Planning Committee; 243ZE Metropolitan Planning Committee; 243ZF continuance of existing laws; 243ZG bar to interference by courts.

### Notes: Twelfth Schedule: the eighteen functions

- 16 Vital statistics including registration of births and deaths; 17 public amenities including street lighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences; 18 regulation of slaughter houses and tanneries.

- **F-COI-A243Q (verified):** Article 243Q(1): every State shall have (a) a Nagar Panchayat for a transitional area (an area in transition from rural to urban), (b) a Municipal Council for a smaller urban area, and (c) a Municipal Corporation for a larger urban area; a Municipality need not be constituted in an area the Governor specifies as an industrial township. The Governor specifies these areas by notification having regard to population, density, revenue generated, non-agricultural employment and economic importance [243Q(2)]. Part IXA was inserted by the 74th Amendment, w.e.f. 1-6-1993.
  Source: [The Constitution of India [as on 1st May, 2026], Legislative Department, Ministry of Law and Justice, Government of India](https://legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf)

  Source: [The Constitution of India [as on 1st May, 2026], Legislative Department, Ministry of Law and Justice, Government of India](https://legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf)

  Source: [The Constitution of India [as on 1st May, 2026], Legislative Department, Ministry of Law and Justice, Government of India](https://legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf)
