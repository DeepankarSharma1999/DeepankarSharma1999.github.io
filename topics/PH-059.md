## PH-059: Zone plates and Fresnel circular aperture

Physics Paper I · Physics · Wave optics: diffraction

### Bridge: Adding vibrations as arrows (phasors) and the vibration circle

A light vibration at a point can be written A cos(wt - phi), where A is the amplitude, w the angular frequency and phi the phase lag. Because every vibration in this lesson has the same w, only A and phi matter. Draw one **arrow** (a phasor) of length A pointing at angle phi from a fixed reference line. Two vibrations are added by placing the second arrow at the head of the first; the straight arrow from the first tail to the last head is the resultant, and its length is the resultant amplitude. Intensity is proportional to the square of that length.

Three facts are all you need. An arrow turned by 180 degrees (a phase change of pi) points the opposite way, so it cancels an equal arrow: this is a half-wave path difference lambda/2. An arrow turned by 360 degrees (phase 2 pi, path difference one lambda) points the same way, so the two add. If a source of vibrations is divided into many tiny pieces whose phases increase steadily, the arrows turn steadily, and the head-to-tail chain bends into a **circle**. The length of the chord between two points of that circle is the resultant of the pieces between them. That is the vibration circle used in module 3.

Phase and path are linked by phi = 2 pi (path difference)/lambda. A path difference of lambda/2 means phi = pi.

### Bridge: Lens formula and the sign convention used for zone plates

A thin lens of focal length f obeys 1/v - 1/u = 1/f in the **Cartesian convention**: light travels from left to right, distances are measured from the lens (vertex), a distance to the right of the lens is positive and to the left is negative. A real object on the left has u < 0; a real image on the right has v > 0; a virtual image on the left has v < 0. A converging lens has f > 0.

The zone-plate derivation in the book uses the distances as plain lengths: a is the distance of the source from the plate and b the distance of the point P from the plate on the other side. Then a = -u and b = v, and the lens law becomes

$$ 1/a + 1/b = 1/f $$

which is the real-image form. If the right-hand side gives a negative b, the image is virtual and lies on the source side at distance |b|. The linear magnification is v/u = -b/a in magnitude b/a. A parallel beam is a = infinity, so b = f. This lesson uses the Cartesian form whenever a sign matters and the positive-distance form for real images, and says which one it uses.

### 2. Foundation: the recall list as arrows, and what exactly n counts

*Objective:* Turn the Day 18 zone results into an arrow picture and define the zone number n exactly, with conventions for wavelength and distances.

## Conventions used throughout
- **lambda** is the wavelength in air: 500 nm = 5.0 x 10^-7 m, 6000 Angstrom = 6.0 x 10^-7 m.
- **a** is the distance from the point source S to the screen with the opening; **b** is the distance from that screen to the axial observation point P. A plane wave has a = infinity. S, the centre O of the opening and P lie on one line, the **axis**.
- **r** (or R) is a radius, never a diameter. **r_1** is the radius of the first zone of a zone plate. N means all zones (open and blocked) of a plate; N_t = N/2 are open.
- The model is **ideal**: monochromatic point source, small angles, sharp edges, obliquity factor taken as 1. Where the model is stretched the text says so.

## The zone number n, defined by path
Let A be a point on the edge of the opening. The edge ray S to A to P is longer than the axial path S to O to P by delta = SA + AP - (a + b). Define n as the number of half-wavelengths in that excess:

$$ n = delta/(lambda/2) = 2 delta/lambda $$

For small r, delta = (r^2/2)(1/a + 1/b) (PH-058), hence n = (r^2/lambda)(1/a + 1/b); for a plane wave n = r^2/(b lambda). The path form is valid for **any real number n**: n = 0.5 means the edge ray is a quarter of a wavelength longer than the axial ray. The two forms agree to about one part in a million here (python: r = 0.5 mm, b = 0.25 m gives n = 1.9999980 against 2).

## The recall list as arrows
Each half-period zone, seen from P, sends the previous zone's vibration turned through a half turn (phase pi). Draw it as an arrow (bridge B1): zone 1 an arrow m_1 to the right, zone 2 an arrow of nearly equal length to the left, zone 3 to the right again.
- one zone: length m_1;
- two zones: right then left, nearly zero;
- three zones: right, left, right, about m_1;
- the whole wavefront: the chain never settles, but its **average** position is half an arrow, m_1/2 (the PH-058 result).

## From a staircase to a smooth curve
A zone is a coarse step. Cut each zone into thin rings of equal area; for a plane wave each sends an arrow of the same small length, and its phase turns steadily with the path to P. Equal arrows turning steadily make a **circle**, and one zone covers half a turn of it. This smooth version gives intensities for fractional n (module 3).

## Why intensity is quoted against I_0
Let I_0 be the intensity at P with the screen removed (the **free wave**). It gives amplitude m_1/2, so one open zone, amplitude m_1, gives 4 I_0. Quoting I/I_0 removes constants and the 1/distance fall of a spherical wave.

### 3. Core: the vibration circle and the axial intensity 4 sin^2(pi n/2) for any n (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive I/I_0 = 4 sin^2(pi n/2) for a circular aperture from the vibration circle, check it against an exact integral and a limiting case, and read it for fractional n.

## Setting, assumptions, sign convention
A circular opening of radius R; P on the axis; n defined by path (module 2). Assume a monochromatic point or plane source, small angles (obliquity factor 1), and the PH-058 equal-area result: thin rings of equal area send arrows of equal length. Phase is a **lag** (longer path, larger phase) and the pole O has phase 0.

## Step 1: one arrow per thin ring
The ring at the edge of zone j has path excess j lambda/2, hence phase pi j. Phase grows in proportion to the zones covered: phi runs from 0 at the centre to pi n at the edge of the opening.

## Step 2: the arrows form a circle
Equal arrows whose directions turn at a constant rate, head to tail, form a circular arc. Let the chain for one full zone (phi = pi, half a turn) have resultant equal to the **diameter** D of the circle: m_1 = D. Two zones close the circle: m_1 - m_2 = 0 for equal amplitudes.

## Step 3: the resultant for a general n
After n zones the arrow has turned through pi n. The resultant is the chord subtending the angle pi n at the centre, and a chord is the diameter times the sine of half the subtended angle:

$$ A(n) = D sin(pi n/2) $$

Check: n = 1 gives D; n = 2 gives 0.

## Step 4: the free wave
Let the chain grow without limit. In the ideal model it goes round and round the circle and never ends; its natural value is the **centre of the circle**, a distance D/2 from the start. That is the PH-058 result m_1/2 = D/2 (the statement that the last term m_N tends to zero), so A_0 = D/2.

## Step 5: the intensity ratio
$$ I/I_0 = [D sin(pi n/2)/(D/2)]^2 = 4 sin^2(pi n/2) $$

| n | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 2.5 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I/I_0 | 0 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 0 | 2 | 4 |

Maxima 4 I_0 at odd n, zeros at even n (python values of 4 sin^2(pi n/2); n = 0.25 and 0.75 give 0.586 and 3.414). Half a zone gives 2 I_0: the chord is 0.707 D, squared 0.5 D^2, over (D/2)^2.

## Step 6: an exact check (plane wave)
The contribution of the ring at distance s from P is proportional to e^(i k s) ds, because rho d rho = s ds cancels the 1/s. Integrating from s = b to sqrt(b^2 + R^2), and dividing by the free value (the integral to infinity with the oscillating tail dropped, the same convention as above), gives I/I_0 = |1 - e^(i phi)|^2 = 4 sin^2(phi/2) with phi = (2 pi/lambda)(sqrt(b^2 + R^2) - b) = pi n. This is the same formula with the exact n (python: R = 0.5 mm, b = 0.25 m gives 4 x 10^-11, a dark centre).

## Step 7: limiting cases and caveat
- **Small hole (n much less than 1):** 4 sin^2(pi n/2) is about (pi n)^2 = (pi R^2/(lambda b))^2, the central intensity of the far-field Airy pattern of Day 17 lesson PH-057 (amplitude proportional to the hole's area over lambda b). The two theories join (python: n = 0.002 gives 3.9478e-5 both ways).
- **Large hole:** the real obliquity factor shrinks successive zones slowly, so the circle spirals inward and the swings fade towards I_0.
- **Dimensions:** n and I/I_0 are pure numbers.

### 4. Core: moving the screen, the source or the hole radius (maxima, minima, book problems)

*Objective:* Locate the bright and dark axial points for a plane or spherical wave as b, a or r changes, and solve Example 17.2 and Problem 4 with the correct bookkeeping.

## The rule
Axial centre bright (maximum 4 I_0) when n is an odd integer; dark (zero in the ideal model) when n is an even integer; between them I/I_0 = 4 sin^2(pi n/2). Real minima are not perfectly dark because zone amplitudes are not exactly equal (the book notes that diffraction minima are not perfectly dark, PDF p. 420).

## Plane wave, moving the screen (a = infinity)
n = r^2/(b lambda), so b = r^2/(n lambda).
- Bright at b = r^2/lambda, r^2/(3 lambda), r^2/(5 lambda), ...; dark at r^2/(2 lambda), r^2/(4 lambda), ...
- **The farthest dark centre is n = 2**; beyond it n is below 2 and the centre is never dark. The farthest maximum is n = 1.

Python: r = 0.5 mm, lambda = 500 nm give r^2/lambda = 0.50 m (bright at 0.50, 0.167, 0.100 m; dark at 0.250, 0.125 m). PH-058 (section on a circular aperture) works this sequence; new below are Problem 4, the finite source and the iris.

**Example 17.2 (book PDF pp. 439 to 440, recomputed; also in PH-058).** Diameter 1.2 mm, first dark centre at 30 cm: n = 2 with r = 0.6 mm, so lambda = r^2/(2b) = 6.0 x 10^-7 m = 6000 Angstrom. It uses the **radius**; 1.2 mm would give four times too much.

**Problem 4 (book PDF p. 441, recomputed).** 5000 Angstrom, hole diameter 1 mm (r = 0.5 mm). Farthest black centre: n = 2, b = r^2/(2 lambda) = 0.25 m. The next black centre is n = 4: b = 0.125 m. Distance moved = 0.125 m; the book prints 0.12 m, a rounding.

## Spherical wave, source at a finite distance
From n = (r^2/lambda)(1/a + 1/b):

$$ 1/b = n lambda/r^2 - 1/a $$

A real screen needs 1/b > 0, so the sequence starts at n_inf = r^2/(a lambda) (the value for b = infinity).

Python: a = 2.0 m, r = 0.5 mm, 500 nm: r^2/lambda = 0.50 m and n_inf = 0.25 (I/I_0 = 0.586 far away). Bright: b = 0.667 m (n = 1), 0.182 m (n = 3). Dark: b = 0.2857 m (n = 2), 0.1333 m (n = 4). Check n = 1: 0.5 x (1/2 + 1/0.667) = 1.00. The plane-wave result for the same hole is 0.50 m, so the finite source moves the first maximum **outwards** to 0.667 m. Dropping the 1/a term is a common slip.

## Changing the hole instead of the screen
The radius that exposes n zones is r_n = sqrt(n lambda a b/(a + b)); for a plane wave sqrt(n b lambda). At b = 1.00 m and 500 nm: n = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3 need r = 0.500, 0.707, 0.866, 1.000, 1.118, 1.225 mm, giving I/I_0 = 2, 4, 2, 0, 2, 4. Opening an iris from 0.707 mm to 1.000 mm radius takes the centre from its brightest to dark.

## Off the axis
Off-axis points have a shifted pole and part of the zones cut off by the edge, so the screen shows concentric bright and dark rings round the axial point (PH-058, section on a circular aperture; book PDF p. 422). Ring radii are not derived here.

## Unit checks
All lengths in metres in the formula; b is measured **from the aperture to the screen**, not from the source.

### 5. Core: the opaque disc, the bright spot and the hole-disc link (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Show with the vibration circle that the centre of a round disc's shadow has intensity I_0 in the ideal model, explain what lowers it in practice, and link the disc to the hole.

## Setting
An opaque disc of radius R covers k half-period zones of P, k = (R^2/lambda)(1/a + 1/b) (book PDF p. 424; PH-058, section on the opaque circular disc and the bright spot in its shadow). P is on the axis behind the disc. Same assumptions as module 3. Phase is a lag; the pole has phase 0.

## Step 1: which part of the circle is left
The disc blocks the rings with phase 0 to pi k. Everything beyond, to infinity, is open. On the vibration circle the open part starts at the point E_k reached after k zones and runs on round the circle for ever.

## Step 2: the resultant
The chain from E_k wobbles about the **centre C**, exactly as the full chain did from S, and its natural end is C (the same rule as for the free wave). The resultant is the arrow from E_k to C, a **radius** of the circle: length D/2 for every k. The free wave is also a radius, so

$$ I_disc/I_0 = (D/2)^2/(D/2)^2 = 1 $$

The centre of the shadow is as bright as the open wave in the ideal model, however large the disc. Its phase is turned by pi k relative to the free wave, which an intensity measurement cannot see.

## Step 3: agreement with the series
The book (PDF pp. 424 to 425) covers k = 1 (amplitude m_2/2); the regrouping to m_(k+1)/2 is in PH-058 (section on the opaque circular disc), so the two methods agree. The book's remark that the spot weakens as the disc grows is true only through the slow fall of m_(k+1): python gives (m_(k+1)/m_1)^2 = 0.9999975 for k = 10 and 0.99975 for k = 1000 at b = 1 m, 500 nm.

## Step 4: why the spot is rarely seen
- It needs a point-like monochromatic source and a very round disc with a smooth edge (a rough rim changes the zone count by a fraction of a zone around the edge and smears the spot).
- The spot is tiny; PH-058 puts the first dark ring 0.096 mm from the axis for R = 1 cm, b = 5 m and 500 nm (standard; verify).
- An extended source washes it out (book PDF p. 413).
The history of the Poisson or Arago spot (about 1818; standard; verify) is told in PH-058.

## Step 5: the hole-disc link (Babinet idea)
A hole and a disc of the same radius are **complementary** screens. At any point the two amplitudes add to the free amplitude: A_hole + A_disc = A_free. On the circle, with S at the bottom, E_1 at the top and C the centre: hole (n = 1) is the arrow S to E_1, length D; disc (k = 1) is the arrow E_1 to C, which points back down, so it is -D/2 along the first; the sum D - D/2 = D/2 is the free arrow S to C. Complementary screens give complementary **amplitudes**, not complementary intensities: the hole gives 4 I_0 and the disc I_0, not 4 I_0 and 0. Only where the free amplitude is zero (outside the geometrical beam in the far field) do both screens give the same intensity (standard; verify).

## Hole against disc
A hole of n zones swings between 0 and 4 I_0 as n changes (module 3). A disc never gives less than I_0 at the centre, because the chain left after the disc always starts on the circle and ends at its centre.

### 6. Core: designing a zone plate, its focal length, its image law and the gain on the axis

*Objective:* Design a zone plate from f, lambda or from an object-image pair, compute its zone count and outer zone width, and apply 1/a + 1/b = 1/f with signs and units correct.

## What it is
A zone plate blocks every second half-period zone (book PDF p. 416). The zone radii r_n = sqrt(n) r_1, f = r_1^2/lambda and 1/a + 1/b = 1/f are derived in Day 18 lesson PH-058 (section on the zone plate, its radii, focal length and lens formula). This module adds design rules, counts and numbers.

## Design rule 1: from f and lambda
$$ r_1 = sqrt(f lambda), r_n = sqrt(n) r_1 $$
f = 0.50 m, lambda = 500 nm: r_1 = sqrt(2.5 x 10^-7) = 0.500 mm; edges at 0.500, 0.707, 0.866, 1.000, 1.118 mm, ... The exact plane-wave edges are r_n^2 = n f lambda + n^2 lambda^2/4; at n = 100 the neglected term changes r_n^2 by the fraction n lambda/(4 f) = 2.5 x 10^-5.

## Design rule 2: from an object and an image distance
r_1^2 = lambda a b/(a + b). Book Problem 3 (PDF p. 441): central zone diameter 3 mm, so r_1 = 1.5 mm; lambda = 6000 Angstrom; a = 5 m. f = (1.5 x 10^-3)^2/(6.0 x 10^-7) = 3.75 m and 1/b = 1/3.75 - 1/5 = 0.0667 m^-1, so b = 15 m, as the book says.

## Counting zones
For a plate of outer radius R with N zones in all, N = R^2/(lambda f) and R = r_1 sqrt(N). The outermost zone has width about r_1/(2 sqrt(N)) = lambda f/(2R). Python: f = 0.50 m, 500 nm, N = 100 give R = 5.0 mm, outer width 25 micrometres, numerical aperture R/f = 0.01. A bigger plate of the same f needs finer outer zones, so good plates are made by photographic reduction (book PDF p. 417) or lithography (standard; verify).

## Open centre or opaque centre
Both kinds have the same foci. They differ by a half turn of phase at the focus, invisible in intensity.

## The image law and its signs
With a, b positive lengths, 1/a + 1/b = 1/f; in the Cartesian form (bridge B2) u = -a, v = b gives 1/v - 1/u = 1/f. If 1/f - 1/a is negative (a < f) the image is virtual, on the source side. Magnification in size is b/a. A source at a = 2f gives b = 2f; at a = f the beam leaves parallel.

## Why the axis is so bright
At the main focus every open zone sends an arrow m_1 in the same direction (successive open zones differ by lambda). The amplitude is N_t m_1, the free wave gives m_1/2, so the amplitude is N times and the intensity **N^2 times** the free value. Python sums over exact path lengths (source 5.0 m, r_1 = 1.2 mm, 600 nm, b = 4.615 m) give 400.0 for N = 20 and 1600.0 for N = 40. The plate creates no energy: it sends the open zones' light to the focus and the rest elsewhere (module 9).

## Three checks
- **Dimension:** f lambda is an area.
- **Limit:** lambda tending to 0 with fixed r_1 makes f infinite (no focusing by diffraction in geometrical optics).
- **Sanity:** f/r_1 = 1000 for f = 0.5 m and 500 nm, so the first zone is tiny against f and the small-angle algebra is safe.

### 7. Core: multiple foci, virtual foci and the images of a point source

*Objective:* Find all the real axial foci f/(2m - 1) and the matching image distances for a spherical wave, say why even fractions are dark, and state the virtual image.

## The question
A lens has one focus. For a **parallel** beam, PH-058 (section on the multiple foci of a zone plate and how it differs from a convex lens) showed bright points at f, f/3, f/5, ... because each plate zone must hold an **odd** number of half-period zones of the observation point. This module extends the result to a source at distance a, checks it by an exact sum, and adds the virtual focus.

## Step 1: the condition for brightness
The edge of plate zone n is at r_n = sqrt(n) r_1 with path excess delta_n = (r_n^2/2)(1/a + 1/b) (small angles). It holds delta_n/(lambda/2) = n q half-period zones of P, where

$$ q = r_1^2 (1/a + 1/b)/lambda = f (1/a + 1/b) $$

Odd q: each open plate zone holds an odd number of half-zones whose arrows add to about one m_1, and successive open zones differ by q lambda, a whole number of wavelengths, so they add in phase. Even q: two, four, ... half-zones cancel (m_1 - m_2 about 0) and the axis is dark. With q = 2m - 1:

$$ 1/a + 1/b_m = (2m - 1)/f, m = 1, 2, 3, ... $$

For a = infinity this is the book's f_m = f/(2m - 1) (eq. 17.10, PDF p. 419).

## Step 2: the images for the 2024 plate
r_1 = 1.2 mm, lambda = 600 nm give f = 2.4 m; source at a = 5.0 m.

| m | q | b_m (m) | b/a |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 4.615 | 0.923 |
| 2 | 3 | 0.952 | 0.190 |
| 3 | 5 | 0.531 | 0.106 |
| 4 | 7 | 0.368 | 0.074 |
| dark | 2 | 1.579 | none |

Python sums over exact path lengths for 40 zones (20 open) give 1600 I_0 at each real focus and zero at 1.579 m; the tiny drop at m = 3, 4 (1599.9, 1599.0) is a finite-N and exact-path effect.

## Step 3: "the other images are fainter"
The book says the brightness falls as the focus moves nearer (PDF p. 419). The peak axial intensity of the ideal model is the same at every odd focus, but the spot at f/(2m - 1) is narrower, so its **power** is smaller by (2m - 1)^2 (module 9). Write "fainter" in an answer; the power statement is a bonus.

## Step 4: the virtual focus
The plate also acts as a **diverging** lens (book PDF p. 420: convex and concave at once). For the same plate and source the diverging wave appears to come from

1/b = -1/f - 1/a, so b = -1/(1/2.4 + 1/5.0) = -1.622 m,

a virtual image 1.62 m from the plate on the **source side**, between the source (at -5.0 m) and the plate, negative in Cartesian form. It cannot be caught on a screen. Module 9 shows that these are other diffraction orders.

## Step 5: what a screen shows
A screen at 4.615 m sees the bright principal image over a weak background; the undiverted light and the other foci carry the energy that is not in the principal image (module 9).

## Quick table
| Source | Real foci | Dark |
|---|---|---|
| a = infinity | f, f/3, f/5, ... | f/2, f/4, ... |
| finite a | 1/b = (2m - 1)/f - 1/a | 1/b = 2/f - 1/a, 4/f - 1/a, ... |

The principal image is real only if a > f; for a < f the m = 1 image is virtual, but higher m can still be real if (2m - 1)/f > 1/a.

### 8. Core: zone plate against a convex lens, with numbers (colour, bandwidth, resolution)

*Objective:* Quantify the chromatic behaviour of a zone plate against a glass lens, state the limit on its zone count from coherence, and write a compact comparison table for an answer.

## Colour behaviour: the numbers
From f = r_1^2/lambda, f is proportional to 1/lambda. Python for r_1 = 0.6 mm:

| lambda (nm) | 400 | 486.1 (F) | 587.6 (d) | 656.3 (C) | 700 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| f (m) | 0.900 | 0.741 | 0.613 | 0.549 | 0.514 |

Violet and blue focus **farther** than red. A glass lens does the opposite: blue has the larger refractive index and focuses nearer (book PDF p. 419). For the plate f_F - f_C = +0.192 m, +31% of f at 587.6 nm. For a crown-glass lens of Abbe number about 60 (standard; verify) the spread is about -1.7% of f. The plate is roughly 17 to 19 times more chromatic, and of the opposite sign.

## The Abbe-number view
For a lens, V = (n_d - 1)/(n_F - n_C) and (f_F - f_C)/f = -1/V. The same relation holds to first order for a zone plate (exactly for the powers) with V_zp = lambda_d/(lambda_F - lambda_C) = 587.6/(486.1 - 656.3) = -3.45: negative because the dispersion is reversed, and about 17 times smaller in size than 60 (standard treatment of diffractive lenses; verify). Powers split as phi_zp/phi_glass = -V_zp/V_glass, about 5.8%, so a weak plate added to a glass lens can cancel its colour error: the idea of a diffractive-refractive hybrid (standard; verify).

## How many zones can be used: the coherence limit
The edge of zone N is N lambda/2 farther than the axis and the two lights must still interfere. The book gives the coherence length l_coh = lambda^2/(delta lambda) and N_osc = lambda/(delta lambda) oscillations in a wave train (PDF pp. 405 to 406). Demand N lambda/2 not larger than l_coh:

$$ N less than about 2 lambda/(delta lambda) $$

For delta lambda/lambda = 10^-3 about 2000 zones can be used; for 0.1 only about 20. Order of magnitude, not a sharp edge.

## Resolution
The ideal focal spot radius is about 1.22 lambda f/(2R), 1.22 times the outer zone width (standard; verify). For the plate of module 6 (outer width 25 micrometres) that is about 30 micrometres.

## Compact comparison for an answer (book items, PDF pp. 419 to 420)
| Feature | Zone plate | Convex lens |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | diffraction by zones | refraction at the surfaces |
| Foci | f, f/3, f/5 ... real; negative ones virtual | one |

### 9. Master: diffraction orders, the phase-reversal zone plate and intensity relative to the free wave

*Objective:* Explain the foci f/m as diffraction orders of a periodic structure, derive the order powers 1/(m pi)^2, and show that a phase-reversal plate gives four times the intensity of the amplitude plate at the main focus (derivation 3).

## The structure is periodic in rho squared
Let rho be the distance from the axis and u = rho^2. Zone edges are at u = n r_1^2, so open and blocked zones alternate with **period P = 2 r_1^2 in u**. Let t(u) = 1 on open zones and 0 on blocked ones (amplitude plate, centre open). Expand in a Fourier series in u:

$$ t(u) = sum over m of c_m e^(i m pi rho^2/r_1^2) $$

## Each term is a lens
The paraxial phase of a thin lens of focal length F has size pi rho^2/(lambda F). Since r_1^2 = lambda f, term m has phase m pi rho^2/(lambda f), a lens of focal length **f/m**. Orders m = +1, +3, +5 are real foci at f, f/3, f/5; m = -1, -3 are virtual foci; m = 0 is the undeviated beam (assumptions: paraxial, plane wave, many zones). This explains modules 7 and 8 in one stroke.

## The coefficients
c_m = (1/P) times the integral of e^(-i 2 pi m u/P) over the open half = (1 - (-1)^m)/(i 2 pi m). Even m (not 0): 0. So **even foci are absent**, because open and blocked parts are equal (50% duty cycle). Odd m: |c_m| = 1/(pi |m|). And c_0 = 1/2.

## Power in each order
Orders carry power |c_m|^2 (Parseval; verify): 1/4 in the undeviated beam; 1/pi^2 = 10.1% in each of m = +1, -1; 1/(9 pi^2) = 1.13% at m = +3; 1/(25 pi^2) = 0.41% at m = +5. Check: 1/4 + 2(1/pi^2)(1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + ...) = 1/4 + 2(1/pi^2)(pi^2/8) = 1/2, the open fraction; the rest is absorbed by the blocked zones. The main focus holds only about **10%** of the incident power and the f/3 focus 1/9 of that: the sharp meaning of "fainter".

## Phase-reversal zone plate
Instead of blocking the even zones, cover them with a transparent layer that delays the light by half a wavelength. Then t = +1 on odd and -1 on even zones: a square wave with no mean. Its coefficients are c_0 = 0 and |c_m| = 2/(pi |m|) for odd m. Powers: 4/pi^2 = 40.5% in each of m = +1, -1 and 4/(9 pi^2) = 4.5% at m = +3. Check: 2(4/pi^2)(pi^2/8) = 1, so **all** the light goes into odd orders, none absorbed, no undeviated beam. The main focus gets 4 times the amplitude plate's power in the same spot, so the on-axis intensity is **4 times larger**.

## The same factor 4 from arrows
N zones in all. Amplitude plate: N/2 open zones, each arrow m_1, amplitude (N/2) m_1. Phase plate: all N zones add (the extra half-wave retardation turns each even-zone arrow a further half turn, so it points the same way as the odd ones), amplitude N m_1. Free wave: m_1/2. Intensity relative to the free wave:
- amplitude plate: [(N/2) m_1/(m_1/2)]^2 = N^2;
- phase plate: [N m_1/(m_1/2)]^2 = 4 N^2.
Python (exact path sums): N = 20 gives 400 and 1600; N = 40 gives 1600 and 6400.

## Thickness of the layer
A layer of index mu and thickness t adds optical path (mu - 1) t. A half-wave retardation needs

$$ t = lambda/[2(mu - 1)] $$

For mu = 1.5 and 600 nm: t = 600 nm. The plate is perfect only at its design wavelength. With the index taken constant, a phase step phi = pi lambda_0/lambda gives order-1 efficiency (4/pi^2) sin^2(phi/2): at 500 nm for a 600 nm design, 36.7% instead of 40.5%, with 9.5% undeviated (python).

### Notes: Definitions

- Half-period zone of P: a ring of the wavefront chosen so that its edges are lambda/2 farther from P than the edges of the previous ring (Day 18 lesson PH-058).
- Zone number n of an opening: n = 2 delta/lambda, where delta is the extra length of the edge ray S-A-P over the axial path S-O-P; it need not be a whole number.
- Free wave: the wave at P with no screen; intensity I_0, amplitude m_1/2 where m_1 is the amplitude from one zone.
- Vibration circle: the circle traced by the head-to-tail chain of equal arrows (one per thin ring of equal area) whose phase turns steadily; one half-period zone is a half turn and its chord is the diameter D = m_1.
- Zone plate: a screen blocking every second half-period zone (amplitude plate); phase-reversal zone plate: every second zone covered by a layer giving a half-wave retardation.
- N: the number of zones in all (open and blocked) of a plate; N_t = N/2 the open ones; R = r_1 sqrt(N) the outer radius.
- Principal image: the real image given by order m = 1 (1/a + 1/b = 1/f); other orders give the foci f/(2m - 1) and virtual images.
- Poisson (Arago) spot: the bright point at the centre of the geometrical shadow of a round opaque disc.

### Notes: Equations to know

- n = 2 delta/lambda = (r^2/lambda)(1/a + 1/b); plane wave n = r^2/(b lambda).
- Hole: A(n) = D sin(pi n/2), A_0 = D/2, so I/I_0 = 4 sin^2(pi n/2); maxima 4 at odd n, zeros at even n.
- Hole radius for n zones: r_n = sqrt(n lambda a b/(a + b)); screen distance for a plane wave b = r^2/(n lambda); spherical wave 1/b = n lambda/r^2 - 1/a.
- Disc covering k zones: I_centre = I_0 (ideal); hole + disc amplitudes = free amplitude (Babinet idea).
- Zone plate: r_n = sqrt(n) r_1, r_1 = sqrt(f lambda), f = r_1^2/lambda; N = R^2/(lambda f); outer zone width about lambda f/(2R).
- Image law: 1/a + 1/b = 1/f (real-positive form) or 1/v - 1/u = 1/f (Cartesian, u = -a, v = b); magnification size b/a.
- Foci: 1/a + 1/b_m = (2m - 1)/f; parallel beam f_m = f/(2m - 1); virtual 1/b = -(2m - 1)/f - 1/a.
- Chromatic: f proportional to 1/lambda; V_zp = lambda_d/(lambda_F - lambda_C) = -3.45 (standard; verify).

### Memory skeleton

- Zone number n = 2δ/λ = (r²/λ)(1/a + 1/b); plane wave n = r²/(bλ); n may be fractional
- Axial centre of a hole: odd n bright, even n dark; r is a radius, never a diameter
- Moving the screen: plane wave bright at r²/λ, r²/(3λ), …, farthest dark r²/(2λ); finite source 1/b = nλ/r² − 1/a
- Zone plate design: r₁ = √(fλ), r_n = √n r₁, N = R²/(λf)
- Images: 1/a + 1/b = 1/f; foci 1/a + 1/b_m = (2m − 1)/f; virtual image 1/b = −1/f − 1/a
- Colour: f = r₁²/λ, so red focuses nearest and violet farthest, the reverse of a lens
- 2024: diameter 2.4 mm → r₁ = 1.2 mm, f = 2.4 m, source 5.0 m, image 4.615 m
