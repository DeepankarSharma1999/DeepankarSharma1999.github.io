## PH-022: Elastic constants and interrelations

Physics Paper I · Physics · Continuous media

### Bridge: First-order arithmetic, the moment of a force and the integral of x cubed

**1. First-order arithmetic.** A strain is a tiny number, such as 0.001. When two such numbers multiply, the result (0.000001) is a thousand times smaller and is dropped. So (1 + a)(1 + b) = 1 + a + b + ab is written 1 + a + b, and (1 + a)ⁿ is written 1 + n a. Check: 1.001³ = 1.003003, and 1 + 3(0.001) = 1.003. This is how a volume change is obtained from three edge changes.

**2. Moment (torque) of a force.** A force F acting at perpendicular distance d from an axis turns the body with moment F d (unit N m). Two equal and opposite forces F with different lines of action form a **couple** of moment F times the distance between the lines. In twisting, 'couple' and 'torque' mean the same thing in this lesson, written τ.

**3. Integral.** ∫₀ᴿ x³ dx = R⁴/4, because the antiderivative of x³ is x⁴/4. A ring of radius x and thickness dx has area 2π x dx (circumference times width); adding rings from the axis to the rim is how the twisting couple is built in module 7. Units: 1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa = 10⁹ N/m²; 1 dyne/cm² = 0.1 Pa.

### 2. Foundation: Y, K, n and Poisson's ratio with signs, units and isotropy

*Objective:* Define the three moduli and Poisson's ratio, fix their sign conventions and dimensions, and explain why a liquid has K but not n.

**Isotropic** means the material responds in the same way along every direction (as a fine-grained polycrystalline metal does, to a good approximation). Only for such a material do two constants suffice; a single crystal needs more (standard knowledge, flagged in completeness).

A body can be deformed in three basic ways, and each has a modulus (stress divided by the strain it produces, within the elastic limit):

- **Stretch (a simple tension).** A force F normal to a face of area A, with the sides free to contract. Stress S = F/A; strain ε = ΔL/L; **Young's modulus** Y = S/ε = FL/(AΔL).
- **Squeeze (hydrostatic pressure).** The same pressure p on every face. The volume V changes by ΔV (negative under compression) with no change of shape. **Bulk modulus** K = −p/(ΔV/V). The minus sign makes K positive: p and ΔV have opposite signs (NCERT 8.5.3, Mathur 13.7). The reciprocal 1/K is the **compressibility**.
- **Slide (a shear).** A tangential force F on a face of area A; layers slide over one another, changing the shape but not the volume. The shear strain φ is the angle (radians) through which a line perpendicular to the fixed face turns: φ = Δx/L ≈ tan φ for a small angle (Δx is the sideways shift of the top face, L the height). The **rigidity modulus** n = (F/A)/φ. NCERT calls it the shear modulus G; Upadhyaya writes η. All three symbols mean the same quantity.

**Poisson's ratio.** A stretched bar grows longer and thinner. Extension is a positive strain and the lateral strain is negative, so Poisson's ratio is defined with a minus sign:

$$ σ = −(lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain) = −(Δd/d)/(ΔL/L) $$

It is positive for ordinary solids, whose width shrinks as the length grows, and negative for auxetic materials (module 6). The books quote a ratio of magnitudes, which gives the same value for ordinary solids. NCERT quotes 0.28 to 0.30 for steels and about 0.33 for aluminium alloys; Mathur says most materials lie between 0.2 and 0.4.

**Dimensions and units.** Strains, φ and σ are ratios, so they have no dimension and no unit. Y, K, n and S have dimension ML⁻¹T⁻² and unit pascal (Pa = N/m²). Typical solids: steel Y ≈ 2 × 10¹¹ Pa (the value in the NCERT worked examples), G = 84 GPa and K = 160 GPa (NCERT tables, separate samples, so do not combine them casually).

**Sign conventions used throughout.** Axes x, y, z run along the edges of a cube. A tensile stress is positive; a compressive stress is negative. An extension is a positive strain. Pressure p is positive when it pushes inward, and then ΔV < 0.

**Why a liquid has K but not n.** A liquid or gas at rest can sustain a pressure but not a lasting tangential force: it flows. So the only elasticity they have is K (Mathur 13.7).

### 3. Core: deriving Y = 3K(1 − 2σ) from a cube under three stresses

*Objective:* Derive the volume strain of a cube under three normal stresses and from it the relation between Y, K and σ, with checks.

**Setting.** An isotropic unit cube (edge 1) with edges along x, y, z. Normal tensile stresses S_x, S_y, S_z act on the three pairs of opposite faces. Strains are small, so effects add (superposition) and second-order products are dropped (bridge B1).

**Step 1: two constants per unit stress.** A stress S along one edge stretches that edge by α S and, perpendicular to it, contracts each other edge by β S. By the definition of Y, α = 1/Y. By the definition of σ, σ = β/α, so β = σ/Y.

**Step 2: the three edges.** Edge along x: stretched by α S_x, shortened by β S_y and β S_z. Hence

$$ length_x = 1 + α S_x − β S_y − β S_z $$

and likewise length_y = 1 + α S_y − β S_x − β S_z, length_z = 1 + α S_z − β S_x − β S_y.

**Step 3: the volume.** Multiply the three lengths and keep only first-order terms:

$$ V = 1 + (α − 2β)(S_x + S_y + S_z) ,    ΔV/V = (α − 2β)(S_x + S_y + S_z) $$

Collect the S_x terms: α from the x edge, and −β from each of the other two, giving (α − 2β) S_x; the same for S_y and S_z. (Mathur's printed line has a sign slip, S_x + S_y − S_z; the sum of all three is correct.)

**Step 4: hydrostatic case.** Let each face carry a pressure p inward, that is S_x = S_y = S_z = −p. Then

$$ ΔV/V = −3p(α − 2β) $$

and by the definition of K,

$$ K = −p/(ΔV/V) = 1/[3(α − 2β)] $$

**Step 5: substitute.** α − 2β = (1 − 2σ)/Y, so K = Y/[3(1 − 2σ)], that is

$$ Y = 3K(1 − 2σ) $$

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* both sides are pascals, (1 − 2σ) being a pure number. *Consistency with PH-021:* for a wire, S_y = S_z = 0 and Step 3 gives ΔV/V = α S_x(1 − 2σ) = ε(1 − 2σ), exactly the module 7 result of PH-021. *Number:* steel with σ = 0.3 and Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa gives K = 2 × 10¹¹/(3 × 0.4) = 1.67 × 10¹¹ Pa, close to NCERT's 1.6 × 10¹¹ Pa (different samples). *Limit:* as σ → ½, K → ∞: no volume change whatever the stress, an incompressible material (rubber is the usual model; Upadhyaya example 11 finds σ = ½ directly from V = π r² l constant).

**Alternative method.** The three edge equations combine into one compact statement, ε_x = (1/Y)[S_x − σ(S_y + S_z)] and its cyclic partners (the generalised Hooke's law); adding the three gives ΔV/V = [(1 − 2σ)/Y](S_x + S_y + S_z). Every relation of this lesson can be read from this formula.

### 4. Core: a pure shear is a tension plus an equal compression at right angles

*Objective:* Show by geometry and by force resolution that a shear equals a diagonal extension and an equal diagonal compression, each of half the shear strain and the same stress as the shear.

**Setting.** Look at the face of a cube of edge L as a square D C (bottom, fixed) and A B (top). Coordinates: D = (0, 0), C = (L, 0), A = (0, L), B = (L, L); x to the right, y upward. A tangential force F in the +x direction acts on the top face; the bottom face is held. The top shifts sideways by l, so the shear strain is φ = l/L (small).

**Part 1: geometry of the strain.** After shearing, B = (L + l, L) and A = (l, L). The diagonal DB had length L√2. Its new length is

$$ DB′ = √[(L + l)² + L²] = L√(2 + 2φ + φ²) = L√2 √(1 + φ + φ²/2) ≈ L√2 (1 + φ/2) $$

using (1 + a)^(½) ≈ 1 + a/2 and dropping φ². So the diagonal DB is stretched by L√2 · φ/2, a **tensile strain φ/2**. The other diagonal AC runs from A′ = (l, L) to C = (L, 0): its length is √[(L − l)² + L²] ≈ L√2(1 − φ/2), a **compressive strain φ/2**. (Mathur and Upadhyaya reach the same result with a 45° right-angled triangle: the diagonal's change is l cos 45° = l/√2 over L√2, which is l/2L = φ/2.)

**Part 2: the forces.** The force F on the top face and the equal and opposite force on the bottom face form a clockwise couple F L. The block does not rotate, so a balancing anticlockwise couple F L must exist; it comes from tangential forces F on the two side faces (right face upward, left face downward). Thus **every face carries a tangential force F**: the shearing stress q = F/L² acts on all four faces.

**Part 3: the stress on a diagonal plane.** At corner B the forces F (top face, +x) and F (right face, +y) have the resultant F√2 pointing outward along DB; at corner A the forces F (+x) and F (left face, −y) have the resultant F√2 pointing inward along AC. Now cut the square along the diagonal AC and look at the triangle containing B. The external forces on it are F (+x) on AB and F (+y) on BC, with resultant F√2 along DB. Equilibrium needs the remaining part of the body to act on the cut face AC (area L√2 × L) with an equal and opposite force F√2, directed from B towards D, that is, along the outward normal of the cut face; a force along the outward normal is a **tension**:

$$ S_DB = F√2/(L√2 · L) = F/L² = q $$

Cutting along BD and examining the triangle containing A in the same way gives a compressive normal stress of the same size F/L² along AC. So **a shearing stress q equals a tensile stress q along one diagonal and a compressive stress q along the perpendicular diagonal.** (The books describe the same cut in words; the triangle bookkeeping above shows why the stress on the plane perpendicular to a diagonal is the stress 'along' that diagonal.)

**Check.** For a general stress state with S_DB = +q and S_AC = −q, the volume change is (α − 2β)(q − q) = 0, from module 3 with the third stress zero; this agrees with the statement that a shear changes the shape but not the volume.

### 5. Core: deriving n = Y/[2(1 + σ)] from the pure-shear equivalence

*Objective:* Combine the strain read off the geometry with the strain computed from the equivalent stresses to obtain the relation between n, Y and σ.

**Setting.** The block of module 4, with a pure shear of stress q (so q = nφ by definition of n). Equivalent stress state: tension q along DB, compression q along AC, nothing else. Strains remain small and the effects superpose.

**Step 1: the strain along DB from the stresses.** The tension q stretches DB by the fraction α q = q/Y. The compression q along AC makes the perpendicular direction DB stretch as well (the lateral effect has the opposite sign of the compression): the extra strain is β q = σ q/Y. Total:

$$ ε_DB = q/Y + σ q/Y = q(1 + σ)/Y $$

This is the same as the general formula ε = (1/Y)[S_DB − σ S_AC] with S_DB = q and S_AC = −q.

**Step 2: the same strain from the geometry.** Module 4 gave ε_DB = φ/2.

**Step 3: equate.** φ/2 = q(1 + σ)/Y, so q/φ = Y/[2(1 + σ)]. Since n = q/φ,

$$ n = Y/[2(1 + σ)]  ,    Y = 2n(1 + σ) $$

Mathur and Upadhyaya present the same step as: the diagonal's total extension is L√2 · q(α + β) (their α and β are per unit stress), and equating it to the geometric change l/√2 gives n = 1/[2(α + β)], then α = 1/Y and β = σ/Y give the result.

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* n and Y are both pascals. *Volume:* the stresses +q and −q give zero volume change, consistent with a shear. *Limits:* for σ = 0, n = Y/2; for σ = ½ (incompressible), n = Y/3. Hence for 0 ≤ σ ≤ ½ the ratio n/Y lies between 1/3 and 1/2, consistent with NCERT's remark that G is generally less than Y and for most materials about Y/3. *Number:* iron with Y = 20 × 10¹¹ and n = 8 × 10¹¹ dyne/cm² (Mathur prints N/m², a unit slip; in SI 2.0 × 10¹¹ and 8 × 10¹⁰ Pa) gives σ = Y/(2n) − 1 = 0.25 (Mathur 13.13); steel with Y = 2 × 10¹¹ and n = 8 × 10¹⁰ also gives σ = 0.25 (Upadhyaya example 10). The check from the other side: Y = 2n(1 + σ) = 2 × 8 × 10¹⁰ × 1.25 = 2 × 10¹¹ ✓.

**Physical meaning.** A shear involves no volume change, so it senses only how readily the material changes shape; Young's modulus also involves the lateral contraction. The factor (1 + σ) records that a stretching stress produces its extension partly through the contraction in the other direction.

**Caution (Mathur's note).** These relations hold for an isotropic bulk material. For a drawn wire the outer layers are harder than the inner, n is relatively larger, and the relations are not reliable.

### 6. Master: all the relations, and why −1 < σ < ½

*Objective:* Eliminate σ to get Y = 9Kn/(3K + n), solve for σ in terms of K and n, prove the limits of σ with the positivity of K and n, and use the relations in numerical problems.

**Relations in hand.** (1) Y = 3K(1 − 2σ); (2) Y = 2n(1 + σ).

**Y in terms of K and n.** Rearrange (1) as 1 − 2σ = Y/(3K) and (2) as 2(1 + σ) = Y/n. Add them: the σ terms cancel (−2σ + 2σ), leaving 1 + 2 = 3 on the left:

$$ 3 = Y/(3K) + Y/n = Y(n + 3K)/(3Kn) $$

$$ Y = 9Kn/(3K + n)  ,    9/Y = 3/n + 1/K $$

**σ in terms of K and n.** Equate the two expressions for Y: 3K(1 − 2σ) = 2n(1 + σ). Expand: 3K − 6Kσ = 2n + 2nσ. Collect σ: σ(6K + 2n) = 3K − 2n, so

$$ σ = (3K − 2n)/[2(3K + n)] $$

**Limits of σ.** The books assert that K and n are positive (a positive pressure must decrease the volume, and a shear must be resisted by the material). A further reason, standard knowledge and flagged for verification, is that the stored energy densities ½K(ΔV/V)² and ½nφ² must be positive for a stable solid. Then: if σ > 0, the right side 2n(1 + σ) of 3K(1 − 2σ) = 2n(1 + σ) is positive, so 1 − 2σ > 0, giving σ < ½; if σ < 0, the left side is positive since 1 − 2σ > 1, so the right side must be positive, giving 1 + σ > 0, that is σ > −1. A one-line version: put r = 3K/n > 0 in the formula, so σ = (r − 2)/[2(r + 1)]. Its derivative 3/[2(r + 1)²] is positive, so σ rises steadily from −1 (r → 0) to ½ (r → ∞) and takes every value strictly between. Hence the theoretical limits are

$$ −1 < σ < ½ $$

**Reading the limits.** σ → ½ means K/n → ∞: the solid resists volume change infinitely more than shape change (rubber-like), and Y → 3n. σ → −1 means K/n → 0: it resists shape change infinitely more than volume change, never seen in practice. Real solids lie near 0.2 to 0.4 (Mathur). A negative σ is not forbidden by these limits: materials that widen when stretched (auxetics, such as some engineered foams) exist; this is standard knowledge outside the extracts and is flagged for verification.

**Numerical practice.** Silver, Y = 7.25 × 10¹⁰, K = 11 × 10¹⁰ Pa (Mathur 13.15, Upadhyaya example 8): σ = ½ − Y/(6K) = 0.5 − 0.1098 = 0.390. A solid whose volume does not change under pressure has K → ∞, so 1 − 2σ = Y/(3K) → 0 and σ = ½ (Mathur 13.16). Steel (Y = 2 × 10¹¹, n = 8 × 10¹⁰): σ = 0.25 and K = Y/[3(1 − 2σ)] = 1.33 × 10¹¹ Pa (Upadhyaya example 10). With K = 1.6 × 10¹¹ and n = 8.4 × 10¹⁰ (the NCERT steel values): Y = 9Kn/(3K + n) = 2.14 × 10¹¹ Pa and σ = 0.277.

### 7. Core: torsion of a cylinder, the twisting couple τ = πnR⁴θ/2L

*Objective:* Derive the couple needed to twist a solid cylinder through θ, find the stress distribution, and interpret the R⁴ dependence.

The couple per unit twist of a wire is derived in PH-017 module 8 (torsion pendulum). A four-step recap follows because both PYQs need it; the new content here is the stress distribution q(x), the share of the couple carried by the inner half, and the shaft context.

**Setting.** A solid cylinder (or wire) of radius R and length L has its upper end fixed. A couple τ acts on the lower end in a plane perpendicular to the axis (the z-axis along the cylinder) and twists it through the small angle θ (radians). **Assumptions:** the material is isotropic and obeys Hooke's law; θ is small; each cross-section turns rigidly, so radii stay straight; the shear is the pure shear of module 4 in planes along the axis. Source: Mathur 13.17 (the Upadhyaya extract, ch. 4, has no torsion section).

**Recap in four steps (the ring integral of PH-017 module 8).**

1. *Strain.* On the tube of radius x, a line AB parallel to the axis turns into AB′, with arc BB′ = xθ over length L, so φ(x) = xθ/L.
2. *Stress.* q(x) = nφ = n x θ/L, directed along the circle.
3. *Ring.* The end area is 2π x dx, so dτ = x · q · 2π x dx = (2π n θ/L) x³ dx.
4. *Sum.*

$$ τ = (2π n θ/L) ∫₀ᴿ x³ dx = π n R⁴ θ/(2L) ,    C = τ/θ = π n R⁴/(2L) $$

**New here: the stress distribution.** q rises linearly from zero on the axis to q_max = nRθ/L = 2τ/(πR³) at the rim. It does not depend on the distance from the fixed end (Mathur 13.18: a section at distance αL has twist αθ and the same φ). The material near the axis hardly works, which motivates the hollow shaft of module 8.

**Interpretation.** Because dτ ∝ x³ dx, the part inside half the radius carries only (½)⁴ = 6.25 % of the couple. Doubling the radius multiplies C by 16.

**Checks.** *Dimensions:* n R⁴/L is (ML⁻¹T⁻²)(L³) = ML²T⁻², the dimension of a couple (θ is dimensionless). *Limits:* C → 0 as R → 0, and C varies as 1/L, so a long thin fibre twists easily. *Numerical:* a steel wire with n = 8 × 10¹⁰ Pa, R = 0.5 mm and L = 1 m has C = π × 8 × 10¹⁰ × (5 × 10⁻⁴)⁴/2 = 7.85 × 10⁻³ N m per radian.

### 8. Core: hollow shafts, strain energy of twist and power transmission

*Objective:* Obtain the hollow-cylinder couple, prove a hollow shaft is stiffer than a solid one of equal mass, derive the twisting energy ½Cθ², and connect the twisting couple to transmitted power.

**Hollow cylinder.** For inner and outer radii R₁ and R₂, the same ring integral runs from R₁ to R₂:

$$ τ = (2π n θ/L) ∫_{R₁}^{R₂} x³ dx = π n θ (R₂⁴ − R₁⁴)/(2L) ,    C′ = π n (R₂⁴ − R₁⁴)/(2L) $$

**Hollow versus solid, equal mass.** Same material, length and mass means equal cross-section area: π(R₂² − R₁²) = π R², where R is the radius of the solid shaft. Hence R₂² − R₁² = R². Now factorise the fourth powers:

$$ C′/C = (R₂⁴ − R₁⁴)/R⁴ = (R₂² + R₁²)(R₂² − R₁²)/R⁴ = (R₂² + R₁²)/R² $$

Since R₂² = R² + R₁², the numerator is R² + 2R₁² > R², so C′/C > 1: **the hollow shaft is stiffer**, because the material is moved to a larger radius where it carries more couple. (In the extract Mathur's ratio line is a garbled fraction, an extraction ambiguity rather than a book slip; the book conclusion C′ > C is correct.) For R₂ = 3 cm and R₁ = 2 cm: R² = 9 − 4 = 5 cm² and C′/C = 13/5 = 2.6. Limit: R₁ → 0 gives C′/C → 1, as it should.

**Strain energy of twist.** The energy per unit volume in shear is q²/(2n) (the form ½ × stress × strain of PH-021 module 5). With q = n x θ/L and a ring of volume 2π x L dx:

$$ U = ∫₀ᴿ [n x²θ²/(2L²)] (2π x L dx) = (π n θ²/L) ∫₀ᴿ x³ dx = π n R⁴ θ²/(4L) = ½ C θ² $$

This agrees with the work done by a couple that grows linearly from 0 to τ = Cθ: ∫ τ dθ = ½ C θ². It is half the energy that would be stored if the whole shaft carried the rim stress throughout, as Mathur 13.19 notes.

**Power transmission.** A shaft turning at angular speed ω (rad/s) while transmitting a steady couple τ delivers power

$$ P = τ ω  ,    so  τ = P/ω  ,    ω = 2π N/60  (N in revolutions per minute) $$

*Why:* in time dt the shaft turns through dθ = ω dt and the couple does work τ dθ. Twisting energy is not the work done per revolution: the shaft turns continuously, and the strain energy ½Cθ² remains stored at the fixed twist, while the work τ × 2π per revolution is passed on to the load. Confusing the two is the slip in Mathur example 13.23 (module 9).

**Steps in a power-transmission problem.** (1) Convert N to ω. (2) τ = P/ω. (3) θ = τ/C = 2Lτ/(π n R⁴). (4) Quote θ in radians (the 'relative shift' between the ends); if a linear shift of the rim is asked, the arc is Rθ.

### 9. Exam: the 2020 and 2025 shaft questions, solved and presented

## 2020 Q2(b), 15 marks

*Question (verified wording):* a shaft of diameter 8 cm and length 5 m transmits 8 kW at 300 revolutions per minute; the coefficient of rigidity is 8 × 10¹¹ dynes/cm²; find the relative shift between the ends.

**Data in SI.** R = 0.04 m, L = 5 m, P = 8000 W, n = 8 × 10¹¹ dyne/cm² = 8 × 10¹⁰ Pa.

1. ω = 2π × 300/60 = 31.42 rad/s.
2. τ = P/ω = 8000/31.42 = 254.6 N m.
3. C = π n R⁴/(2L) = π × 8 × 10¹⁰ × 2.56 × 10⁻⁶/10 = 6.434 × 10⁴ N m/rad.
4. θ = τ/C = 254.6/(6.434 × 10⁴) = **3.96 × 10⁻³ rad**, the relative angular twist between the ends.

**Reading the question.** 'Relative shift' is the twist angle θ; if a distance is wanted, it is the rim displacement Rθ = 0.04 × 3.96 × 10⁻³ = 1.58 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.158 mm. Mathur example 13.19 (which the paper reproduces) multiplies the angle by the length, 0.004 × 500 cm = 2.0 cm; that product has no physical meaning, so do not copy it. **Extra checks:** maximum shear angle at the rim φ = Rθ/L = 3.17 × 10⁻⁵ and maximum stress nφ = 2.53 × 10⁶ Pa, far below the elastic limit of steel; stored twisting energy ½τθ = 0.50 J.

## 2025 Q4(c), 15 marks

*Question (verified wording, abridged):* a solid shaft of mass M, length l and radius r is replaced by a lighter hollow shaft of the same length and material and the same τ/θ; find the percentage reduction in mass if the outer radius is twice the inner radius.

**Reading the question.** 'The same ratings of τ/θ' means the same torsional rigidity C. Same l and n, so C ∝ R⁴ (solid) and ∝ R₂⁴ − R₁⁴ (hollow).

1. Equal C: R₂⁴ − R₁⁴ = r⁴. With R₂ = 2R₁: 16R₁⁴ − R₁⁴ = 15R₁⁴ = r⁴, so R₁ = r/15^¼ = 0.508 r and R₂ = 1.016 r.
2. Masses are proportional to cross-sectional areas: hollow π(R₂² − R₁²) = 3π R₁² = 3π r²/√15; solid π r².
3. Mass ratio = 3/√15 = 0.7746.
4. Reduction = (1 − 0.7746) × 100 = **22.5 %**.

**Check.** Equal-mass hollow shafts are stiffer (module 8); here equal stiffness requires less mass, so a saving is expected ✓. Limit: if R₁ → 0, the hollow shaft becomes the solid one and the saving → 0.

### Notes: Definitions and symbols

- Isotropic: properties the same in every direction; only two of Y, K, n, σ are then independent.
- Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L): normal stress per unit longitudinal strain, lateral contraction allowed. Dimension ML⁻¹T⁻², unit Pa.
- K = −p/(ΔV/V): hydrostatic pressure per unit volume strain; the minus sign makes K positive. 1/K is the compressibility.
- n (also η, G) = (F/A)/φ: tangential stress per unit shear strain; φ is an angle in radians. Called modulus of rigidity, shear modulus or torsion modulus.
- σ = −(lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain) = −(Δd/d)/(ΔL/L): positive for ordinary solids (width shrinks as length grows), negative for auxetic materials; dimensionless.
- Axial modulus Z (Mathur 13.7(4)): longitudinal stress per unit linear strain when lateral strain is prevented. Standard result, not in the extracts and flagged: Z = Y(1 − σ)/[(1 + σ)(1 − 2σ)]; check Z > Y for σ > 0.
- Torsional rigidity C = τ/θ: couple per unit angle of twist; for a solid cylinder C = π n R⁴/2L.

### Notes: The relations at a glance

- Y = 3K(1 − 2σ)
- Y = 2n(1 + σ)
- Y = 9Kn/(3K + n),  equivalently 9/Y = 3/n + 1/K
- σ = (3K − 2n)/[2(3K + n)] = Y/(2n) − 1 = 1/2 − Y/(6K)
- K = Y/[3(1 − 2σ)],   n = Y/[2(1 + σ)]
- Limits: −1 < σ < ½; real solids about 0.2 to 0.4.
- Torsion: τ = π n R⁴ θ/(2L);  C = π n R⁴/(2L);  hollow C′ = π n (R₂⁴ − R₁⁴)/(2L);  energy U = ½Cθ²;  power P = τω.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- n, η and G are the same constant; Y is the same as E in engineering books; Poisson's ratio σ is not a stress.
- A shear involves no volume change; a hydrostatic pressure involves no shape change: that is why the three moduli are different quantities.
- Shearing stress q on a face equals the diagonal tension and compression stress q, but the diagonal strain is φ/2, not φ.
- Twisting energy ½Cθ² is not the work per revolution of a rotating shaft, which is τ × 2π.
- σ = ½ is the incompressible limit (K → ∞, Y = 3n); σ = 0 gives n = Y/2.

### Core concepts

- Four constants Y, K, n, σ describe an isotropic solid; only two are independent.
- Shear = diagonal tension plus equal diagonal compression; stress equal to q, strain φ/2.
- Torsional rigidity C = π n R⁴/2L; the outer material carries most of the couple.

### Practice

**Q.** Write the three modulus formulas and state which strain each uses.

**A.** Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) longitudinal; K = −p/(ΔV/V) volume; n = (F/A)/φ shear angle.

**Q.** Why is K defined with a minus sign?

**A.** Pressure and volume change have opposite signs; the minus sign makes K positive.

**Q.** What is the unit of n and the unit of σ?

**A.** n: pascal; σ has no unit.

**Q.** Explain in words why a shear changes shape but not volume, using the diagonal picture.

**A.** One diagonal lengthens by φ/2 and the other shortens by φ/2, so the first-order volume change is zero.

**Q.** Why does a long thin wire twist easily?

**A.** C = π n R⁴/2L: C is proportional to R⁴ and to 1/L.

**Q.** What does σ → −1 mean physically in terms of K and n?

**A.** K/n → 0: the solid resists shape change infinitely more than volume change.

### Memory skeleton

- Isotropic: only two of Y, K, n, σ independent; σ = −(Δd/d)/(ΔL/L)
- Cube under S_x, S_y, S_z: α = 1/Y, β = σ/Y; hydrostatic gives Y = 3K(1 − 2σ)
- Pure shear = tension q plus compression q on the diagonals; diagonal strain φ/2
- Equate diagonal strains: Y = 2n(1 + σ)
- Eliminate σ: Y = 9Kn/(3K + n); σ = (3K − 2n)/[2(3K + n)]; −1 < σ < ½
- Torsion: φ = xθ/L, ring integral, τ = πnR⁴θ/(2L); hollow shafts; P = τω
