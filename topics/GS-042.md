## GS-042: Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words for the Rowlatt crisis

Eight words carry this topic.

A **bill** is a proposed law; it becomes an **Act** once passed. The **Imperial Legislative Council** was the central law-making body of British India, with official nominees who could outvote the elected Indian members. **Habeas corpus** is the old legal safeguard that a person held by the state must be brought before a court so that the detention can be examined; the Rowlatt law was said to suspend it. **Detention without trial** means imprisonment with no court verdict.

A **hartal** is a general shutdown of shops, offices and work as a protest. **Satyagraha** is Gandhi's method of resisting a wrong by truth and non-violence, with readiness to suffer (the fuller definition is in GS-041-B1). **Martial law** is rule by the army in place of ordinary civil authority, with the commander able to issue binding orders. An **inquiry committee** is a body appointed to examine events and report; its power is to recommend, not to punish.

Keep the order of events in one line: a harsh law, a peaceful protest, violence in Punjab, martial law, the massacre, an inquiry, and a turn in the national movement.

### 1. Orientation: from a harsh law to a massacre, 1919

*Objective:* Place the Rowlatt Act, the satyagraha, Jallianwala Bagh and the inquiries in one timeline.

In March 1919 the Government of India passed the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, popularly the **Rowlatt Act**. Gandhi called a nationwide protest. In April 1919 the protest turned violent in several places, above all in Punjab. On **13 April 1919**, the day of Baisakhi, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd in the **Jallianwala Bagh**, Amritsar. The nation was stunned, an official inquiry followed, and by 1920 the political ground had shifted towards Non-Cooperation.

| When | Event |
|---|---|
| 1915 | Defence of India Regulations Act, the wartime law the Rowlatt Act was meant to replace |
| Before 1919 | Rowlatt committee (Sir Sidney Rowlatt) on 'seditious conspiracy' |
| March 1919 | Rowlatt Act passed over the votes of all elected Indian members |
| 6 April 1919 | Hartal and 'humiliation and prayer' called by Gandhi |
| 9 or 10 April 1919 | Arrest of Kitchlew and Satyapal in Amritsar (sources differ; verify) |
| 13 April 1919 | Firing at Jallianwala Bagh |
| 18 April 1919 | Gandhi withdraws the movement |
| 14 October 1919 | Disorders Inquiry Committee (Hunter) announced |
| March 1920 | Hunter report released and Dyer relieved (Spectrum; verify) |

Three points matter most. First, the Rowlatt Satyagraha was Gandhi's first countrywide struggle and, in NCERT's phrase, the first all-India struggle against the government although largely restricted to cities. Second, Jallianwala Bagh was not the whole of the Punjab story: martial law, floggings and humiliations followed. Third, the aftermath, including the Hunter inquiry and the support Dyer received in Britain, hardened Indian opinion.

### 2. Foundation: the war, the carrot and the stick

*Objective:* Explain why 1919 felt like a betrayal and where the Rowlatt Act came from.

India supported Britain in the First World War with men and money, and Indian leaders expected a reward. Spectrum describes the British reply as 'carrot and stick'. The carrot was the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (announced July 1918 and enacted as the Government of India Act, 1919), which Spectrum calls insubstantial. The stick was measures like the Rowlatt Act. Spectrum says the government wanted to arm itself with extraordinary powers just when it was introducing the reforms, to suppress any voice against them.

## The wartime law

During the war the British had put the **Defence of India Regulations Act, 1915** into force. NCERT (Class XII) says that during the war the British had instituted censorship of the press and permitted detention without trial, and that the Rowlatt measures continued these tough measures. Spectrum says the object of the 1919 law was to replace the wartime Defence of India Act with a permanent law, so that restrictions on speech and assembly came back in peacetime.

## The Rowlatt committee

The law rested on a committee headed by a British judge, **Sir Sidney Rowlatt**, appointed to investigate 'seditious conspiracy'. Spectrum says it reported the previous year (1918) to the Legislative Council, recommending that activists be deported or imprisoned without trial for two years and that possession of seditious newspapers count as adequate evidence. The appointment date and the names of the other committee members are standard knowledge not in the extracts (verify).

## Why Punjab was especially tense

NCERT (Class XII) says the protests were particularly intense in the Punjab, where many men had served the British in the war expecting a reward and instead were given the Rowlatt Act. Spectrum adds that Punjab was explosive because of wartime repression, forcible recruitments and the ravages of disease, so that the army had to be called in.

## Post-war pressure

Spectrum's wider chapter gives the setting: post-war price rises, taxes and falling production, disillusionment with imperialism and the shadow of the Russian Revolution. Gandhi, who had offered cooperation and even recruitment, felt betrayed. He called the act the 'Black Act' (Spectrum), while NCERT Class VIII says he and Jinnah criticised it as 'devilish' and tyrannical.

## Learn the pairing

- Wartime law: Defence of India Act, 1915.
- Peacetime replacement: Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919.
- Person to attach: Sir Sidney Rowlatt, a judge, not a politician.
- Popular name: the Rowlatt Act, or the 'Black Act' in Gandhi's phrase.

## Common confusion

The name Rowlatt belongs to the committee and the law that followed it; it does not name the satyagraha's date or a rebel. And the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms and the Rowlatt Act came in the same years (1918-19), and Spectrum treats them as the carrot and the stick of one policy.

### 3. Core: the Rowlatt Act and its provisions

*Objective:* State what the Act allowed and how the legislative council reacted.

Spectrum says two bills were introduced in the Imperial Legislative Council 'just six months before the Montford Reforms were to be put into effect' (Spectrum's words; the bills came in February 1919, the Government of India Act was passed in December 1919 and the reforms took effect in 1921, so read the phrase loosely; verify). One was dropped; the other, an extension of the Defence of India Regulations Act, 1915, was passed in March 1919. Its official title was the **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act** (the standard date is 18 March 1919; verify). It was popularly called the Rowlatt Act after the committee.

## What the Act allowed

Spectrum lists the powers:

- Political activists could be tried **without juries** or even imprisoned without trial.
- Indians could be arrested **without a warrant** on mere suspicion of treason.
- Suspects could be tried **in secret**, without legal help.
- A special court of **three high court judges** would try such suspects, with **no appeal** above that panel.
- That panel could accept **evidence not admissible under the Indian Evidence Act**.
- The law of **habeas corpus**, the basis of civil liberty, was sought to be suspended.
- Strict control over the press returned, as did wartime limits on speech and assembly.

NCERT Class VIII summarises it as an Act that curbed freedom of expression and strengthened police powers.

## The Council vote

Spectrum says all elected Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council voted against the bill, but being a minority they were outvoted by the official nominees. Spectrum says the elected members resigned in protest, naming Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Madan Mohan Malaviya and Mazhar-ul-Haq; standard accounts name these three among those who resigned rather than every elected member (verify). The detail teaches that the legislative route was shut: the Act passed over the unanimous Indian elected opinion.

## Why Indians objected

The ground of objection was not that revolutionary crime should go unpunished but that punishment without trial, evidence or appeal was unjust. Gandhi argued, in Spectrum's account, that not everyone should be punished for isolated political crimes. A popular slogan summing up the objection (no lawyer, no appeal, no argument) is standard knowledge not in the extracts (verify the wording).

## Provisions at a glance

| Feature | What the Act did | Principle violated |
|---|---|---|
| Trial | Special panel of three judges, no jury, secret | Open and fair trial |
| Appeal | None above the panel | Right of appeal |
| Evidence | Admissible even if the Evidence Act barred it | Rules of evidence |
| Arrest | Without warrant on suspicion | Protection from arbitrary arrest |
| Detention | Without trial | Habeas corpus |

## Source and date notes

Spectrum's chapter summary prints 'Rowlatt Satyagraha (1918)'; the chapter text (Spectrum PDF p368) says the Act passed in March 1919 and NCERT Class XII lists the satyagraha as March to April 1919. Teach 1919. Also note that Spectrum describes the Rowlatt committee's recommendations as made 'in the previous year' to the Council; the committee reported in 1918 and the Act followed in 1919 (verify the exact report date).

### 4. Core: the satyagraha, the hartal and the first all-India struggle

*Objective:* Describe how Gandhi organised the protest and why it is called the first all-India struggle.

Spectrum says Gandhi first called for a mass protest at the all-India level. Seeing constitutional protest meet harsh repression, he organised a **Satyagraha Sabha** and drew in the younger members of the Home Rule Leagues and the Pan-Islamists. NCERT Class VIII adds that Satyagraha Sabhas were set up to launch the movement. (The Sabha's founding in February 1919, in Bombay, is standard knowledge; verify.)

## The programme

The forms of protest chosen were:

1. A nationwide **hartal** (strike) accompanied by fasting and prayer.
2. **Civil disobedience** against specific laws.
3. Courting arrest and imprisonment.

Gandhi asked the people to observe **6 April 1919** as a day of non-violent opposition, of 'humiliation and prayer' and hartal (NCERT Class VIII). Spectrum says the satyagraha was to be launched on 6 April. Some standard books note that Delhi observed its hartal earlier, on 30 March, and that violence followed (verify; outside the extracts).

## Why 'first all-India'

NCERT Class VIII says the Rowlatt Satyagraha turned out to be the first all-India struggle against the British government, although largely restricted to cities. NCERT Class XII adds that in towns across north and west India life came to a standstill as shops and schools closed. Spectrum's chapter summary calls it the 'first mass strike' and lists the changes in the movement: masses found a direction and could 'act' instead of merely speaking; peasants, artisans and the urban poor were from now to play a growing part; and the movement turned permanently to the masses. NCERT Class XII adds that the Rowlatt satyagraha made Gandhi a truly national leader.

Read these claims with care. 'First all-India' contrasts the movement with the earlier local struggles of 1917 to 1918 (GS-041). 'Largely urban' reminds you that peasants did not yet join in great numbers; that came later.

## Unity

NCERT Class VIII says participants tried to ensure Hindus and Muslims united in the fight, a call of Gandhi, and Spectrum's later chapter says the Rowlatt agitation brought Hindus, Muslims and other sections together (it also names the Lucknow Pact of 1916 as an earlier step).

## Gandhi's own movements

NCERT Class XII says Gandhi was detained while proceeding to Punjab and that prominent local Congressmen were arrested. The standard account is that he was stopped near Palwal and sent back to Bombay on about 10 April (verify). This is why Punjab's leaders stood alone when the crisis came.

## What the protest was not

It was not yet Non-Cooperation, which asked people to withdraw cooperation from government and which belongs to GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written. The Rowlatt programme was narrower: protest this law, disobey chosen laws, accept arrest.

## Short test

If a question asks what was new about the Rowlatt Satyagraha, you can answer with three nouns: *reach* (countrywide), *method* (hartal with fasting and prayer plus civil disobedience) and *base* (towns, with the masses beginning to move).

### 5. Core: Punjab in April 1919, from arrests to martial law

*Objective:* Narrate the Amritsar events before 13 April and say where the sources differ.

Spectrum says April 1919 saw the biggest and most violent anti-British upsurge since 1857, and that before the Satyagraha could be launched on 6 April there were large violent demonstrations in Calcutta, Bombay, Delhi and Ahmedabad. (Source caution: in standard accounts only Delhi's clash of 30 March came before 6 April; the violence in Ahmedabad, Bombay and Calcutta followed news of Gandhi's detention on about 10 April, verify.) It adds that in Punjab the Army had to be called in and that the Lieutenant Governor, **Sir Michael O'Dwyer**, is said to have used aircraft to strafe violent protestors. Treat the aircraft detail as 'said to', as Spectrum does (standard accounts add aircraft at Gujranwala on 14 April; verify).

## Amritsar before the massacre

Spectrum's account of Amritsar runs as follows.

1. At first the protest was peaceful: shops shut, trade stopped and empty streets showed displeasure.
2. Two nationalist leaders, **Saifuddin Kitchlew** and **Dr Satyapal**, were arrested without provocation (Spectrum: on 9 April, apart from having addressed protest meetings) and taken to an unknown place. Many standard books give 10 April; the dates differ by a day (verify).
3. On 10 April crowds came out in thousands to show solidarity. The protests turned violent after police firing in which some protestors were killed.
4. In the riot that followed, five Englishmen are reported killed and an English woman missionary, **Marcella Sherwood**, who was cycling, was beaten.
5. Troops were sent to quell the disturbances.

## The officer and the order

**Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer** was the senior British officer responsible for imposing martial law and restoring order. Spectrum says that by then the city had returned to calm and the protests that were being held were peaceful. On **13 April**, which was Baisakhi, Dyer issued a proclamation forbidding people to leave the city without a pass, to hold demonstrations or processions or to assemble in groups of more than three.

## Who is who

| Person | Role |
|---|---|
| Sir Michael O'Dwyer | Lieutenant Governor of Punjab |
| Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer | Officer in charge at Amritsar once civil authority was handed to the military on 11 April; martial law was formally proclaimed on 15 April (verify) |
| Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal | Nationalist leaders arrested before the massacre |
| Marcella Sherwood | English missionary beaten on 10 April; the lane where she was attacked became the site of the crawling order |

## A trap in the chain

It is easy to compress the story to 'Gandhi's hartal led to the massacre'. The sources say otherwise: the Amritsar crowd of 10 April was provoked by the arrests and the firing, and by 13 April the city was calm. The massacre was an act of the military commander, not an outcome of a Gandhian plan.

## Where Gandhi was

Gandhi was not in Amritsar. NCERT Class XII says he was detained while proceeding to the Punjab (module 4), which left local leaders to face the crisis.

### 6. Core: Jallianwala Bagh, 13 April 1919

*Objective:* Describe the massacre, the numbers and the immediate responses with the right cautions.

## The gathering

The **Jallianwala Bagh** was a popular enclosed public place in Amritsar. On Baisakhi, a large crowd, mostly from neighbouring villages and unaware of the prohibitory orders in the city, gathered there to celebrate the festival. Local leaders had also called a protest meeting. Spectrum says it is not clear how many of the roughly 20,000 present were political protestors, and that the majority came for the festival. The meeting was peaceful and had passed two resolutions, one asking repeal of the Rowlatt Act and the other condemning the firing of 10 April.

## The firing

Dyer arrived with his troops, surrounded the gathering and blocked the main entrance (Spectrum: 'the only exit point'; the other openings were narrow or locked, verify), and opened fire on the unarmed crowd. Spectrum says no warning was issued and no instruction to disperse was given; men, women and children were fired upon as they tried to flee. NCERT Class XII puts it briefly: a British Brigadier ordered troops to fire on a nationalist meeting, and more than four hundred people were killed.

## The numbers

| Source | Dead | Wounded |
|---|---|---|
| Official British Indian sources (Spectrum) | 379 identified | about 1,100 |
| Indian National Congress estimate (Spectrum) | about 1,000 | more than 1,500 |
| NCERT Class XII | more than 400 | not given |

Spectrum adds that it is precisely known that 1,650 bullets were fired. Treat all death figures as contested; learn the official 379 and the Congress estimate of about 1,000 as labelled estimates (verify the standard figures).

## After the firing

Martial law was proclaimed in Punjab; public floggings and other humiliations followed. As an example, Spectrum says Indians were made to crawl on their bellies along the road on which the missionary had been assaulted.

## Responses

- **Rabindranath Tagore** renounced his knighthood in protest (Spectrum and NCERT Class VIII; the standard date is 30 May 1919, verify). Spectrum quotes his letter to the Viceroy about badges of honour making shame glaring.
- **Gandhi** gave up the **Kaiser-i-Hind** medal (Spectrum: awarded for his Boer War work). Spectrum places this beside Tagore's protest, but standard accounts date the return to 1 August 1920, at the start of Non-Cooperation, with the Punjab and Khilafat wrongs as reasons (verify). It was a delayed response to the massacre, not one made in 1919.
- Gandhi, overwhelmed by the atmosphere of violence, **withdrew the movement on 18 April 1919** (Spectrum). Gandhi's own phrase for the lapse, a 'Himalayan blunder', is standard but not in the extracts (verify).
- Spectrum says Gandhi concluded that cooperation with a 'satanic regime' was now impossible and that the way to Non-Cooperation was ready (GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written).

## Links to later events

Spectrum reports that Bhagat Singh was eleven in 1919 and that the Bharat Naujawan Sabha used the massacre as a symbol. Udham Singh later killed Michael O'Dwyer and was hanged in 1940 (Spectrum; the standard date of the shooting is 13 March 1940 in London, verify).

### 7. Core: the Hunter Committee, the Indemnity Act and the Congress inquiry

*Objective:* Explain what the official and unofficial inquiries found and what followed for Dyer.

## The Hunter Committee

The Secretary of State, Edwin Montagu, ordered an inquiry. On 14 October 1919 the Government of India announced the **Disorders Inquiry Committee**, better known as the **Hunter Committee** after its chairman, **Lord William Hunter**, a former Solicitor General for Scotland. Its task was to investigate the disturbances in Bombay, Delhi and Punjab, their causes and the measures taken to cope with them.

Spectrum names three Indian members: **Sir Chimanlal Setalvad**, **Pandit Jagat Narayan** and **Sardar Sahibzada Sultan Ahmad Khan**. The British members and the committee's size are not in the extracts (verify). The committee met in Delhi on 29 October, heard witnesses from Delhi, Ahmedabad, Bombay and Lahore, and reached Lahore in November.

## Dyer before the committee

Dyer said he was confident he had done his duty and that his intention was to strike terror across Punjab and reduce the moral stature of the 'rebels'. Spectrum reports that he said he could probably have dispersed the crowd without firing but that they would have come back and laughed, and that he made no effort to help the wounded because he did not see it as his job.

## The report

Spectrum says the report, released in March 1920 (the standard release date is May 1920; verify), unanimously condemned Dyer: the lack of warning to disperse was an error, the length of firing a grave error, his motive of producing a 'moral effect' was to be condemned, he had overstepped his authority, and there was no conspiracy to overthrow British rule in Punjab. The Indian members' **minority report** added that the proclamations were insufficiently publicised, that innocent people were present, that there was no violence in the Bagh beforehand, that Dyer should have helped the wounded and that his actions were 'inhuman and un-British'.

## The weakness of the result

- The committee imposed **no penal or disciplinary action**, because Dyer's superiors had condoned his actions (later upheld by the Army Council).
- Before it began, the government passed an **Indemnity Act** to protect its officers; the 'white washing bill', as it was called, was severely criticised by Motilal Nehru and others.
- In Britain, Spectrum says Churchill, then Secretary of State for War, called the events 'monstrous' and the cabinet agreed Dyer could not stay; H. H. Asquith called it one of the worst outrages in British history. Dyer was relieved of command in March 1920, recalled to England, and drew half pay and a pension, without legal action.
- Dyer was also supported: the House of Lords passed a motion in his support; a fund was raised for him through the *Morning Post* (about 26,000 pounds, Spectrum p374, often given as 26,317; verify; the summary's 30,000 is the outlier); the clergy of the Golden Temple declared him a Sikh, which helped provoke the Gurdwara Reform movement.

## The Congress inquiry

The Indian National Congress appointed its own non-official committee with **Motilal Nehru, C. R. Das, Abbas Tyabji, M. R. Jayakar and Gandhi**. It criticised Dyer's act as inhuman and found no justification for martial law in Punjab. The Congress also called the Hunter report 'blatantly partisan' (Spectrum's later chapter), and counted the Punjab events among reasons for losing faith in constitutional methods.

### 8. Master: significance, legacy and the road to Non-Cooperation

*Objective:* Evaluate the Rowlatt Satyagraha and Jallianwala Bagh and connect them to what came next.

## What the satyagraha achieved and failed to achieve

It did not repeal the Act at the time. Standard accounts say the Act was hardly ever used and was repealed in 1922 on the recommendation of the Repressive Laws Committee (outside the extracts; verify). But Spectrum credits it with giving the masses a direction, drawing in peasants, artisans and the urban poor, and turning the national movement permanently towards the masses; NCERT Class XII says it made Gandhi a truly national leader. Against that, NCERT Class VIII says it was largely restricted to cities, so the claim of a mass base needs a qualifier: it opened the door rather than filling the room.

## Why Gandhi stopped

Gandhi withdrew on 18 April 1919 because the protest had produced violence he had not intended, in Punjab and elsewhere. For a movement built on non-violence, that was a failure of discipline (his own phrase, 'Himalayan blunder', is standard; verify). This is the first example in his career of a pattern: he halts a movement when violence breaks out, and it recurs in 1922 (GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written).

## Why the massacre mattered

Spectrum says the massacre made Gandhi declare that cooperation with a 'satanic regime' was impossible and prepared the way for Non-Cooperation, quotes A. J. P. Taylor (Spectrum prints 'A.P.J Taylor'; learn A. J. P. Taylor) as calling it the 'decisive moment' when Indians were alienated from British rule, and argues that Dyer ensured the beginning of the end of the Raj. Treat 'the beginning of the end' as Spectrum's judgment, not a fact.

## Effects, by area

| Area | Effect |
|---|---|
| National movement | From local grievances to a countrywide protest; the movement's method grew in scale |
| Hindu-Muslim relations | The agitation brought communities together; the Khilafat issue joined it (NCERT Class VIII) |
| Faith in constitutional methods | Eroded by the Punjab events and the Hunter report (Spectrum) |
| Punjab politics | The massacre shaped resistance there; Bhagat Singh's circle used it as a symbol |
| Sikh politics | Honouring Dyer at the Golden Temple fed the Gurdwara Reform movement |
| British opinion | Divided: Churchill and Asquith condemned, while the Lords and a press fund supported Dyer |

## The hinge to Day 14

Spectrum says that Congress, losing faith in constitutional struggle after the Punjab events and the Hunter report, was ready for Gandhi's non-cooperation, and NCERT Class VIII has Gandhi urging the Congress to campaign against 'Punjab wrongs', the Khilafat wrong and for swaraj. The story of that movement, its programme and its end is GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written. This lesson ends at its threshold.

### Practice

**Q.** What was the Rowlatt Act officially called?

**A.** The Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919.

**Q.** What did Gandhi ask people to do on 6 April 1919?

**A.** Observe a hartal and a day of humiliation and prayer against the Act.

**Q.** Who ordered the firing at Jallianwala Bagh?

**A.** Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer.

**Q.** Why is the Rowlatt Satyagraha called the first all-India struggle, and what qualifies the claim?

**A.** It was countrywide in reach unlike the local struggles of 1917 to 1918, but NCERT says it was largely restricted to cities.

**Q.** Why did the Hunter Committee fail to satisfy Indians?

**A.** It condemned Dyer but imposed no penalty, the Indemnity Act had already protected officers, and Dyer was supported in Britain.

**Q.** Why is 'the hartal caused the massacre' a weak explanation?

**A.** The Amritsar violence followed the arrests and the firing of 10 April, the city was calm by 13 April, and the firing was a commander's act.

**Q.** An option says 'O'Dwyer ordered the firing at the Bagh and was later condemned by the Hunter Committee'. Find the two errors.

**A.** Dyer commanded the troops; O'Dwyer was Lieutenant Governor, and the Hunter Committee imposed no penal action.

**Q.** A question asks for the sequence of the withdrawal, the massacre and the Hunter announcement. Give it.

**A.** Massacre (13 April), withdrawal (18 April), Hunter announcement (14 October 1919).

**A.** Trial without jury and in secret, no appeal, arrest without warrant, and detention without trial with habeas corpus suspended.
