### Bridge: Bridge: per sq km, per cent, percentage points and the Census

A **ratio** compares two numbers by division. **Density** is the ratio of people to land area, written as persons per sq km (the unit is square kilometres). If 1,50,000 people live on 100 sq km, the density is 1,50,000 divided by 100, which is 1,500 persons per sq km (NCERT Class XI uses exactly this example).

A **per cent** means out of 100. If a state has 5 per cent of the country's people, it has 5 out of every 100. A **percentage point** is the unit for the gap between two per cents. If one decade's growth rate was 20 per cent and the next decade's was 17 per cent, the rate fell by 3 *percentage points*, not by 3 per cent (a fall of 3 per cent of 20 would be only 0.6). NCERT's sentence 'fall of 6.7 percentage points for Maharashtra' uses this meaning.

The **Census** is the full count of the population held every 10 years in India. NCERT says the first population Census was conducted in 1872 and the first complete Census in 1881. 'Census 2011' is the 2011 count; every density and share in this lesson comes from it unless a year is printed next to the number. Later counts will change the numbers, so check the latest release before quoting one in an answer.

### Bridge: Bridge: reading the physical regions of India by name

The NCERT paragraphs on India's population use a few region names. Here is what each means in plain words; the full physical geography is taught in Day 21 lesson GS-113 (India relief and physiographic divisions), and the Himalaya, plains and plateau are taught in later lessons.

- **Himalaya**: the high mountain wall along the northern border. Slopes are steep and winters are cold, so settlement is thin.
- **North Indian Plains**: the broad flat land south of the Himalaya, built by rivers such as the Ganga. Flat land, river water and farm soil sit together here.
- **Deltas**: low land at a river's mouth where the river splits into branches before reaching the sea; the Ganga delta is in West Bengal.
- **Coastal plains**: the narrow low belts along the sea coast, with ports and sea trade.
- **Interior districts of the peninsula**: the inland plateau of the south and centre, away from the coast, with less river water than the plains.

When NCERT says 'the North Indian Plains, deltas and coastal plains have a higher proportion of population than the interior districts of southern and central states', it is comparing these five kinds of place. A hand-drawn outline of India with these five areas shaded lightly is a useful picture to keep.

### 1. Orientation: where people live, how tightly, and how they move

*Objective:* Define distribution, density and migration in your own words and state the two world spacing facts NCERT gives.

## What this topic asks

Every country has people, and the first geographic questions about them are simple. Where do they live? How tightly packed are they? How do they move? This lesson answers those three questions for the world and for India, using NCERT Class XI (chapter 2 of Fundamentals of Human Geography) and NCERT Class XII (chapter 1 of India: People and Economy). Study this module first; it is short on purpose.

## Four words, defined once

- **Population distribution**: the way people are spaced over the earth's surface (NCERT Class XI).
- **Density of population**: the ratio of people to land area, usually written as persons per sq km. It shows how much land each person shares.
- **Migration**: the movement of people from one place to another. The place they leave is the *place of origin*; the place they reach is the *place of destination*.
- **Census**: the count of the population, held every 10 years in India. Bridge B1 explains the units used with it.

## Two spacing facts from NCERT Class XI

- Broadly, 90 per cent of the world's people live on about 10 per cent of its land area.
- The 10 most populous countries hold about 60 per cent of the world's people, and 6 of the 10 are in Asia. Naming those six is a map exercise: do it with an atlas and mark it verify, because the extract does not name them.

## India in one paragraph

NCERT Class XII says India is the second most populous country after China, with 1,210 million people in 2011, a number larger than North America, South America and Australia put together. That ranking is NCERT's 2011-era statement; later estimates differ: UN estimates (World Population Prospects) place India above China from about 2023 (standard knowledge; verify), so do not carry the old ranking into a Prelims answer. The same chapter says the first population Census was held in 1872 and the first complete one in 1881.

### 2. Foundation: density, the three ways to measure it, and one worked example

*Objective:* Calculate crude, physiological and agricultural density from a data set and say which one shows pressure on farmland.

## Crude density

NCERT Class XI defines density as population divided by area. Its worked example: Region X has 1,50,000 people on 100 sq km, so density is 1,500 persons per sq km. This simple ratio is the **crude (or arithmetic) density**.

$$ Density = Population / Area $$

NCERT Class XII calls it a crude measure of human and land relationship. It treats a desert, a lake and a field as the same kind of land, so it cannot show how hard farmland is being pressed.

## Two sharper measures

NCERT Class XII gives the formulae for two other measures, which it says matter for a country with a large farming population:

$$ Physiological density = total population / net cultivated area $$

$$ Agricultural density = total agricultural population / net cultivable area $$

*Agricultural population* includes cultivators, agricultural labourers and their family members (NCERT). A note on wording: the extract prints 'net cultivable area' in the second formula, while many textbooks use 'net cultivated area' for both. If a question gives only one area figure, use that one; if it names both, read the wording and say which area you used. Mark this check.

## A worked example (original numbers, not from NCERT)

Region R has 6,00,000 people on 400 sq km. Its net cultivated area is 150 sq km and 2,70,000 people belong to farming families.

1. Crude density = 6,00,000 / 400 = 1,500 persons per sq km.
2. Physiological density = 6,00,000 / 150 = 4,000 persons per sq km of cultivated land.
3. Agricultural density = 2,70,000 / 150 = 1,800 farming persons per sq km of cultivated land.

Reading: the crude figure looks moderate, but each cultivated sq km supports 4,000 people in all. Pressure on farmland is much higher than the crude density suggests.

## Which measure to use when

| Question asked | Best measure |
|---|---|
| How crowded is the area overall? | Crude density |
| How many people depend on each unit of cultivated land? | Physiological density |
| How many farming people depend on each unit of cultivable (NCERT wording) land? | Agricultural density |

## Where density figures come from

For India, NCERT quotes density from the Census. Dividing India's 2011 population by its printed density of 382 gives an implied area of about 3.17 million sq km. The extracts do not state which area the Census used, so do not rebuild a density from a total-area figure remembered from elsewhere; quote the printed density instead.

> Trap: crude density cannot be used to say that 'pressure on farmland' is low. Check physiological and agricultural density.

### 3. Core: why people cluster, three sets of factors

*Objective:* Name the geographical, economic and social factors behind world population distribution and give one NCERT example for each.

## One idea, three layers

People settle where life is easier and livelihoods are available. NCERT Class XI sorts the controls into **geographical**, **economic** and **social and cultural** factors. The first set decides where settlement *starts*; the second set can add or move people later.

## I. Geographical factors

- **Availability of water.** Fresh water is needed for drinking, cooking, cattle, crops, industry and navigation, so river valleys are among the most densely populated areas of the world.
- **Landforms.** People prefer flat plains and gentle slopes, which favour crops, roads and industry. Hills and mountains hinder transport and early development, so they carry fewer people. NCERT's pair: the Ganga plains are among the most densely populated in the world; the mountain zones of the Himalaya are scarcely populated.
- **Climate.** Extreme heat or cold is uncomfortable; areas with a comfortable climate and little seasonal variation attract more people. Very heavy rainfall or harsh climates have low population. NCERT notes Mediterranean regions were settled early because of their pleasant climate.
- **Soils.** Fertile loamy soil supports intensive farming, so such areas hold more people. Poor soil leaves areas thin; the book asks you to name Indian examples, which you can do after Day 17 lesson GS-123.

## II. Economic factors

- **Minerals.** Mineral areas attract industries, jobs and skilled workers. NCERT's example is the Katanga-Zambia copper belt in Africa (Katanga lies in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the copper belt runs into Zambia; standard knowledge, verify for map items).
- **Urbanisation.** Cities offer jobs, schools, hospitals, transport and communication, so rural-to-urban migration swells them. NCERT adds that city life can be 'very taxing'.
- **Industrialisation.** Industrial belts draw factory workers and also transport operators, shopkeepers, bank staff, doctors and teachers. The Kobe-Osaka region of Japan is the example.

## III. Social and cultural factors

Places of religious or cultural significance attract people. People move away from social and political unrest. Governments sometimes give incentives to settle sparsely populated areas or to leave overcrowded ones.

## How the layers combine

A sentence that earns marks links them: *plains and rivers start the settlement, farming fills it, and roads, minerals and industry add more.* Day 17 lesson GS-123 (agriculture), Day 18 lesson GS-124 (minerals and energy), Day 19 lesson GS-125 (irrigation) and Day 20 lesson GS-126 (transport) each supply one of the added layers.

## A diagram to draw

Draw three circles labelled Geographical, Economic, Social and Cultural. Inside the first write water, land, climate, soil; inside the second write minerals, cities, industry; inside the third write sacred places, unrest, incentives. An arrow from the first to the second shows the usual order in time; the third circle has no arrow because social and cultural factors do not follow in time.

> Trap: do not list 'fertile soil' as an economic factor; NCERT places it under geographical factors.

### 6. Core: population growth, natural against actual

*Objective:* Write the balancing equation of population change, calculate CBR, CDR, growth and growth rate for a small example, and place migration in it.

## Growth, change and rate

**Growth of population** is the change in the number of people between two points of time (NCERT). It can be positive or negative and can be given as an absolute number or as a **growth rate** (a per cent). NCERT's example: the difference between India's 2011 and 2001 populations is the growth in actual numbers.

NCERT Class XII gives the decadal growth rate formula: growth rate = (p2 minus p1) / p1 times 100, where p1 is the base-year population and p2 the present-year population.

## Two kinds of growth

- **Natural growth** = births minus deaths.
- **Actual growth** = births minus deaths plus in-migration minus out-migration.

$$ Actual growth = Births − Deaths + In-migration − Out-migration $$

NCERT Class XII says growth has a *natural* and an *induced* component. Natural growth is judged from the crude birth and death rates; the induced part is the inward and outward movement of people. This is where migration enters the population count.

## Crude birth rate and crude death rate

The **crude birth rate (CBR)** is live births in a year per thousand of population: CBR = (B / P) times 1000, where P is the estimated mid-year population. **Crude death rate (CDR)** = (D / P) times 1000. 'Crude' means that it uses the whole population as the base, not only women of child-bearing age.

## A worked example (original numbers)

Town T has 1,00,000 people at the start of a year. During the year there are 2,000 births, 800 deaths, 3,500 in-migrants and 1,200 out-migrants. Take the mid-year population as 1,01,750.

1. Natural growth = 2,000 minus 800 = +1,200.
2. Net migration = 3,500 minus 1,200 = +2,300.
3. Actual growth = 1,200 + 2,300 = +3,500; end population 1,03,500; growth rate = 3,500 / 1,00,000 times 100 = 3.5 per cent.
4. CBR = 2,000 / 1,01,750 times 1000 = about 19.7 per thousand; CDR = 800 / 1,01,750 times 1000 = about 7.9 per thousand.

Migration supplies 2,300 of the 3,500 added people, more than natural growth does. A small-town reading would call it a migration-led place.

## A negative case

Negative growth occurs when deaths exceed births or when people leave in greater numbers than arrive (NCERT). If a village loses 500 to out-migration and has 100 more births than deaths, actual growth is minus 400 even though natural growth is positive.

## India's decadal figures

India's decadal growth rates from 1901 to 2011 are in Master notes (recomputed). The detailed phases, birth-rate trends and demographic transition are for later population lessons; here remember only that NCERT's Phase III (1951 to 1981) mentions international migration from Tibet, Bangladesh, Nepal and Pakistan as one contributor to high growth.

> Trap: a rise in growth rate does not always mean births rose; check migration.

### 7. Core: migration, terms, types, push and pull

*Objective:* Define the migration terms, list the types by duration and by stream, and sort causes into push and pull factors.

## The movement of people

When people move from one place to another, the place they move from is the **place of origin** and the place they move to is the **place of destination**. The origin shows a decrease in population and the destination an increase (NCERT Class XI). NCERT calls migration 'a spontaneous effort to achieve a better balance between population and resources'.

## Immigrants and emigrants: the same person

- **Immigration**: migrants who move *into* a new place are **immigrants**.
- **Emigration**: migrants who move *out of* a place are **emigrants**.

NCERT's reminder: the same person is both an emigrant (from the origin) and an immigrant (at the destination). Use the word that matches the place you are describing.

## Types of migration

By duration: **permanent**, **temporary** or **seasonal**. By stream: rural to rural, rural to urban, urban to urban, and urban to rural.

| Stream | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rural to rural | between villages |
| Rural to urban | village to town or city |
| Urban to urban | between towns and cities |
| Urban to rural | town or city back to a village |

Also by boundary (standard teaching): **internal** (inside one country) and **international** (across a border). NCERT Class XII mentions international migration into India in its growth phases (module 6).

## Why people move: push and pull

People migrate for a better economic and social life. Two sets of factors act together.

**Push factors** make the origin seem less attractive: unemployment, poor living conditions, political turmoil, unpleasant climate, natural disasters, epidemics, socio-economic backwardness.

**Pull factors** make the destination seem more attractive: better job opportunities and living conditions, peace and stability, security of life and property, a pleasant climate.

## A sorting rule that works

Ask: *is this a reason to leave, or a reason to arrive?* A reason to leave is push; a reason to arrive is pull. The same fact can sit on both sides. Water shortage at the origin is a push factor; reliable water at the destination is a pull factor.

## The distinction that decides marks

Forced migration (flight from war or disaster) is driven mostly by push factors; voluntary migration (for jobs) combines push and pull. This split is standard teaching, not a NCERT sentence; refugees come up again in module 10.

> Trap: 'medical and educational facilities' is a pull factor, not a push factor. NCERT's own exercise tests exactly this sort.

### 8. Core: rural-urban migration in India and the pull of big cities

*Objective:* Explain rural-urban migration in India with the extract's facts and give a structured reason why large cities draw more migrants than small towns.

## India is still mostly rural

NCERT Class XII (Census 2011): about 68.8 per cent of the population lives in villages. India has 640,867 villages, of which 597,608 (93.2 per cent) are inhabited. The share of urban population is 31.16 per cent, which is low, but it is growing faster over the decades. NCERT gives two causes of faster urban growth: enhanced economic development and better health and hygiene.

## Rural-urban migration, where it is conspicuous

NCERT says rural-urban migration is most conspicuous in urban areas along the main road links and railroads in the North Indian Plains and in industrial areas around Kolkata, Mumbai, Bengaluru-Mysuru, Madurai-Coimbatore, Ahmedabad-Surat, Delhi-Kanpur and Ludhiana-Jalandhar. In almost all states and Union Territories urban population has increased considerably, which shows both better urban development and more rural-urban migration.

## Where urbanisation stayed low

The degree of urbanisation stayed low in agriculturally stagnant parts of the middle and lower Ganga Plains, Telangana, non-irrigated Western Rajasthan, remote hilly tribal areas of the north-east, flood-prone parts of Peninsular India and eastern Madhya Pradesh (NCERT). Reading this as push and pull: stagnant farming areas push people out, but low urbanisation there means few local pull centres (a reading, not an NCERT sentence).

## Why big cities pull more than small towns (PYQ theme)

NCERT Class XI says cities offer better employment opportunities, educational and medical facilities, better transport and communication, and good civic amenities, so mega cities continue to attract large numbers of migrants. Building on this, a four-part frame:

1. **Jobs and wages**: industries and services cluster in big cities, giving more and more varied work (module 3's industrialisation and urbanisation).
2. **Services**: hospitals, colleges, transport.
3. **Connectivity**: the largest cities sit on the main roads and railways.
4. **Weakness of the alternative**: small towns in developing countries offer fewer jobs. This reading, and any claim about migrant networks or informal work, is standard teaching and must be marked verify; the extracts do not state it.

Add a balancing point: NCERT notes city life can be 'very taxing', so the pull has costs (crowding, strain on services).

## Remittances and effects

The extracts do not give figures for money sent home or for effects on origin areas. Describe the effects qualitatively: the origin loses people, often young workers (verify), and the destination gains labour but faces strain.

## A picture to draw

On an outline map of India mark the seven industrial areas named above with a star, and shade the six low-urbanisation areas with hatching (Telangana is spelt Telengana in NCERT). The star-and-hatch pairing is a useful India illustration for the 2024 PYQ.

### 9. Master: five tidy rules tested on edge cases, and the source checks

*Objective:* Test five neat rules about population against edge cases and name the source numbers that need a recheck.

A neat rule helps memory but can mislead. Each rule below is tested on at least three cases from this lesson.

## Rule 1: flat land plus water means dense

Cases that fit: the Ganga plains (NCERT Class XI); river valleys; deltas. Cases that bend the rule: Rajasthan, where irrigation raised numbers in a once thin area; Jharkhand, where mineral and energy resources raised numbers; the Peninsular states, where transport did. Rule, repaired: *physical factors set the first pattern; irrigation, minerals and transport can change it.*

## Rule 2: high density means heavy pressure on land

Test it on Delhi (11,297 per sq km, crude): the figure measures pressure on urban land (housing, water, transport), not on farmland, so physiological density is the wrong lens there. Test on Kerala (859) against Uttar Pradesh (828): density and population size differ. Test on module 2's Region R: crude density 1,500 hides physiological density 4,000. Rule, repaired: *use crude density for crowding and physiological or agricultural density for pressure on cultivated land.*

## Rule 3: migrants are new people

An in-migrant is new only at the destination; the origin loses one. At national level, migration inside India adds nobody. Edge case: a village with natural growth +180 and net migration minus 350 shrinks by 170 (module 6 check). Rule, repaired: *migration moves people; it does not create them, except across a country's border.*

## Rule 4: a falling growth rate means falling numbers

Two separate claims, tested on Table 1.1 as printed. (a) A falling rate does not mean a falling population: in 2001-2011 the rate fell to 17.64 per cent (from 21.54, about 3.9 percentage points) and the population still grew by 181,583,094. (b) A falling rate does not even mean fewer persons added: the rate fell from 24.66 (1971-81) to 23.85 (1981-91) to 21.54 per cent (1991-2001), yet the additions rose from 135,169,445 to 162,973,591 to 182,307,640. From 1981-91 to 1991-2001 the rate fell 2.31 points yet the addition rose by 19,334,049 (larger base). 2001-2011 is the exception: additions dipped by 724,546 to 181,583,094.

## Rule 5: a low urban share means little rural-urban movement

India's urban share is 31.16 per cent, yet NCERT says urban growth is faster and rural-urban migration is conspicuous along main roads and in industrial areas. A national share hides local streams. Rule, repaired: *judge migration by streams and places, not by the national share.*

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Population distribution: the way people are spaced over the earth's surface (NCERT Class XI).
- Density of population: persons per unit area, usually per sq km; density = population / area.
- Physiological density: total population divided by net cultivated area (NCERT Class XII formula); agricultural density: agricultural population divided by net cultivable area as the extract prints it (check wording).
- Growth of population: change in the number of people between two points of time; growth rate: that change as a per cent.
- Natural growth = births minus deaths; actual growth = births minus deaths plus in-migration minus out-migration.
- Crude birth rate (CBR) = live births in a year per 1000 of mid-year population; crude death rate (CDR) = deaths in a year per 1000 of mid-year population.
- Place of origin and place of destination; emigrants leave, immigrants arrive; the same person is both.
- Push factors make the origin less attractive; pull factors make the destination more attractive.
- Census: the count held every 10 years in India; first population Census 1872, first complete Census 1881 (NCERT).

### Notes: Three density measures compared

- Use crude density for crowding and the other two for pressure on cultivated land.

### Notes: Causes and effects of uneven distribution

- Fresh water and river valleys lead to dense settlement (NCERT Class XI).
- Flat plains and gentle slopes lead to farming, roads and industry, hence more people; mountains hinder transport, hence fewer people.
- Fertile loamy soil supports intensive farming, hence more people.
- Minerals lead to mining and industry, jobs, and skilled migrants; example Katanga-Zambia copper belt.
- Cities and industrial belts lead to rural-urban migration; example Kobe-Osaka (Japan).
- Irrigation in Rajasthan, minerals and energy in Jharkhand and transport in Peninsular states raised numbers in once thin areas (NCERT Class XII).
- Early settlement and transport keep river plains and coasts crowded even where land and water degrade.
- Migration lowers numbers at the origin and raises them at the destination.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Density against population size: Kerala (859) is denser than Uttar Pradesh (828) but Uttar Pradesh is far more populous.
- Crude against physiological density: whole area against cultivated area.
- Natural growth against actual growth: actual includes migration.
- Emigrant against immigrant: leaving against arriving; same person.
- Push against pull: reason to leave against reason to arrive; medical and educational facilities are pull.
- Percentage points against per cent: a fall from 21.54 to 17.64 is 3.9 percentage points.
- Refugee (forced) against economic migrant (voluntary): standard teaching, not a NCERT sentence.

### Memory skeleton

- Three words: distribution (where), density (how many per sq km), migration (moving from origin to destination)
- Density measures: crude (total area), physiological (net cultivated area), agricultural (farming population)
- Why people cluster: geographical (water, landforms, climate, soils), economic (minerals, cities, industry), social and cultural
- India 2011: ten states about 76 per cent; density 382 against 117 in 1951; Arunachal lowest, Bihar highest state, Delhi a UT
- Growth: natural (births minus deaths) against actual (plus in-migration minus out-migration); falling rate is not falling population
- Migration: origin and destination, emigrant and immigrant; duration and stream types; push and pull
- Rural-urban migration in India: 68.8 per cent rural; conspicuous corridors; why big cities pull
