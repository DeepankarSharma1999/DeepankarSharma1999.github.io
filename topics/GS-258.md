## GS-258: Land reforms and tenancy

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Agriculture and food

*Goal:* Explain Land reforms and tenancy and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Goals: equity and incentives

At independence, land tenure was dominated by intermediaries such as zamindars and jagirdars who collected rent without improving farms. Land reform aimed to change ownership. Abolition of intermediaries brought about 200 lakh tenants into direct contact with government and freed them from exploitation. NCERT's box on ownership and incentives explains the logic: a cultivator who owns land keeps the gains from extra output and so invests more, whereas a tenant has little reason to improve land that benefits the owner. Ceiling laws added an equity tool by capping the size of individual holdings.

### Why implementation fell short

Results were uneven. Some former zamindars kept large areas by exploiting loopholes; landlords evicted tenants and claimed to be self-cultivators; the poorest — sharecroppers and landless workers — often did not benefit. Ceiling laws were challenged in courts, and landlords used the delay to register land in relatives' names. Kerala and West Bengal succeeded because their governments were committed to land to the tiller. The geography textbook adds that state governments avoided tough decisions against landlord lobbies, leaving inequitable land distribution in place. The Bhoodan–Gramdan movement of Vinoba Bhave, beginning at Pochampally, sought voluntary land gifts instead.

### Fragmentation and consolidation

Beyond ownership, the size and shape of holdings matter. Inheritance divides land among heirs each generation, producing small, scattered plots that are uneconomic. Consolidation of holdings was given priority after independence along with cooperation and abolition of zamindari, yet some states have never carried out consolidation even once, and others need a second round because land has fragmented again. Since the 1980s and 1990s comprehensive land development combined institutional and technical reforms, including crop insurance, Grameen banks and cooperative credit. NCERT treats unequal land distribution as detrimental to agricultural development and small fragmented holdings as uneconomic.

### Worked example

Answer frame — 'Assess land reforms in India': Objectives (abolish intermediaries, land to the tiller, ceilings, consolidation) → Achievements (200 lakh tenants freed; Kerala, West Bengal) → Failures (loopholes, evictions, benami transfers, weak political will) → Continuing issues (fragmentation, small holdings) → Way forward.

### Recall

- - **q:** Which two states does NCERT cite as land-reform successes?
  - **a:** Kerala and West Bengal.
- - **q:** What is a land ceiling?
  - **a:** The maximum size of land that an individual may own.
- - **q:** Where did the Bhoodan movement begin?
  - **a:** Pochampally (Telangana), when Ram Chandra Reddy offered 80 acres for 80 landless villagers.

### Common trap

Assuming abolition of zamindari delivered equity: former zamindars retained land through loopholes, tenants were evicted as owners claimed self-cultivation, and sharecroppers and landless labourers often gained nothing.
