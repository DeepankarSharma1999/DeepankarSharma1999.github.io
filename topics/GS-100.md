## GS-100: Fluvial landforms and river cycles

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · Geography · Physical fundamentals

### Bridge: Speed, energy and why a river drops its load

A river is moving water, and moving things carry **energy** (the capacity to do work). Two things set a river's energy: its **volume** (how much water passes a point each second) and its **velocity** (how fast it moves). Leong (PDF p. 70) says exactly this: the power of a river depends essentially on its volume and velocity.

Think of a bucket of sand poured into a garden hose of running water. If the water is slow, the sand sinks to the bottom and stays. If the water is fast, it lifts the sand and carries it away. Fast water can move large grains; slow water can move only fine ones. The rule you need is short:

1. **Fast and strong: pick up and carry.** A flood, with a large volume and a high speed, can move even boulders.
2. **Slow down: drop.** When the river enters a gentler slope, a wider channel, a lake or the sea, its speed falls at that point, its energy falls, and it can no longer carry its heaviest pieces. NCERT (kegy206 p4) says the coarsest material settles first and the finer silt and clay are carried farther.
3. **Deposition is a consequence of erosion.** Erosion supplies the sand; the change of slope decides where it is dropped (Day 21 lesson GS-099).

A small calculation to feel the idea. Suppose a hill stream drops 300 m over 10 km and a plain river drops 3 m over the same 10 km. The first has a gradient of 300 / 10,000 = 0.03 (3 in 100) and the second 3 / 10,000 = 0.0003 (3 in 10,000). The hill stream is 100 times steeper (0.03 / 0.0003 = 100), so gravity pulls its water downhill much harder, and it works mainly on its bed. The plain river has little slope left to push it and drops most of its load.

*Where a figure helps:* draw a stream coming off a steep hillside onto a flat plain, with large boulders dropped at the foot of the slope and a spread of finer silt beyond them. That picture is an alluvial fan, met in module 7.

### Bridge: Reading a river's left bank, right bank and its mouth

Three small ideas keep the later modules clear.

**Upstream and downstream.** Upstream is towards the source (the start of the river); downstream is towards the mouth (where it enters the sea or a lake). When a river is said to 'cut back' or 'migrate upstream' (a waterfall or a river capture), the feature moves towards the source.

**Left bank and right bank.** Stand facing downstream: the bank on your left hand is the left bank and the bank on your right is the right bank. This is the convention used in NCERT sentences such as 'important left bank tributaries are the Ramganga, the Gomati, the Ghaghara, the Gandak, the Kosi and the Mahananda' (kegy103 p6).

**Concave and convex.** On a curve of a river, the outer bank is **concave** as seen from the channel (it wraps round the water like the inside of a bowl) and the inner bank is **convex** (it bulges into the channel). A long, gentle bank on the inside of a bend is a **slip-off slope**; a steep bank on the outside is a **river cliff** or **cut-off bank**. You will need these words for meanders in module 5.

**Fluvial.** The word means 'made by or belonging to a river'. A fluvial landform is a shape made by a river's erosion or deposition. Do not confuse it with **fluvio-glacial** (made by meltwater of a glacier; Day 23 lesson GS-101) or **alluvial** (made of the sediment, called alluvium, that rivers deposit).

### 1. Orientation: a river as a conveyor belt of rock

*Objective:* Say in one minute what a fluvial landform is, what the three jobs of a river are, and what this lesson owns and does not own.

## One picture to hold

A river is a **conveyor belt of rock**. In the hills it picks up weathered pieces, on the way it carries them, and where its slope eases it drops them. Those three jobs are **erosion, transport and deposition**. The shapes left behind are **fluvial landforms**; 'fluvial' means made by a river. This lesson is the study of those shapes and of how a river-shaped landscape changes with time.

## Words you need on day one

- **Landform:** in NCERT's words, a small to medium tract of the earth's surface with its own shape, size and materials, produced by certain geomorphic processes and agents (kegy206 p1). A **landscape** is several related landforms together, a large tract.
- **Running water** has two parts (kegy206 p1): **overland flow** spread over the ground as a sheet, and **linear flow** in streams and rivers in valleys. In humid regions NCERT calls running water the most important agent that wears down the land.
- **Evolution of a landform** means the stages by which a landform changes into another or changes itself. NCERT compares a landmass to a life: **youth, maturity and old age** (kegy206 p1).

## Why running water matters most

Leong (PDF p. 68) compares agents: glaciers work only in cold and temperate latitudes, waves only on coasts, winds are efficient only in deserts, but running water works wherever water is present. He therefore calls it the most important single agent of denudation (the general lowering of the land).

## What this lesson owns, and what it does not

This lesson owns the **landforms of rivers and the cycle of river erosion**. It does not re-teach weathering, mass wasting or the general rules of erosion (Day 21 lesson GS-099), the names and routes of India's rivers (Day 18 lesson GS-117), ocean coasts and deltaic coasts (Day 17 lesson GS-130), or glacial and wind landforms (Day 23 lesson GS-101).

### 2. Foundation: from rain to a river system and its drainage patterns

*Objective:* Follow rain from the sky to a river system, name the three kinds of early streams, and match each drainage pattern to the rock structure that makes it.

## From rain to run-off

When rain falls, part of it is evaporated, part is taken up by plants and returned by transpiration, part sinks into the soil and rocks as **groundwater** (percolation), and the rest runs off the slopes as **run-off** and joins streams and rivers (Leong PDF p. 67). The whole circuit of evaporation, cloud, rain, run-off and percolation is the **water cycle** or **hydrological cycle**.

## Sheet, rill, gully, valley

NCERT (kegy206 p2) describes the first steps. Overland flow causes **sheet erosion**. Where the ground is uneven, the flow gathers into narrow paths and the friction of the water removes material, so small **rills** form. Rills grow into long, wide **gullies**; gullies deepen, widen, lengthen and join to make a network of **valleys**. In the early stage down-cutting dominates and irregularities such as waterfalls are removed.

## Catchment, divide and basin

The source of a river may be a spring, a lake or a marsh, usually in an upland where rainfall is heavy. The uplands are the **catchment area**. The crest of the mountains is the **divide** or **watershed**, from which streams flow down both sides (Leong p. 68). NCERT (kegy103 p2-3) adds: the area drained by a river and its tributaries is a **drainage basin**; the boundary separating one basin from another is the **watershed**; large rivers have basins, small rivulets have watersheds.

## The three kinds of early stream (Leong p. 68)

1. **Consequent stream:** the first stream, which exists as a consequence of the slope.
2. **Insequent stream:** a tributary that joins the main valley **obliquely** when the rocks are uniform; the pattern that results is tree-like.
3. **Subsequent stream:** a tributary that follows the rock structure and joins the main valley **at right angles** when hard and soft rock outcrops alternate across the valley.

## Four drainage patterns (recap)

The full table with NCERT examples is in Day 18 lesson GS-117, module 2 'Foundation: river words, banks and drainage patterns'. In short: **dendritic** is tree-like on uniform rock; **trellis** has right-angle tributaries on alternating hard and soft belts; **radial** spreads outwards from a dome or cone; **centripetal** converges inwards on a lake or depression (NCERT kegy103 p1).

## The regime of a river

The seasonal variation in the volume of water is the **regime** (Leong p. 69); it depends on the pattern of rain and snow, rock permeability, catchment size and vegetation.


### 3. Core: how a river erodes, carries and drops its load

*Objective:* Name and explain the four processes of river erosion and the four ways of carrying a load, and state the rule for where material is dropped.

## Weathering first

Weathering aids erosion but is **not a pre-condition** for it (NCERT, taught in Day 21 lesson GS-099 'Erosion'). Leong p. 70 goes further and says erosion cannot occur without weathering; teach the NCERT position. Soil creep and landslides are mass movements, also owned by GS-099.

## Energy of a river

The power of a river depends on its **volume and velocity** (bridge GS-100-B1). Leong notes that research shows the average velocity of a stream increases downstream even when the gradient decreases (p. 70). So the lower river is not necessarily slower in the main channel: deeper, smoother channels lose less energy to friction (standard; verify). Deposition happens where the velocity falls locally: at a sudden break of slope (fan), where flood water leaves the channel (levee, flood plain), on the inside of bends and at the mouth (delta). It also happens because the lower river carries far more load than its little slope can keep moving.

## The load and how it is carried

The material a river transports is its **load**.

| Mode | What moves | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Suspension** | Smallest particles | The yellow-brown colour of rivers after heavy rain |
| **Saltation** | Larger particles | Series of hops along the bed |
| **Traction** | Still larger fragments | Rolled or pushed along the bed |
| **Solution** | Dissolved material | Invisible; calcium carbonate of limestone dissolves easily |

## The four processes of river erosion (Leong p. 70-71)

1. **Hydraulic action:** scouring of the channel and carrying of material by the sheer force of the water. It helps to hollow the **plunge pools** below waterfalls.
2. **Corrasion:** wearing of the sides and floor by the boulders, pebbles, sand and silt carried. In the upper river, turbulence makes **pot-holes**, round cavities drilled by pebbles swirling in eddies. NCERT (kegy206 p2-3) says the same: stones collect in a small depression, are rotated by the water, and deepen it; a series of potholes joins and deepens the valley.
3. **Attrition:** wearing down of the load itself as it strikes the floor, banks and other pieces; pieces become smaller and rounder downstream.
4. **Solution:** chemical action of river water on rocks such as limestone.

## The rule for deposition

NCERT (kegy206 p3-4): when a stream with a coarse load meets a gentler slope, the load becomes too heavy to carry and is dumped; large sizes are deposited first and fine sand, silt and clay are carried farther. The gentler the channel, the greater the deposition. When the bed turns gentle through continued erosion, down-cutting weakens and **lateral erosion of the banks** increases.

## Worked example

A hill stream carries boulders, pebbles and sand to the foot of a mountain, where the slope drops from steep to almost level. Predict the order of deposition from the foot of the slope outwards: **boulders first, then pebbles, then sand, then silt and clay**. This is the plan of an alluvial fan (module 7).

### 4. Core: the long profile and the upper course - valleys, falls and captures

*Objective:* Draw the long profile, name the dominant process in each of the three courses, and explain the landforms of the upper course: V-valley, gorge, canyon, waterfall, rapids, interlocking spurs and river capture.

## The long profile and the three courses

A line drawn from the source to the sea is the **long profile**. It is convenient to divide a river into three courses (Leong p. 71, Fig. 5.6):

| Course | Dominant work | Valley |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper (mountain)** | Erosion, vertical corrasion (cutting downwards) | Steep, narrow V-shaped, gorge-like |
| **Middle (valley)** | Transport with some deposition; lateral corrasion widens the valley | Open, widening, gently sloping |
| **Lower (plain)** | Deposition | Very gentle, smooth and wide |

Draw it as a curve concave upwards, steep at the source and flattening towards sea level. These stages of a river's length are **not** the same as the stages of youth, maturity and old age of a landscape (module 9).

## V-shaped valley, gorge and canyon

In the upper course the river is swift, cuts down quickly and lateral corrasion cannot keep pace, so the valley is deep, narrow and **V-shaped**. NCERT (kegy206 p2): a **gorge** is a deep valley with very steep, almost straight sides, about equal in width at top and bottom; a **canyon** has steep **step-like** sides and is wider at the top than at the bottom, and is a variant of a gorge. Valley type depends on rock: canyons form commonly in horizontally bedded sedimentary rock and gorges in hard rock. Indian examples: the Kaveri near Hogenekal, Tamil Nadu (a gorge, kegy206 p3); the Indus and Satluj gorges (kegy103 p4-5).

## Waterfalls, rapids and the plunge pool

Rapids arise where bands of hard rock cross the river; larger falls are cataracts; a plunge from a height is a **waterfall** with a **plunge pool** below it (Leong p. 74-75). Four ways to make a waterfall: (i) a bar of resistant rock across the valley (Niagara); (ii) a fault scarp across the river (Victoria Falls); (iii) the edge of a plateau; (iv) a hanging valley left by glaciation. Corrasion and hydraulic action in the plunge pool undermine the edge; blocks fall; the waterfall **migrates upstream**, leaving a gorge in front. Indian cases: the Dhuandhar waterfall on the Narmada near Jabalpur and the waterfalls of the Son at the edge of the Amarkantak plateau (kegy103 p6, p8).

## Interlocking spurs

The stream follows the line of least resistance and winds; fast water on the outside of each bend erodes more, leaving **interlocking spurs** that project from alternate sides (Leong p. 72).

## River capture (river piracy)

If one side of a divide has a steeper slope or more rain, its stream cuts back (**headward erosion**) faster, enlarges its basin, may break through the divide and **capture** the stream on the other side. The bend where it happened is the **elbow of capture**; the beheaded stream is a **misfit**; the dry valley below the elbow is a **wind gap**, often useful for roads and railways (Leong p. 72-73).

### 5. Core: the middle course and the meander

*Objective:* Explain how a meander forms and grows, which bank is eroded and which built up, and name the landforms that result.

## What changes in the middle course

Downstream the river is joined by many tributaries, so its volume and load increase. Lateral corrasion now tends to dominate over vertical corrasion; active erosion of the banks widens the V-shaped valley. The work is predominantly **transport with some deposition**. The interlocking spurs are cut back into a line of bluffs, and rain-wash, soil creep, landslides and gullying widen the valley further (Leong p. 75). A flood greatly raises both erosive power and the ability to carry load.

## Meander: a channel pattern, not a landform

A river on a gentle slope rarely runs straight for long. It swings in loops called **meanders**, a word taken from the winding river Meanderez in Asia Minor (Leong's spelling; the standard name is the Maeander, now the Buyuk Menderes in Turkey; verify) (Leong p. 75). NCERT (kegy206 p5) stresses that a meander is **a type of channel pattern**, and lists causes: (i) water on a very gentle gradient works sideways on the banks; (ii) the banks of loose alluvium have irregularities that the water can use; (iii) the Coriolis force deflects flowing water as it deflects wind. Item (iii) is NCERT's statement; the effect on a small channel is tiny, so treat it as a minor cause (verify).

## How a meander grows (the one mechanism to master)

1. A slight irregularity makes the flow bend, so the bank gets a small curvature.
2. On the **outside (concave) bank** the water is fast. It undercuts the bank into a steep **river cliff** (NCERT: cut-off bank, a steep scarp).
3. On the **inside (convex) bank** the water is slow and drops sediment, building a long gentle **slip-off slope**.
4. Leong's explanation (p. 76): the flow dashes into the outer bank at Q; a bottom current is set up in a corkscrew motion and hurled back into mid-stream and the inner bank, where shingle is dropped at S. The outer bank is the bank of continual erosion and the inner bank the bank of continual deposition. Wording note: the water's inertia carries the fast surface flow into the outer bank and piles it up (super-elevation); the resulting pressure gradient drives the slower near-bed flow towards the inner bank, giving the helical (corkscrew) flow that drops sediment there. Leong's 'centrifugal force' is valid only in a frame turning with the bend (standard; verify).
5. The loop deepens and migrates outward; in the middle course the loops are held back by interlocking spurs, but in the lower course they enlarge across the plain.

## A check on a point-bar slip

NCERT (kegy206 p5) puts point bars 'on the concave side', yet the same chapter has deposition on the convex bank. Teach the point bar on the **inside (convex)** bank and treat the printed wording as a slip (verify).

### 6. Core: the lower course - flood plain, levees, point bars, ox-bow lakes and braids

*Objective:* Explain how a flood plain, natural levee, point bar, ox-bow lake and braided channel form, and why a lowland river changes course.

## What the lower river does

On the broad plain the river is heavy with debris from the upper course. Vertical corrasion has almost ceased, though lateral corrasion still erodes the banks. The work is mainly **deposition**, building up the bed and forming extensive flood plains. Coarse material is dropped and finer silt is carried towards the mouth (Leong p. 76).

## Flood plain (active and inactive)

Deposition builds a **flood plain** just as erosion makes valleys. NCERT (kegy206 p4): fine sand, silt and clay are carried by slow water in gentle channels and laid down on the bed and, when water spills over the banks, above it. The river bed made of its deposits is the **active flood plain**; the plain above the banks is the **inactive flood plain**, holding **flood deposits** (finer silt and clay) and **channel deposits** (coarser, in abandoned or cut-off channels). Each flood adds a fertile layer, so flood plains are among the richest farm lands (Leong p. 81).

## Natural levee and artificial embankment

**Natural levees** form during floods: as water spills over the banks it slows sharply and drops its coarsest load beside the channel, building low, linear, parallel ridges of coarse deposits along the banks (NCERT kegy206 p5); finer silt and clay settle farther out on the flood plain. Between floods the bed itself is raised by deposition (Leong p. 77, whose wording puts levee building at the river's normal level; check). People raise **artificial embankments** on them to prevent floods, but this lets the bed rise further; when the banks can no longer hold, they burst.

 ____/levee\______ flood plain ______/levee\____
          \ river channel (bed raised by silt) /

Draw this as a cross-section across the plain and mark the bed above the surrounding land.

## Point bar

A **point bar** (meander bar) is a linear deposit on the inner bank of a meander of mixed sediment sizes (NCERT p5; see module 5).

## Ox-bow lake

As a meander in the lower course becomes more pronounced, the outside bank is eroded so fast that the loop nearly closes into a circle. At some flood, the river cuts through the narrow **neck**, takes the shorter route, and abandons the loop as an **ox-bow lake** (also cut-off, bayou in the Mississippi basin, mortlake meaning dead lake). The lake silts up, becomes marshy and dries (Leong p. 77-78; NCERT p5). The ox-bow marks the old course.

## Braided stream

If large sheets of material split the river into several channels on the plain, it is **braided** (Leong p. 76; NCERT kegy103 p4).

## Why a plain river moves

The Kosi, 'the sorrow of Bihar', brings huge sediment from the upper reaches; its course gets blocked and it changes course (kegy103 p4). The Brahmaputra floods, shifts channel and erodes banks because its large tributaries carry heavy loads from a rainy catchment (kegy103 p7).

### 7. Core: alluvial fans and deltas - where the river unloads

*Objective:* Explain how an alluvial fan and a delta form, list the conditions that favour a delta, and name the delta types with examples.

## Alluvial fan: unloading at the foot of the mountain

NCERT (kegy206 p3-4): streams from higher levels break into foot-slope plains of low gradient. The very coarse load carried over the mountain slope is too heavy for the gentler gradient, so it is dumped and spread as a broad cone-shaped deposit, an **alluvial fan**. Streams on a fan shift position and make many channels called distributaries. In humid areas fans are low cones with gentle slope; in arid and semi-arid climates they are high cones with steep slope. NCERT's picture shows a fan on the way to Amarnath, Jammu and Kashmir.

## Delta: unloading at the sea

A **delta** is like an alluvial fan but at a different place: the load is dumped and spread into the sea. If it is not carried far away or along the coast, it accumulates as a low cone. Unlike a fan, delta deposits are **well sorted with clear stratification**: the coarsest settle first and the silts and clays are carried farther. As the delta grows, the distributaries lengthen and the delta builds seaward (NCERT p4). Leong (p. 78): a delta is a fan-shaped alluvial area at the river mouth, named from the Greek letter delta; it is a seaward extension of the flood plain; the river splits into several channels called **distributaries**.

| Feature | Alluvial fan | Delta |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Foot of mountain, on land | River mouth, in sea or lake |
| Sorting | Poorly sorted, coarse | Well sorted, stratified |
| Channels | Distributaries across the fan | Distributaries lengthening seawards |

## Conditions favouring a delta (Leong p. 78-79)

1. Active vertical and lateral erosion upstream, to give abundant sediment.
2. A sheltered coast, preferably tideless.
3. A shallow sea, or the load disappears in deep water.
4. No large lake in the river's course to 'filter off' the sediment.
5. No strong current running at right angles to the river mouth, washing the sediment away.

## Types of delta (Leong p. 78-79)

**Arcuate** (fan-shaped: Nile, Niger, Ganges, Mekong); **bird's-foot** (Mississippi); **estuarine** (partly submerged: Amazon, Ob, Vistula); **cuspate** (tooth-like: Ebro).

## India

NCERT shows the Krishna delta in Andhra Pradesh (kegy206 p4) and the Godavari delta with a spit (p13). Day 18 lesson GS-117 lists the east-coast deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) and the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta; the west-flowing Narmada and Tapi form none because, in NCERT's account, they flow in trough faults and fill the cracks with detritus (kegy103 p7). NCERT calls the east coast of India a low sedimentary coast where depositional forms dominate, and the west coast a high rocky retreating coast (kegy206 p12).

## Delta versus estuary

An **estuary** is a funnel-shaped mouth where fresh and salt water mix and sediment is carried away; deltas silt up port approaches (defined in Day 18 lesson GS-117 module 9 and Day 17 lesson GS-130 bridge 1; Leong p. 81 only compares the two as port sites).

### 8. Core: rejuvenation - terraces, knickpoints and incised meanders

*Objective:* Define rejuvenation, distinguish a fall in relative sea level from a rise, and explain terraces, knickpoints and incised (entrenched) meanders.

## What rejuvenation means

The earth's crust is not stable. Parts of a river basin may be lifted or lowered, or the sea level may change. Where this gives the river renewed power to cut down, the river is said to be **rejuvenated**, that is, made young again (Leong p. 79).

## Negative movement: uplift of land or fall of sea level

A **negative movement** steepens the slope, so active down-cutting is renewed. After a fall of sea level the old flood plain stands at greater height. The river, with renewed vigour, cuts into its former flood plain and leaves **terraces** on both sides. There is also a break in the graded profile, often marked by rapids; the point where the old and the rejuvenated profiles meet is the **knickpoint** (knick point, or rejuvenated head) (Leong p. 79-80).

NCERT (kegy206 p3): **river terraces** are surfaces marking old valley floors or flood plain levels. They may be bedrock surfaces without alluvial cover or **alluvial terraces** made of stream deposits. They are basically products of **erosion**, caused by vertical erosion of the stream into its own depositional flood plain. There can be several at different heights, marking former river bed levels. Terraces at the same height on both sides are **paired terraces**.

## Incised or entrenched meanders

If rejuvenation affects the upper course, valleys are deepened and steep-sided gorges form. In the middle and lower courses, vertical corrasion replaces lateral corrasion, so existing meanders are cut downward and a new inner trench is carved in the old valley. The result is a deep valley with **entrenched (incised) meanders**. Leong's best example is the Colorado River, USA, where an uplift of 2,100 m in the Tertiary period renewed down-cutting. NCERT (p3) says very deep and wide meanders can also be cut in hard rocks, and shows the entrenched meander loop of the Colorado with step-like side slopes typical of a canyon.

## Positive movement: depression of land or rise of sea level

A **positive movement** submerges the coast and 'drowns' valleys; the river's erosive power weakens and it drops sediment, so deposition features shift upstream (Leong p. 80).

## Table: negative against positive

| | Negative movement | Positive movement |
|---|---|---|
| Land or sea | Land uplifted or sea level falls | Land sinks or sea level rises |
| River's energy | Increased | Reduced |
| Main work | Down-cutting renewed | Deposition, drowned valleys |
| Landforms | Terraces, knickpoint, rapids, incised meanders, gorges | Submerged lower course, deposition shifted upwards |

### 9. Master: the cycle of erosion - youth, maturity, old age - and Indian cases

*Objective:* Describe the three stages of a river-shaped landscape, define peneplain and monadnock, and place Indian rivers and landforms on the scheme without over-claiming.

## The idea

NCERT (kegy206 p1-2) says a landmass passes through stages somewhat like the stages of a life: **youth, maturity and old age**. Most erosional landforms of running water belong to vigorous, youthful rivers on steep gradients. As continued erosion makes the channel gentler, the river loses speed, deposition increases, down-cutting weakens and lateral erosion of banks increases, so hills and valleys are reduced towards plains. NCERT asks whether complete reduction of the relief of a high landmass is possible; the answer in the text is that it ends in a **peneplain**, an 'almost plain' of faint relief, with a few low resistant remnants called **monadnocks** standing out.

## The three stages in NCERT's words (except the last row), as a table

| Feature | Youth | Mature | Old |
|---|---|---|---|
| Streams | Few, poorly integrated, flowing on original slopes | Plenty, well integrated | Few small tributaries with gentle gradients |
| Valleys | Shallow V-shaped | Deep V-shaped | Streams meander freely over vast flood plains |
| Flood plain | None or very narrow along trunk streams | Wider; meanders confined within the valley | Vast, with natural levees and ox-bow lakes |
| Divides | Broad and flat, with marshes, swamps and lakes | Sharp; marshes disappear | Broad and flat again, with lakes, swamps and marshes |
| Falls and rapids | May exist on hard rock | Disappear | Not mentioned |
| Relief (standard, not NCERT; verify) | High | Maximum dissection | Most land at or slightly above sea level |

The trap is that **divides are broad in youth, sharp in maturity and broad again in old age**.

## What the scheme is and is not

1. It describes a **landscape**, not the three courses of one river. A mature landscape can still have a young upper course. Do not mix the upper, middle and lower course (module 4) with the stages.
2. The stages are not a calendar. A new uplift or a fall in sea level starts a fresh cycle in part of the landscape (rejuvenation, module 8).
3. The cycle is usually credited to W. M. Davis; later writers argued for slope retreat (neither is in the extracts; verify).

## Indian cases (NCERT kegy103)

- **Himalayan rivers** (p3-4): perennial; gorges, V-valleys, rapids and waterfalls in the mountains; on the plains ox-bow lakes, flood plains, braided channels and deltas; the Kosi shifts because of its sediment.
- **Peninsular rivers** (p7): older, with broad, largely graded, shallow valleys; fixed course, no meanders, non-perennial flow, except the Narmada and Tapi in rift valleys.
- **Chambal**: badland topography, the Chambal ravines (p6).
- **Antecedent rivers:** NCERT names the Satluj, Kosi and Subansiri; the word means a river older than the uplift that keeps its course by cutting down as the land rises (standard, verify).

### 10. Exam: how the topic is tested, answer frames and traps

*Objective:* Handle a statement-based Prelims item and a course-by-course Mains answer on fluvial landforms, using the traps and frames in this module.

## What the exam record shows

The project's PYQ file lists **no verified official question** for this topic, so nothing here is an official key. The Prelims clause covers physical geography of India and the world; Mains GS I-10 asks for the salient features of the world's physical geography, and Mains GS I-12 covers changes in critical geographical features such as water-bodies. The sensible preparation is precise definitions and cause-effect pairs.

## Prelims traps in this topic

1. **Bank mix-up:** erosion on the outer (concave) bank, deposition on the inner (convex) bank. A statement that swaps them is false.
2. **Erosional or depositional?** Gorge, canyon, pot-hole, waterfall, interlocking spur and terrace are products of erosion (terrace: erosion into a flood plain). Flood plain, levee, point bar, ox-bow lake, delta and alluvial fan are depositional.
3. **Course mix-up:** vertical corrasion dominates the upper course; lateral corrasion the middle; deposition the lower.
4. **Stage mix-up:** divides are broad in youth, sharp in maturity, broad in old age; waterfalls and rapids disappear by maturity.
5. **Gorge versus canyon:** canyon is wider at the top, with step-like sides; the gorge is about equally wide top and bottom.
6. **Fan versus delta:** the delta is well sorted and stratified; the fan is coarse and poorly sorted (NCERT contrasts them).
7. **Rejuvenation sign:** terraces, knickpoints and incised meanders follow uplift or a fall of sea level; drowned valleys follow submergence.
8. **'Always' and 'only':** absolute words (rivers always deposit coarse first; deltas form only in tideless seas) are danger signs; use the lesson's conditions.

## How to eliminate

Remove statements that swap a bank, a course or a stage; then test the rest against the erosional and depositional lists. Eliminating even one option makes guessing worthwhile (a wrong answer costs one-third of the marks).

## Mains frame 1: the work of a river from source to mouth (10 marks, 150 words)

1. Introduction: a river erodes, transports and deposits; running water is the most widespread agent.
2. Upper course: vertical corrasion, V-valley, gorge, waterfalls, interlocking spurs, capture.
3. Middle course: lateral corrasion, transport, meanders.
4. Lower course: deposition, flood plain, levees, ox-bow lakes, delta.
5. Indian examples: Himalayan gorges; Kosi shifting; Ganga-Brahmaputra delta.
6. Conclusion: landforms record how the river's energy changes.

## Mains frame 2: rejuvenation and cycles (15 marks, 250 words)

Define; give youth, maturity and old age; explain interruption by uplift or sea-level fall; list the landforms; caution that the stages describe landscapes, not river courses; close with an Indian illustration and sketches (long profile with knickpoint, terrace cross-section).

### Notes: Core terms

- Landform: a small to medium tract of the earth's surface with its own shape, size and materials (NCERT kegy206 p1); landscape: several related landforms together.
- Fluvial: made by a river. Alluvium: sediment deposited by rivers. Load: material carried by a river.
- Catchment: the upland area drained by a river. Watershed or divide: the boundary line between two drainage basins (NCERT kegy103 p3; Leong p. 68).
- Consequent stream: the first stream, flowing down the original slope; insequent: tributary joining obliquely on uniform rock; subsequent: tributary following rock structure and joining at right angles (Leong p. 68).
- Regime: seasonal variation in the volume of water in a river (Leong p. 69).
- Long profile: a line drawn from source to mouth showing height against distance (Leong p. 71).
- Rejuvenation: renewal of a river's down-cutting power by uplift of land or fall of sea level; knickpoint: the break where the old and new profiles meet (Leong p. 79-80).
- Peneplain: an 'almost plain' of faint relief left by long stream erosion; monadnock: a low resistant remnant standing above it (NCERT kegy206 p2).

### Notes: Four ways to make a waterfall (Leong p. 74-75)

- A bar of resistant rock lies across the river valley (example given: Niagara Falls, USA).
- A fault-line scarp lies across the river (example: Victoria Falls on the Zambezi).
- Water plunges over the edge of a plateau (example: the Zaire at Livingstone Falls).
- Glaciation leaves a hanging valley whose stream falls into the main U-shaped valley (example: Yosemite Falls).
- After formation the fall retreats upstream by undercutting at the plunge pool, leaving a gorge in front (Leong p. 71).

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Gorge versus canyon: gorge about equal width top and bottom, canyon wider at the top with step-like sides.
- Alluvial fan versus delta: fan on land at a mountain foot, coarse; delta at the sea, sorted and stratified.
- Delta versus estuary: delta builds seaward; estuary is a funnel-shaped mouth where sediment is carried away.
- Distributary versus tributary: a distributary leaves the main river (delta, fan); a tributary joins it.
- Meander versus ox-bow lake: a meander is a bend in an active channel; an ox-bow is a cut-off, abandoned loop.
- Stages of a landscape versus courses of a river: youth-maturity-old age is not upper-middle-lower.
- Watershed (a boundary line) versus watershed as a small basin: NCERT uses both senses (kegy103 p3).

### Notes: Human aspects of rivers (Leong p. 80-81)

- Rapids and waterfalls interrupt navigation; deposition silts ports; deltas are less satisfactory port sites than estuaries.
- Floods burst levees and damage crops, but add fertile silt; excessive flooding may discourage cultivation.
- Steep gorges and waterfalls in the upper course give hydro-electric sites; dams hold back flood water.
- Flood plains and deltas are rich farmland supporting dense populations; Leong names the Ganges delta for jute and Calcutta as the port of the Indo-Gangetic Plain (older spelling of Kolkata).
- Wind gaps of river capture offer road and rail routes.

### Core concepts

- A river erodes (hydraulic action, corrasion, attrition, solution), transports (suspension, saltation, traction, solution) and deposits; its energy depends on volume and velocity.
- Vertical corrasion dominates the upper course, lateral corrasion and transport the middle, deposition the lower.
- On a meander the outer bank is eroded and the inner bank built up; a cut-off neck leaves an ox-bow lake.
- A fan forms where a mountain stream meets a gentle plain; a delta forms where the river meets the sea and conditions allow deposits to stay.
- Rejuvenation by uplift or sea-level fall gives terraces, a knickpoint and incised meanders.
- Youth, maturity and old age describe a landscape and end in a peneplain with monadnocks.

### Practice

**Q.** What are the three jobs of a river?

**A.** Erosion, transport (transportation) and deposition.

**Q.** Define the catchment area and the watershed of a river.

**A.** The catchment is the upland area drained by the river; the watershed (divide) is the boundary line between two drainage basins.

**Q.** Name the four processes by which a river erodes.

**A.** Hydraulic action, corrasion, attrition and solution (Leong p. 70-71).

**Q.** What is the load of a river, and what are its four modes of transport?

**A.** Load is the material it carries; modes are suspension, saltation, traction and solution.

**Q.** Why does a hill stream cut mainly downwards while a plain river works mainly sideways and by deposition?

**A.** On a steep gradient most energy works on the bed, so vertical corrasion dominates; on a gentle gradient there is little slope to push the water and the river carries more load than it can keep moving, so it drops it and works laterally on loose banks (NCERT kegy206 p1-2).

**Q.** Why are the outer bank of a meander steep and the inner bank gentle?

**A.** The fast, deep flow on the outside undercuts the bank into a river cliff, while slow flow on the inside drops sediment as a slip-off slope.

**Q.** Why does a delta not form at every river mouth?

**A.** It needs abundant sediment, a sheltered shallow sea, no large lake trapping sediment upstream and no strong cross-current; deep, tidal or current-swept coasts remove the load (Leong p. 78-79).

**Q.** Why are river terraces called products of erosion even though many are made of alluvium?

**A.** They result from vertical erosion into the river's own earlier flood plain; the alluvium is the old deposit that remains as a step (NCERT kegy206 p3).

**Q.** A river shows a sharp rise in gradient at one point, with rapids, and flat benches on both sides above the present channel. What has happened and what will the river do next?

**A.** Uplift or a fall of sea level has rejuvenated it; the rapids mark the knickpoint and the benches are paired terraces. The knickpoint will migrate upstream as the river cuts a new valley.

**Q.** On a plain, a village behind a high embankment on a river's bank finds the river bed higher than its fields. Explain how this came about.

**A.** Floods build levees, and between floods deposition raises the bed between the embankments set on them; the water level rose with the bed, so it is now above the surrounding land and a breach threatens a flood (Leong p. 77).

**Q.** A map shows a river with crescent lakes beside it and a stream splitting into many channels near the sea. Name the features and the course of the river.

**A.** Ox-bow lakes (abandoned meander loops) and distributaries of a delta; both are lower-course depositional features.

### Memory skeleton

- River as a conveyor belt: erosion, transport, deposition; energy from volume and velocity; slow water drops coarse load first
- Rain to river: sheet, rill, gully, valley; catchment, divide, basin; four drainage patterns
- Erosion (hydraulic action, corrasion, attrition, solution) and load (suspension, saltation, traction, solution)
- Long profile: upper course cuts down (V-valley, gorge, canyon, falls), middle widens and carries (meanders), lower deposits
- Lower course and mouth: flood plain, levee, point bar, ox-bow, braids; alluvial fan and delta (five conditions, four types)
- Rejuvenation: terraces, knickpoint, incised meanders; land sinking or sea rising drowns valleys
- Cycle of erosion: youth, maturity, old age, peneplain with monadnocks; stages of a landscape are not courses of a river
