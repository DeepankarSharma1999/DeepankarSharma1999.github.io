## GS-066: Imperialism and African/Asian colonisation

Mains GS1 · World History · 18th–20th century

*Goal:* Explain Imperialism and African/Asian colonisation and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Economic drivers of late nineteenth-century empire

After Britain scrapped the Corn Laws, cheap imported food replaced home production, and land was cleared in eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia to feed British demand. Railways, ports and settlements needed capital, largely from London, and labour, which drove mass migration. Colonisation itself stimulated investment in faster railways and larger ships, so technology and empire reinforced each other. Specialised commodity production — wheat, cotton, rubber — spread so fast that world trade multiplied many times over by 1914, dominated by primary products. Cheaper meat and food in Europe raised living standards and, NCERT notes, encouraged social peace at home and backing for imperialism abroad.

### Africa: partition, labour and rinderpest

European powers formalised the partition of Africa at Berlin in 1885. Explorers such as Henry Morton Stanley travelled armed, mapped terrain and fought local groups, so exploration served conquest. Europeans wanted plantations and mines but faced a labour shortage, because Africans with ample land and cattle had little reason to work for wages. Colonisers responded with heavy taxes payable only through wage work, inheritance rules that let one family member inherit and pushed others into the labour market, and closed compounds for miners. Rinderpest then wiped out most cattle; planters, mine owners and colonial governments monopolised the surviving herds, forcing Africans into wage labour and helping Europeans subdue the continent.

### Asia, forms of control and exam angles

In Asia the East India Company paid for Chinese tea, silk and porcelain by selling Indian opium in China — a triangular trade between Britain, India and China — and Britain's use of force to expand that trade produced the First Opium War. NCERT's timeline also notes French occupation of Indo-China from 1862 and Britain's annexation of Burma in 1885–86. Indian indentured workers on five-year contracts went to plantations abroad as cottage industries declined at home. Prelims distinctions: Berlin 1885 for Africa; settler colony versus direct rule versus semi-colony. Mains dimensions: ecological shocks, coerced labour, migration, and anti-imperial nationalism that challenged colonial exploitation almost everywhere.
