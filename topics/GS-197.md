## GS-197: Local body finances and devolution

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · States, local governance and federalism

### Bridge: Tax, fee, user charge, grant and loan: the money words

A **tax** is a compulsory payment to government with no direct return service to the payer (for example property tax). A **fee** is a payment for a particular government service or licence (a trade licence fee). A **user charge** is a price for using a public service such as water supply or sewerage. A **grant** is money *given* by one government to another or to a local body, which may be **tied** (to a purpose) or **untied**; it need not be repaid. A **loan** is money *borrowed* and must be repaid with interest. These matter because a local body's money is classified by source: **tax** and **user charges** are **own revenue**; **grants** are **transfers**; **loans** are **debt**. A **toll** is a charge for crossing or using a road or bridge, and a **cess** is a tax levied for a stated purpose (such as education) on top of another tax. When UPSC says 'sources other than grants', it is asking about own revenue (own taxes, fees, user charges, non-tax income) and, in some readings, borrowing. Keep three transfers apart: grants-in-aid (from the State's Consolidated Fund, Art. 243H(c)), assigned taxes (collected by the State and handed over, Art. 243H(b)) and tax shares or devolution (SFC formula); all three are transfers but only the first is a grant.

### Bridge: Own revenue against transfers: assigned, shared, tied and untied

**Own revenue** is money a body raises itself under powers given by law. **Transfers** are money received from a higher government. Among transfers: **assigned revenue** means a tax collected by the State and *handed over* to the local body; **devolution** (or 'sharing') means a **share** of State tax proceeds allocated to local bodies on a formula, usually from a Finance Commission; **grants-in-aid** are payments from the State's Consolidated Fund. Assigned taxes and tax shares are transfers but not grants-in-aid. A grant is **tied** if it must be spent for a stated purpose, and **untied** if the receiver decides. Why it matters: own revenue gives **autonomy and accountability**; transfers give **adequacy and equity** (poorer areas get more) but reduce independence. For Mains, 'responsibility without resources' means a body with many functions but low own revenue and mostly tied transfers.

### 1. Orientation: why money decides whether local government works

*Objective:* Define the three Fs (funds, functions, functionaries) plus functionality, and place this topic between the structure lessons (GS-195, GS-196) and the Finance Commission lesson (GS-193).

## The question this topic answers

GS-195 (the 73rd Amendment) and GS-196 (the 74th Amendment) gave local bodies a *structure*: elected councils, fixed terms, reserved seats, State Election Commissions. This lesson asks the harder question that UPSC keeps asking: **do those bodies have the money and powers to do anything?** Syllabus language is 'devolution of powers and finances up to local levels and challenges therein' (Mains GS II).

## The three Fs

Mains answers on local government use a formula: **functions, funds and functionaries**.

- **Functions**: the subjects a local body is actually allowed to handle (the 29 items of the Eleventh Schedule for panchayats, the 18 of the Twelfth Schedule for municipalities).
- **Funds**: the money it can raise or receive.
- **Functionaries**: the staff who work under its control.

The 2020 Mains question adds a fourth word, **functionality**: whether the body works as a living institution rather than only existing on paper. **Devolution** means passing powers and resources *down* to a lower level; it is different from *delegation*, where the higher level keeps control and merely asks the lower level to carry out a task.

## Why money is the hinge

Laxmikanth quotes the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (Second ARC, 2005-2009): Part IX of the Constitution deals with *structural* empowerment, but the real strength of panchayats in autonomy and efficiency depends on their **financial position**, including their capacity to generate own resources (p. 845). NCERT agrees: local bodies earn little, spend more, and become 'agencies implementing the welfare and development schemes' of the Centre and States.

## A simple picture

Imagine a village school that needs a new roof. Who decides? Who pays? Who hires the mason? If the panchayat decides but the State pays and the block office hires, the panchayat is a messenger, not a government. Each of the three Fs answers one of those questions.

## What is not repeated here

Structure, reservation and committees are in GS-195 and GS-196. The Union Finance Commission, Articles 268-275 and Article 282 are in GS-193. Federal tensions are in GS-201. This lesson works only on the money, the powers and the staff of local bodies, with the links given in each module.

## Roadmap

Modules 2-4: what money local bodies can have and where it comes from. Modules 5-6: the Finance Commissions. Module 7: functions and functionaries. Module 8: the problems. Module 9: audit, bonds and reform ideas. Module 10: how UPSC tests it.

### 2. The legal gateway: Articles 243H and 243X, and why every power is a 'may'

*Objective:* State the four things a State legislature may do on local body finances and explain why local bodies have no independent taxing power.

## Who holds the taxing power

Under the Seventh Schedule (the list of subjects, GS-201 bridge) taxing power lies with the **Union and State legislatures**. A panchayat or municipality has **no power of its own to tax**: it can levy only what a **State law** authorises. This is the root of every fiscal problem in this lesson. Standard knowledge (verify): 'local government' is item 5 of the State List and 'taxes on lands and buildings' is item 49, which is why property tax is a State-law tax collected by local bodies.

## Article 243H (panchayats) and Article 243X (municipalities)

Both Articles use the same four-part scheme. The **State legislature may**, by law:

1. **Authorise** the local body to **levy, collect and appropriate** (or keep) taxes, duties, tolls and fees;
2. **Assign** to it taxes, duties, tolls and fees **levied and collected by the State government**;
3. Provide **grants-in-aid** to it from the **Consolidated Fund of the State**;
4. Provide for the constitution of **funds** to credit all moneys of the local body.

Source: Laxmikanth p. 835 (panchayats) and p. 865 (municipalities).

## The word that matters: 'may'

The Constitution says the legislature **may**, not **must**. Compare Part IX's compulsory provisions (elections, reservation, five-year term, State Election Commission, State Finance Commission) with these voluntary ones: the funds and powers sections are **permissive**. That is why NCERT says the actual transfer 'depends upon State legislation' and why 'each State decides how many of these 29 subjects' go to the local bodies. This is the 'State discretion' problem you will meet again in modules 7 and 8.

## What each of the four options means

- **Own taxes and fees** (option 1): the body collects and spends. An example from Laxmikanth's list of village panchayat taxes is the house or property tax; it is **own revenue**.
- **Assigned revenue** (option 2): the State collects but gives the money to the local body; the body does not control the rate.
- **Grants-in-aid** (option 3): a transfer from the State to the local body; the local body has little say over the amount.
- **Funds** (option 4): the accounting arrangement; money must be credited to a fund.

(See the bridge modules for the plain-language difference between tax, fee and grant.)

## Two cautions

First, the **ceiling on profession tax**: the Constitution (Article 276) limits the tax on professions, trades, callings and employments to **Rs 2,500 per person per year** (Laxmikanth p. 345, State level; verify whether later changes have been made and how it applies to local bodies). Second, taxes that a State leaves to the local level can also be taken back: a State may change its laws, and the 101st Amendment (GST, 2016) deleted the State List entry for entry taxes (verify; GS-193 for GST); this is how **octroi** (a tax on goods entering a local area) was abolished in most States and why municipalities lost a major source (module 4).

### 3. Panchayat finances: five channels and why own revenue is thin

*Objective:* Name the five channels of panchayat funding, show which tier has which taxes, and explain the weak own-revenue base.

## The five channels (Second ARC)

Laxmikanth (pp. 845-846) gives the Second ARC's summary. Panchayats receive funds as:

1. **Grants from the Union** on the recommendations of the **Central (Union) Finance Commission**, Article 280.
2. **Devolution from the State** on the recommendations of the **State Finance Commission**, Article 243I.
3. **Loans and grants from the State government.**
4. **Programme-specific allocations** under **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)** and Additional Central Assistance.
5. **Internal resource generation**, tax and non-tax.

Channels 1-4 are **transfers**; only channel 5 is **own revenue**.

## Own revenue: the tier difference

The Second ARC notes that **Gram Panchayats** are better placed than the other two tiers because they have a **tax domain of their own**; the intermediate and district tiers depend on **tolls, fees and non-tax revenue**, confined to areas such as ferry services, markets, water and conservancy services, vehicle registration and cess on stamp duty. State Panchayati Raj Acts have given **most taxation powers to village panchayats**.

The taxes, duties, tolls and fees in various State laws for village panchayats include **octroi, property or house tax, profession tax, land tax or cess, taxes or tolls on vehicles, entertainment tax or fees, licence fees, tax on non-agricultural land, fee on registration of cattle, sanitation, drainage or conservancy tax, water rate, lighting rate, education cess and tax on fairs and festivals**.

## The diagnosis

The Second ARC draws six conclusions:

- **Internal resource generation is weak**: partly a **thin tax domain**, partly the panchayats' own **reluctance** to collect.
- Panchayats are **heavily dependent on grants** from the Union and the State.
- A major share of those grants is **scheme-specific**, leaving panchayats limited discretion.
- Because of their own tight fiscal position, **States are not keen to devolve** funds.
- In critical Eleventh Schedule matters such as primary education, health, water supply, sanitation and minor irrigation, it is the **State government** that implements and spends.
- Overall, a situation has arisen where panchayats have **responsibility but grossly inadequate resources**.

Even in Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, regarded as progressive in empowering panchayats, they remain heavily dependent on grants.

## Why own revenue matters

Laxmikanth (p. 846) records the ARC's view that, although transfers form the major part of receipts in absolute terms, **own resources are the 'soul'** of financial standing, because a local tax system gives people a stake in an elected body and makes it **accountable** to its citizens. NCERT adds that dependence on grants erodes capacity to operate effectively: it gives a dated figure (rural local bodies raise about 0.24 per cent of total revenues but account for about 4 per cent of total expenditure), a number to use only with its date.

## Reluctance to tax

Laxmikanth's list of reasons for poor performance (p. 847) includes **reluctance to use fiscal powers**: very few panchayats levy tax on property, business, markets or fairs because it is difficult to tax your own neighbours.

### 4. Municipal finances: five sources, the property tax and municipal bonds

*Objective:* List the five sources of municipal revenue, explain why the property tax is central and octroi is gone, and outline municipal bonds with the right flags.

GS-196 (module 8) lists municipal revenue; here it is read through the lens of the three Fs. Learn the revenue list once from GS-196 module 8 and use the list below as the finance view of the same items.

## Five sources (Laxmikanth p. 878)

1. **Tax revenue**: property tax, entertainment tax, taxes on advertisements, professional tax, water tax, tax on animals, lighting tax, pilgrim tax, market tax, bridge tolls, **octroi** and cesses. **Property tax is the most important tax revenue**.
2. **Non-tax revenue**: rent on municipal property, fees and fines, royalty, profits and dividends, interest, and **user charges** for water, sanitation and sewerage.
3. **Grants** from the Union and State for programmes, infrastructure and reforms.
4. **Devolution** from the State on the **State Finance Commission's** recommendations.
5. **Loans** from the State and from financial institutions for **capital expenditure**; borrowing from financial institutions needs **State approval**.

**Octroi**, a tax on the entry of goods into a local area, has been **abolished in most States**, through State law changes and the 101st Amendment (GST, 2016), which deleted the State List entry for entry taxes (verify; GS-193 for GST). That removed a large and buoyant (fast-growing) source, so cities now depend even more on property tax and transfers (analytical point; verify with a current report).

## Constitutional anchor

Article 243X mirrors Article 243H (module 2); the State legislature **may** authorise taxes, assign State taxes, provide grants and create funds. Article 243Y deals with the finance commission and Article 243Z with accounts and audit (modules 5 and 9).

## Why the property tax is a weak pillar (qualitative)

The following are standard explanations, to be supported by a current report before you write them as facts: property **valuation is outdated**; **exemptions and under-assessment** shrink the base; **collection is poor**; rate-setting is **politically sensitive** because councillors face voters; and State law often limits local discretion on rates. The thought to hold on to: the municipality's most natural tax is a *State-law tax* that the municipality is reluctant or restricted to use.

## User charges

User charges (payment for a public service, such as water supply) are a non-tax source. They help only if the body can set sensible rates and collect them; many bodies charge below cost, leaving the service short of money (analytical; verify).

## Municipal bonds: a flagged outline

A **municipal bond** is a **loan taken from investors by a city**, repaid with interest from future revenues; it is meant to finance capital projects without waiting for grants. The extracts do **not** cover bonds. Standard knowledge, **to be verified**: the securities market regulator SEBI issued rules for municipal debt securities in 2015; a few cities (reported: Bangalore in 1997, usually cited as earlier than Ahmedabad in 1998, and Pune in 2017; verify) raised bonds; and uptake remains small because of weak own revenue and credit ratings. Do not quote amounts or the number of cities without a dated source.

## Link to the Central Council

The Central Council of Local Government (GS-196 module 8) is advisory and can recommend central financial assistance (Article 263 origin).

### 5. The State Finance Commission: Articles 243I and 243Y

*Objective:* State who constitutes the State Finance Commission, what it recommends, how its report is handled and why it is weaker in practice than on paper.

GS-195 (module 6) and GS-196 (module 5) introduce this; here we deepen it from the money angle.

## What it is

A **State Finance Commission (SFC)** is a body the **Governor** constitutes **after every five years** to review the **financial position of the local bodies** and recommend how State money should reach them. Panchayats are covered by **Article 243I** and municipalities by **Article 243Y**, and the same commission reviews both (Laxmikanth: the commission for the panchayats 'shall also' review municipalities).

## What it recommends

To the **Governor**:

1. The principles that should govern: (a) the **distribution between the State and the local bodies of the net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls and fees** levied by the State, and the **shares among local bodies at all levels**; (b) which **taxes, duties, tolls and fees may be assigned** to local bodies; (c) **grants-in-aid** to local bodies from the State's Consolidated Fund.
2. **Measures to improve the financial position** of local bodies.
3. Any other matter referred by the Governor for **sound finance** of local bodies.

The **State legislature decides the composition**, qualifications and selection of members. The Governor places the recommendations before the **State legislature with an action-taken report** (ATR).

## Why the Constitution created it

NCERT: the SFC reviews the distribution of revenues between the State and local governments **and between rural and urban local governments**, and 'ensures that allocation of funds to the rural local governments will not be a political matter'. Before 1992 funds depended on what a State government chose; the SFC substitutes a **periodic, rule-based review**, much as the Union Finance Commission does for States (GS-193).

## The chain

SFC recommends -> Governor lays the report and ATR before the State legislature -> the State may accept some, none or all -> **the SFC's recommendation is advisory**, like the Union Finance Commission's (Laxmikanth p. 938). **Acceptance is the State's political choice**; the legislature can debate the ATR.

## Gaps in practice

Laxmikanth (p. 847), citing the Ministry's roadmap: many States have not devolved the three Fs, and while SFCs have submitted recommendations, **few States have implemented them** or taken steps to ensure the **fiscal viability** of panchayats. Standard points to verify before writing: some SFCs were **delayed or irregular**, ATRs were not always tabled on time, and reports were not always available publicly. Note also that, by Article 243I, the first commission was due within one year of the 73rd Amendment's commencement and then every five years (verify the exact timing).

## Why it matters for the Union

The Union Finance Commission looks at SFC reports when it suggests top-ups for local bodies (module 6). A poor SFC process weakens that chain.

## Quick compare

- **SFC**: Governor, every five years, State to local bodies, advisory.
- **Union FC**: President, every fifth year, Union to States (and, on SFC advice, supplementing local bodies), advisory.

### 6. Union Finance Commission, central grants and tied funds

*Objective:* Explain how the Union Finance Commission supplements State resources for local bodies, what basic and tied grants mean, and the role of centrally sponsored schemes.

## The Union Finance Commission's duty

GS-193 (module 4) covers Article 280. For local bodies the key function (Laxmikanth pp. 937, 836, 866) is: the Union Finance Commission recommends **measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of the panchayats and the municipalities in the State, on the basis of the recommendations made by the State Finance Commission**. This was **added by the 73rd and 74th Amendments** (1992). In the Article text this is standard knowledge: **Article 280(3)(bb)** for panchayats and **280(3)(c)** for municipalities (verify the lettering in the Constitution).

Reading it slowly: the SFC speaks first about local needs; the Union Finance Commission then sees how much the State can be topped up. It is an **advisory** body: nothing in the Constitution makes its recommendations binding on the Union (Laxmikanth p. 938).

## How the money reaches local bodies

The extract does not describe the mechanics. Standard knowledge, **flagged to verify**: Union Finance Commissions since the 1990s have recommended **grants for local bodies**; the **15th Finance Commission** (chair N. K. Singh; period 2020-21 to 2025-26 as Laxmikanth lists it) is the previous award, and it recommended grants for rural and urban local bodies; as of October 2026 the 16th Finance Commission's award (2026-31) is in force and the current local-body grants come from it (verify the details). The two-part pattern below is the 15th's, described as the earlier pattern. These grants are commonly described in two parts: **basic (untied) grants**, which local bodies can spend on their own priorities within the Constitutional functions (with some exclusions such as salaries), and **tied grants**, earmarked for named purposes such as sanitation and drinking water. Entitlement is reported to depend on **conditions**: constituting an SFC, publishing audited accounts, and, for urban bodies, notifying minimum property-tax rates. Release is **through the State**. The **16th Finance Commission** was constituted in December 2023 and its award covers 2026-31; **verify** its local-body grants, conditions and the Union's acceptance. Do not quote amounts or shares from memory.

## Programme money: CSS and Additional Central Assistance

Besides Finance Commission grants, **channel 4** of the Second ARC list is **programme-specific allocations** under **Centrally Sponsored Schemes** and **Additional Central Assistance**: money for a named scheme with its own rules. Laxmikanth notes that a major share of Union and State grants is **scheme-specific**, so local bodies have **little discretion**. Article 282 (GS-193 module 6) is the usual legal channel: a discretionary grant for any public purpose. The same worry applies to **parallel bodies** and missions created to deliver faster, which can bypass the elected local body (module 7).

## Tied against untied: why it matters

A **tied grant** is money that must be used for a specified purpose; an **untied grant** can be spent on the recipient's own priorities. Tied money is useful when the Centre wants a national outcome. But Laxmikanth records that scheme-based funds may be unsuitable for local conditions, leading to **wrong activities or under-spending**, and that **overwhelming dependence** on outside funds means people are less likely to demand **social audit** of money they did not raise.

## Three clean lines for an answer

1. The Constitution gives the Union Finance Commission a **supporting** role for local bodies; the **primary** relationship is State-to-local.
2. Union money for local bodies tends to be **conditional and tied**, which strengthens national goals but weakens local autonomy.
3. Fiscal federalism for local bodies is therefore **three-layered**: Union to State, State to local, and **Union to local** through schemes.

### 7. Devolution of functions and functionaries: the 'State discretion' problem and activity mapping

*Objective:* Explain Article 243G and 243W devolution, the 'may' problem, activity mapping and why functionaries decide whether a function is really devolved.

## What the Constitution devolves

**Article 243G** (panchayats) and **Article 243W** (municipalities): the State legislature **may** endow local bodies with powers and authority to function as **institutions of self-government**, and may devolve responsibility for (a) **plans for economic development and social justice** and (b) **implementing schemes**, including the **29 matters of the Eleventh Schedule** (panchayats) and the **18 matters of the Twelfth Schedule** (municipalities). Lists are in GS-195 and GS-196.

## The 'State discretion' problem

The key word is again **may**. NCERT: 'The actual transfer of these functions depends upon the State legislation. Each State decides how many of these twenty-nine subjects would be transferred.' It adds that many States have not transferred most subjects, so elections risk becoming 'somewhat symbolic'. Result: **de jure** (in law) a list of 29 or 18 functions; **de facto** (in practice) control by State departments.

Why do States hold back? The sources give clues: States themselves are fiscally tight and **not keen to devolve funds**; in critical matters like primary education, health, water supply, sanitation and minor irrigation, **State government implements and spends directly**. There is also **bureaucratic control**: sarpanches visit block offices for funds and technical approval, which distorts their role (Laxmikanth p. 847).

## Activity mapping

**Activity mapping** (standard knowledge: verify) is the exercise of taking each subject and dividing its **activities** among the three levels: Union or State, intermediate or district, and village or ward, specifying who **plans, finances, implements and employs staff**. For example 'primary education' becomes many activities: setting the curriculum, constructing buildings, hiring teachers, running the midday meal. Without mapping, a transfer of the 'subject' on paper means little. Link to **subsidiarity** (module 9): each activity should go to the lowest level that can perform it.

## Functionaries: the third F

The third F, **functionaries**, is the hardest. Local bodies do not control the staff who work on their subjects: officials are State employees who answer to State departments. Laxmikanth records that many Gram Panchayats lack a **full-time Secretary**, around a quarter (Laxmikanth's 'around 25 percent', an undated roadmap figure; verify) lack office buildings and elected members are often semi-literate or untrained (figures printed in the roadmap are old; use them only with a date). For municipalities, the **Commissioner is appointed by the State** (GS-196) and the three personnel systems (separate, unified, integrated) show different degrees of local control (GS-196 module 8).

## Parallel bodies

Laxmikanth warns about **parallel bodies** created for 'speedy implementation': little evidence they avoid corruption or elite capture, and they **usurp the space of panchayats** and demoralise them, being better resourced. This links to the NCERT comment that local bodies often act as 'agencies implementing the welfare and development schemes' of the Centre and States.

## Functionality

The 2020 Mains question's word **functionality** adds: even if functions, funds and functionaries are formally present, does the body **plan, decide and deliver**? The tests are: **meetings held, Gram Sabha active, plans made, funds spent on local priority, accountability to citizens** (analytical framework; not from a single source).

## Three lines for a Mains answer

1. Constitution made structure compulsory and devolution **voluntary**. 2. State control and parallel agencies kept power above. 3. Reform requires **statutory, mapped, resourced** devolution.

### 8. The fiscal problems of local bodies: a cause-and-effect diagnosis

*Objective:* State and explain the main fiscal problems qualitatively (own revenue, tied grants, unfunded mandates, property tax, dependence) without unsourced numbers.

This module is the one most often turned into a Mains answer. Use causal chains, not lists.

## 1. Low own revenue

**Cause**: thin tax domain (State law decides), reluctance to tax neighbours, poor capacity to assess and collect. **Effect**: dependence on transfers; weak accountability to residents. The Second ARC called own resources 'the soul' of financial standing.

## 2. Dependence on tied grants

**Cause**: Union and State funds flow mainly through **scheme-specific** channels (CSS, Additional Central Assistance). **Effect**: local bodies lose discretion; activities may mismatch local needs; money may go **unspent** or be spent wrongly (Laxmikanth p. 847).

## 3. Unfunded mandates

An **unfunded mandate** is a duty placed on a lower level of government without the money to carry it out. **Example in the sources**: the Constitution lists 29 and 18 functions, but funds are not automatically transferred (analytical use of the term; verify before quoting examples). **Effect**: 'responsibility without resources' (Second ARC).

## 4. State reluctance and weak SFCs

**Cause**: State governments are themselves short of money and are 'not keen to devolve'; SFC recommendations are **advisory** and few States have implemented them. **Effect**: local bodies cannot plan; the 3Fs stay incomplete.

## 5. Property tax and user charges (qualitative)

**Cause** (standard; verify): outdated valuation, exemptions, under-collection, political sensitivity, below-cost user charges. **Effect**: urban bodies fall back on State grants and loans, which also limits borrowing capacity.

## 6. Loss of octroi

Octroi was abolished in most States, by State law changes and the 101st Amendment (GST, 2016), which deleted the State List entry for entry taxes (verify; GS-193 for GST); no equally buoyant tax replaced it in many places. **Effect**: pressure on city budgets (analytical).

## 7. Staffing and capacity

A large number of Gram Panchayats lack full-time Secretaries (Laxmikanth ch. 38; figures dated) and basic facilities in the roadmap's figures (dated); many members are untrained. **Effect**: weak budgeting, accounting and project management, which hurts both **absorption of funds** and **accountability**.

## 8. Parallel bodies and bureaucratic control

Missions and special agencies create a competing structure; block offices control approvals. **Effect**: demoralised local bodies and unclear accountability (Laxmikanth p. 847-848).

## What the data says (use carefully)

NCERT gives the dated example that rural local bodies raise about **0.24 per cent of total revenues** but account for about **4 per cent of expenditure**. Treat this as an illustration with a date, not as current. One other dated figure appears in module 7: Laxmikanth's 'around 25 percent' of Gram Panchayats lacking office buildings (roadmap figure, undated; verify). No other statistic is used in this lesson.

## The compact answer line

'Local bodies have gained **structure** (Part IX and IX-A) but not **substance**: **funds** are meagre and tied, **functions** are devolved at the State's discretion, and **functionaries** remain under State control, so **functionality** suffers.'

## Where the 'positive' column goes

For balance, record gains: regular elections, reserved seats for women and weaker sections, SFCs and the State Election Commission, and the possibility of social audit (GS-195 module 9).

### 9. Audit, accountability, reform ideas, the Second ARC and the principle of subsidiarity

*Objective:* Explain Articles 243J and 243Z, the flagged role of the Examiner of Local Fund Accounts and the CAG, and state the principle of subsidiarity with reform ideas.

## Audit of accounts

**Article 243J** (panchayats) and **Article 243Z** (municipalities): the State legislature **may** make provisions on the **maintenance of accounts** and their **audit**. Again permissive. The extracts do not name the auditors. Standard knowledge, **flagged to verify**: in many States, audit of local bodies is done by the **Examiner of Local Fund Accounts** (a State office) or similar State auditors; **the CAG is not the constitutional auditor of local bodies**, but Finance Commissions and later arrangements are reported to have given the CAG a **'Technical Guidance and Supervision' (TGS)** role over this audit, to improve standards. Confirm the current arrangement before you write it in an answer.

## Accountability beyond audit

Audit is after-the-fact. Other tools named in the sources: the **Gram Sabha** (social audit; in Scheduled Areas under PESA a village panchayat must obtain the Gram Sabha's certification of utilisation of funds, GS-195 module 7); **State Finance Commission** ATRs tabled in the legislature; **State Election Commission** elections. The Second ARC's point is that **own revenue** strengthens citizens' demand for accountability: when people pay local taxes, they ask what the money does.

## The Second ARC

The **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005-2009)** is quoted in Laxmikanth for the five channels and the diagnosis. Its report on local governance (Sixth Report, 2007; verify the title and recommendations) is reported to have used the principle of **subsidiarity**. The extract gives only the finance summary: use only that, and flag the rest.

## Subsidiarity

**Subsidiarity** means that a function should be performed by the **lowest level of government that can perform it effectively**, and higher levels step in only when the lower cannot. In India's setting: villages decide village matters; districts do what villages cannot; the State does what districts cannot. It supports the 2025 Mains question about decision-making being too far from information and execution: local officials know local needs, so planning and spending should be close to the problem. Note the **limit**: subsidiarity assumes the lower level has capacity and funds, which is why devolution of money and staff has to come with devolution of functions.

## Reform ideas (as a checklist)

1. **State laws** that make devolution of all three Fs real; **activity mapping**.
2. **Implement SFC recommendations** and table ATRs on time.
3. **Untie funds** or reduce earmarking; allow local priorities.
4. **Strengthen own revenue**: property-tax reform (valuation, collection), user charges, and local willingness to tax, coupled with services residents can see.
5. **Union grants with conditions that promote reform** (as in the 15th Finance Commission reporting, the previous award; the current grants come from the 16th; verify), not conditions that reduce local choice.
6. **Integrate parallel bodies** with local government.
7. **Build capacity**: full-time secretaries, training, technology and data.
8. **Better audit**: timely, public and linked to Gram Sabha follow-up.
9. **Market access** for creditworthy urban bodies: municipal bonds (flagged), with safeguards.

## An integrating idea

Fiscal devolution has **three** layers (Union to State, State to local, Union to local through schemes), and each layer has its **own accountability** question: who decides, who pays, who answers?

## Elimination habits for Prelims

Reject **absolute claims** ('all States', 'only', 'always'). Check the **authority**: Governor (not CM) for SFC; State legislature (not Parliament) for taxes of local bodies. Check **numbers**: 29, 18, 21 years, five years. Beware options that mix **Union** and **State** Finance Commissions.

## Common traps

- Treating Article 243H as a **list** of taxes.

- Writing old dated percentages as current.

### Notes: Money vocabulary

- Tax: compulsory payment without a specific service in return.
- Fee: payment for a service or licence.
- User charge: price for a public service such as water.
- Toll and cess: a charge for passing or using a facility; a purpose-linked levy.
- Grant-in-aid: transfer that need not be repaid; tied (earmarked) or untied.
- Assigned revenue: State-collected tax handed to local bodies.
- Devolution: sharing of State tax proceeds or powers with local bodies.
- Loan: borrowed money to be repaid.
- Unfunded mandate: a duty without the money to perform it.
- Activity mapping: dividing a subject's activities among the levels.
- Subsidiarity: do it at the lowest level that can do it well.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Tax versus fee versus user charge.
- Own revenue versus assigned revenue versus devolution.
- Tied grant versus untied grant.
- State Finance Commission (Governor) versus Union Finance Commission (President).
- Devolution versus delegation.
- Functions (subjects) versus functionaries (staff) versus functionality (working).

### Core concepts

- Local body money flows from State law: the Constitution says 'may', not 'must' (Articles 243H, 243X).
- Own revenue is thin; transfers are mostly tied.
- The State Finance Commission links the State to local bodies; the Union Finance Commission supplements on its advice.
- Functions, funds and functionaries must be devolved together; functionality is whether they work.
- Subsidiarity and activity mapping guide devolution.

**Q.** Why does the word 'may' in Articles 243G, 243H, 243W and 243X matter?

**A.** It makes devolution of powers and finances depend on State legislation, so the 'State discretion' problem arises.

**Q.** Why do own revenues strengthen accountability?

**A.** Residents who pay local taxes are more likely to demand to know how the money is spent, which gives the body a stake in citizens' trust.

**Q.** Why are tied grants both useful and harmful?

**A.** They secure national or State outcomes, but reduce local discretion, may mismatch local needs and can lead to under-spending.

**Q.** What does subsidiarity imply for planning?

**A.** Planning and spending decisions should be placed with the lowest level that can handle them, close to information and execution, with funds and staff devolved.

**Q.** A State has not constituted an SFC for ten years. Using the Constitution, say what has gone wrong and what the 15th Finance Commission is reported to do about it (flag verify).
