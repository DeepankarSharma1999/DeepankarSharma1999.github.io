### Bridge: Exponentials, the number e and the natural logarithm

**e and exponential decay.** The number e = 2.71828... is chosen so that the function e^(ct) is its own rate of change up to the factor c: d/dt e^(ct) = c e^(ct). A quantity with dN/dt = −N/τ therefore equals N₀ e^(−t/τ): it falls to 1/e = 0.368 of its start in time τ, to about 0.135 (= e⁻²) in 2τ, and so on.

**Logarithm.** The natural logarithm ln undoes e^x: ln(e^x) = x, ln(ab) = ln a + ln b, ln(1/a) = −ln a. Useful values: ln 2 = 0.693, ln 4 = 1.386, ln 5 = 1.609, ln 10 = 2.303. To find the time at which e^(−γt) = ½ take ln of both sides: −γt = −ln 2, so t = ln 2/γ.

**Small-quantity expansions.** For small x: e^x ≈ 1 + x, √(1 − x) ≈ 1 − x/2. Example: √(1 − 0.0002) ≈ 0.9999.

### Bridge: Complex numbers in one page: i, Euler's formula and conjugates

**i** is a number with i² = −1. A complex number is a + i c with real a, c. Its **conjugate** is a − i c; the sum of a number and its conjugate is real (2a).

**Euler's formula.** e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ for real θ (θ in radians). Hence e^(iθ) + e^(−iθ) = 2 cos θ, and |e^(iθ)| = 1: multiplying by e^(iθ) only turns a number, it never changes its size.

**Why it is allowed.** Differential equations like the one below are linear with constant coefficients; trial solutions e^(pt) with complex p are legitimate, and because the equation has real coefficients, the real and imaginary parts (or any combination that is real) are each solutions. This is the only use made of complex numbers in the lesson. PH-035 bridge B2 (solving a differential equation, initial conditions) is assumed; complex numbers are new here.

### 2. Foundation: the damping force, the equation of motion and the first-order special case

*Objective:* Write m ẍ + b ẋ + k x = 0 with signs and units, define γ, ω₀ and τ, and solve the drag-only case.

**What damping is.** In a real oscillator (a pendulum in air, a mass on a spring in oil, charge in a circuit with resistance) energy is steadily transferred to the surroundings as heat and the swings shrink. The loss is called **damping**; the forces responsible are dissipative (non-conservative), so the mechanical energy is not conserved.

**The model.** For slow motion in a fluid the resisting force is, to a first approximation, proportional to the speed and opposite to the velocity (Mathur 8.1 credits Mayevski; Upadhyaya 8.1 says 'at ordinary speeds'):

$$ F_d = −b v = −b dx/dt $$

This is a model, not a law of nature: at high speed the drag grows faster than v, and dry friction is nearly constant. The model is used because it gives an equation that can be solved exactly.

**Setting.** A mass m on a spring of constant k; coordinate x measured from the equilibrium position, positive to the right; v = dx/dt. The spring force is −k x (PH-035) and the drag is −b v, both opposing displacement or motion. Newton's second law gives

$$ m ẍ = −k x − b ẋ  ⟹  m ẍ + b ẋ + k x = 0 $$

**Units.** F = b v gives b = N/(m/s) = kg/s (Mathur example 8.5 shows that r/2m, called b there, has unit s⁻¹).

**Dividing by m.** Define

$$ γ = b/2m  (s⁻¹, decay constant),   ω₀² = k/m  (rad²/s²),   τ = m/b = 1/2γ  (s, relaxation time) $$

so that the equation becomes

$$ ẍ + 2γ ẋ + ω₀² x = 0 $$

With γ = 0 this is exactly PH-035's ẍ = −ω₀² x. The factor 2 in 2γ is chosen to make later formulas tidy.

**The first-order special case (no spring).** Remove the spring (k = 0): m v̇ = −b v, so v̇ = −v/τ. Separate and integrate: ∫dv/v = −∫dt/τ, ln v = −t/τ + const, and with v = v₀ at t = 0, 

$$ v = v₀ e^(−t/τ) $$

The speed falls to v₀/e = 0.368 v₀ after the time τ (Mathur 8.1). The distance travelled is x = v₀τ(1 − e^(−t/τ)), which tends to v₀τ. This shows the pattern that survives in the full problem: an **exponential** envelope with a time scale set by m/b.

**Number (the 2025 question's data).** m = 0.25 kg and drag coefficient 0.07 kg/s give γ = 0.14 s⁻¹ and τ = 3.57 s.

### 3. Core: solving the equation and the three regimes

*Objective:* Obtain the characteristic equation, its roots and the three cases from the sign of γ² − ω₀², and express them in terms of b, m, k and Q.

**Why exponentials.** The equation ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = 0 is **linear** (x appears only to the first power) with constant coefficients, and a function whose derivatives are all multiples of itself is the exponential e^(pt). Two further facts: (i) if x₁ and x₂ are solutions, so is C₁x₁ + C₂x₂ (substitute and use linearity); (ii) a second-order equation needs two constants, which two initial conditions x(0) = x₀ and v(0) = v₀ will fix (PH-035 bridge B2).

**Characteristic equation.** Try x = e^(pt), ẋ = p e^(pt), ẍ = p² e^(pt) and divide by e^(pt) (never zero):

$$ p² + 2γ p + ω₀² = 0 $$

Quadratic formula:

$$ p = −γ ± √(γ² − ω₀²) $$

**Both roots always decay.** Their sum is −2γ < 0 and product ω₀² > 0, so real roots are both negative, and complex roots have real part −γ < 0. Every solution therefore dies out, as it must because the drag only removes energy.

**Three cases.** Let D = γ² − ω₀².

1. **D < 0** (γ < ω₀): two complex conjugate roots, p = −γ ± iω′ with ω′ = √(ω₀² − γ²). The solution oscillates inside a decaying envelope: **underdamped** (module 4).
2. **D = 0** (γ = ω₀): one repeated root p = −γ. **Critical damping** (module 5).
3. **D > 0** (γ > ω₀): two distinct negative real roots. No oscillation: **overdamped**, also called aperiodic or dead beat (module 5).

**In terms of the physical quantities.** γ < ω₀ means b/2m < √(k/m), that is **b < 2√(mk)**. The critical drag coefficient is b_c = 2√(mk) = 2mω₀. With the quality factor Q = ω₀/2γ = √(mk)/b (derived in module 7): underdamped means Q > ½, critical means Q = ½, overdamped means Q < ½.

**Numbers.** Mathur example 8.2: m = 0.25 kg, k = 25 N/m, so b_c = 2√(0.25 × 25) = 5 kg/s. Mathur example 8.3: m = 0.025 kg, k = 25 N/m, b = 1.5 kg/s gives γ = 1.5/0.05 = 30 s⁻¹, ω₀² = 1000, γ² = 900, so D = −100 < 0: underdamped, ω′ = √100 = 10 rad/s, T′ = 2π/10 = 0.628 s. Mathur example 8.4 gives f₀/f′ = 2/√3 and asks for the damping: ω′/ω₀ = √3/2, so γ = ω₀/2; with m = 1 kg and k = 25 N/m, ω₀ = 5, γ = 2.5 s⁻¹ and b = 2mγ = 5 kg/s.

**Checks.** Dimensions: γ and ω₀ have dimension T⁻¹, so γ² − ω₀² is T⁻² and the root has dimension T⁻¹, as p must. Limit b → 0: γ → 0, p → ±iω₀, the SHM of PH-035 (module 8 of this lesson uses this).

### 4. Core: the underdamped solution, frequency, amplitude and initial conditions

*Objective:* Derive x = A e^(−γt) cos(ω′t + φ), check it by substitution, fix A and φ from x₀ and v₀, and explain the effect of damping on amplitude and frequency.

**Setting.** γ < ω₀; roots p = −γ ± iω′, ω′ = √(ω₀² − γ²) (real). x from equilibrium, positive to the right. Two constants.

**Step 1: general complex form.** x = e^(−γt)(C₁e^(iω′t) + C₂e^(−iω′t)).

**Step 2: make it real.** x must be real, so C₂ is the conjugate of C₁. Write C₁ = ½A e^(iφ) with A, φ real; then, by Euler (bridge B2),

$$ x = A e^(−γt) cos(ω′t + φ) $$

**Step 3: check by substitution.** Let θ = ω′t + φ, c = cos θ, s = sin θ. Then ẋ = −A e^(−γt)(γc + ω′s) and ẍ = A e^(−γt)[(γ² − ω′²)c + 2γω′ s]. Substituting in ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x: the s terms cancel (2γω′ − 2γω′ = 0) and the c terms give γ² − ω′² − 2γ² + ω₀² = ω₀² − γ² − ω′² = 0. ✓

**Step 4: constants.** x(0) = x₀ and v(0) = v₀ give x₀ = A cos φ and v₀ = −A(γ cos φ + ω′ sin φ), so A sin φ = −(v₀ + γx₀)/ω′ and

$$ A = √[x₀² + ((v₀ + γ x₀)/ω′)²] ,   φ = atan2(−(v₀ + γx₀)/ω′ , x₀) $$

(use the signs of both components to get the quadrant, as in PH-035 module 3). Equivalent form: x = e^(−γt)[x₀ cos ω′t + ((v₀ + γx₀)/ω′) sin ω′t]. **Released from rest at x₀:** x = x₀ e^(−γt)[cos ω′t + (γ/ω′) sin ω′t]. Numerical check: m = 0.5, k = 50, b = 2 (γ = 2, ω′ = 9.798), x₀ = 0.1 m gives x(0.3 s) = −0.0515 m, which agrees with a direct numerical integration of the equation.

**Effect on amplitude.** The envelope A e^(−γt) falls exponentially; the amplitude is not constant. The function is not periodic in the strict sense, but successive maxima on one side are exactly T′ = 2π/ω′ apart (ẋ = 0 requires tan(ω′t + φ) = −γ/ω′, which recurs every T′/2, alternately a maximum and a minimum), and the zeros are T′/2 apart.

**Effect on frequency.** ω′ = √(ω₀² − γ²) < ω₀: damping lowers the frequency and lengthens the period. The shift is tiny for weak damping: ω′/ω₀ = √(1 − 1/4Q²) ≈ 1 − 1/(8Q²). For the 2025 data, T′ = 0.340763 s and T₀ = 0.340754 s, a relative change of 2.9 × 10⁻⁵.

**Limit and interpretation.** As γ → 0 the solution tends to A cos(ω₀t + φ), the SHM of PH-035 with φ matching the old initial conditions. Physically, drag subtracts energy continuously, so each swing is lower than the last, and it also slows the motion slightly, hence a slightly longer period.

### 5. Core: critical damping and overdamping

*Objective:* Derive both non-oscillatory solutions, fix their constants, and compare how fast each regime returns to equilibrium.

**Critical damping, γ = ω₀.** The characteristic equation has the single root p = −γ, giving one solution e^(−γt); a second-order equation needs another. Try x = t e^(−γt): ẋ = e^(−γt)(1 − γt), ẍ = e^(−γt)(−2γ + γ²t). Substituting in ẍ + 2γẋ + γ²x gives e^(−γt)[−2γ + γ²t + 2γ − 2γ²t + γ²t] = 0 ✓. So

$$ x = (C₁ + C₂ t) e^(−γt) $$

Constants: x(0) = x₀ gives C₁ = x₀; ẋ(0) = C₂ − γC₁ = v₀ gives C₂ = v₀ + γx₀. Hence x = [x₀ + (v₀ + γx₀)t] e^(−γt). (Mathur and Upadhyaya reach the same form as the limit h → 0 of the overdamped solution, with h = √(γ² − ω₀²).)

**Released from rest.** x = x₀(1 + γt)e^(−γt) and ẋ = −x₀γ²t e^(−γt), which is never positive: the displacement falls steadily and does not overshoot. If the mass is pushed inward hard (v₀ < −γx₀), x can cross zero once, at t = −x₀/(v₀ + γx₀), and then creeps back; it can never cross twice. (The books say the displacement 'first increases'; that is true of the second term t e^(−γt) alone, and of the whole only if v₀ points away from equilibrium.)

**Overdamping, γ > ω₀.** Put q = √(γ² − ω₀²), so 0 < q < γ and the roots are −(γ − q) and −(γ + q), both negative:

$$ x = C₁ e^(−(γ−q)t) + C₂ e^(−(γ+q)t) $$

Constants: C₁ + C₂ = x₀ and −(γ − q)C₁ − (γ + q)C₂ = v₀, giving C₁ = [(γ + q)x₀ + v₀]/(2q) and C₂ = [(q − γ)x₀ − v₀]/(2q). From rest: x = x₀[(γ + q)e^(−(γ−q)t) − (γ − q)e^(−(γ+q)t)]/(2q). Number: γ = 5, ω₀ = 3 (q = 4) gives x = x₀[(9/8)e^(−t) − (1/8)e^(−9t)], and at t = 0.5 s x = 0.681 x₀, checked numerically. The fast term dies quickly; the slow term, with decay rate γ − q, controls the return, and the motion never changes sign.

**Which returns fastest without oscillating?** The slow decay rate of the overdamped case is γ − q = ω₀²/(γ + q), which is less than ω₀ because γ + q > ω₀. At critical damping the rate is γ = ω₀. So critical damping is the fastest non-oscillatory approach. The underdamped envelope decays at the slower rate γ < ω₀, and it overshoots, crossing zero again and again. **Uses:** pointer instruments (voltmeter, galvanometer) settle quickest at critical damping, and door closers are set near it.

**Check.** The critical solution is the limit of the underdamped one as ω′ → 0 (module 8): cos ω′t → 1 and sin(ω′t)/ω′ → t, so x₀e^(−γt)[cos ω′t + (γ/ω′)sin ω′t] → x₀e^(−γt)(1 + γt).

### 6. Core: logarithmic decrement, relaxation time and counting oscillations

*Objective:* Define the logarithmic decrement and relaxation time with their conventions, derive the half-amplitude time and the number of oscillations, and avoid the convention trap.

**Setting.** Underdamped, envelope a(t) = A e^(−γt) (module 4). Successive maxima on one side occur T′ = 2π/ω′ apart, so

$$ a(t)/a(t + T′) = e^(γT′) $$

**Definition.** The **logarithmic decrement per period** is Λ = ln[a(t)/a(t + T′)] = γT′ (dimensionless). After n complete oscillations a_n = a₀ e^(−nΛ), so

$$ Λ = (1/n) ln(a₀/a_n) $$

**The convention trap.** The books' text defines λ with successive swings on opposite sides, T′/2 apart, giving λ = γT′/2 = Λ/2 (Mathur 8.2, Upadhyaya 8.2). Their worked examples, however, use the full period: Mathur example 8.8 gets λ = bT = 0.023, and Upadhyaya example 6 divides ln(a₀/a_n) by the number of complete oscillations. A question that says 'after n complete oscillations' is answered by Λ = (1/n) ln(a₀/a_n); **state the convention in the answer**, and mention the half-period value if in doubt.

**Relaxation time and decay times.** (Mathur example 8.8 also prints the relaxation time as 1/b = 100 s; by the book's own τ = 1/2b it is 50 s, the slip of example 8.10 again.) Three times appear:

- **Amplitude e-fold time** 1/γ = 2τ (Upadhyaya calls it the decay modulus): a falls to a₀/e.
- **Energy e-fold time** τ = m/b = 1/2γ, the **relaxation time** (derived in module 7): E falls to E₀/e.
- **Half-amplitude time** t½ = ln 2/γ = 2m ln 2/b: from e^(−γt) = ½, as in bridge B1.

**Number of oscillations to halve the amplitude.** n½ = t½/T′ = ln 2/(γT′) = ln 2/Λ.

**Link to Q (derived in module 7).** For weak damping, Λ = γT′ = 2πγ/ω′ ≈ 2πγ/ω₀ = π/Q.

**Worked example, with the book's slip corrected.** Upadhyaya example 6: amplitude 20 cm falls to 2 cm after 100 oscillations of period 4.6 s. Λ = ln 10/100 = 0.0230 per period, γ = Λ/T′ = 5.01 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹, and n½ = ln 2/Λ = 30.1 oscillations (the book's answer, 30). The book prints γ = 10⁻² s⁻¹ from 2λ/T with λ = 0.023, which does not follow from its own definition λ = γT′/2 (that would need λ = 0.0115); the printed number of oscillations is right.

**Use of Λ (first throw correction, Mathur 8.2).** In a ballistic galvanometer the first throw θ₁ is smaller than the undamped value θ₀ by the damping during the quarter-swing: θ₀ ≈ θ₁(1 + λ/2), where λ is the log of the ratio of successive swings on opposite sides. This is why the decrement is measured.

**Checks.** Λ is dimensionless; as γ → 0, Λ → 0 and the number of oscillations to halve → ∞, the undamped limit.

### 7. Core: energy decay, power loss and the quality factor Q

*Objective:* Derive the exact energy loss law, the exponential decay of the energy for weak damping, the average power loss, and Q with its relations to τ, Λ and the number of oscillations.

**Step 1: an exact law.** With E = ½mẋ² + ½kx², dE/dt = mẋẍ + kxẋ = ẋ(mẍ + kx) = ẋ(−bẋ), so

$$ dE/dt = −b ẋ² ≤ 0 $$

This holds for every b: the energy never increases, and the loss is the rate at which the drag does negative work.

**Step 2: energy of the underdamped motion.** Insert x = A e^(−γt)cos θ and ẋ = −A e^(−γt)(γc + ω′s) (θ = ω′t + φ). Then E = ½mA²e^(−2γt)[(γc + ω′s)² + ω₀²c²]. Expand: γ²c² + 2γω′sc + ω′²s² + ω₀²c² = ω′² + 2γ²c² + 2γω′sc = ω₀² + γ²cos 2θ + γω′ sin 2θ, using ω′² + γ² = ω₀² and 2c² = 1 + cos 2θ. The last two terms combine to γω₀ sin(2θ + ψ) with tan ψ = γ/ω′. Hence

$$ E = ½kA² e^(−2γt)[1 + (γ/ω₀) sin(2θ + ψ)] $$

(confirmed to machine precision by computation). The ripple has relative size γ/ω₀ = 1/2Q, small for weak damping, so

$$ E ≈ E₀ e^(−2γt) = E₀ e^(−t/τ) ,   τ = 1/2γ = m/b $$

**Relaxation time** τ is therefore the time for the energy to fall to 1/e (Upadhyaya 8.3, Mathur 8.3).

**Step 3: average power loss.** From E ≈ E₀e^(−2γt): ⟨dE/dt⟩ = −2γE = −E/τ. Cross-check by averaging Step 1 over a cycle: ⟨ẋ²⟩ ≈ ½ω₀²A²e^(−2γt), so −b⟨ẋ²⟩ = −(b/m)(½mω₀²A²e^(−2γt)) = −2γ·E ✓ (b/m = 2γ and ½mω₀²A² = ½kA²).

**Step 4: quality factor.** Define Q = 2π × (energy stored)/(energy lost in one period). Exactly, E(t + T′) = E(t) e^(−2γT′) (the bracket of Step 2 repeats after T′), so the energy lost per period is E(1 − e^(−2γT′)) ≈ 2γT′ E when 2γT′ ≪ 1. **In that approximation, valid for high Q,** Q ≈ 2πE/(2γT′E) = π/(γT′) = ω′/2γ ≈ ω₀/2γ. The working definition is therefore

$$ Q = ω₀/2γ = ω₀τ = mω₀/b = √(mk)/b $$

and the energy-ratio form agrees with it only for weak damping. Exact-form values: at Q = 5 the loss per period is 71.7 % and 2π/0.717 = 8.76 (against ω₀/2γ = 5); at Q = 50 it gives 53.2. Q is dimensionless. High Q means weak damping; Q → ∞ as b → 0. In the time τ the energy falls by 1/e while the oscillator completes ω₀τ/2π = Q/2π oscillations, so the phase advances by Q radians (Mathur 8.4). Link to module 6: Λ = γT′ ≈ π/Q.

**Numbers.** (i) Mathur 8.10: a pendulum of period 1 s whose amplitude drops from 10° to 5° in 10 oscillations: γ = ln 2/10 = 0.0693 s⁻¹, Q = π/(γT′) = 45.3, τ = 1/2γ = 7.21 s (the book prints 1/γ = 14.4 s as the relaxation time, which is the amplitude e-fold time; its Q is right). (ii) Upadhyaya example 7: a tuning fork of 300 Hz with Q = 5 × 10⁴: τ = Q/ω₀ = 26.5 s and the energy reaches E₀/10 at t = τ ln 10 = 61.1 s ✓ (the book's value).

### 8. Master: limiting cases, the LCR circuit and the galvanometer

*Objective:* Check the solutions against their limits, map the damped oscillator onto the LCR circuit, and apply the theory to galvanometer damping.

**Limiting cases.**

- **b → 0.** γ → 0, ω′ → ω₀, Λ → 0, Q → ∞, τ → ∞: the solution becomes A cos(ω₀t + φ), the SHM of PH-035, with constants from A = √(x₀² + v₀²/ω₀²).
- **Critical (ω′ → 0).** The rest solution x₀e^(−γt)[cos ω′t + (γ/ω′)sin ω′t] tends to x₀e^(−γt)(1 + γt), which is the critical solution of module 5; the period T′ → ∞ because the oscillation never completes.
- **Strong damping, b ≫ 2√(mk).** The slow root is γ − q = ω₀²/(γ + q) ≈ ω₀²/2γ = k/b, so x ≈ x₀e^(−kt/b): the inertia term mẍ is negligible and the mass creeps back with time constant b/k (for example, a heavy piston in thick oil).

**The LCR circuit (Mathur 8.5, Upadhyaya 8.5).** A capacitor C discharging through an inductance L and resistance R obeys L q̈ + R q̇ + q/C = 0 (Kirchhoff). PH-035 module 8 has the L-C analogy (m ↔ L, k ↔ 1/C, x ↔ q); the only new row is b ↔ R. Hence γ = R/2L, ω₀² = 1/LC, the discharge oscillates only if R² < 4L/C, the critical resistance is R_c = 2√(L/C), the damped frequency is (1/2π)√(1/LC − R²/4L²) and Q = ω₀L/R = (1/R)√(L/C). With R = 0 the oscillation never decays, which is why resistance alone causes the damping.

**The moving-coil galvanometer.** The coil of moment of inertia I turns against a restoring couple Cθ (C = torsion constant of the suspension) and two damping couples: a mechanical one, −b_m θ̇, and an electromagnetic one from induced current, which Mathur says varies inversely with the circuit resistance. The equation Iθ̈ + b_totθ̇ + Cθ = 0 has the same form as the oscillator, with critical damping b_tot = 2√(IC). A **dead-beat** galvanometer is made heavily damped (small I, low resistance) so that the pointer settles at once; a **ballistic** galvanometer is made lightly damped (large I, high resistance), its first throw corrected with the decrement (module 6).

**External critical damping resistance (CDR).** For a coil of resistance R_g in a circuit with external resistance R, the standard expression for the electromagnetic damping coefficient is (NBA)²/(R_g + R) (N turns, B field, A area; standard knowledge, not derived in the extracts and flagged). Setting b_tot = 2√(IC):

$$ R_CDR = (NBA)²/[2√(IC) − b_m] − R_g $$

For negligible mechanical damping, R_CDR = (NBA)²/(2√(IC)) − R_g. A smaller external resistance than R_CDR gives overdamping; a larger one, underdamping.

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Decay constant γ = b/2m (s⁻¹) versus drag coefficient b (kg/s): the papers call the latter γ.
- The oscillator energy e-fold time and the pure-drag velocity e-fold time are both m/b; the amplitude e-fold time is 2m/b = 1/γ.
- Logarithmic decrement per period Λ = γT′ versus per half period Λ/2.
- Underdamped motion is not periodic, only the zeros and the maxima are equally spaced.
- Critical damping: fastest non-oscillatory decay, not the fastest decay of all possible motions.
- Damped ω′ is lower than ω₀, but the Q-based shift is usually negligible.
