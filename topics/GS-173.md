### Bridge: Courts and judgments: the six words you need

A **judgment** is the written decision of a court on one dispute. It has two layers. The **ratio decidendi** (usually just 'ratio') is the legal principle that was necessary to decide the case; this is the part that binds later courts. **Obiter dicta** ('things said in passing', singular *obiter dictum*) are remarks that were not necessary for the decision; they are persuasive but do not bind. UPSC questions about 'what the Court held' almost always mean the ratio.

A **bench** is the group of judges who hear the case. A larger bench outranks a smaller one: a decision of five judges can be overruled only by a larger bench; a bench of equal strength that doubts it refers the question to a larger bench. Cases that raise a substantial question about the Constitution go to a **Constitution Bench** of at least five judges (Article 145(3), standard knowledge; verify). Judges may agree (unanimous) or split; a **majority** judgment decides the case, and a **minority** or **dissent** is a view that lost but may be adopted later.

**Precedent** means that earlier decisions guide later ones. NCERT notes that decisions of the Supreme Court bind all other courts in India, while the Supreme Court itself is not bound by its own decisions and can reconsider them. To **overrule** an earlier case is to declare it wrongly decided; Golaknath (1967) overruled Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh, and Kesavananda (1973) overruled Golaknath. A **writ** is a formal court order, such as *habeas corpus*, issued under Article 32 or 226 to enforce rights.

### Bridge: How to read a case name, a year and a bench

A case name such as **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)** has three parts. The first name is the **petitioner**, the person who came to court. The 'v.' (or 'vs', read 'versus') separates the second name, the **respondent**, who defends. The number in brackets is the **year of the judgment**, not the year of the dispute. In writs against the government the respondent is usually 'Union of India' or a State, so many names end the same way; it is the first name that you remember.

Other forms appear. **In re** (or 'Re') means 'in the matter of' and is used when no opponent exists, for example a **Presidential Reference** under Article 143 such as *In re Berubari Union (1960)*. Popular nicknames are common in books: 'Fundamental Rights Case' for Kesavananda, 'Mandal Case' for Indra Sawhney, 'Election Case' for Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (Laxmikanth gives these nicknames). The Judges Cases are numbered First to Fourth; Laxmikanth calls the 1993 case the Second Judges case, the 1998 reference the Third and the 2015 NJAC case the Fourth.

A tip for memory: attach **one year, one number and one noun** to every case, for example 'Kesavananda, 1973, thirteen judges, basic structure'. Two traps recur: confusing the year of the Amendment with the year of the case (the 24th Amendment is 1971; the case upholding it is 1973) and confusing two cases with similar names (Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain 1975 versus Indra Sawhney 1992 - similar-sounding names, different issues).

### Bridge: Seven constitutional terms this lesson keeps using

**Fundamental Rights** are the enforceable rights in Part III (Articles 12-35), for example equality (Article 14), freedoms (Article 19) and life and personal liberty (Article 21). **Directive Principles** (Part IV) are goals the State should pursue; they are not enforceable in court (see GS-165). **Article 13** says a law that violates a Fundamental Right is void; the cases in this lesson turn on whether the word 'law' in Article 13 covers a constitutional amendment.

**Article 368** is the provision under which Parliament amends the Constitution; its procedure is explained in GS-172. The central question of the whole topic is how much power Article 368 gives. The **Ninth Schedule** is a list attached to the Constitution; Article 31B (added by the First Amendment, 1951) says laws placed in it cannot be challenged for violating Fundamental Rights, so governments used it to protect land reform laws.

**Ultra vires** is Latin for 'beyond the powers': a law is ultra vires when the body that made it had no power to make it, or when it breaks the Constitution. **Judicial review** is the court power to declare such laws void (module 7). **Article 32** lets a person go straight to the Supreme Court to enforce a Fundamental Right; **Article 226** gives the High Courts a wider writ power. Finally, the **basic structure** is a set of core features that Parliament may not alter by amendment; the Constitution never uses the phrase, and the Court itself developed it.

## Why cases matter

The Constitution is a written text, but the Supreme Court decides what its words mean. NCERT (chapter 9) calls the Constitution a **living document** because it responds to experience through amendments, interpretation and political practice; many of the biggest changes came not from Parliament but from judgments. A **landmark judgment** is a decision that changed the law, set a lasting principle or settled a long conflict. This topic is about the dozen or so decisions UPSC treats as the pillars of the subject.

### 2. The conflict begins: Champakam, Shankari Prasad, Sajjan Singh, Golaknath

*Objective:* Explain, in order, how Parliament and the Supreme Court first clashed over amending Fundamental Rights, and the case-by-case turns until 1967.

## The trigger: land reform versus the right to property

NCERT explains that soon after the Constitution began working, Parliament wanted to restrict the **right to hold property** so that land reform could proceed. The Court held that Parliament could not thus restrict Fundamental Rights; Parliament then amended the Constitution; the Court later said even an amendment could not abridge a Fundamental Right. Four questions ran through the whole fight: the scope of the right to property; Parliament's power to curtail Fundamental Rights; Parliament's power to amend the Constitution; and whether laws that implement Directive Principles may abridge Fundamental Rights.

## Step 1: Champakam Dorairajan (1951) [verify details]

A State order reserving college seats by community was struck down as violating the equality-type Fundamental Rights. The Court said that if a Directive Principle conflicts with a Fundamental Right, the Right prevails and Directives must run as subsidiary to it (also in GS-165). Parliament answered with the **First Amendment (1951)**, which, among other things, allowed special provisions for backward classes (Article 15(4)) and added Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule (module 4).

## Step 2: Shankari Prasad (1951)

Laxmikanth: the validity of the First Amendment, which curtailed the right to property, was challenged. The Court held that Article 368 includes the power to amend Fundamental Rights, because the word **'law' in Article 13 means only ordinary laws, not constitutional amendment acts** ('constituent laws'). So an amendment that abridges a right is not void under Article 13. Parliament therefore had a free hand.

## Step 3: Sajjan Singh (1965) [verify]

The Seventeenth Amendment (1964) put more State land laws into the Ninth Schedule. By a divided Court, the earlier Shankari Prasad ruling was reaffirmed, but two judges doubted whether Fundamental Rights could be amended so easily. This dissenting doubt is the seed of the later doctrine. Treat the exact vote and judges as [verify].

## Step 4: Golaknath (1967)

Laxmikanth: the validity of the Seventeenth Amendment was challenged again, and this time the Court **reversed its stand**. It held that Fundamental Rights have a 'transcendental and immutable' position, so Parliament cannot abridge or take away any of them; a **constitutional amendment is a 'law' under Article 13** and is void if it violates a Fundamental Right. The bench was large (eleven judges, narrow majority) [verify]. The Court also used prospective overruling so earlier amendments were not undone [verify].

## Parliament's reply (1971)

Laxmikanth: the **24th Amendment (1971)** amended Articles 13 and 368 and declared that Parliament can abridge or take away any Fundamental Right under Article 368 and that such an act is not a 'law' under Article 13. The 25th Amendment inserted Article 31C (GS-165). The stage was set for Kesavananda.

> Memory line: **Shankari - yes it can; Sajjan - still yes (a doubt appears); Golaknath - no it cannot; 24th - Parliament says yes again.**

### 3. Kesavananda Bharati and the basic structure doctrine

*Objective:* State what Kesavananda held, how Parliament and the Court answered each other until Waman Rao, and what the doctrine does and does not do.

## The case and its holding

Laxmikanth: in **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)**, the Supreme Court overruled Golaknath. It upheld the 24th Amendment and stated that Parliament can abridge or take away any Fundamental Right, but it laid down the new doctrine of the **basic structure**: the constituent power under Article 368 does not let Parliament alter the basic structure of the Constitution. So Parliament cannot abridge a Fundamental Right that forms part of that structure. The date, **24 April 1973**, is the cut-off used later in Waman Rao and Coelho. The bench had thirteen judges and the majority was 7 to 6 [verify]. The petitioner was the head of a Kerala religious mutt challenging State land reform laws [verify].

## What NCERT says the ruling did

NCERT (chapter 9) lists three effects: it **sets a limit** on Parliament's power (no amendment may violate the basic structure); it **still lets Parliament amend any part** of the Constitution within that limit; and it makes the **judiciary the final authority** on whether an amendment violates the basic structure and what the structure is. NCERT also says the Court held the right to property was not part of the basic structure. The doctrine appears nowhere in the text; it came from the **spirit rather than the letter** of the Constitution, and NCERT calls it a judicial amendment without a formal amendment.

## The ping-pong after 1973

1. **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975).** The 39th Amendment (1975) put election disputes involving the Prime Minister and Speaker outside all courts. The Court invalidated that provision as beyond the amending power because it affected the basic structure; it listed sovereign democratic republic, equality, secularism, rule of law, judicial review and free and fair elections (Laxmikanth Table 11.1). Bench of five [verify].
2. **42nd Amendment (1976).** Parliament amended Article 368 to declare that there is no limit on its constituent power and that no amendment can be questioned in any court on any ground, including contravention of Fundamental Rights.
3. **Minerva Mills (1980).** The Court invalidated that provision because it excluded judicial review, a basic feature. Laxmikanth quotes the key principle: a limited power cannot be enlarged by its holder into an unlimited one. Minerva also held that harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is basic (GS-165). Bench of five, 4 to 1 [verify].
4. **Waman Rao (1981).** The Court adhered to the doctrine and clarified that it applies to constitutional amendments **enacted after 24 April 1973**.

## What basic structure does not mean

It is not a fixed list: Laxmikanth says the Court is yet to define it, and NCERT warns that claiming the Court has given a clear list is wrong. Laxmikanth lists about twenty elements that have emerged from various judgments (module 4). Nor is it a bar on amendment: Parliament can still amend any part, even Fundamental Rights, unless the change damages a basic feature.

> Mains line (2019 PYQ): **Parliament's amending power is limited; a limited power cannot be turned into an absolute one (Minerva Mills).**

### 4. The Ninth Schedule, Coelho and the elements of the basic structure

*Objective:* Explain how Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule were tamed by I.R. Coelho (2007) and list the main elements of the basic structure with their cases.

## Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule

Laxmikanth: Article 31B saves the acts and regulations in the Ninth Schedule from challenge for violating Fundamental Rights. Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule were added by the **First Amendment (1951)**. Originally it held 13 acts; Laxmikanth reports 282 in 2016 (a dated count; verify). State laws in it mainly concern land reform and abolition of zamindari. Table 27.1 splits the additions into those before and after 24 April 1973: the First (13), Fourth (7), the amendment printed as 'Seventh (1964)' (44; probably the Seventeenth, verify) and Twenty-ninth (2) before; the 34th, 39th, 40th, 47th, 66th, 76th and 78th after.

## I.R. Coelho (2007)

Laxmikanth: the Court ruled that there can be **no blanket immunity** from judicial review for Ninth Schedule laws. Judicial review is a basic feature and cannot be removed by putting a law into the Schedule. Laws placed there **after 24 April 1973** are open to challenge if they violate Fundamental Rights under Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 or the basic structure. Key points from the conclusions:

- The test is **effect, not form**: the 'direct impact' or **rights test** asks what the law does to rights. A law that abrogates a right may or may not damage the basic structure; if it does, it is invalid whether it came by amendment of Part III or by insertion into the Ninth Schedule.
- Each new amendment is judged on its own merits.
- Protection is 'not blanket'; the Court examines the nature and extent of the infraction under Article 21 read with Articles 14 and 19 (the 'triangle', module 5).
- If a law was **already upheld** by the Court, it cannot be challenged again on these principles; and actions finalised under the Acts stay safe.
- The bench was nine judges [verify].

## Elements of the basic structure

Laxmikanth Table 11.1 shows that different cases added different elements. The twenty elements include: supremacy of the Constitution; sovereign, democratic, republican nature; secularism; separation of powers; federalism; unity and integrity; welfare state; judicial review; freedom and dignity of the individual; parliamentary system; rule of law; harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directives; equality; free and fair elections; independence of the judiciary; limited amending power; effective access to justice; essence of the fundamental rights; powers of the Supreme Court under Articles 32, 136, 141, 142; and of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227.

> Distinction to hold: **Ninth Schedule laws are not beyond judicial review. Laws placed there after 24 April 1973 can be tested on rights and the basic structure; the earlier ones are not reopened on these principles.**

### 5. Article 21 - procedure established by law to due process: Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi

*Objective:* Contrast 'procedure established by law' with 'due process of law', and explain how Gopalan (1950) and Maneka Gandhi (1978) changed Article 21.

## Two phrases, two models

Article 21 says no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty **except according to procedure established by law**. Laxmikanth (judicial review chapter) contrasts this with the American **due process of law** clause. Under due process, a court can strike down a law on **procedural and substantive** grounds, including that it is unreasonable. Under procedure established by law, the Indian Court was expected to check only whether the law was within the authority's powers, not whether it was reasonable, suitable or wise. That is why Laxmikanth says the scope of judicial review in India is narrower than in the USA. Indian practice is a **synthesis** of the American judicial supremacy and the British parliamentary supremacy.

## A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras (1950) [verify]

A communist leader was held under a preventive detention law. The Court (Chief Justice Kania) read 'procedure established by law' literally: any procedure enacted by a valid law was enough, and it did not examine fairness. Each Fundamental Right was treated as a separate compartment, so the Article 19 freedoms did not test a detention law [verify]. Laxmikanth cites a Kania passage from Gopalan on judicial review of legislation, so the case is also a judicial-review reference.

## Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978) [verify]

The Government impounded a passport without giving reasons. Seven judges held that Articles 14, 19 and 21 are **interlinked**; a procedure that deprives liberty must be **fair, just and reasonable** and not arbitrary. Thus the 'procedure' must pass a test of reasonableness under Article 14 and the content of the law must satisfy Article 19. Laxmikanth's Coelho conclusions speak of the 'triangle' of Article 21 read with Articles 14 and 19 (commentators call it the golden triangle). In practice, the Indian position moved close to due process without changing the words of Article 21.

| Feature | Gopalan (1950) | Maneka Gandhi (1978) |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning of 'procedure' | Any procedure in a valid law | Procedure must be fair, just, reasonable |
| Relation of Articles 14, 19, 21 | Separate compartments | Interlinked |
| Effect on Article 21 | Narrow | Gateway to many rights (module 6) |

## Rule of law and the Emergency

**Rule of law** means government by laws, not by men; Laxmikanth lists it as a basic feature. The sharpest test was the Emergency: in **ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976)** a majority held that, while Article 21 was suspended, detenus could not seek habeas corpus; Justice H.R. Khanna's lone dissent became famous [verify]. The 44th Amendment (1978) made Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable during an Emergency [verify]. The 2017 privacy judgment is reported to have declared the ADM Jabalpur majority wrong [verify].

> Prelims trap: **Maneka did not amend or replace the words 'procedure established by law'; it changed how the Court reads them.**

### 6. How Article 21 grew: dignity, livelihood, environment, death and privacy

## The privacy judgment (2017)

Before 2017 two older decisions, M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1962), were read as saying that privacy is not a Fundamental Right. In **K.S. It is not absolute: any restriction must satisfy legality, a legitimate aim and proportionality [verify]. It also supports the reading that ADM Jabalpur (1976) was wrongly decided [verify]. ## Death with dignity

PYQ 2026 Q12 asks about judicial legislation, passive euthanasia and living wills. Passive euthanasia means withdrawing or withholding life-sustaining treatment; a living will is a written advance directive. ### 7. Judicial review, judicial activism and PIL

*Objective:* Define judicial review, activism and PIL precisely, explain how they relate, and state the safeguards that stop PIL from becoming misuse.

## Judicial review

Laxmikanth: judicial review is the power of the judiciary to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders of the Centre and the States and to declare those found ultra vires void. It began in the USA with **Marbury v Madison (1803)**; in India the Constitution itself confers the power on the Supreme Court and High Courts, and the Court has declared it a **basic feature**, so even an amendment cannot remove it (this is why Minerva Mills struck down the 42nd Amendment clause). Justice Quadri's three categories: review of constitutional amendments; of legislation and subordinate legislation; of administrative action. The Court used it in Golaknath, the Bank Nationalisation case (1970), the Privy Purses case (1971), Kesavananda and Minerva Mills, and in 2015 it struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act.

**Why it is needed:** supremacy of the Constitution, federal balance, protection of Fundamental Rights. **Basis:** Article 13, 32, 131-136, 143, 226, 227, 245-246, 251, 254 and 372 (Laxmikanth). **Grounds:** a law may be challenged if it infringes Fundamental Rights, is outside the competence of the authority, or is repugnant to constitutional provisions.

## Activism and PIL

**Judicial activism** is the proactive role of the judiciary in protecting rights and pushing the other organs to do their duty; it is the opposite of **judicial restraint**. Laxmikanth says PIL is its most popular form. **Public Interest Litigation** departs from the traditional rule of **locus standi** (only the injured person may sue): any public-spirited citizen or organisation with 'sufficient interest' can move the Court for others who are unable to do so through poverty or disadvantage. NCERT dates the change to around 1979 (Hussainara Khatoon) and 1980 (Sunil Batra, where a prisoner's note was converted into a petition). Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer pioneered it.

Laxmikanth's **features**: a strategic arm of legal aid; non-adversarial and co-operative; the Court is creative and assertive; no determination of individual rights; the purposes are the rule of law, access to justice for the weak and realisation of Fundamental Rights. **Categories ordinarily entertained** include bonded labour, neglected children, labour law violations, prison conditions, police inaction and custodial deaths, atrocities on women and Scheduled Castes and Tribes, and environmental pollution; **not entertained** include landlord-tenant, service and pension matters, and admission to colleges.

## The overreach critique and the safeguards

NCERT and Laxmikanth both record the critique: PIL can overburden courts and blur the line with the executive and legislature. Laxmikanth warns against 'publicity', 'politics', 'private' and 'paisa' interest litigation. The Court's guidelines: verify the petitioner's credentials and the content, ensure substantial public interest and urgency, discourage petitions with oblique motives, and impose exemplary costs on busybodies. It also laid down principles that it will ordinarily **not transgress into policy**.

> Mains hinge (PYQ 2020): **Judicial legislation is acceptable only to fill a vacuum, with guidelines that last until Parliament acts, and within the limits of Article 14 and 21 - not as a replacement for the legislature.** Link: the Constitution rests on limited separation of powers and checks and balances (NCERT; PYQ 2019).

### 8. Institutions and federalism: Berubari, Bommai, the Judges cases, Delhi

*Objective:* State the main holding and significance of Berubari, Bommai, the First to Fourth Judges cases and the Delhi governance cases.

## Berubari (1960) [verify]

A Presidential Reference under Article 143: ceding Indian territory needs a constitutional amendment under Article 368, and the Court's remark that the Preamble is not part of the Constitution was corrected in Kesavananda (1973) [verify]. Full detail: see Master notes: 'Extended case detail - institutions and federalism'.

## S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994)

Laxmikanth (ch.11) tags the case with federalism, secularism, democracy, unity and integrity, social justice and judicial review as basic features. Its best-known effect is on **Article 356** (President's rule): the Court held that a proclamation is subject to judicial review and that the power must be used only for a genuine failure of constitutional machinery [verify]. Laxmikanth's Sarkaria discussion says the guidelines in Bommai should be incorporated by suitable amendments and that Articles 352 and 356 are measures of last resort. The 2023 PYQ on the reduced use of Article 356 since the mid-1990s combines this legal check with political factors (coalition governments, strong regional parties) [verify]. Bench of nine [verify].

## The Judges cases (appointments)

Laxmikanth, ch.34: in the **Second Judges case (1993)** the Court ruled that no High Court appointment may be made unless it conforms to the opinion of the Chief Justice of India; in the **Third Judges case (1998)**, a Presidential Reference, the CJI must consult a collegium; in 2015 (**Fourth Judges case**) it held the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act 2014 unconstitutional because they would affect judicial independence, and the collegium system revived. First Judges case (S.P. Gupta, 1981) gave primacy to the executive; Second reversed it [verify]. Collegium strength is explained in GS-186 (the CJI plus four senior-most judges for the Supreme Court). PYQ 2017 asks you to evaluate the NJAC judgment.

## Delhi (2018) [verify]

A five-judge bench (July 2018) held that the elected Delhi government decides matters within its legislative competence and the Lieutenant Governor is bound by aid and advice except in reserved matters; control of services went to a 2023 Constitution Bench and then a Parliamentary Act (see GS-200; verify the latest position). Full detail: see Master notes: 'Extended case detail - institutions and federalism'.

## Two doctrines named in PYQ 2019

**Harmonious construction** reads conflicting provisions together so that both operate; **federal supremacy** means that where a Union law and State law truly conflict on a subject in the Concurrent List, the Union law prevails (Articles 251 and 254, in Laxmikanth's list). Under Article 246 the Union List prevails over the State List where their entries overlap; Article 254 governs repugnancy between Union and State laws within the Concurrent List (standard knowledge; verify). Both emerged from disputes over legislative powers.

### 9. Equality and social justice: Indra Sawhney, Vishaka, Shayara Bano, Navtej Johar

*Objective:* Summarise the holding and significance of the key reservation, gender and liberty cases and identify where critics see judicial legislation.

## Reservation: Indra Sawhney (1992) and after

Laxmikanth names Indra Sawhney as the 'Mandal Case' (rule of law) and Indra Sawhney II (2000) and M. Nagaraj (2006) under equality. NCERT records two ideas that came from the Court: reservations must not exceed **fifty per cent** of the seats, and the **creamy layer** among other backward classes is not entitled to benefit. Further holdings are standard knowledge and need checking: the 27 per cent OBC reservation in central jobs was upheld; reservation was confined to initial appointments, not promotions (later overcome by the 77th Amendment); and a separate 10 per cent quota on economic grounds alone was struck down in 1992. Verify all of this. Nagaraj upheld later amendments on promotion subject to quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequate representation and efficiency [verify]. Article 335 requires that claims of SC and ST be considered consistent with maintenance of **efficiency of administration**; it does not itself define efficiency (PYQ 2023 Prelims). The 103rd Amendment (EWS quota) and the 2022 verdict are post-source topics: verify.

## Vishaka (1997): the Court fills a vacuum [verify]

Sexual harassment at workplaces was not covered by a specific law. The Court framed guidelines (the Vishaka Guidelines), deriving them from Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 and international conventions such as CEDAW, to apply until Parliament legislated. Parliament passed the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act in 2013. Vishaka is the textbook example of **judicial legislation** (PYQs 2020 and 2026).

## Shayara Bano (2017) [verify]

A five-judge bench by 3 to 2 set aside instant triple talaq; cite it in Mains as advancing gender justice through Articles 14 and 15 while showing a divided Court. Full detail: see Master notes: 'Extended case detail - equality and gender'.

## Navtej Singh Johar (2018): constitutional morality [verify]

A five-judge bench read down Section 377 of the IPC for consensual same-sex conduct between adults, overruling Suresh Kumar Koushal (2013), and relied on dignity, equality, privacy and **constitutional morality** - the values of the Constitution, not popular or religious morality, guide the Court (PYQ 2021 Q1). Full detail: see Master notes: 'Extended case detail - equality and gender'.

## Gender justice in a Mains frame (PYQ 2023 Q12)

Combine Articles 14, 15 (including 15(3)), 16, 21 and 51A(e) with Vishaka (workplace), Shayara Bano (personal law), Joseph Shine (adultery, 2018) and Sabarimala (2018) [verify]; the 73rd/74th and women's reservation amendments are covered in GS-172.

### 10. Examination application: how UPSC tests landmark judgments

*Objective:* Decode Prelims traps and Mains directives for landmark judgments, and use the case chain to eliminate options and structure a 15-mark answer.

## Prelims patterns

Four shapes recur. (1) **Ninth Schedule/basic structure statements**: does the statement say the Court cannot examine a Ninth Schedule law at all? After Coelho that is the trap (PYQ 2018 Q5). (2) **Amendment-and-judgment statements**: which amendment introduced which clause, and which Court decision struck which (PYQ 2019 Q45 combines the 44th Amendment and the 99th Amendment). (3) **Right-source questions**: under which Article is privacy protected (PYQs 2018 Q92 and 2024 Q76). (4) **Article-interpretation assertion-reason**: reservation versus efficiency (PYQ 2023 Q40).

How to read PYQ 2019 Q45 as analysis, not as an official key: statement 1 attributes an Article that places a Prime Minister's election beyond review to the 44th Amendment, but Laxmikanth's text attributes the removal of PM election disputes from courts to the 39th Amendment (1975), which the Court struck in Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain; the 44th Amendment is associated with undoing that period's changes (NCERT). Statement 2 matches Laxmikanth on the 2015 verdict. Reasoning from the sources suggests statement 1 is the suspect one.

## Mains directives

- **Discuss / Examine** (15 marks): give context, the case chain, both sides, conclusion.
- **Critically examine** (NJAC 2017): state the judgment, the reasoning, then strengths and weaknesses of the collegium and the Court's role.
- **Explain / Illustrate** (basic structure, constitutional morality, living instrument): define, give three to four cases as evidence, one balanced conclusion.

## A 15-mark skeleton: judicial review versus parliamentary power

1. **Introduction**: limited government - the Constitution is supreme; Parliament's amending power is limited (Minerva).
2. **Phase one**: Shankari Prasad/Sajjan Singh (Parliament free), Golaknath (limit), 24th Amendment (reply).
3. **Phase two**: Kesavananda (basic structure), Indira v Raj Narain, 42nd Amendment, Minerva Mills, Waman Rao.
4. **Phase three**: Coelho (Ninth Schedule), NJAC (2015).
5. **Balance**: legitimacy of review; countervailing view that unelected judges decide; NCERT's rigid-yet-flexible point.
6. **Conclusion**: checks and balances, not supremacy of either organ.

## Prelims arithmetic and elimination

Prelims GS I carries 2 marks per question; a wrong answer costs one-third of the marks, that is 2/3 = 0.67 marks. A blind guess among four options has expected score 0.25 x 2 minus 0.75 x 0.67 = 0.5 - 0.5 = 0. If you can eliminate two options, a guess between two has expected score 0.5 x 2 - 0.5 x 0.67 = 1 - 0.33 = 0.67, which is positive. Use the case chain to eliminate options that reverse the direction of a ruling (for example 'Shankari Prasad held amendments are laws under Article 13').

### Notes: The amending-power storyline as cause and effect

- Land reform laws (zamindari abolition) are challenged for violating the right to property -> Parliament passes the First Amendment (1951), adding Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule so listed laws cannot be challenged under Part III.
- Challenge to the First Amendment -> Shankari Prasad (1951): amendments are not 'law' under Article 13, so Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights.
- More laws are added through the 4th (1955) and 17th (1964) Amendments -> Sajjan Singh (1965) follows Shankari Prasad but two judges doubt it.
- Challenge to the 17th Amendment -> Golaknath (1967): Fundamental Rights cannot be abridged by amendment.
- Parliament replies with the 24th and 25th Amendments (1971) -> Kesavananda (1973): Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights, but not the basic structure.
- Emergency: 39th Amendment removes courts from the PM's election dispute -> Indira v Raj Narain (1975): clause invalid.
- 42nd Amendment (1976): unlimited constituent power and no judicial challenge -> Minerva Mills (1980) invalidates it; Waman Rao (1981) fixes the date of application.
- Many Ninth Schedule laws added after 1973 -> Coelho (2007): they can be reviewed against Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 and the basic structure.
- Net effect: Parliament has wide amending power; the Court is the final judge of its limits (NCERT: rigid and flexible together).

### Notes: Frequently confused concepts

- Basic structure is not a fixed list; Parliament still has wide amending power (NCERT).
- Judicial review is not review under Article 137 (the Court re-examining its own judgment); it is also not a power to question the wisdom of a law.
- Judicial activism is not the same as judicial review, but the former is a form of the latter (Laxmikanth); PIL is the most popular form of activism.
- Article 31B/Ninth Schedule: protection exists only against Part III challenges and, after Coelho, not for laws placed after 24 April 1973.
- Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) is not Indra Sawhney (1992).
- The year of the Amendment is not the year of the case (24th Amendment 1971; Kesavananda 1973).
- The 'Second Judges case' (1993) is not the NJAC case (Fourth, 2015).

### Notes: Extended case detail - institutions and federalism

- ## Berubari (1960) [verify]

In 1960 the President made a Reference under Article 143 on whether Parliament could cede part of Berubari Union to Pakistan by ordinary law. The Court advised that ceding territory needs a **constitutional amendment under Article 368**, and, in the same opinion, said the **Preamble is not a part of the Constitution**. The latter view was corrected in Kesavananda (1973), which treated the Preamble as part of the Constitution [verify]. The Ninth Amendment (1960) followed [verify].
- ## Delhi (2018) [verify]

A five-judge bench in July 2018 held that the elected Delhi government, not the Lieutenant Governor, decides in matters within its legislative competence; the LG is bound by the aid and advice of ministers except in specific reserved matters (public order, police and land) and cannot act as an independent obstacle. The later fight over control of services reached a Constitution Bench in 2023 and a Parliamentary Act followed; see GS-200 and verify the latest position.

### Notes: Extended case detail - equality and gender

- ## Shayara Bano (2017) [verify]

A five-judge bench by 3 to 2 set aside instant triple talaq. The majority gave different reasons (arbitrariness under Article 14; not protected under the Shariat Act) and the minority held the practice protected by Article 25 but asked Parliament to legislate. A 2019 Act followed [verify]. A safe way to cite it in Mains: it advanced gender justice through Articles 14 and 15, while showing a divided Court.
- ## Navtej Singh Johar (2018): constitutional morality [verify]

PYQ 2021 Q1 asks for the doctrine of constitutional morality; Navtej is the central case, with Sabarimala (2018) and others [verify].

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** What is the difference between ratio decidendi and obiter dicta?
    - **a:** Ratio is the principle necessary to decide the case and binds later courts; obiter is a remark made in passing and only persuades.
  - - **q:** Which Article lets a person go directly to the Supreme Court for a Fundamental Right?
    - **a:** Article 32.
  - - **q:** Who is the petitioner in 'Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala'?
    - **a:** Kesavananda Bharati.
  - - **q:** What does 'ultra vires' mean?
    - **a:** Beyond the powers; a law made outside the authority's competence or against the Constitution.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why did the Court in Kesavananda not simply restore Shankari Prasad?
    - **a:** It wanted to keep Parliament's power to amend (including Rights) but also prevent amendment from destroying the Constitution's identity, so it created a middle path of 'amend but not alter the basic structure'.
  - - **q:** Why is judicial review itself called a basic feature?
    - **a:** Because without it, Fundamental Rights and the limits on Parliament would have no remedy; Minerva Mills struck down the clause barring review for this reason.
  - - **q:** How did Maneka Gandhi change Article 21 without changing its words?
    - **a:** By reading Articles 14, 19 and 21 together, requiring the procedure itself to be fair, just and reasonable.
  - - **q:** Why did PIL need the relaxation of locus standi?
    - **a:** Many affected persons are too poor or uninformed to approach the court, so public-spirited persons must be allowed to sue on their behalf.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A new law puts a controversial tax statute in the Ninth Schedule in 2012 and says no court may question it. What is the likely legal position?
    - **a:** Under Coelho it is open to challenge because it was placed after 24 April 1973; the court can test it against Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 and the basic structure, and a blanket bar on courts would itself offend judicial review.
  - - **q:** A government passes an amendment saying Parliament's power to amend is absolute. Which case answers this?
    - **a:** Minerva Mills (1980): Parliament cannot enlarge its limited power into an absolute one.
  - - **q:** A person sues on behalf of workers in another city who are being denied minimum wages. Is this a PIL? Why?
    - **a:** Yes; non-payment of minimum wages is in the categories Laxmikanth lists as ordinarily entertained, and locus standi is relaxed for disadvantaged persons.

### Last-week summary

- 1951 First Amendment (Art 31B, Ninth Schedule); Shankari Prasad: amendment not 'law' under Art 13; Golaknath 1967: it is, Fundamental Rights cannot be abridged.
- 24th Amendment 1971 reasserts power; Kesavananda 24 April 1973: amend anything but not the basic structure, Golaknath overruled; no closed list of basic features.
- Raj Narain 1975 voids the 39th Amendment PM-election clause; Minerva Mills 1980 voids 42nd Amendment bar on review; Waman Rao 1981 and Coelho 2007 use the 24 April 1973 cut-off.
- Art 21: procedure established by law (competence and stated procedure) versus US due process (reasonableness); PIL relaxes locus standi; NJAC and 99th Amendment struck down 2015.
