## PH-052: Multiple-beam interference

Physics Paper I · Physics · Wave optics: interference

### Bridge: Bridge: the geometric series and the rotating arrow e^(i delta)

A **geometric series** is a list in which each term is a fixed multiple of the one before: 1, q, q², q³, and so on. If the multiplier q has size less than 1 (for example q = 0.9), the terms shrink and the infinite sum is finite:

$$ 1 + q + q² + q³ + ... = 1 / (1 − q) ,   |q| < 1 $$

Test it: for q = 0.5, 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + ... = 2 = 1/(1 − 0.5). The multiplier may also be a **complex number**. The symbol e^(−iδ) is a 'rotating arrow' (phasor) of length 1 that has turned clockwise through the angle δ (in radians; 2π radians is a full turn). It obeys e^(−iδ) = cos δ − i sin δ, so the arrow's length is 1 for every δ. The number R e^(−iδ) is the same arrow shrunk to length R. Because its size R is less than 1, the geometric series with q = R e^(−iδ) converges to 1/(1 − R e^(−iδ)).

The **intensity** of a wave is the square of the size (modulus) of its amplitude. For any complex number z, |z|² = z times its complex conjugate z*, where the conjugate flips the sign of i. For q = R e^(−iδ): |1 − q|² = (1 − R e^(−iδ))(1 − R e^(+iδ)) = 1 − 2R cos δ + R². Finally, cos δ = 1 − 2 sin²(δ/2), which turns 1 − 2R cos δ + R² into (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2). These three steps are all the mathematics the derivation in module 3 needs.

### Bridge: Bridge: amplitude coefficients, intensity coefficients and the Stokes relations

At a partly reflecting surface a wave of amplitude a splits into a reflected wave of amplitude a·r and a transmitted wave of amplitude a·t. The numbers r and t are the **amplitude coefficients** (reflection and transmission). Intensity is the square of amplitude, so the **intensity coefficients** are R = r² (the fraction of intensity reflected) and T is the fraction of intensity transmitted, T = (n₂/n₁)t² at normal incidence for a crossing from index n₁ to n₂ (the index factor drops out when the same medium lies on both sides, as for a coated plate). If the surface absorbs nothing, R + T = 1.

A beam that has already entered the plate meets the same surface from the other side. Let r′ and t′ be the coefficients for travel in that reverse direction. Reversing the direction of all motion in a lossless system must give back a valid motion; applying this 'time reversal' to the incident, reflected and transmitted waves (the Stokes argument, derived in the Day 17 lesson PH-050, module 8) gives two relations:

$$ r′ = −r ,   t t′ = 1 − r² = 1 − R $$

The first says the reflections from the two sides have opposite sign (consistent with the π jump on reflection from the denser side; it says the two sides differ by π, not which side carries it). The second says that going in and coming out through the same lossless surface passes the fraction 1 − R of the intensity, T = 1 − R. For a single dielectric crossing t² alone is not T; the product t t′ = 1 − R is what the derivation needs. We shall use r′² = R (positive, real) for the bounce inside the plate, and t t′ = T = 1 − R for entering and leaving. Do not confuse these two products with the amplitude r itself: amplitude r = √R, for glass in air about 0.206 (R = 0.0426 for n = 1.52, PH-050).

## What is asked

Three official Physics Optional Paper I questions are tagged to this topic (all verified against the printed papers):

- **2025 Q4(b), 20 marks:** consider multiple reflections from a plane parallel film of thickness h and index n₂ and derive an expression for the total reflectivity.
- **2026 Q4(a)(ii), 10 marks:** a Fabry-Perot interferometer with λ = 500 nm and mirror separation d = 0.8 mm; find 'the smallest wavelength difference Δλ that can be resolved', defined through two successive transmitted maxima at λ and λ + Δλ (module 9 explains the wording), and explain why it depends on the separation.

All three use the same engine: add the infinite family of beams that bounce between two partly reflecting surfaces. The scope split is firm. This lesson owns the **theory of the fringes** (the Airy distribution, finesse, sharpness). The **Fabry-Perot instrument** (construction, free spectral range, resolving power, uses) belongs to the Day 20 lesson PH-053; it appears here only as a pointer.

## Words to know before we start

- **Two-beam interference:** only two coherent waves are added (Young's slits, a thin film with weak reflectance, Michelson).
- **Multiple-beam interference:** many coherent waves, each a repeat bounce of the one before, are added.
- **Reflectance R:** fraction of the intensity reflected at one surface. **Transmittance T:** fraction transmitted. Lossless means T = 1 − R.
- **Phase difference δ:** the extra phase a beam acquires after one round trip inside the gap; here δ = 4π n h cos θ / λ₀.
- **Order m:** the integer m for which δ = 2π m (a bright transmitted fringe).
- **Coefficient of finesse F = 4R/(1 − R)²** (Brij Lal Eq. 15.72). **Half-width:** the width of a fringe at half its peak height. **Finesse:** the ratio of the spacing of fringes to their half-width (module 5).

Module 6 handles the reflected pattern and the energy check. Modules 7 and 8 compare, then add real-mirror effects. Module 9 writes the three answers. The Day 17 lesson PH-050 (module 8) already derived the *reflected* total reflectivity; this lesson uses that result as a cross-check and spends its words on the transmitted pattern, sharpness and finesse, which are new.

### 2. Foundation: why two beams cannot give sharp fringes

*Objective:* Show from the two-beam intensity law that the fringe profile is a fixed broad cosine and that two close wavelengths can never be seen as two separate lines.

## The two-beam profile

Two coherent waves of intensities I₁ and I₂ with phase difference δ give (Brij Lal Eq. 14.22, PDF p. 336; PH-038)

$$ I = I₁ + I₂ + 2 √(I₁ I₂) cos δ $$

For equal beams I₁ = I₂ = I₀ this is I = 2I₀(1 + cos δ) = 4I₀ cos²(δ/2) (Eq. 14.22 with I₁ = I₂; Eq. 14.23a gives the maximum 4I₀). The **maximum** is 4I₀ at δ = 0, 2π, 4π, ... and the **minimum** is 0 at δ = π, 3π, ... Normalise by dividing by 4I₀ and the profile is cos²(δ/2): a smooth hump, repeating every 2π.

## How wide is a hump?

Half the peak height means cos²(δ/2) = ½, so δ/2 = π/4 on either side of the peak, and the full width at half maximum is FWHM = π. The period is 2π. So the ratio 'spacing divided by width' is 2π/π = 2. This ratio is the **finesse** of the pattern (defined in module 5): for two-beam fringes it is exactly 2 and no choice of mirrors, lamp or lens can change it. The bright fringe is as wide as the dark gap. That is what 'cosine fringes' means.

## Two wavelengths merge

Suppose a source has two close wavelengths whose fringes are shifted against one another by the phase s. With equal intensities the combined normalised pattern is

$$ cos²(δ/2) + cos²((δ − s)/2) = 1 + cos(s/2) · cos(δ − s/2) $$

(use cos²x = (1 + cos 2x)/2 and add the two cosines with the sum-to-product rule). The result is again a **single cosine**, now with the reduced contrast cos(s/2) and shifted by s/2. For every shift s less than π there is only one hump; at s = π the contrast is zero and the fringes wash out. Two separate maxima never appear. This is exactly the visibility cycle for the sodium D lines in the Day 18 lesson PH-051 (module 6): a Michelson can find Δλ from the *disappearance* of fringes, but cannot display the two lines side by side.

## What would a better pattern look like?

To see two lines you need each line to give a narrow peak with a wide dark gap, so that peaks of different wavelengths sit side by side without overlapping. The shape cos² cannot do that. We need a profile whose width is much less than its period, i.e. a finesse much greater than 2. The only way to build such a profile out of waves is to add **many** waves that agree only in a narrow range of δ. A familiar example: a diffraction grating has N slits and its peaks are roughly N/2 times narrower than two-slit fringes (width falls as 1/N). Multiple reflections give a series of beams in the same way, with one difference: the beams are not equal. Each bounce loses intensity, so the effective number of beams depends on the reflectance R (module 3).

A price must be paid: strong reflecting surfaces send most of the light back and pass little, so the bright rings are narrow and the energy per ring is small. The next modules show how much sharpness each unit of R buys.

### 3. Core: summing the beams - the transmitted amplitude and the Airy intensity

*Objective:* Derive the transmitted intensity I_T / I₀ = 1 / (1 + F sin²(δ/2)) for a lossless plane-parallel plate by summing the infinite family of transmitted beams, stating the conventions.

## Set-up and assumptions

Two parallel partly reflecting surfaces (a glass plate in air, or two mirrored plates enclosing an air gap) are separated by a distance h, the gap having index n. A plane monochromatic wave of amplitude a falls at angle θ (θ inside the gap). Assume: the surfaces are exactly parallel and lossless (T = 1 − R); the outside medium is the same on both sides; every beam keeps overlapping the others (a lens in front of the screen brings all parallel beams to one point); the reflections are the real-coefficient case r′ = −r (dielectric plate), so r′² = R is a positive real number. Use the time factor e^(iωt) (the Brij Lal convention); a **delay** of δ in phase multiplies a beam by e^(−iδ). Coordinates: angles from the normal; light travels left to right and downward.

## The successive beams

Beam 1 passes straight through both surfaces: amplitude a t t′ = a T. Beam 2 passes the first surface, is reflected at the second surface (r′), reflected back at the first (r′), then transmitted out: a t · r′ · r′ · t′ e^(−iδ) = a T R e^(−iδ), because t t′ = T and r′² = R. Each further double bounce multiplies by R e^(−iδ). The extra optical path of beam 2 over beam 1 is 2n h cos θ (the transmitted-light result of PH-050, module 4, with no half-wave term because both bounces are inside), so

$$ δ = (2π/λ₀) · 2 n h cos θ = 4π n h cos θ / λ₀ $$

The beams are therefore a T, a T R e^(−iδ), a T R² e^(−2iδ), ... (the same list as Brij Lal on PDF p. 387, written in his T and R, with intensities falling as R², R⁴, ... ).

## The sum

$$ A = a T [1 + R e^(−iδ) + R² e^(−2iδ) + ...] = a T / (1 − R e^(−iδ)) $$

because |R e^(−iδ)| = R < 1 (bridge B1). After N beams the leftover is the factor (1 − R^N e^(−iNδ)); for R = 0.9 the 44th beam still has amplitude 0.9⁴³ ≈ 1.1 percent of the first beam, so about 44 beams exceed a 1 percent cut-off, though about 1/(1 − R) = 10 carry most of the sum (module 7); for glass (R = 0.0426) only the first two or three matter.

## The intensity

I_T = A A* = a² T² / (1 − R e^(−iδ))(1 − R e^(+iδ)) = a² T² / (1 − 2R cos δ + R²). With cos δ = 1 − 2 sin²(δ/2) this becomes

$$ I_T = a² T² / [ (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2) ]   (Brij Lal Eq. 15.67) $$

With T = 1 − R the numerator over (1 − R)² is 1, so, dividing by the incident intensity I₀ = a²,

$$ I_T / I₀ = 1 / [1 + F sin²(δ/2)] ,   F = 4R/(1 − R)²   (Eqs. 15.72, 15.73) $$

This is the **Airy distribution**, and F is the **coefficient of finesse**.

## Checks

- **Dimensions:** δ is an angle in radians, R and F are pure numbers, so I_T / I₀ is dimensionless as it should be.
- **Peak:** at δ = 2π m, sin(δ/2) = 0 and I_T = I₀. All the light is transmitted at a resonance, even for R = 0.99. The beams are individually tiny (T = 0.01) but are added in phase: their phasors lie head to tail along one line and the total length is T/(1 − R) = 1.
- **Numbers:** R = 0.9: F = 360. Off resonance at δ = π: I_T/I₀ = 1/361 = 0.00277.
- **A series check:** summing 20000 beams numerically gave |A|²/a² = 0.110626 at R = 0.9, δ = 0.3, equal to the closed form to 15 digits.

### 4. Core: the Airy function - maxima, minima, contrast and the rings

*Objective:* Read off the maxima, minima, visibility and ring geometry from I_T/I₀ = 1/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)) and see how R reshapes the pattern.

## Maxima and minima

The denominator is smallest when sin²(δ/2) = 0, i.e. δ = 2π m with m = 0, 1, 2, ... Using δ = 4π n h cos θ / λ₀ this is the bright-ring condition

$$ 2 n h cos θ = m λ₀   (m = integer, the order) $$

and there I_max = I₀ (Brij Lal Eq. 15.68 with T = 1 − R). The minima come at sin²(δ/2) = 1, i.e. δ = (2m + 1)π, with

$$ I_min = I₀ / (1 + F) = I₀ [(1 − R)/(1 + R)]²   (Eq. 15.69) $$

because 1 + F = [(1 − R)² + 4R]/(1 − R)² = (1 + R)²/(1 − R)². So the positions of maxima and minima are *the same* for all R; only the depth of the minima and the width of the maxima change.

## Visibility

With V = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) (Eq. 14.40) and I_min/I_max = 1/(1 + F),

$$ V = F / (F + 2) = 2R / (1 + R²)   (Eq. 15.75) $$

Check: R = 0.5 gives V = 1/1.25 = 0.80, the value Brij Lal quotes. V rises from 0 (R = 0) towards 1 (R = 1); it depends on R alone, not on h or λ.

| R | F | I_min / I_max | V | what the pattern looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0426 (glass, n = 1.52) | 0.186 | 0.843 | 0.085 | faint cosine ripple on a bright field |
| 0.5 | 8 | 0.111 | 0.800 | clear fringes, still broad |
| 0.8 | 80 | 0.0123 | 0.976 | narrow bright rings |
| 0.9 | 360 | 0.00277 | 0.9945 | sharp rings, nearly black gap |
| 0.95 | 1520 | 0.00066 | 0.9987 | very sharp rings |
| 0.99 | 39600 | 0.000025 | 0.99995 | knife-edge rings |

The glass row agrees with the thin-film figure V = 0.085 of PH-050 (module 4, transmitted fringes).

## The rings (equal inclination)

With a broad source and a lens, each direction θ gives one value of δ, so the pattern is concentric circles about the normal, the same kind as the Michelson circles of PH-051. Take an air gap (n = 1) and suppose the centre is exactly on a maximum of order m₀ = 2h/λ₀ (an integer). Ring p lies at 2h cos θ = (m₀ − p)λ₀; with cos θ ≈ 1 − θ²/2 this gives hθ² = pλ₀, so

$$ θ_p = √(p λ₀ / h) $$

For h = 0.8 mm and λ₀ = 500 nm: m₀ = 3200, θ₁ = 0.0250 rad (1.43°), θ₂ = 0.0354 rad (2.03°), θ₃ = 0.0433 rad (2.48°). The ring *positions* are those of any equal-inclination pattern; what multiple beams change is the ring *width*.

## Reading the figure

The text graph shows the cos² curve (two beams, 'o') and the R = 0.9 Airy curve ('*') over one period centred on a maximum. The Airy peak is the same height but only about one-thirtieth of a period wide at half maximum (1/finesse = 1/29.8; 12° out of 360°, module 5).

### 5. Core: sharpness - the half-width and the finesse

*Objective:* Derive the exact and approximate half-width of a transmitted fringe, define the (reflecting) finesse ℱ = π√R/(1 − R), and compare with the two-beam value 2.

## Why a measure of width

The Airy function has the same height I₀ for all R, so 'sharper' means *narrower*. Brij Lal (PDF p. 389) measures the sharpness by the **half-width**: the width of the I–δ curve where the intensity has fallen to one half of its maximum. Use δ = 2πm + ε, so ε is the offset from the centre of a maximum. Then sin²(δ/2) = sin²(ε/2) (the shift by πm leaves sin² unchanged).

## Derivation

Set I_T/I_max = 1/2:

$$ 1 / [1 + F sin²(ε/2)] = 1/2  ⟹  F sin²(ε/2) = 1  ⟹  sin(ε/2) = 1/√F = (1 − R) / (2√R) $$

This is the book's Eq. 15.76 (offset of the half-maximum point from the peak; the extracted text prints it in broken form, rebuilt here). The half-maximum points are at ε = ±2 arcsin(1/√F), so the **full width at half maximum** is

$$ Δδ_{1/2} = 4 arcsin(1/√F) ≈ 4/√F = 2(1 − R)/√R   (F ≫ 1) $$

The approximation uses arcsin x ≈ x for small x, valid when 1/√F is small, i.e. R above about 0.6. A half-maximum exists only if F ≥ 1, i.e. R ≥ 3 − 2√2 = 0.172; below that the intensity never falls to one half of its maximum (glass in air, R = 0.0426, has I_min/I_max = 0.84).

## The finesse

The peaks repeat every 2π, so the **finesse** is the ratio of the spacing to the width,

$$ ℱ = 2π / Δδ_{1/2} ≈ π√F / 2 = π√R / (1 − R) $$

This ℱ is the *reflecting finesse*: the finesse set by the reflectance alone (it is distinct from the coefficient of finesse F; confusing them is a common trap). For two beams the same ratio is 2π/π = 2 (module 2).

| R | F | FWHM exact (rad) | FWHM 4/√F (rad) | ℱ exact | ℱ = π√R/(1 − R) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 8 | 1.445 | 1.414 | 4.35 | 4.44 |
| 0.8 | 80 | 0.448 | 0.447 | 14.02 | 14.05 |
| 0.9 | 360 | 0.2109 | 0.2108 | 29.79 | 29.80 |
| 0.95 | 1520 | 0.1026 | 0.1026 | 61.23 | 61.24 |
| 0.99 | 39600 | 0.0201 | 0.0201 | 312.6 | 312.6 |

The approximation is within 2 percent at R = 0.5 and better than 0.1 percent for R ≥ 0.9.

## Interpretation and limits

- Width scales as (1 − R): the fraction of an order occupied by a ring is 1/ℱ. To get ℱ = 30 one needs R ≈ 0.9006; for ℱ = 100, R ≈ 0.969.
- Physical reason: with R = 0.9 about 1/(1 − R) = 10 round trips carry most of the sum (about 44 are above 1 percent in amplitude); a beam that has made k round trips is out of step with beam 1 by kδ, and with that many beams even a small δ off resonance brings some of them into antiphase with others, so the sum collapses.
- R → 0: F → 0 and the pattern fades to the weak two-beam cosine. As R falls to 3 − 2√2 = 0.172 (F = 1) the exact FWHM grows to a whole period (2π) and the exact finesse to 1; below that no half-maximum exists and the finesse is undefined (π√R/(1 − R) is not valid there). R → 1: width → 0, ℱ → ∞ (ideal limit; absorption and flatness prevent it, module 8).
- A real instrument has a finesse smaller than π√R/(1 − R) because of surface roughness and non-parallelism (stated qualitatively; the quantitative budget belongs to the Day 20 lesson PH-053).

### 6. Core: the reflected pattern, energy conservation and the limiting cases

*Objective:* Derive the reflected intensity, prove I_T + I_R = I₀, show that the average over a period equals the incoherent sum, and state the R → 0 and R → 1 limits.

## The reflected sum (a cross-check of PH-050)

The Day 17 lesson PH-050 (module 8) summed the reflected beams of a plate in air and found the amplitude ratio E_R/a = (r − r e^(−iδ)) / (1 − R e^(−iδ)) when the two outside media are alike (r₁ = r, r₂ = −r). Use that result here. Its modulus squared:

$$ I_R / I₀ = R |1 − e^(−iδ)|² / |1 − R e^(−iδ)|² = 4R sin²(δ/2) / [ (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2) ] $$

because |1 − e^(−iδ)|² = 2 − 2cos δ = 4 sin²(δ/2). Dividing top and bottom by (1 − R)²:

$$ I_R / I₀ = F sin²(δ/2) / [ 1 + F sin²(δ/2) ]   (Eq. 15.74) $$

## Energy conservation (the T + R = 1 check)

The transmitted intensity is I_T/I₀ = (1 − R)² / [ (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2) ] (module 3, using T = 1 − R). Add the two over the common denominator:

$$ (I_T + I_R)/I₀ = [ (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2) ] / [ (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2) ] = 1 $$

For every δ, with no absorption, what is not transmitted is reflected: I_R = I₀ − I_T. The two patterns are **complementary**: the reflected pattern is bright wherever the transmitted pattern is dark, and the reflected pattern has narrow *dark lines* on a bright background, at δ = 2πm, where I_R = 0 and I_T = I₀. Its brightest value is F/(1 + F) = 4R/(1 + R)² (0.157 for glass, matching PH-050). Brij Lal's Eqs. 15.71 and 15.74 give the same result in his notation (rebuilt from the broken text layer).

## The mean over a period

If the beams were *incoherent* their intensities would simply add: Σ T² R^(2k) = T²/(1 − R²) = (1 − R)/(1 + R). The coherent pattern oscillates, but its average over one period of δ is the same. Reason: |A|² = a² T² |Σ (R e^(−iδ))^k|² contains cross terms proportional to cos((j − k)δ) with j ≠ k, whose average over δ from 0 to 2π is zero. Numerical check: R = 0.9 gives mean 0.0526 = 0.1/1.9; R = 0.5 gives 1/3; glass gives 0.9183. This explains why a very reflective gap passes so little light on average yet gives a peak of full height: the energy is concentrated into narrow rings.

## Limiting cases

- **R → 0:** F ≈ 4R, I_T/I₀ ≈ 1 − 4R sin²(δ/2) = 1 − 2R(1 − cos δ) and I_R/I₀ ≈ 2R(1 − cos δ): a two-beam cosine of tiny contrast (the PH-050 limit).
- **R → 1:** F → ∞; I_T/I₀ → 0 for every δ except in a vanishing range around δ = 2πm, where it stays 1. The mirror becomes opaque away from resonance.
- **Units:** all ratios are dimensionless; the phase δ is in radians.
- **Intracavity build-up (standard result, verify):** inside the gap the field at resonance is t a / (1 − R), so the intensity in the gap is T/(1 − R)² times the incident one: 10 times for R = 0.9. This is why the output equals the input despite T = 0.1.

### 7. Master: multiple-beam versus two-beam interference, and the advantages

*Objective:* Define multiple-beam interference and compare it point by point with two-beam interference, as the 2017 Q3(a) answer needs.

## Definition

**Multiple-beam interference** is the interference of many coherent beams obtained by division of amplitude, as when light bounces repeatedly between two partly reflecting parallel surfaces and a small fraction escapes at each bounce. Brij Lal (PDF p. 386) notes that it must be considered when the reflectance of the surfaces is not small, so that the higher-order reflections cannot be ignored. The beams are not equal: the k-th has amplitude a T R^k, and the family sums to the Airy distribution (module 3).

## Comparison

| Feature | Two-beam (thin film at small R, Michelson, Young) | Multiple-beam (R large) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of beams that matter | 2 | effectively about 1/(1 − R) = 10 for R = 0.9 (amplitudes stay above 1% for about 44 beams) |
| Intensity law | I = 2I₀(1 + cos δ) = 4I₀cos²(δ/2) | I_T/I₀ = 1/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)) |
| Fringe profile | smooth cosine; bright and dark equally wide | narrow bright lines, wide dark gaps |
| Finesse | 2 (fixed) | π√R/(1 − R); 30 for R = 0.9 |
| Visibility | up to 1 only for equal beams | 2R/(1 + R²), 0.9945 at R = 0.9 |
| Two close wavelengths | merge into one cosine (module 2) | separate rings once their spacing exceeds the width |
| Fringe-position setting | a fraction of a broad fringe | a fraction of a very narrow fringe (finer by about finesse/2, i.e. about 15 for R = 0.9, since two-beam fringes already have finesse 2) |
| Brightness of the maxima | 4 times one beam's intensity (equal beams), twice the average | equal to the incident intensity for lossless mirrors, many times the average |
| Average intensity over a period | equals the sum of the two beam intensities | (1 − R)/(1 + R) of the incident, small when R is large |

## Advantages of multiple-beam interferometry

The sharpness gives four gains that a 2017 answer should list, each with its reason:

1. **Sharp fringes:** the half-width falls like (1 − R) (module 5), so the position of a fringe can be set and read more precisely than a cosine maximum.
2. **Ability to separate close lines:** two wavelengths show as two rings (not as a washing-out of contrast) once their ring positions differ by more than the width. The criterion and the free spectral range are the content of the Day 20 lesson PH-053.
3. **High contrast:** the minima are almost dark (I_min/I_max = ((1 − R)/(1 + R))²) and V approaches 1; for a bare glass plate (R = 0.0426) the transmitted fringes have V = 0.085 and are barely visible.
4. **Light is concentrated into thin rings,** and a surface with R close to 1 can still pass the whole intensity at resonance, so a bright narrow ring is seen on a dark background.

## What multiple-beam interferometry costs

- High-quality reflecting coatings, very flat surfaces and a very good parallelism; the finesse falls if they are not so (stated qualitatively).
- A low *average* transmitted light (1 − R)/(1 + R); the energy sits in the rings.
- Lines of different orders overlap if the wavelength range is wide (module 8 and PH-053).

## Common wrong turn

It is wrong to say that a higher R makes the peak brighter. For lossless mirrors the peak is I₀ for all R; R changes the *width* (and the average). It is also wrong to say 'more beams, therefore more energy': no more energy is created; energy is redistributed from the gaps to the rings.

### 8. Master: real mirrors - reflection phase, absorption, unequal reflectances

*Objective:* Modify the Airy derivation for a phase change on reflection, absorption in the mirrors and two different mirrors, and state what changes (peak height, positions) and what does not.

## Phase change on reflection (Brij Lal review Question 20)

Review question 20 on PDF p. 400 (Punjab 2005) asks for the effect of the phase change on reflection in a Fabry-Perot. For a dielectric plate in air the two inside reflections both have the coefficient r′ = −r, so their product r′² = R is positive real and there is no net phase (module 3). A metal-coated mirror may reflect with a complex coefficient ρ = √R e^(iφ). Both internal reflections of a round trip then give ρ² = R e^(2iφ), and the ratio of successive transmitted beams is R e^(2iφ) e^(−iδ) = R e^(−i(δ − 2φ)). Everything in modules 3 to 6 holds with δ replaced by δ − 2φ:

$$ I_T / I₀ = 1 / [1 + F sin²((δ − 2φ)/2)] ,   maxima at 2 n h cos θ = (m + φ/π) λ₀ $$

So φ does **not** change F, the width or the finesse. It only shifts the ring order by φ/π, slightly altering every ring angle. If the order is used to infer the gap h, the shift must be allowed for; φ may also depend on wavelength, which matters when comparing close lines (standard result, verify).

## Absorption in the mirrors

A real metal film also absorbs a fraction A, so R + T + A = 1. The first transmitted beam is still a t t′ = a T, the ratio is still R e^(−iδ), so

$$ I_T / I₀ = T² / [ (1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2) ] ,   peak = [T/(1 − R)]² = [1 − A/(1 − R)]² $$

The *shape* is the same Airy shape (F depends on R only); the *height* drops. Example: R = 0.9, A = 0.05, T = 0.05 gives a peak of 0.25 (25 percent). With R = 0.92, A = 0.03, T = 0.05: 0.39. The higher the R, the more a small A costs, because the beam makes about 1/(1 − R) = 10 passes (R = 0.9). This is why high-reflectance dielectric stacks (PH-050 module 9) replace metals when high finesse is needed. In Brij Lal's Eq. 15.68 the factor a²T²/(1 − R)² is this peak; it equals a² only for T = 1 − R.

## Unequal mirrors

With reflectances R₁ and R₂ and transmittances T₁, T₂, the ratio of successive beams is ρ₁ρ₂ e^(−iδ) = √(R₁R₂) e^(−iδ). Write R̄ = √(R₁R₂). The same series gives

$$ I_T / I₀ = T₁T₂ / [ (1 − R̄)² + 4R̄ sin²(δ/2) ] ,   peak = T₁T₂ / (1 − R̄)² $$

For lossless mirrors with R₁ = 0.9 and R₂ = 0.8 (T₁ = 0.1, T₂ = 0.2): R̄ = 0.8485, F′ = 4R̄/(1 − R̄)² = 148 and the peak is 0.02/0.0229 = 0.872. Only matched mirrors reach 100 percent. Check: R₁ = R₂ recovers module 3.

## Oblique and finite beams

At oblique incidence the coefficients depend on polarisation (a later optics topic), and in a real instrument the beam walks sideways at each bounce so only a finite number of beams overlap; both effects lower the contrast and are noted but not derived here. The instrument consequences (aperture, tilt, finite finesse) belong to the Day 20 lesson PH-053.

### Notes: Symbols and definitions (use these consistently)

- R = r² is the intensity reflectance of one surface; T is its transmittance; for lossless surfaces T = 1 − R. With absorption A, R + T + A = 1.
- r, t are amplitude coefficients going into the gap; r′ = −r and t t′ = 1 − R by the Stokes relations (bridge B2; derived in PH-050 module 8).
- δ = 4π n h cos θ / λ₀ is the phase lag between successive transmitted beams (round-trip phase). The bright-ring condition is δ = 2πm, i.e. 2 n h cos θ = mλ₀, m the order.
- F = 4R/(1 − R)² is the coefficient of finesse (Brij Lal Eq. 15.72). It is not the finesse.
- ℱ = (spacing of maxima)/(full width at half maximum) = 2π/Δδ_{1/2} ≈ π√R/(1 − R) is the (reflecting) finesse. Two-beam fringes have ℱ = 2.
- Airy distribution: I_T/I₀ = 1/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)); reflected I_R/I₀ = F sin²(δ/2)/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)). 'Airy function' here (Sir George Airy's plate formula) is a different object from the Airy disc of Fraunhofer diffraction (Day 17 lesson PH-057).
- Visibility V = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) (Brij Lal Eq. 14.40); for the Airy pattern V = F/(F + 2) = 2R/(1 + R²).
- Half-width: width of the I–δ curve at half the peak height (Brij Lal p. 389). Here FWHM = full width at half maximum, i.e. twice the offset from the peak to one half-maximum point.

### Notes: Equations to memorise (with the module in which each is derived)

- δ = 4π n h cos θ / λ₀ (module 3).
- Beam k (k = 0, 1, 2, ...): amplitude a T R^k e^(−ikδ); sum A = a T/(1 − R e^(−iδ)) (module 3).
- I_T = a² T²/[(1 − R)² + 4R sin²(δ/2)] = I₀ /[1 + F sin²(δ/2)] when T = 1 − R (module 3).
- I_max = I₀ at δ = 2πm; I_min = I₀/(1 + F) = I₀[(1 − R)/(1 + R)]² at δ = (2m + 1)π (module 4).
- V = F/(F + 2) = 2R/(1 + R²) (module 4).
- sin(ε/2) = 1/√F = (1 − R)/(2√R) at the half-maximum points; FWHM = 4 arcsin(1/√F) ≈ 2(1 − R)/√R (module 5).
- ℱ = 2π/FWHM ≈ π√R/(1 − R) (module 5).
- I_R/I₀ = F sin²(δ/2)/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)); I_T + I_R = I₀; period average of I_T/I₀ = (1 − R)/(1 + R) = incoherent sum (module 6).
- Absorbing mirrors: peak = [T/(1 − R)]² = [1 − A/(1 − R)]² (module 8). Unequal mirrors: replace R by R̄ = √(R₁R₂), numerator T₁T₂ (module 8). Reflection phase φ: replace δ by δ − 2φ (module 8).

### Notes: Confused concepts

- F (coefficient of finesse, 4R/(1 − R)²) versus ℱ (finesse, about π√R/(1 − R)): for R = 0.9, F = 360 and ℱ = 29.8; they are related by ℱ ≈ π√F/2.
- Reflecting finesse versus the finesse of a real instrument: the former depends only on R; real surfaces (roughness, non-parallelism, finite aperture) reduce it (qualitative; Day 20 lesson PH-053).
- Spacing of orders (free spectral range, about λ²/2d) versus the smallest resolvable difference (about the spacing divided by the finesse): module 9, 2026 stem.
- Airy distribution of a plate (this lesson) versus the Airy disc of a circular aperture (Day 17 lesson PH-057): different things sharing a name.
- Transmitted maxima at δ = 2πm (bright rings) versus reflected maxima at δ = (2m + 1)π: complementary, with I_T + I_R = I₀ for lossless mirrors.

### Core concepts

- Two-beam fringes have a fixed cos² profile (finesse 2); two close wavelengths merge into one cosine.
- A plane-parallel gap with large R gives an infinite geometric series of transmitted beams of ratio R e^(−iδ).
- The Airy law I_T/I₀ = 1/(1 + F sin²(δ/2)) with F = 4R/(1 − R)²; peak 1, minimum 1/(1 + F).
- R sets the sharpness (half-width, finesse), not the peak height (for lossless mirrors).
- Reflected and transmitted patterns are complementary: I_T + I_R = I₀; the period average of I_T/I₀ is (1 − R)/(1 + R).

**Q.** Explain in words why raising R narrows the rings but leaves their height unchanged for lossless mirrors.

**Q.** Why is the transmitted multiple-beam pattern usually preferred to the reflected one for measurement, although both carry the same information?
