## GS-164: Fundamental Rights limitations and case-law basics

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: Five verbs for limiting a right: restrict, suspend, abrogate, abridge and save

Examination questions on Fundamental Rights use precise verbs. Learn them before the lesson.

- A **restriction** limits how a right may be used while the right stays alive. A ban on speech that incites an offence is a restriction on the freedom of speech. Restrictions in Article 19 must be reasonable.
- **Suspension** switches off a right (or the court remedy for it) for a time. The Emergency provisions suspend; when the Emergency ends the right revives.
- **Abrogation** means taking a right away completely. Article 33 lets Parliament restrict **or abrogate** the rights of the armed forces.
- To **abridge** or **curtail** is to cut a right down without destroying it entirely. The Constitution's text uses 'take away or abridge' in Article 13.
- **Saving** a law (Articles 31A to 31C) means shielding it from challenge under certain rights. The right survives for everybody else, but this law cannot be attacked under it.

There is one more word: **void**. A court declares a law void when it is inconsistent with a right. A void law cannot be enforced. The courts, and not the legislature, make this declaration.

A simple picture helps. Think of a right as a gate. A restriction narrows the gate, a suspension locks it for a while, abrogation removes it, and a saving clause builds a side door through which certain laws can pass without being checked at the gate.

### Bridge: Reading a constitutional challenge: petitioner, respondent, ultra vires, void, bench, holding

Every case in this lesson has the same shape, and six words are enough to read it. The **petitioner** is the person (or organisation) who goes to court to complain; the **respondent** is the party complained against, usually the Union or a State. The court is asked whether a law or order is **ultra vires** (Latin: 'beyond the powers') of the authority that made it or of the Constitution. If the court agrees, it declares the law **void**, which means that it has no legal force and cannot be enforced. A **bench** is the group of judges who hear the case; a larger bench (for example a five-judge or thirteen-judge bench) can overrule a smaller one. The **holding** is the rule of law that the judgment actually decides, as distinct from remarks made in passing.

Case names are written 'Petitioner v Respondent', for example *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), and the year tells you which cases came earlier. When a later case reverses an earlier holding, the later case **overrules** the earlier one: Maneka Gandhi overruled Gopalan. GS-173 has a longer bridge on reading a case name, a year and a bench; use it if this one feels too short.

### 1. Orientation: why rights have limits, and a map of seven kinds of limit

*Objective:* Explain why Fundamental Rights are qualified and sort the limits on them into seven kinds that the later modules take one by one.

## Rights are strong, but not unlimited

A **Fundamental Right** (FR) is a claim against the State that the Constitution itself guarantees (GS-163 module 1). A beginner often assumes that a guaranteed right is a right without limit. The sources say the opposite: Laxmikanth lists as a feature that FRs are **not absolute but qualified**: the State may impose **reasonable restrictions**, and the **courts** decide whether a restriction is reasonable, striking a balance between individual liberty and social control.

## Why a free society still needs limits

NCERT's chapter on freedom explains why: nobody in a society can enjoy a total absence of constraint, so the real question is which constraints are **justified**. It borrows John Stuart Mill's **harm principle**: power may be used against a person only to prevent serious harm to others; minor harm calls for social disapproval, not the force of law. In India justified constraints are called **reasonable restrictions**: capable of being defended by reason, not excessive and not out of proportion to the action restricted.

## Seven kinds of limit: your map for this lesson

Learn the limits as seven kinds; each later module takes one or two.

| Kind of limit | Where it sits | Modules |
|---|---|---|

| 2. Exceptions and scope limits inside an Article | Articles 14, 15, 16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 29, 30 | 4 |
| 3. Saving clauses that shield certain laws | Articles 31A, 31B, 31C | 6 |

### 2. Reasonable restrictions on Article 19: the grounds, freedom by freedom

*Objective:* State the grounds on which each of the six freedoms in Article 19 may be restricted and explain why the lists differ.

## The rule before the list

Article 19 gives **citizens** six freedoms (GS-163 module 5). Laxmikanth states three rules for every restriction on them: (1) only **reasonable** restrictions; (2) **only on the grounds mentioned in Article 19 itself**; (3) the **courts** decide whether a restriction is reasonable. The freedoms belong to citizens (and to shareholders of a company) but not to foreigners or to companies as such. Traditionally they are protected against State action (Laxmikanth). [Standard knowledge, verify: *Kaushal Kishor v State of UP* (2023), a five-judge bench deciding 4:1, is reported to hold that Articles 19 and 21 can in some cases be enforced against non-State actors; see GS-163 module 9.]

Each freedom has its own clause of restrictions: Article 19(2) for speech, 19(3) assembly, 19(4) association, 19(5) movement and residence, 19(6) profession. [Laxmikanth's extract gives the grounds but not the clause numbers; standard knowledge: verify against the official text.]

## The grounds, freedom by freedom

| Freedom | Clause | Grounds on which reasonable restrictions may be imposed |
|---|---|---|
| Speech and expression | 19(2) | sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence |
| Assembly (peaceably, without arms) | 19(3) | sovereignty and integrity of India; public order (Laxmikanth adds: including maintenance of traffic in the area) |
| Association, unions, co-operative societies | 19(4) | sovereignty and integrity of India; public order; morality |
| Movement | 19(5) | interests of the general public; protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe |
| Residence and settlement | 19(5) | the same two grounds |
| Profession, occupation, trade, business | 19(6) | interests of the general public; the State may prescribe professional or technical qualifications; the State may itself carry on a trade or business, even to the exclusion of citizens |

## Reading the table

Speech has eight grounds; assembly and movement two each; association three. Notice what is **missing**: 'morality' is a ground for speech and association but **not** assembly; 'public order' for speech, assembly and association but **not** movement or profession; the Scheduled Tribe ground **only** for movement and residence. Questions swap grounds between freedoms (module 10).

## Why the lists differ

Each ground fits the freedom: a procession can block traffic and disturb public order; outsiders entering tribal areas can harm Scheduled Tribe culture and property (Laxmikanth). The profession clause differs in kind: the State may prescribe qualifications and may run a business itself, even as a monopoly, without justifying it.

Three details carry marks: speech and assembly include demonstration and picketing but **not a right to strike**, which industrial law may regulate; movement has two dimensions, internal (Article 19) and external (Article 21), and overlaps with residence (the idea behind PYQ 2022 Mains Q2, module 10); **recognition** of an association is not a Fundamental Right.

[Dated flag: Laxmikanth's examples (persons affected by AIDS; Section 144 CrPC; Section 141 IPC) are dated: the CrPC and IPC were replaced in 2024. Check current law before using them in Mains.]

### 3. What makes a restriction 'reasonable': tests, proportionality and two landmark lessons

*Objective:* Apply the two-step check (listed ground, then reasonableness) and the proportionality test to a challenged restriction.

## Two questions a court asks

A court asks two questions in order. **First: is the ground listed?** Laxmikanth says the grounds in Article 19 are the only permitted ones, so a restriction justified on any other ground (for example, 'the Government finds the speech inconvenient') fails at once. **Second: is it reasonable?** NCERT's working definition: capable of being defended by reason, not excessive and not out of proportion to the action restricted. NCERT's example: people running a hate campaign may face limits on movement or on holding public meetings, but not life imprisonment.

## What courts weigh: the older test

Standard knowledge (verify): in *State of Madras v V.G. Row* (1952) the Court weighed the nature of the right, the purpose and urgency of the evil, whether the restriction goes beyond what is needed and the conditions of the time; reasonableness covers both **substance** and **procedure**. Laxmikanth names the case only in his notes on the supremacy of the Constitution.

## The modern tool: proportionality

**Proportionality** asks whether the means fit the aim. In the usual formulation of Indian courts (standard knowledge; verify): (1) the restriction is **backed by law** (legality); (2) it serves a **legitimate aim**; (3) the means are **suitable** (a rational link); (4) it is **necessary**: no less intrusive, equally effective measure exists; (5) there is a fair **balance** between benefit and harm to the right. GS-173 module 6 records that *Puttaswamy* (2017) requires legality, a legitimate aim and proportionality for any restriction on privacy (flagged there). *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020, internet shutdowns) is widely cited as applying proportionality to Article 19 [verify].

## Two landmarks that show how the grounds work

- *Romesh Thappar* (1950) and *Brij Bhushan* (1950): laws restricting the press on grounds like public safety were struck down because the then text of Article 19(2) did not list that ground. The **First Amendment (1951)** added **public order**, **friendly relations with foreign States**, **incitement to an offence** and the word **reasonable**; the **16th Amendment (1963)** added **sovereignty and integrity of India** [standard knowledge; verify]. Lesson: the list of grounds can be widened only by **constitutional amendment**.
- *Shreya Singhal* (2015): Section 66A of the Information Technology Act was struck down because its vague, wide words restricted speech beyond the listed grounds [verify]. Lesson: a law can fail even when its aim sounds respectable.

## Public order is narrower than it sounds

Standard knowledge (verify): in *Ram Manohar Lohia v State of Bihar* (1966) the Court described three concentric circles: **law and order** (widest), **public order** (inside it) and **security of the State** (narrowest). Not every disturbance of law and order disturbs public order, so a bare claim of 'public order' does not justify every restriction.

## Guided example

A district order bans all processions for a year. Ground: public order is listed for assembly, so step 1 is met. Reasonable: a year-long ban on every procession regardless of route or risk is probably excessive; a short order for one route during a tense period is a less intrusive alternative. So the outcome turns on proportionality, not on the ground alone.

### 4. Limits built into other Articles: 14, 15, 16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 29 and 30

*Objective:* Identify the limit inside each of these Articles and use two contrasts to avoid the standard exam traps.

## A second kind of limit: the scope of the right itself

| Article | The built-in limit | Laxmikanth page |
|---|---|---|
| 14 | Reasonable classification is allowed; class legislation is not | p.198 |
| 15, 16 | Special provisions for women, children, backward classes, SC/ST and EWS; reservation; residence rule only by Parliament | pp.200-203 |
| 20 | Criminal laws only; bars conviction, not trial; double jeopardy only before courts or tribunals; self-incrimination only in criminal proceedings and not for material objects, thumb impression, specimen signature or blood sample | p.212 |
| 21 | Deprivation allowed by 'procedure established by law', now read as a fair, just and reasonable procedure | p.213 |
| 22 | Ordinary-arrest safeguards are not available to enemy aliens or preventive detainees; preventive detention is allowed with an advisory board after three months | pp.216-217 |
| 23 | State may impose compulsory service for public purposes | p.219 |
| 25 | Subject to public order, morality, health and the other FR provisions; State may regulate secular activity and promote reform | p.221 |
| 26 | Subject to public order, morality and health; the text does not add the other FR provisions (courts have harmonised; contrast 1) | p.222 |
| 29 | Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture may conserve it; no denial of admission to State or State-aided institutions on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language; citizens only, not confined to minorities | p.224 |
| 30 | No reverse discrimination; State may regulate standards in institutions that seek aid or recognition | pp.225-226 |

## Two contrasts worth memorising

1. **Article 25 versus 26**: Article 25 is expressly subject to the other FR provisions; the text of Article 26 is not (Laxmikanth). Courts have nevertheless harmonised them: denominational rights under 26(b) yield to temple-entry laws under 25(2)(b) (*Devaru*, 1958; verify), and the interplay is pending before a larger bench in the Sabarimala reference [verify current status].
2. **Ordinary arrest versus preventive detention**: ordinary arrest gives four rights (grounds, lawyer, magistrate within 24 hours, release after 24 hours unless the magistrate authorises more); preventive detention gives three protections (advisory board beyond three months, grounds, opportunity to represent). The 44th Amendment's cut from three months to two has not been brought into force (Laxmikanth; verify).

## Guided example: judicial custody (PYQ 2021 Prelims Q82)

Article 22 puts a **magistrate** between the arrested person and the police: production within 24 hours, journey time excluded (Laxmikanth prints 'including', a source slip; check the official text). Standard knowledge (verify; the CrPC has been replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, so section numbers changed): in **police custody** the police hold the accused for questioning; in **judicial custody** the accused is in the magistrate's custody, normally in jail, and the police may question there only with the court's permission.

## Who may make preventive-detention laws

Parliament alone for reasons of defence, foreign affairs and the security of India; Parliament and the State legislatures both for the security of a State, public order and essential supplies and services (Laxmikanth).

### 5. Article 13 in practice: void laws, severability, eclipse, waiver and amendments

*Objective:* Explain what happens to a law that violates a right and apply four working doctrines to simple cases.

## Recap, with a link

Article 12 defines 'State'. Article 13 makes any law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right **void** ('law' includes Acts, ordinances, delegated legislation and custom or usage having the force of law). The reading is in **GS-163 module 2**; this module treats Article 13 as the limit on the limiters.

## Four working doctrines (standard knowledge; verify; the Laxmikanth extract does not describe them)

- **Severability.** A law is void only *to the extent* of the inconsistency; the court strikes out the offending part and keeps the rest if it can stand alone. Test: would the legislature have passed the remainder without the struck part? Edge cases: (a) one bad section among many workable ones: the rest survives; (b) the bad section is the heart of the law: the whole law falls; (c) good and bad parts interwoven: the whole law falls.
- **Eclipse.** A pre-1950 law that conflicts with a right is not wiped out; the right 'overshadows' it, and the law can revive if the conflict is removed (*Bhikaji Narain Dhakras*, 1955). A law made after 1950 that violates a right is void from the start (*Deep Chand*, 1959). Refinement [verify]: in *State of Gujarat v Ambica Mills* (1974) such a law is reported to be void only against citizens and to stay operative against non-citizens, who have no Article 19 right; this supports edge case (2) below.
- **Waiver.** A person cannot give up a Fundamental Right by consent, because the rights serve public policy and not only private convenience (*Basheshar Nath*, 1959).
- **Prospective operation of Article 13(1).** Pre-constitution laws became void only from 26 January 1950; acts done before that date remain valid.

## Amendments and Article 13

Article 13 says a constitutional amendment is not a 'law', so an amendment cannot be challenged under Article 13 *as such* (Laxmikanth). Yet in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) the Court held that an amendment is open to challenge if it violates the **basic structure**. The earlier swings (Shankari Prasad, Golaknath) are in **GS-173 module 2** and **GS-167 module 6**; the doctrine is in **GS-168**. Remember the direction of travel: from 'Parliament can amend anything' to 'anything except the basic structure'.

## Rule-testing exercise

Rule: 'A law that violates a Fundamental Right is void.' Test it against three edge cases. (1) A **constitutional amendment** that violates a right: not void under Article 13 itself, but open to attack under the basic structure doctrine. (2) A law that limits the movement of a **foreigner**: Article 19 is not a foreigner's right, so there is no Article 19 violation to void. (3) A law that takes property **after 1978**: no Part III right is violated, because property is now Article 300A, outside Part III; the challenge lies under Article 300A, not Article 13 (module 6). Written accurately: 'A law or order that violates a right in Part III, as it applies to the person complaining, is void to the extent of the violation.'

### 6. Saving clauses: Articles 31A, 31B and 31C, the Ninth Schedule and the right to property

*Objective:* State what each saving clause protects, how far, and how the Supreme Court limited each, and what remains of the right to property.

## Recap, with links

Saving clauses, the Ninth Schedule and the history of the right to property: **GS-163 module 9**; basic structure: **GS-168 module 8**; Coelho also in GS-173 module 4; the land-reform clash: GS-165 module 6. The recap for recall:

- **31A** (1st Amendment, 1951): five categories of law (estates, management of property, amalgamation of corporations, rights of directors and shareholders, mining leases) shielded against Articles 14 and 19; State laws need the President's assent; personal-cultivation land within the ceiling needs market-value compensation.
- **31B** (1951): Ninth Schedule Acts shielded against any FR.
- **31C** (25th Amendment, 1971): laws implementing Article 39(b) or (c) shielded against Articles 14 and 19; *Kesavananda* (1973) struck the part barring the courts; *Minerva Mills* (1980) struck the 42nd Amendment's extension to all Directives.

## New here: Coelho and the later rulings

*I.R. Coelho* (2007): **no blanket immunity**; Ninth Schedule laws placed **after 24 April 1973** can be challenged on Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 or the basic structure. The test looks at the **effect** on rights (the 'rights test' and the 'essence of the right' test, *Nagaraj*, 2006), not the form of placement; laws already upheld are not reopened.

Dated note (verify): in *Property Owners Association v State of Maharashtra* (November 2024) a nine-judge bench is reported to have held that Article 31C survives in its pre-42nd-Amendment form, and that not every privately owned resource is a 'material resource of the community' under Article 39(b) (see GS-165 module 6).

## What remains of the right to property

The 44th Amendment (1978) repealed Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31 and inserted **Article 300A**: no person shall be deprived of his property except by authority of law. Property is a constitutional or legal right, not an FR and not part of the basic structure (Laxmikanth). **Laxmikanth's consequences (2020):** Parliament can regulate it by ordinary law; protection against executive but not, in his account, legislative action; no guaranteed compensation; the remedy lies in the High Court (Article 226), not Article 32. Two compensation guarantees survive in Part III: Article 30(1A) and the personal-cultivation clause in Article 31A.

Dated note (verify): later rulings are reported to read more into Article 300A. *Vidya Devi v State of Himachal Pradesh* (2020) is reported to call property a human right, and in *Kolkata Municipal Corporation v Bimal Kumar Shah* (2024) the Supreme Court is reported to have identified seven procedural sub-rights under Article 300A, including fair compensation; property remains outside Part III.

PYQ 2021 Prelims Q92 (position of the right to property) uses the last two paragraphs: Article 300A speaks of 'person' (module 10).

### 7. Limits by status and situation: Articles 33, 34, 35, martial law and the Emergency

*Objective:* State what Articles 33 to 35 allow, distinguish martial law from a National Emergency, and apply the rules on Articles 358 and 359 to cases.

## Recap, with links

Articles 33 to 35 are taught in **GS-163 module 8** and the Emergency provisions in **GS-170 module 5**. One line each, for the recall prompt:

- **Article 33**: only Parliament may restrict or abrogate FRs of the armed forces, para-military and police forces, intelligence agencies and analogous forces, for discipline; such a law cannot be challenged in court as violating an FR. Examples: the Army Act (1950), the Navy Act (1957) and the Air Force Act (1950) [source slip: Laxmikanth prints 'Navy Act (1950)'; verify], the Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act 1966 and the Border Security Force Act.
- **Article 34**: while martial law is in force in an area, Parliament may restrict FRs there, **indemnify** officials for acts done to maintain or restore order and **validate** sentences and acts; an Act of Indemnity cannot be challenged on FR grounds.
- **Article 35**: only Parliament may make laws giving effect to certain rights (residence rules under Article 16, powers of other courts under Article 32, laws under Articles 33 and 34, punishment for untouchability, forced labour and trafficking).
- **Article 358**: suspends the six freedoms of Article 19 automatically, only when the Emergency is declared on **war or external aggression**, not armed rebellion (44th Amendment).
- **Article 359**: a Presidential Order suspends the **right to move the courts** for the rights named in it; enforcement of **Articles 20 and 21** can never be suspended (44th Amendment). **358 suspends the right; 359 suspends the remedy.** Standard knowledge (verify; GS-170 module 5): since the 44th Amendment a law or executive action is protected under Articles 358(2) and 359(1B) only if it recites that it relates to the Emergency.

## Martial law versus National Emergency: what is new here

**Martial law** is military rule over a particular area in extraordinary circumstances (war, invasion, insurrection, rebellion, riot). It is not defined in the Constitution and not expressly provided for; it is implicit in Article 34. The Supreme Court has held that declaring martial law does not by itself suspend habeas corpus. It differs from **military law**, which governs the armed forces. The five-row table is in Master notes. In short: martial law affects only FRs, suspends the government and ordinary courts in an area, and has no express provision; a National Emergency also changes Centre-State relations, can extend the life of Parliament, continues the government and courts, and is explicit (Article 352).

## Rule-testing exercise

Rule: 'FRs can be suspended in an Emergency except Articles 20 and 21.' Edge cases: (1) an Emergency on the ground of armed rebellion: Article 19 is **not** suspended; (2) a Presidential Order names only Article 14: only the remedy for Article 14 is suspended; (3) martial law in one district: rights elsewhere are untouched and habeas corpus is not automatically suspended. Accurate rule: Article 19 only on war or external aggression; remedy only for the rights named in the Order; enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 never.

### 8. Remedies and their boundaries: Articles 32 and 226, the writs and PIL

*Objective:* State the limits of Article 32 and Article 226, the limits inside each writ, and the role and limits of Public Interest Litigation.

## Why remedies belong in a lesson on limits

A limit is meaningful only if someone can test it. The writ definitions and the limits inside each writ are in **GS-163 module 8**; this module covers the boundaries of the two courts and of PIL.

## Article 32: reach and boundaries

The right to move the Supreme Court is itself a Fundamental Right; Dr Ambedkar called Article 32 the very soul and heart of the Constitution (Laxmikanth), and the Court has held it a **basic feature**, so no amendment can remove it. Its jurisdiction is **original** and **wide** (writs of all kinds) but **not exclusive**: High Courts have concurrent power under Article 226. Boundaries:

- **A violation of an FR is the precondition.** Article 32 cannot be used for ordinary legal rights or simply to test a law or order that infringes no FR.
- **Alternative remedy is no bar,** but where the High Court can give relief, the petitioner should usually go there first.
- **Suspension**: a Presidential Order under Article 359 can suspend the remedy during a National Emergency, never for Articles 20 and 21.

## Supreme Court versus High Court (Laxmikanth)

| Point | Supreme Court (Article 32) | High Court (Article 226) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Only enforcement of FRs | FRs and any other purpose |
| Territory | Throughout India | Within its territory, or where the cause of action arises |
| Nature | The remedy is itself an FR; the Court may not refuse | Discretionary; the High Court may refuse |

## PIL: widening who may use the remedy

Normally only the person whose right is infringed may sue (**locus standi**). **Public Interest Litigation** relaxes it: any public-spirited person or organisation may move the court for people too poor or disadvantaged to do so. PIL came to India in the early 1980s through judicial activism (Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer and P.N. Bhagwati) and rests on Articles 32 and 226 (Laxmikanth). The Court may treat a letter as a petition, relax procedure and appoint commissions to find facts. NCERT's Asiad workers' case (workers paid below the minimum wage; *People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India*, 1982 [verify]) is the standard example.

PIL has **limits**: the Court will not ordinarily enter into policy; a private dispute cannot be agitated as PIL; High Courts should not ordinarily entertain a PIL questioning the validity of a statute; and guidelines against misuse require the petitioner's credentials and the contents to be verified, substantial public interest, and exemplary costs on busybodies. Landlord-tenant, service and pension matters, college admissions and petitions for early hearing are not entertained. [Source slip: Laxmikanth says the guidelines were formulated 'in 1998' and 'modified in 1993 and 2003'; the first date is probably 1988: verify.]

### 9. Master view: the case-law spine in two chains

*Objective:* Place the main cases on two chains (Parliament's power over rights; the State's power over life and liberty) and recite one sentence for each.

## How to learn case law as a beginner

Each case answers one question about limits. Use two chains and a few stand-alone cases. The full accounts are in **GS-173 modules 2-6** and **GS-168 modules 3-4 and 8**; here is one line per case, in order. Holdings from Laxmikanth or NCERT are stated plainly; holdings from GS-173 or standard knowledge carry [verify].

## Chain A: how far can Parliament go in cutting a right?

1. **Shankari Prasad (1951)**: 'law' in Article 13 means ordinary law, so amendments can cut rights [verify; GS-173 module 2].
2. **Golaknath (1967)**: an amendment is a 'law' and cannot abridge FRs [verify; GS-173 module 2].
3. **24th and 25th Amendments (1971)**: power restored; Article 31C inserted (GS-163 module 9).
4. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**: amend even FRs but not the **basic structure**; second part of 31C struck; reference date 24 April 1973; NCERT adds that property is not part of the basic structure (GS-168 modules 3-4).
5. **Raj Narain (1975) and Minerva Mills (1980)**: provisions putting elections or amendments beyond the courts, and 31C's extension to all Directives, struck down; judicial review is basic (GS-168 modules 3-4).
6. **Coelho (2007)**: Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 reviewable (GS-168 module 8; GS-173 module 4).

## Chain B: how far can the State go in taking life and liberty?

1. **Gopalan (1950)**: 'procedure established by law' read narrowly: any procedure in a valid law sufficed (GS-173 module 5).
2. **ADM Jabalpur (1976)**: the Emergency habeas corpus case, flagged for checking in GS-173 module 5; the 44th Amendment's protection of Articles 20 and 21 is the constitutional answer (module 7).
3. **Maneka Gandhi (1978)**: overruled Gopalan; the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable. This imported both limbs of **due process**: a procedural limb (notice, hearing, an unbiased decision-maker: natural justice) and a substantive limb (the law itself must be fair and reasonable) [limbs: standard knowledge; verify].
4. **After Maneka (no single date)**: the Court declared many rights part of Article 21; Laxmikanth lists 35, including dignity, environment, livelihood, privacy, shelter, health, legal aid and speedy trial.
5. **Puttaswamy (2017)**: privacy intrinsic to Article 21 and Part III; any restriction needs legality, a legitimate aim and proportionality [GS-173 module 6; verify].

## Stand-alone cases

- **Indra Sawhney (1992)**: 27 per cent OBC reservation upheld with conditions: creamy layer excluded, no reservation in promotions (later countered by the 77th Amendment), 50 per cent ceiling (Laxmikanth).
- **Malankara Syrian Catholic College (2007)**: principles for minority institutions under Article 30.
- **Balaji Raghavan (1996)**: national awards are not hereditary titles under Article 18.

Across both chains the message is the same: a power to limit rights is itself limited by reasonableness, fairness and the basic structure. NCERT adds that rights are continually reinterpreted, so case law is a moving record, not a closed list.

### 10. Examination application: traps, a worked question, the 2018 Aadhaar facts and Mains frames

*Objective:* Recognise the standard traps on limits of Fundamental Rights, solve one statement question by elimination and outline Mains answers on privacy and the expanding right to life.

## How this topic is asked

The seven verified questions fall into four patterns: scope and limits of a right (privacy 2017, movement and residence 2022, expanding right to life 2023, all Mains); a named doctrine (due process 2023, right to property 2021, Prelims); procedure and custody (2021 Prelims); a judgment applied to a policy (Aadhaar, 2020 Prelims).

## Eight traps to expect

- A ground borrowed from the wrong freedom ('morality' for assembly).
- 'Public order' for movement or profession.
- Suspension of the right (358) versus of the remedy (359).
- 'All FRs suspended in an Emergency': Articles 20 and 21 never; Article 19 only on war or external aggression.
- 'Article 32 for any legal right': FRs only.
- 'Right to property is an FR': Article 300A since 1978.
- 'Ninth Schedule laws are beyond review': not those placed after 24 April 1973.
- 'Martial law is express like Article 352': it is implicit in Article 34.

## A worked question (thinking aloud)

*Consider: 1. Article 359 can suspend the right to move court for enforcing Article 21. 2. ### Notes: Core definitions for limits on Fundamental Rights

- Reasonable restriction: a limit on a right that can be defended by reason, is not excessive and is not out of proportion to the action restricted (NCERT); the courts decide whether it is reasonable (Laxmikanth).
- Qualified right: a right that the State may restrict on stated grounds; the opposite of an absolute right.
- Harm principle (J.S. Mill, NCERT): power may be used against a person only to prevent serious harm to others.
- Proportionality (standard knowledge; verify): a restriction must be backed by law, serve a legitimate aim, be suitable, be necessary (no less intrusive alternative) and strike a balance with the harm to the right.
- Void: a law that is inconsistent with a right has no legal force and cannot be enforced, to the extent of the inconsistency (Article 13).
- Severability (standard knowledge; verify): only the offending part of a law is struck down if the rest can stand alone.
- Eclipse (standard knowledge; verify): a pre-1950 law inconsistent with a right is dormant, not dead, and revives if the conflict is removed.
- Waiver (standard knowledge; verify): a Fundamental Right cannot be given up by consent.
- Saving clause: a constitutional provision that shields certain laws from challenge under certain rights (Articles 31A, 31B, 31C).
- Ninth Schedule: the Schedule added by the 1st Amendment (1951) in which listed laws were protected by Article 31B; open to review for laws placed after 24 April 1973 (Coelho).
- Martial law: military rule over an area in extraordinary circumstances; implicit in Article 34; not defined in the Constitution.
- Indemnity: protection from legal liability for acts done in good faith in maintaining or restoring order (Article 34).
- Locus standi: the right to sue; PIL relaxes the rule that only the injured person may sue.
- Judicial review: the power of courts to examine the constitutionality of laws and executive orders and to declare violating ones void; a basic feature (Laxmikanth).

### Notes: Article 19: grounds of restriction (from Laxmikanth)

- Speech and expression: sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence (eight grounds).
- Assembly: sovereignty and integrity of India; public order (Laxmikanth adds: including maintenance of traffic in the area). Under Section 144 CrPC a magistrate can restrain an assembly (CrPC now replaced; verify current section).
- Association: sovereignty and integrity of India; public order; morality.
- Movement and residence: interests of the general public; protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.
- Profession, occupation, trade or business: interests of the general public; State may prescribe qualifications and carry on trade or business by itself (even as a monopoly); immoral or dangerous trades may be banned or licensed.
- Right to strike: not included in speech, assembly or association; may be regulated by industrial law.
- Right to property under Article 19(1)(f): deleted by the 44th Amendment (1978).

### Notes: Case-law spine: one line per case

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Restriction versus suspension: a restriction limits use of a right permanently on stated grounds; suspension is a temporary switch-off in an Emergency.
- Article 358 versus Article 359: suspends the right (Article 19) versus suspends the remedy (court access for rights named in an Order).
- Martial law versus National Emergency: area-specific military rule implicit in Article 34 versus an explicit constitutional proclamation under Article 352.
- Article 31A versus 31B versus 31C: five categories of law shielded from Articles 14 and 19; Ninth Schedule laws shielded from all FRs; laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c) shielded from Articles 14 and 19.
- Article 32 versus Article 226: FRs only, nationwide, not refusable versus any legal right, within territory, discretionary.
- Public order versus law and order versus security of the State: three concentric circles (Ram Manohar Lohia v State of Bihar, 1966; standard knowledge; verify).
- Judicial custody versus police custody (standard knowledge; verify).
- Due process of law versus procedure established by law: wide judicial review of fairness versus review of competence only; Maneka Gandhi moved India's reading of Article 21 towards due process without changing the words. Due process has two limbs: procedural (notice, hearing, an unbiased decision-maker: natural justice) and substantive (the law itself must be fair and reasonable); Maneka Gandhi imported both into Article 21 [standard knowledge; verify].

### Core concepts

- Fundamental Rights are qualified, not absolute: the State may impose reasonable restrictions and the courts decide reasonableness.
- Restrictions on Article 19 are allowed only on the grounds listed in Article 19 itself.
- Limits come in seven kinds: written-in grounds, internal exceptions, saving clauses, class of person, situation, who may legislate, and limits on the limiters.
- Article 13 makes laws inconsistent with rights void to the extent of inconsistency; doctrines of severability, eclipse and waiver refine it.
- Articles 31A, 31B and 31C shield some laws but the Court has limited each (Kesavananda, Coelho, Minerva Mills).
- Article 358 suspends Article 19 (war or external aggression only); Article 359 suspends the remedy and never for Articles 20 and 21.
- Article 32 requires an FR violation; Article 226 is wider and discretionary; PIL relaxes locus standi.

### Practice

**Q.** What two rules govern every restriction on an Article 19 freedom?

**A.** It must be reasonable and it must rest on a ground listed in Article 19 itself; the courts decide whether it is reasonable.

**Q.** Name the ground that applies only to movement and residence and not to speech.

**A.** Protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.

**Q.** Who alone may make a law restricting the Fundamental Rights of members of the armed forces?

**A.** Parliament (Article 33); State legislatures cannot.

**Q.** Is the right to property now a Fundamental Right? Give the Article.

**A.** No; since the 44th Amendment (1978) it is a constitutional right under Article 300A.

**Q.** Explain the difference between restricting a right and suspending a right, with an example of each.

**A.** A restriction limits use of a right on stated grounds while the right stays alive (a ban on speech inciting an offence). Suspension switches the right or its remedy off temporarily (Article 19 during an Emergency declared on war or external aggression).

**Q.** Why is Article 31B wider in effect than Article 31A, and how did Coelho reduce its width?

**A.** Article 31A shields five categories of law against Articles 14 and 19; Article 31B shields any Ninth Schedule law against all FRs. Coelho held there is no blanket immunity: laws placed after 24 April 1973 can be tested on Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 and the basic structure.

**Q.** Why can a person not go straight to the Supreme Court under Article 32 for loss of property after 1978?

**A.** Because the right to property is now Article 300A, not a Fundamental Right, and Article 32 enforces only Fundamental Rights; the remedy is the High Court under Article 226 or an ordinary suit.

**Q.** In what sense does the Constitution place limits on the limiters of rights?

**A.** A law restricting a right can be struck down under Article 13 if unreasonable or outside the grounds, and Parliament's power to amend rights cannot damage the basic structure (Kesavananda); judicial review itself cannot be removed.

**Q.** A State law bans all public meetings in a town for a year to prevent possible disturbances. Test it step by step.

**A.** Freedom: assembly, Article 19(1)(b). Ground: public order is listed in 19(3), so the first test is met. Reasonable and proportionate: a blanket year-long ban is probably excessive and a narrower, time-limited order would achieve the aim; so the law is likely to be struck down as unreasonable.

**Q.** An Emergency is declared on the ground of armed rebellion and a Presidential Order names Articles 14 and 19. A person is detained and seeks habeas corpus for Article 21. What is the position?

**A.** Article 358 does not suspend Article 19 for armed rebellion. The Order can suspend the remedy only for the rights it names; enforcement of Article 21 can never be suspended, so the habeas corpus petition can be heard.

**Q.** A Ninth Schedule law was placed in 1985 and restricts the profession of a group. Can it be challenged?

**A.** Yes: it was placed after 24 April 1973, so under Coelho it can be tested against Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 and the basic structure; Article 31B alone does not bar the challenge.
