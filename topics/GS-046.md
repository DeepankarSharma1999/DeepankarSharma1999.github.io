## GS-046: Round Table Conferences and Gandhi-Irwin Pact

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words you need: dominion, federation, safeguards, separate electorate, ordinance

- **Viceroy**: the Crown's representative in India; **Secretary of State for India**: the British cabinet minister in charge of Indian policy; **Prime Minister**: head of the British government.
- **Dominion status**: self-government within the British Empire under the Crown. **Purna swaraj**: complete independence.
- **Federation**: a union of units that keep some powers (provinces and princely states).
- **Safeguards and reservations**: powers the British wished to keep, such as defence and finance, during any transfer.
- **Minority and separate electorate**: a group demanding seats elected only by its own voters, rather than by all voters together.
- **Truce**: a pause in the struggle by agreement. **Ordinance**: an executive order issued without the legislature.

Dates are CE.

### 1. Orientation: from the salt law to the negotiating table

*Objective:* State in six sentences who met whom between 1930 and 1932, what each meeting produced and where related topics are taught.

## The meaning in plain words

A **Round Table Conference (RTC)** was a meeting in London where the British government, Indian princes and Indian political groups sat as participants to discuss India's constitutional future. There were **three** (1930-31, 1931 and 1932). The **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** (also called the **Delhi Pact**) was a settlement between Gandhi, for the Congress, and Lord Irwin, the Viceroy, which paused the Civil Disobedience Movement. This lesson follows the *negotiating track* that ran beside the street struggle taught in GS-045.

## The story in six sentences

1. After the Salt March and the repression of 1930 (GS-045), Viceroy Irwin's promise of a Round Table Conference was no longer enough for the Congress.
2. The **first RTC** (November 1930 to January 1931) met without the Congress and achieved little.
3. Gandhi and the Congress Working Committee were released on 25 January 1931, and the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** was signed on **5 March 1931**.
4. The **Karachi session** (March 1931) endorsed it and adopted the Fundamental Rights and economic resolutions.
5. At the **second RTC** (September to December 1931) Gandhi was the only Congress representative; it deadlocked on the minorities question.
6. Civil disobedience resumed in January 1932; a **third RTC** (November-December 1932) met without the Congress, and its work led to the Government of India Act 1935.

## What other lessons teach

- Lahore 1929, Dandi and the first phase of the movement: GS-045.
- The Communal Award (August 1932) and the Poona Pact (September 1932): GS-052, module 6. This lesson only names them where the chronology needs them.
- Gandhi's anti-untouchability campaign: GS-052, module 7.
- The Government of India Act 1935 itself: a later day, not yet written.
- Simon Commission and the Nehru Report: GS-044, to come on Day 15, not yet written.

### 2. Background: Irwin's declaration to the failed Yerawada talks

*Objective:* Explain the sequence of offers and refusals from October 1929 to August 1930 that set the terms of the first RTC.

## Recap from GS-045 (not re-taught here)

- **31 October 1929**: Irwin's declaration of dominion status as the natural issue of India's progress and a promised RTC, with no timescale; NCERT Class X calls it a vague offer (GS-045 module 2).
- **2 November 1929**: the Delhi Manifesto's three conditions (a constitution for dominion status, a Congress majority, a general amnesty and conciliation); Irwin rejected them in December 1929 (GS-045 module 2).
- **1930**: ordinances and a gagged press, with the government ambivalent (GS-045 module 8).

## The new offer, July 1930

In **July 1930** Irwin suggested a Round Table Conference and repeated the goal of dominion status. He accepted that **Tej Bahadur Sapru** and **M.R. Jayakar** should explore peace between the Congress and the government.

## The Yerawada talks, August 1930

In **August 1930** Motilal and Jawaharlal Nehru were taken to Yerawada Jail to confer with Gandhi. They restated three demands: the right of secession from Britain; a complete national government with control of defence and finance; and an independent tribunal to settle Britain's financial claims. Talks broke down.

## Why this background matters

The distance between the two sides explains the first RTC. The government held the conference *to discuss the form* of dominion status; the Congress demanded that the conference *begin from* dominion status as settled and with a Congress majority. The Congress therefore refused to attend in 1930. Note also that Spectrum says the Prime Minister (Ramsay MacDonald) and Irwin agreed the conference was needed because the Simon recommendations were clearly inadequate.

### 3. The first Round Table Conference, November 1930 to January 1931

*Objective:* Describe the first RTC: dates, who sat there, who was absent, what was agreed and why it did not change the situation.

## Dates and setting

The first RTC met in **London between November 1930 and January 1931**. King George V opened it on **12 November 1930**, and the British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** chaired it. Spectrum calls it the first conference arranged between the British and Indians *as equals*.

## Who attended

The **Congress and some leading business figures refused to come**; many other groups attended. Spectrum lists representatives of:

- **Princely states**: for example the Maharajas of Alwar, Baroda, Bikaner and Patiala (who was Chancellor of the Chamber of Princes) and the Nawab of Bhopal.
- **Muslims**: the Aga Khan III (leader of the British-Indian delegation), Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Muhammad Shafi, Maulana Mohammad Ali and others. Spectrum lists them under the Muslim League; verify, since delegates were named by the government.
- **Hindu Mahasabha**: B.S. Moonje and M.R. Jayakar.
- **Liberals**: Tej Bahadur Sapru, C.Y. Chintamani, V.S. Srinivasa Sastri and others.
- **Depressed Classes**: B.R. Ambedkar and Rettamalai Srinivasan.
- Sikhs, Parsis, women (Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz, Radhabai Subbarayan), the Justice Party, labour, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans, landlords, universities and Burma.

(Standard accounts add British party delegates; this is not in the extracts, verify.)

## What was agreed

Spectrum: nothing much was achieved. It was generally agreed that India would develop into a **federation**, that there would be **safeguards** regarding defence and finance, and that other departments would be transferred. Little was done to implement this, and civil disobedience continued in India.

## The lesson the British drew

The British government realised that the participation of the **Indian National Congress** was necessary in any discussion of India's constitutional future. NCERT Class XII makes a similar point: held without the pre-eminent political leader, the meeting was an exercise in futility. This realisation led to the pact in module 4.

## Why a federation mattered

A **federation** joins units with their own powers into a larger union. Here the units would be British Indian provinces and the princely states, which is why the princes' agreement mattered. Spectrum notes later that the princes cooled towards federation once the possibility of a Congress government at the centre receded (module 7).

## Check the chronology

Be careful: the Congress's absence applies to the **first** conference only. In Prelims, a statement that says 'the Congress attended all three RTCs' or 'boycotted all three' is wrong in both directions.

### 4. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact, 5 March 1931: how and what

*Objective:* Narrate the path to the pact and state its terms and each side's concessions precisely.

## The path

On **25 January 1931** Gandhi and all members of the Congress Working Committee (CWC) were released unconditionally. The CWC authorised Gandhi to talk with the Viceroy. NCERT Class XII says the Viceroy and Gandhi held several long meetings the following month. Spectrum records that members of the Liberal Party such as Sapru, Chintamani and Srinivasa Sastri had urged Gandhi to talk. The result was a pact signed in **Delhi on 5 March 1931** between the Viceroy, for the British Indian government, and Gandhi, for the Indian people. Spectrum adds that it placed the Congress on an equal footing with the government.

## What Irwin agreed (Spectrum, seven points)

1. Immediate release of political prisoners **not convicted of violence**.
2. Remission of fines not yet collected.
3. Return of lands not yet sold to third parties.
4. Lenient treatment of government servants who had resigned.
5. The right to make salt in coastal villages for **personal consumption** (not for sale).
6. The right to **peaceful and non-aggressive picketing**.
7. Withdrawal of **emergency ordinances**.

## What Irwin refused

The Viceroy turned down two of Gandhi's demands: a **public enquiry into police excesses** and the **commutation of Bhagat Singh's and his comrades' death sentences** to life imprisonment.

## What Gandhi agreed for the Congress

- To **suspend the Civil Disobedience Movement**.
- To **take part in the next Round Table Conference**, on the constitutional question built around three linchpins: federation, Indian responsibility, and the reservations and safeguards necessary in India's interests (defence, external affairs, position of minorities, financial credit and other obligations).

## Keeping the sources apart

NCERT Class XII summarises the pact as: civil disobedience called off, *all prisoners* released, salt manufacture allowed along the coast. NCERT Class X says the government agreed to release the political prisoners. Spectrum is more exact: only those **not convicted of violence**, and salt only for personal use. For a Prelims statement, use the precise form.

## The pact in one table

| Side | Concession |
|---|---|
| Government | Seven points above |
| Government (refused) | Enquiry into police excesses; commutation of Bhagat Singh's sentence |
| Congress | Suspend movement; attend RTC |

## Link

The salt clause shows how the pact answered the 1930 campaign (GS-045). The refusal on Bhagat Singh matters for module 5 and the Karachi session in module 6.

### 5. Reactions to the pact and the retreat debate

*Objective:* Explain who welcomed or criticised the pact and weigh the argument that it was or was not a retreat.

## Spectrum: why it was not a retreat

Spectrum argues that suspending the movement was *not* a retreat because (i) mass movements are necessarily short-lived; (ii) the capacity of the masses to sacrifice, unlike that of activists, is limited; and (iii) signs of exhaustion had appeared after September 1930, especially among shopkeepers and merchants who had joined enthusiastically.

## Who was disappointed

- **Youth**: they wanted the movement to end with a bang, not a whimper. NCERT Class XII says radical nationalists criticised the pact since Gandhi could not obtain a commitment to political independence, only an assurance of talks.
- **Peasants of Gujarat**: their lands were not restored immediately (Spectrum says they were restored only during Congress ministry rule in the province).
- **Those who wanted Bhagat Singh spared**: the refusal to commute his sentence produced protests (module 6).

## Who welcomed it

Spectrum says many people were jubilant that the government had been made to treat their movement as significant and its leader as an equal, and to sign a pact with him. Political prisoners released from jail were given a hero's welcome. Spectrum later notes that senior British officials believed the Delhi Pact had raised the Congress's prestige and the people's morale and undermined British prestige, and wanted to reverse this (module 8).

## Two readings

- **Tactical gain**: the movement kept its popularity, the Congress was recognised as a party to negotiate, and a pause preserved strength for a later round.
- **Missed chance**: no commitment to independence, no commutation, and no enquiry into police excesses; and some gains (Gujarat land) were slow.

## Writing tip

For a 'critically examine' question, give both readings with facts and state a view. Spectrum's own position is that it was not a retreat; NCERT records radical criticism. Presenting both is the 'interpretive uncertainty' the examiners look for.

## Source note

Spectrum's evaluation sits inside its chapter on the Civil Disobedience Movement, so GS-045's evaluation module (significance and limitations of the movement as a whole) covers the wider picture; this module deals only with the pact.

### 6. The Karachi session, March 1931

*Objective:* Explain why the Congress met at Karachi, what it decided and why the Fundamental Rights and economic resolutions matter.

## Why Karachi

A special Congress session was held at **Karachi** in March 1931 to **endorse the Gandhi-Irwin Pact**. Spectrum gives **29 March** as the session date (standard accounts describe a multi-day session in late March 1931; verify); the session president was **Vallabhbhai Patel** (standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify). Six days before it, **Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were executed** (23 March). Throughout Gandhi's route to Karachi, the **Punjab Naujawan Bharat Sabha** greeted him with **black flag demonstrations** over his failure to save them.

## Decisions

- The Congress **disapproved of and dissociated itself from political violence**, but admired the **bravery and sacrifice** of the three martyrs.
- The **Delhi Pact was endorsed**.
- The goal of **purna swaraj** was reiterated.
- Two resolutions were adopted, the reason the session is remembered: **Fundamental Rights** and the **National Economic Programme**.

## Fundamental Rights resolution

It guaranteed free speech and a free press, the right to form associations and to assemble, universal adult franchise, equal legal rights irrespective of caste, creed and sex, neutrality of the State in religious matters, free and compulsory primary education, and protection of the culture, language and script of minorities and linguistic groups.

## National Economic Programme

It included substantial reduction in rent and revenue for landholders and peasants, exemption from rent for uneconomic holdings, relief from agricultural indebtedness, control of usury, better conditions of work (living wage, limited hours, protection of women workers), the right of workers and peasants to form unions, and State ownership and control of key industries, mines and means of transport.

## Why it matters

Spectrum says this was the first time the Congress spelt out what swaraj would mean for the masses, adding that political freedom must include economic freedom for starving millions. The Karachi Resolution remained, in essence, the basic political and economic programme of the Congress in later years. A learner who knows the Constitution should notice the echo: rights such as equality before law and adult franchise appear again in later documents, but that link is an observation, not a source claim.

## Balanced view

The resolution tried to answer the criticism of the left that the Congress ignored class. How far the Congress leadership followed it is a separate question not settled by the extracts.

### 7. The second Round Table Conference, September to December 1931

*Objective:* Describe the second RTC, why little was expected, what Gandhi claimed and why it failed.

## Dates and delegates

The second RTC was held in London from **7 September to 1 December 1931**. The Congress nominated **Gandhi as its sole representative**; Spectrum says A. Rangaswami Iyengar and Madan Mohan Malaviya were also present, and Sarojini Naidu is listed among women. Many other groups attended: princes, Muslims (including Aga Khan III, Jinnah, Iqbal and Shaukat Ali), Hindu groups, Liberals, the Justice Party, Depressed Classes (Ambedkar and Rettamalai Srinivasan), Sikhs, Parsis, Indian Christians, industry (G.D. Birla and others), labour and others.

## Why little was expected (Spectrum)

- **Lord Willingdon had replaced Irwin** as Viceroy, and just before the conference the Labour government in Britain had been replaced by a **National Government**, an uneasy coalition. (Spectrum gives no dates; standard accounts place Willingdon's arrival in April 1931; verify.)
- Conservatives led by Churchill objected to the government negotiating with the Congress on equal terms; Samuel Hoare was the secretary of state.
- The British were angered by the rise in revolutionary activity that had cost European lives.

## What Gandhi claimed and proposed

Gandhi said India needed a partnership with Britain on equal terms and demanded immediate **responsible government** at the centre and in the provinces. He said the **Congress alone represented political India**. He argued that untouchables were Hindus and so not a minority needing **separate electorates**, and that Muslims and other minorities needed no separate electorates or special safeguards. Many delegates disagreed. NCERT Class XII says his claim to represent all India was challenged by the **Muslim League**, the **princes** (who said the Congress had no stake in their territories) and **B.R. Ambedkar**, who argued that Gandhi and the Congress did not really represent the lowest castes.

## Why it failed

- The session **deadlocked on the minorities question**: Muslims, Depressed Classes, Christians and Anglo-Indians combined in a **Minorities' Pact** demanding separate electorates; Gandhi fought hard against making all constitutional progress depend on settling it.
- The **princes** were cool about federation once the Congress's prospects of forming a government at the centre receded after suspension of civil disobedience.
- Spectrum quotes historians that many delegates were conservative, loyalist or communalist, and that the government used them to neutralise Gandhi and to claim that the Congress did not speak for all India.

## Outcome

The session ended with MacDonald's announcement of: the position of the **NWFP and Sindh** as Muslim-majority provinces (Spectrum's wording is short; standard accounts say separate provinces would be created, verify), an **Indian Consultative Committee**, three expert committees (finance, franchise, states), and the prospect of a **unilateral British Communal Award** if Indians failed to agree. The basic demand of freedom was refused. Gandhi returned to India on **28 December 1931**.

## Source discrepancy

NCERT Class X says Gandhi went to London in December 1931; Spectrum's dates show the conference ended on 1 December. Use Spectrum's dates and treat NCERT's remark as imprecise. Gandhi sailed in late August 1931 and reached London in September 1931 (standard; verify).

### 8. Resumption of civil disobedience, the third RTC and the hand-offs

*Objective:* Narrate the truce period, the resumption in 1932, the third RTC and the path to the 1935 Act, and name where later items are taught.

## The truce period, March to December 1931

Spectrum says activity during the truce kept the spirit of defiance alive: in the United Provinces the Congress led a movement for rent reduction and against evictions; in the NWFP severe repression fell on the **Khudai Khidmatgars** and the peasants they led; in Bengal ordinances and mass detention were used in the name of fighting terrorism; in **September 1931** there was a firing on political prisoners at **Hijli Jail**.

## A changed British policy

Senior British officials, believing the Delhi Pact had raised the Congress's prestige and undermined British prestige, adopted three principles: Gandhi must not rebuild the tempo of a mass movement; the goodwill of the Congress was not needed but the confidence of its opponents (officials, loyalists) was; and the national movement must not consolidate in rural areas.

## Resumption

After the second RTC failed, the CWC decided on **29 December 1931** to resume civil disobedience. Viceroy Willingdon refused a meeting with Gandhi on 31 December, and Gandhi was **arrested on 4 January 1932**. Repressive ordinances produced what Spectrum calls a civilian 'civil martial law': Congress organisations at all levels were banned, activists and sympathisers were arrested, property was confiscated, Gandhi ashrams were occupied, and repression was harsh on women. In four months about **80,000** satyagrahis, mostly urban and rural poor, were jailed. Forms of protest included picketing, illegal assemblies, flag hoisting, non-payment of chowkidara tax, salt satyagraha, forest-law violations and a secret radio transmitter near Bombay. It coincided with upsurges in Kashmir and Alwar. It could not be sustained because leaders had no time to build tempo and the masses were unprepared; **Gandhi withdrew it in April 1934** (NCERT Class X: it lost momentum by 1934). NCERT Class X also records that Ghaffar Khan and Nehru were in jail and the Congress declared illegal on Gandhi's return.

## Hand-off: Communal Award and Poona Pact

Between the second and third conferences, the **Communal Award** (16 August 1932) and the **Poona Pact** (24 September 1932) belong to GS-052, module 6; Gandhi's anti-untouchability campaign is GS-052 module 7. Note only the order: Award, fast, Pact, then the Harijan campaign.

## The third RTC, 17 November to 24 December 1932

Spectrum says the Congress and Gandhi did not attend and most other Indian leaders ignored it. Princes were represented by their Dewans and others; individuals included Aga Khan III, Ambedkar, Jayakar and Sapru. Little was achieved. The recommendations appeared in a **White Paper in March 1933**, were debated in Parliament, and a **Joint Select Committee** produced a draft Bill in **February 1935**, which became the **Government of India Act, 1935**. Spectrum ch19 says enforced in July 1935; ch20 says passed by Parliament in August 1935. Standard accounts: Royal Assent August 1935; provincial provisions in force from 1 April 1937; the federation never came into being (verify). Do not write that the Act was enforced in 1935. The Act itself belongs to a later day (not yet written); only a one-line pointer is needed here.

### 9. Evaluation: what the negotiations achieved and failed to achieve

*Objective:* Weigh the achievements and failures of the pact and the three RTCs and name the main interpretive disputes.

## Why the RTCs failed to settle India's future

- **Purpose mismatch**: the British treated the RTC as a place to discuss dominion status; the Congress wanted it to begin from dominion status (modules 2 and 7).
- **Representation**: the first RTC lacked the Congress; the second saw Gandhi claim the Congress alone represented India while others denied it; the third lacked the Congress again.
- **Minorities deadlock**: the Minorities' Pact made agreement on safeguards a precondition, and the British moved towards imposing a Communal Award.
- **Federation depended on the princes**, who lost interest once Congress power at the centre seemed unlikely.
- **British politics**: a National Government and Conservative objections (modules 7 and 8).

## What they achieved

- The British and Indian parties sat together as equals for the first time (Spectrum), and the Congress was treated as a party that had to be negotiated with.
- The principles of a federation, safeguards and Indian responsibility were put on the table and later fed the Government of India Act, 1935.
- The pact shows that the Salt March forced a negotiation, as NCERT Class XII argues that it made the British realise they would have to devolve some power.

## What they did not achieve

No timetable for dominion status or independence; no commitment to the Congress's demand for secession rights or control of defence and finance (Yerawada demands); no resolution of the minorities question; the pact's gains on land and prisoners were limited.

## Interpretive questions

- **Was the pact a retreat?** Spectrum says no; radical nationalists said it gave too little (module 5).
- **Was Gandhi's claim to represent all of India a strength or a flaw?** One reading sees it as asserting national unity against divide and rule; another sees it as ignoring real minority fears that the Poona Pact later showed (GS-052). The extracts present both sides.
- **Were delegates mere instruments of the government?** Spectrum cites historians who say many were loyalists and communalists used to neutralise Gandhi; the extracts do not tell us that every delegate fits this description.

## Discrepancies to remember

NCERT Class X and Class XII compress the story (a December 1931 trip; all prisoners released). Spectrum's two chapters give different months for the 1935 Act. These show why a learner should anchor facts in dated, sourced statements.

## 2017 Prelims Q93: chronological sequence

The events are the Mutiny in the Royal Indian Navy, the launch of the Quit India Movement and the Second Round Table Conference. Anchor dates: Second RTC, September to December 1931 (module 7); Quit India, August 1942 (standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify); the Royal Indian Navy mutiny, 1946 (standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify). The order is therefore Second RTC, then Quit India, then the Navy mutiny. Analysis points to the option with that order, not an official key. **Technique**: place the earliest (RTC, 1931) first; this alone removes two options.

## 2020 Prelims Q27: the Gandhi-Irwin Pact

Statements: (1) invitation to the Congress to participate in the RTC; (2) withdrawal of ordinances; (3) acceptance of the suggestion for an enquiry into police excesses; (4) release of only those prisoners not charged with violence. Using module 4: statement 2 matches the seventh concession; statement 4 matches the first (Spectrum: not convicted of violence, a slightly different phrase from 'not charged'); statement 3 conflicts with the refusal of a public enquiry. Statement 1 is consistent with both sources (the Congress agreed to attend the next RTC; NCERT Class X: Gandhi consented to participate). Eliminating 3 removes (c) and (d); confirming 2 removes (a); analysis therefore points to the option listing 1, 2 and 4. This is analysis, not an official key. **Trap**: confusing what the government gave with what it refused, and the 'convicted' versus 'charged' wording.

## 2025 Prelims Q74: Gandhi and the upliftment of Harijans

The question asks after which event Gandhi decided to include Harijan upliftment in his political and social programme. Spectrum (book pages 411 to 414, PDF 442-445; taught in GS-052 module 7) says that after the Communal Award and the Poona Pact he launched a campaign against untouchability, first from jail (the All India Anti-Untouchability League, September 1932; the weekly *Harijan*; Spectrum: January 1933, standard accounts give the first issue as 11 February 1933; verify). Events after 1932 (the 1935 Act, the Quit India arrests of 1942) cannot have prompted it; the 1931 pact predates the Award. Analysis points to the Poona Pact; not an official key. **Q.** Name the three RTCs with their years.

**A.** First: 1930-31; second: 1931; third: 1932.

**A.** Gandhi and Viceroy Irwin, 5 March 1931.

**A.** The second, through Gandhi.

**Q.** Why was the Delhi Manifesto's demand different from Irwin's offer?

**A.** It demanded that the RTC frame a constitution for dominion status with a Congress majority; Irwin said the conference would discuss, not settle, that.

**Q.** Why did Spectrum say the pact was not a retreat?

**A.** Mass movements are short-lived, the masses' capacity is limited, and exhaustion had appeared by September 1930.
