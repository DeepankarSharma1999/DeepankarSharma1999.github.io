## GS-147: Women’s movements and collectives

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Women’s movements and collectives and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Reform era and women's own voices

The women's question emerged in nineteenth-century middle-class reform movements that drew on both Western democratic ideals and Indian traditions. Rammohun Roy attacked sati using humanitarian arguments and the shastras; M.G. Ranade defended widow remarriage with textual authority; Jotiba Phule, from an excluded caste, attacked caste and gender oppression together; Sir Syed Ahmed Khan supported girls' education, though at home and with a domestic curriculum. Women also spoke for themselves. Tarabai Shinde, a Maharashtrian housewife, wrote Stree Purush Tulana in 1882 to expose double standards after a widow was sentenced to death while the father went unpunished. Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain's Sultana's Dream of 1905 imagined a land where gender roles were reversed and is described as an early example of Indian science fiction.

### Organisations, nationalism and the second phase

From the early twentieth century, national bodies such as the WIA (1917), NCWI (1925) and AIWC (1926) emerged; the AIWC began by separating welfare from politics but soon tied women's freedom to national freedom. Peasant and tribal women joined struggles such as Tebhaga in Bengal, the Telangana armed struggle and the Warli revolt. Congress's 1931 Karachi declaration promised legal equality and political rights for women. After a post-Independence lull, the movement revived in the mid-1970s with new strategies and issues: violence, dowry, land and employment rights, and sexual harassment, with campaigns producing changes such as listing mothers' names on school forms. The movement also recognised that middle-class, peasant, Dalit and upper-caste women face different kinds of discrimination, and that rigid gender identities constrain men too.

### Collectives in local governance

The 73rd Amendment of 1992 gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj, and together with the 74th Amendment reserved one-third of seats in rural and urban local bodies for women, with 17 per cent of seats set aside for SC and ST women according to NCERT. The 1993–94 elections alone brought around 800,000 women into political office. NCERT's case material shows how collective organisation deepens this. A Dalit panchayat member describes gaining confidence and a name of her own instead of being known only as someone's wife or mother. In Uttarakhand, where men often serve far away, women run van panchayats that raise nurseries and guard forests against illegal felling. The NGO Mahila Samakhya used folk scrolls to train women voters to judge integrity, not just to vote.
