## GS-236: Human capital and economic development

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Human capital and economic development and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### How human capital is formed

NCERT compares a family paying for schooling to a firm buying machines: both give up resources now for higher returns later. Health spending, whether preventive, curative or on clean water and sanitation, raises the supply of fit workers. Firms train staff on the job and expect them to stay long enough to recover the cost through higher productivity. Migration costs, including transport, higher living expenses and the strain of an unfamiliar setting, count as investment when the new earnings exceed them. Spending to learn about wages, courses and job markets also builds human capital because it guides better choices. A key point is that good human capital is needed to produce more of it: trained teachers and doctors create the next generation.

### Physical versus human capital, and the growth link

Box 4.1 lists clear contrasts. A bus owner need not be present when the bus is used, but the driver's skill cannot be separated from the driver. Only the services of human capital are sold, not the capital itself. Machines move freely across borders, but nationality and culture restrict the movement of skilled people, so human capital must be built through domestic policy. Both depreciate, yet continued learning and health care can offset the ageing of human capital. On growth, NCERT is careful: educated and healthy workers clearly earn and innovate more, but cross-country evidence is unclear because quality is hard to measure, and causation probably runs both ways between income and human capital.

### Why the state must invest

Education and health decisions are hard to reverse: a child placed in a poor school suffers damage before parents can switch. Consumers also lack full information on quality and price, giving providers market power. Hence government must both provide services and regulate private providers. NCERT records the gap between actual and recommended spending: a little over 4 per cent of GDP against the 6 per cent goal set by the Education Commission. It also notes wide inter-state gaps, with per capita public spending on elementary education in 2020-21 about ₹96,968 in Sikkim against ₹10,710 in Bihar. The 2009 RTE Act and an education cess earmarked for elementary schooling were policy responses. Unemployment among educated youth shows that quality and relevance matter alongside quantity.

### Worked example

Answer frame for 'Distinguish human capital from human development': define each; human capital = education/health as inputs to labour productivity (any investment that does not raise output is 'unproductive'); human development = education/health as rights and ends that widen choices; illustrate with basic literacy for an elderly woman who will not join the workforce; conclude that policy needs both lenses.

### Recall

- - **q:** List the five sources of human capital formation.
  - **a:** Education, health, on-the-job training, migration and information.
- - **q:** Why is human capital not perfectly mobile between countries?
  - **a:** Its movement is restricted by nationality and culture, unlike physical capital, which can be imported.
- - **q:** What share of GDP did the Education Commission (1964–66) recommend for education?
  - **a:** At least 6 per cent.

### Common trap

Using 'human capital' and 'human development' interchangeably: human capital treats education and health as means to productivity, while human development treats them as ends in themselves.
