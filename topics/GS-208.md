## GS-208: National/State commissions: SC ST women children

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Institutions, law and politics

*Goal:* Explain National/State commissions: SC ST women children and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Constitutional commissions for SCs and STs

Article 338 sets up the National Commission for Scheduled Castes and Article 338A the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, each with a Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson and three members appointed by the President. Both monitor safeguards under the Constitution and laws, inquire into specific complaints of deprivation of rights, advise on socio-economic planning and report annually to the President. While investigating, they can summon and examine people on oath, call for documents and take affidavit evidence like a civil court, and the Union and States must consult them on major policy matters affecting these groups. Reports reach Parliament with an action-taken memorandum, and State portions reach State legislatures through the Governor. Their influence thus rests on transparency and political pressure rather than binding orders.

### The National Commission for Women

The NCW draws its authority from the National Commission for Women Act, 1990, which took effect on 31 January 1992. The Centre nominates a Chairperson committed to women's causes, five Members with experience in fields such as law, trade unions, women's organisations, administration, health or education (including at least one Scheduled Caste and one Scheduled Tribe member) and a Member-Secretary. Members hold office for up to three years; removal is on listed grounds such as insolvency or abuse of position, the latter only after a hearing. Under section 10 the Commission reviews safeguards and laws for women, suggests amendments, takes up complaints and suo motu cases, inspects jails and other custodial places and may fund litigation affecting many women. Reports go to Parliament and relevant State legislatures with action-taken memoranda, and key inquiries carry civil-court powers. The Act itself creates no State-level commissions.

### Child-rights commissions, national and State

The Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005 creates a two-tier system. The national commission has a Chairperson known for work on child welfare and six Members, at least two of them women, from areas such as education, child health, juvenile justice, child labour, child psychology and child law; terms are three years, capped at two terms. It reviews legal safeguards, inquires into violations, inspects children's institutions and acts on complaints or suo motu. Using civil-court powers, it can forward cases to a Magistrate, recommend prosecution or interim relief, or approach the Supreme Court or a High Court. States may constitute State Commissions on the same model and designate Children's Courts. The Protection of Human Rights Act links both national commissions to the NHRC by making their chairpersons deemed members.
