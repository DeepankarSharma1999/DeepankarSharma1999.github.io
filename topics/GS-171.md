### Bridge: Four language words: official language, link language, national language, mother tongue

Language questions use four terms that are easily mixed up.

- An **official language** is the language in which a government conducts its official work: laws, orders, records, communication. Part XVII speaks of the official language of the **Union** (the Centre) and of each **State**.
- A **link language** is a language used for communication between governments or regions that do not share a first language. Under Article 346 the language(s) for the time being authorised for the Union's official purposes serve as the link language between the Union and the States and between States (module 4); in practice that means English with non-Hindi States.
- A **national language** would be a language the Constitution declares as the language of the nation. Part XVII uses 'official language' and does not declare a 'national language' (standard knowledge; verify against the official text). So 'Hindi is the national language' is a claim that Part XVII does not support.
- A **mother tongue** is the language a person first learns at home. Article 350A speaks of instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage for linguistic minority children.

Two further words appear in the sources. A **lingua franca** is a language widely used for communication across groups; Article 351 hopes Hindi will become the lingua franca of the composite culture of India. **Classical language** is a separate, recognition-based category (module 7).

### Bridge: Language, script and numerals: Hindi, Devanagari and Hindustani

Article 343 says Hindi written in the **Devanagari script** is the official language of the Union. Three ideas are packed into that sentence. A **language** is a system of spoken and written words (Hindi). A **script** is the set of written characters used to write a language (Devanagari; Hindi, Marathi and Sanskrit are commonly written in it, standard knowledge). The **numerals** are the written forms of numbers. Laxmikanth says the numerals for the Union's official purposes are the **international form of Indian numerals** (the familiar 0, 1, 2, 3 ...), and not the Devanagari forms of numerals. So the Constitution chose a Devanagari script for the language but a different default form for numbers; limited exceptions exist (module 2).

**Hindustani** appears in Article 351: it is a name for the common spoken form of Hindi and Urdu (standard knowledge); the Article asks the Centre to enrich Hindi by assimilating forms, style and expressions from Hindustani and from the languages of the Eighth Schedule. A **Schedule** is a list attached to the Constitution (GS-161 module 3).

### 1. Orientation: Part XVII, the four heads and the two threads

*Objective:* Name the four heads of Part XVII and the Articles under each, and state the two threads that run through the chapter.

## What this topic is about

India has many spoken languages, and the Constitution had to answer practical questions. In which language will the Union government work? In which will a State work? In which will the Supreme Court hear cases? How will people who speak a different language from the majority be protected? **Part XVII** of the Constitution answers these questions in **Articles 343 to 351**. (A Part is a major division of the Constitution; GS-161 explains Parts, Articles and Schedules.) Laxmikanth divides the provisions into four heads.

| Head | Articles | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| Language of the Union | 343, 344 | Hindi in Devanagari as the Union's official language; English for fifteen years; a Commission and a Committee of Parliament |
| Regional languages | 345, 346, 347 | A State's official language or languages; language for communication between the Union and States and between States; recognition of a language spoken by a section of a State's population |
| Language of the judiciary and texts of laws | 348, 349 | English in the Supreme Court, High Courts and in authoritative texts of laws until Parliament provides otherwise; a special procedure for laws on language |
| Special directives | 350, 350A, 350B, 351 | Representations in any language; mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage; a Special Officer for linguistic minorities; development of Hindi |

Laxmikanth's Table 65.2 lists the same Articles; the full table is in Master notes.

## Two threads

Read the whole chapter with two threads in mind. **Thread one promotes Hindi**: Articles 343, 344 and 351. **Thread two protects other languages and keeps a bridge**: Articles 345 and 347, Articles 350 to 350B, the Eighth Schedule, and the continued use of English through the Official Languages Act 1963. Many examination questions are really about the balance between the two threads.

The year-by-year timeline is in Master notes (Chronology). The Eighth Schedule links Part XVII to the rest of the Constitution: it matters for the Commission (Article 344) and for the enrichment of Hindi (Article 351), as module 6 explains.

### 2. Language of the Union: Articles 343 and 344 and the fifteen-year plan

*Objective:* State the rules in Articles 343 and 344, the Commission and Committee they create, and what happened to the plan.

## Article 343: the official language of the Union

Laxmikanth states these rules for the Union.

1. **Hindi written in the Devanagari script** is the official language of the Union.
2. The numerals for official purposes are the **international form of Indian numerals**, not the Devanagari numerals (bridge B2).
3. For **fifteen years** from the commencement of the Constitution (1950 to 1965) English would **continue** to be used for all the official purposes for which it was used before 1950.
4. Even after fifteen years, **Parliament may by law provide** for the continued use of English for specified purposes.

The fourth rule is the key to later events: the Constitution itself allowed Parliament to keep English beyond 1965. Standard knowledge (verify) on numerals: during the fifteen years the President could authorise the Devanagari form of numerals in addition (proviso to Article 343(2)), and afterwards Parliament may provide for Devanagari numerals for specified purposes (Article 343(3)(b)). So 'Union numerals are never Devanagari' fails; the safe rule is that the default form is the international form of Indian numerals.

## Article 344: a Commission and a Committee

Laxmikanth lists two further provisions, which Table 65.2 places under Article 344.

- At the end of **five years** and again of **ten years** from commencement the **President** should appoint a **Commission** to recommend progressive use of Hindi and restrictions on English; it was to have a chairman and members representing the **Eighth Schedule** languages.
- A **Committee of Parliament** of **30 members** (20 Lok Sabha, 10 Rajya Sabha, elected by proportional representation through the single transferable vote) examines the Commission's recommendations and reports its views to the President.

## What actually happened

In **1955** the President appointed an Official Language Commission under the chairmanship of **B.G. Kher**; it reported to the President in **1956**. A Committee of Parliament constituted in **1957** under **Govind Ballabh Pant** examined the report. The Commission that the Constitution envisaged for **1960** was **not appointed**. As 1965 approached, Parliament used its power under Article 343(3) and passed the **Official Languages Act, 1963** (module 3).

## Why the plan did not run as written

Standard knowledge (verify): many people in non-Hindi-speaking States feared that a sudden switch would disadvantage them in Union jobs and examinations, and agitations in the mid-1960s, notably in Tamil Nadu, showed the strength of the concern. The 1963 Act and its 1967 amendment let English continue beside Hindi.

## Guided example

Statement: 'Under Article 343, Hindi is the national language and English ended in 1965.' Step 1: Article 343 speaks of the 'official language of the Union', not a national language (bridge B1). Step 2: the fifteen years gave English a time-limited place, but Article 343(3) let Parliament extend it. Step 3: Parliament did so in 1963. So the statement is wrong on both parts.

### 3. The Official Languages Act 1963, the 1967 amendment and the Committee on Official Language

*Objective:* State what the 1963 Act and the 1967 amendment provide and describe the Committee of Parliament on Official Language.

## The Official Languages Act, 1963

When the fifteen years ended, Parliament acted under Article 343(3). Laxmikanth: the Act provides for the continued use of **English, in addition to Hindi**, for **all official purposes of the Union** and for the transaction of business in Parliament, and it enables the use of English **indefinitely**, without any time limit. Other provisions in the extract:

- English is used for communication between the Union and the **non-Hindi States**; where Hindi is used between a Hindi State and a non-Hindi State, an English translation must accompany it (module 4).
- Hindi translations of Bills and Acts, and the Governor's power over High Court judgments, are in module 5.
- A **Committee of Parliament on Official Language** is to be constituted after ten years.

## The 1967 amendment

Laxmikanth: the Act was amended in **1967** to make English, in addition to Hindi, **compulsory in certain cases**: (a) resolutions, general orders, rules, notifications, administrative or other reports and press communications issued by the Central Government; (b) reports and official papers laid before Parliament; (c) contracts, agreements, licences, permits and notices issued by the Central Government or its corporations and companies. Standard knowledge (verify): the amendment gave statutory form to the Prime Minister's assurance that English would remain as long as non-Hindi-speaking people wished, so English can be dropped only if the legislatures of all non-Hindi States agree and Parliament accepts; hence the Act is read as a safeguard for non-Hindi States.

## The Committee of Parliament on Official Language

The Committee was to be set up after ten years under the Act and was set up in **1976**. [Source slip: Laxmikanth calls 26 January 1965 the Act's 'promulgation'; it is actually the date its key section came into force (the Act was passed in 1963). Ten years from then ends in January 1975, so the Committee could be set up from 1975, and it was set up in 1976; verify.]

- **Composition**: 30 members, 20 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha, elected by the single transferable vote on a resolution moved with the President's previous sanction and passed by both Houses.
- **Duty**: review progress in the use of Hindi for the Union's official purposes and report to the President, who lays the report before each House, sends it to the State governments and, after considering the States' views, may issue directions.
- **Chairman**: elected by the members; by convention the **Union Home Minister**.
- **Working**: three sub-committees inspect central government offices and take evidence; the secretariat is a subordinate office of the Department of Official Language, Ministry of Home Affairs.

Keep two thirty-member committees apart: the Article 344 Committee examines the Commission's recommendations (Pant, 1957); the 1976 Committee under the Act reviews progress of Hindi. The comparison table is in Master notes.

### 4. Regional languages: Articles 345, 346 and 347

*Objective:* Explain how a State chooses its official language, what the link language is and how a minority language gets recognition.

## Article 345: the State decides

The Constitution does not itself name the official language of any State. Under Article 345 the legislature of a State may adopt **any one or more of the languages in use in the State, or Hindi**, as the official language of that State; until it does so, **English continues**. Laxmikanth's examples (2019; dated) are in Master notes: for instance, Meghalaya, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland adopted English; Gujarat adopted Hindi beside Gujarati and Goa Marathi beside Konkani. A State's choice is **not limited to the languages in the Eighth Schedule**.

[Dated flag: Laxmikanth (2019) calls Jammu and Kashmir a State with Urdu as its language. It became a Union Territory in 2019, and a 2020 law is reported to have made Urdu, Kashmiri, Dogri, Hindi and English its official languages. Verify.]

## Article 346: the link language

The language(s) for the time being authorised for the Union's official purposes (Hindi, with English continued under the Official Languages Act 1963) serve as the **link language** for communication between the Union and the States and between States. In practice English is used with non-Hindi States, and Hindi-to-non-Hindi communications carry an English translation. [Laxmikanth's shorthand '(i.e., English)' oversimplifies; verify the wording of Article 346.] Two or more States may agree to use **Hindi** between themselves instead; Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Bihar are examples.

## Article 347: a language spoken by a section of a State's people

If, on a demand being made, the **President** is satisfied that a **substantial proportion of the population** of a State desires a language spoken by them to be recognised by that State, he may direct that it be officially recognised in that State. Standard knowledge (verify): the direction may apply throughout the State or in a part, for the purpose he specifies. Laxmikanth says the provision protects the linguistic interests of minorities in the States.

## Contrast: Article 345 versus Article 347

| Point | Article 345 | Article 347 |
|---|---|---|
| Who acts | The State legislature | The President |
| Trigger | The legislature's own choice | A demand and a substantial proportion of the population |
| Result | One or more State languages, or Hindi, become the State's official language | Another language is also officially recognised in the State |
| Linked to Eighth Schedule? | No | No |

## Guided example

A State has a large group speaking Language X, which is not in the Eighth Schedule. Can the State make X an official language? Under Article 345 the question is whether X is in use in the State, not whether it is in the Schedule, so yes. Could the speakers instead ask for recognition? Yes: they could make a demand to the President under Article 347, who must be satisfied about the substantial proportion.

### 5. Language of the courts and texts of laws: Articles 348 and 349 and the supporting Acts

*Objective:* State the language rules for the Supreme Court, High Courts and texts of laws and the relaxations allowed by the 1963 and 1973 Acts.

## Article 348: English in the courts and for authoritative texts

Until Parliament provides otherwise, the following are in **English only**: (a) all proceedings in the **Supreme Court** and in every **High Court**; (b) the authoritative texts of all Bills, Acts, ordinances, orders, rules, regulations and bye-laws at the Central and State levels (Laxmikanth).

## Relaxation in the High Courts

The **Governor**, with the **previous consent of the President**, can authorise Hindi or another official language of the State in the **proceedings** of the State's High Court. The Constitution keeps **judgments, decrees and orders** in English; the Official Languages Act, 1963 lets the Governor, with the President's consent, authorise Hindi or the State's official language for them too, **with an English translation**. Laxmikanth's examples: Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and Rajasthan.

## The Supreme Court: English only

Parliament has made **no provision** for Hindi in the Supreme Court, so it hears only those who petition or appeal in English. In 1971 a petitioner insisted on arguing a habeas corpus petition in Hindi; the Court cancelled the petition because its language was English (Laxmikanth). [Post-2024 flag: technology-assisted translation of judgments into regional languages is reported as an initiative; the rule on the language of proceedings is the point to learn; verify.]

## Texts of laws

- A **State legislature** may prescribe any language other than English for its Bills, Acts, ordinances and other texts, but an **English translation** must be published.
- Under the 1963 Act the **Hindi translation** of Acts, ordinances, orders, regulations and bye-laws published under the President's authority is **deemed authoritative**; every Bill introduced in Parliament must carry one.
- The **Authorised Translations (Central Laws) Act, 1973**: a translation in an Eighth Schedule language other than Hindi, published under the President's authority in the Gazette, is deemed the authoritative text **in that language**.

## Article 349

Laxmikanth's Table 65.2 names Article 349 as the **special procedure for enactment of certain laws relating to language**. Standard knowledge (verify): during the first fifteen years no Bill on the language matters of Article 348(1) could be introduced without the President's previous sanction, given only after considering the Commission's report and the Committee's views.

## Language in the legislatures

Laxmikanth refers the reader to the chapters on Parliament and State legislatures (not read here). Standard knowledge (verify): Article 120 lets Parliament do business in Hindi or English, with the mother tongue permitted by the presiding officer; Article 210 is the State counterpart.

## Rule-testing exercise

Rule: 'Courts in Hindi-speaking States work in Hindi.' Edge cases: (1) Supreme Court: English only, whatever the State; (2) High Court proceedings: Hindi only if the Governor authorises it with the President's prior consent; (3) High Court judgments: English unless the Governor, with consent, authorises Hindi under the 1963 Act, and then with an English translation. So the rule needs the words 'with presidential consent' and 'High Court' to stay accurate.

### 6. Special directives (Articles 350 to 351) and the Eighth Schedule

*Objective:* State Articles 350, 350A, 350B and 351 and list the 22 Eighth Schedule languages with the amendments that added them.

## Protection of linguistic minorities

**Article 350**: every aggrieved person has the right to submit a representation for redress of a grievance to any officer or authority of the Union or a State **in any of the languages used in the Union or in the State**. A representation cannot be rejected because it is not in the official language.

**Article 350A**: every State and every local authority in the State should provide **adequate facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage** to children of **linguistic minority groups**. The President can issue directions. Laxmikanth notes that this was added by the **7th Amendment (1956)** on the recommendation of the States Reorganisation Commission (GS-169).

**Article 350B**: the President should appoint a **Special Officer for linguistic minorities** to investigate matters relating to their constitutional safeguards and report to him; reports go before Parliament and to the State governments. Also added by the 7th Amendment. (Standard knowledge: the officer is called the Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities; verify.) Linguistic minorities also hold FRs under Articles 29 and 30 (GS-163 module 7).

## Development of Hindi: Article 351

The Constitution imposes a **duty on the Centre** to promote the spread and development of Hindi as the **lingua franca of the composite culture of India**, enriching it with the forms, style and expressions of **Hindustani** and the other **Eighth Schedule** languages, drawing vocabulary **primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on other languages**. It is a directive to the Centre, not a Fundamental Right (GS-165). Laxmikanth's notes record that in 1976 the Supreme Court declared Tamil Nadu's pension scheme for anti-Hindi agitators unconstitutional (verify).

## The Eighth Schedule

At present (2019, per Laxmikanth) the Eighth Schedule specifies **22 languages**, originally **14**. The 22 are: Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Malayalam, Manipuri, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santhali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu. (Laxmikanth's list prints 'Dogri (Dongri)' and 'Mathili (Maithili)'.)

| Amendment | Year | Languages added | Count added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Originally | 1950 | the original list | 14 |
| 21st | 1967 | Sindhi | 1 |
| 71st | 1992 | Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali | 3 |
| 92nd | 2003 | Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali | 4 |

Arithmetic check: 14 + 1 + 3 + 4 = 22. The **96th Amendment (2011)** added no language: it substituted 'Odia' for 'Oriya'. [Verify that no language has been added after Laxmikanth's 2019 count.] The same additions table, in identical wording, is in **GS-161 module 3** and **GS-172 module 5**.

## Why the Schedule exists

Laxmikanth gives two objectives: (a) members of these languages are to be **represented in the Official Language Commission** (Article 344), and (b) their forms, style and expression are to be used for the **enrichment of Hindi** (Article 351). The Schedule is therefore **not** a list of 'national languages', not the list of State official languages (Article 345 is wider) and not the list of classical languages (module 7).

### 7. Classical language status: criteria, benefits, the list and what changed after 2019

*Objective:* State how classical language status arose, its criteria and benefits, the six languages in Laxmikanth's list and the flagged later change.

## What it is

In **2004** the Government of India decided to create a new category of languages called **classical languages**; in **2006** it laid down the criteria for conferring the status (Laxmikanth; official background dates the antiquity criterion to 2005: verify). It is a **recognition** decided by the Government; it is not an Article of Part XVII and not an entry in the Eighth Schedule.

## Criteria as printed by Laxmikanth (2019)

The status is meant for a language that meets all of these conditions.

- **High antiquity** of its early texts or recorded history over a period of **1,500 to 2,000 years**.
- A **body of ancient literature or texts** that generations of speakers consider a valuable heritage.
- A **literary tradition that is original** and not borrowed from another speech community.
- The classical language and its literature are **distinct from the modern**, so there can be a **discontinuity** between the classical language and its later forms or offshoots.

Dated note (verify): October 2024 reports say the Linguistic Experts Committee revised these criteria: the requirement of an 'original' literary tradition, not borrowed from another speech community, was dropped; knowledge texts (prose as well as poetry) and epigraphical or inscriptional evidence were added; and the wording on the relation between the classical language and its later forms was changed. Use the current notification in answers.

## Benefits

Once a language is declared classical it gets **financial assistance for a centre of excellence** for its study, opens the way for **two major awards for eminent scholars**, and the University Grants Commission can be requested to create professional chairs for classical languages, to begin with in Central Universities.

## The six languages in Laxmikanth's list (as of 2019)

| Language | Year of declaration |
|---|---|
| Tamil | 2004 |
| Sanskrit | 2005 |
| Telugu | 2008 |
| Kannada | 2008 |
| Malayalam | 2013 |
| Odia | 2014 |

## After 2019: flag

**Uncertain; verify before use.** Reports of October 2024 say the Union Cabinet approved classical status for Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali, taking the total from six to eleven. Pali and Prakrit are not in the Eighth Schedule, so classical status does not depend on Schedule membership; check the official notification before writing this in an answer, and answer any 2025-2027 question from the latest official list, not Laxmikanth's 2019 table.

## Three traps

- Classical status is a **Government decision**, not a constitutional amendment.
- It is **not** an Eighth Schedule entry or a State's official language.
- The **year** for each language is easy to swap; learn the table as a pattern.

### 8. Master view: balancing Hindi, English and the regional languages, with rule-testing

*Objective:* Explain the logic of the whole scheme and test five tempting rules against edge cases.

## The logic of the scheme

The Constitution handles language in three layers. **The Union**: Hindi in Devanagari, with English as a time-limited, then extendable, companion (Articles 343, 344, 351). **The States**: State choice (Article 345), the President's recognition of a minority language (Article 347), the link language (Article 346). **The courts and laws**: English by default (Article 348), relaxed for High Courts with presidential consent, kept for the Supreme Court. Safeguards for non-dominant languages: Articles 350 to 350B, 29 and 30. The result is a **compromise**: promote one common language without forcing it, and keep English as a bridge.

## Why the compromise matters in a federation

GS-169: States were later organised largely on linguistic lines (the 7th Amendment, 1956, added Articles 350A and 350B), so language sits close to federal bargaining. Article 351's composite culture echoes GS-166's Fundamental Duty to value the rich heritage of our composite culture (standard knowledge; verify the exact text).

## Amendment and review

Standard knowledge (verify): Part XVII is not among the provisions needing State ratification under Article 368(2), so a special majority suffices (GS-167 module 5). Parliament's power over language rules is real: Articles 343(3) and 348 both say 'until Parliament provides otherwise'.

## Rule-testing exercise

Test each tempting rule against three edge cases from the sources.

1. **'Hindi is the national language.'** Article 343 says 'official language of the Union'; English continues under the 1963 Act; Part XVII has no 'national language'. Rule fails.
2. **'English stopped being an official language in 1965.'** Article 343(3) allowed extension; the 1963 Act allows English indefinitely; the 1967 amendment makes English compulsory in some cases. Rule fails.
3. **'A State's official language must be an Eighth Schedule language.'** Meghalaya, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland adopted English; Article 345 says 'any language in use in the State or Hindi'. Rule fails.
4. **'A High Court can use Hindi if the State wishes.'** The Governor needs the President's previous consent; judgments need an English translation; the Supreme Court has no relaxation. Rule is incomplete.
5. **'Eighth Schedule languages are the classical languages.'** Hindi, Gujarati and Punjabi are in the Schedule but not in Laxmikanth's 2019 list of classical languages (verify the current list); the status is a Government recognition under notified criteria; the reported 2024 additions are said to include languages outside the Schedule. Rule fails.

## A boundary note on the three-language formula

The three-language formula is a school-education policy outside Part XVII and the source supplied, so it is **not taught here**; do not use it to answer a question on Articles 343 to 351. The only language-of-instruction provision in this block is Article 350A.

### 9. Examination application: the 2024 question, traps and Mains frames

*Objective:* Solve the verified 2024 question by reasoning, list the standard traps and outline Mains answers on the language scheme.

## How this topic is asked

The one verified question is PYQ 2024 Prelims Q72, on which languages the 71st Amendment (1992) added to the Eighth Schedule (Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali and Maithili appear as statements). It tests recall of additions by Amendment number, discipline about which language went in which year, and elimination in a combination question.

## Worked reasoning on the 2024 question (analysis only)

The lesson states no official key. Use the table in module 6. Laxmikanth says Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali were added by the **71st Amendment (1992)** and Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali by the **92nd Amendment (2003)**. On that table statements 1, 2 and 3 fit the 71st Amendment, while statement 4 (Maithili) belongs to the 92nd Amendment. So the combination that matches the table is the one containing exactly the first three statements. Check the answer options against that reasoning and verify the official key independently.

## Eight traps to expect

- **Number swaps**: 21st (Sindhi), 71st (Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali), 92nd (Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali), 96th (Odia for Oriya, no addition).
- **'National language'** claims: Part XVII declares none.
- **'English ended in 1965'**: Article 343(3), the 1963 Act and the 1967 amendment say otherwise.
- **Two thirty-member committees**: Article 344 (examines the Commission's recommendations) versus 1976 (reviews progress of Hindi).
- **'Eighth Schedule = State official languages'**: Article 345 is wider.
- **'Supreme Court in Hindi'**: English only.
- **'High Court judgments in Hindi'**: Governor, President's consent and an English translation (1963 Act).
- **'Classical status in the Schedule'**: a separate recognition (module 7).

## Mains frames (outlines only)

**Examine the constitutional scheme of the official language of the Union (15 marks).** Article 343 (Hindi, Devanagari, international numerals, English for 15 years, Parliament's power to extend); Article 344 (Kher, Pant); the 1963 Act and 1967 amendment; the 1976 Committee; Article 351; evaluation: a compromise that protects non-Hindi States but leaves Hindi promotion incomplete; conclusion on federal balance.

**Discuss the safeguards for linguistic minorities (10 marks).** Articles 347, 350, 350A, 350B; Articles 29 and 30 (FRs); the Eighth Schedule's role in the Commission; limits: mostly directives and administrative devices.

**Critically examine the language policy (15 marks).** For: accommodates diversity, State choice, English as a bridge. Against: Hindi promotion, uneven access to Union jobs, pressure to join the Eighth Schedule, English in higher courts. Use the reported 2024 classical-language additions only with a verify note.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Official language: the language used for the official work of a government; Part XVII speaks of the official language of the Union and of States.
- Link language: the language used for communication between the Union and States and between States; the language(s) for the time being authorised for the Union's official purposes (Hindi, with English continued under the 1963 Act), in practice English with non-Hindi States (Article 346).
- Devanagari: the script in which Hindi is written for the Union's official purposes (Article 343).
- International form of Indian numerals: the numerals prescribed for the Union's official purposes (Article 343).
- Official Language Commission (Article 344): a body the President appoints at five and ten years to recommend progressive use of Hindi; Kher Commission 1955-56.
- Committee of Parliament (Article 344): 30 members (20 Lok Sabha, 10 Rajya Sabha), single transferable vote; examined the Kher Commission report (Pant Committee, 1957).
- Committee of Parliament on Official Language (1976, Official Languages Act 1963): 30 members; reviews progress in the use of Hindi; reports to the President; the Home Minister chairs by convention.
- Official Languages Act, 1963: allows English indefinitely, in addition to Hindi, for official purposes of the Union and Parliament's business; amended in 1967 to make English compulsory in certain cases.
- Eighth Schedule: the list of 22 languages (as of 2019) used for representation in the Official Language Commission and for enrichment of Hindi.
- Lingua franca: a language widely used for communication across groups; Article 351 aims at Hindi becoming the lingua franca of the composite culture of India.
- Classical language: a recognition category created in 2004; criteria laid down in 2006; a Government decision, not a constitutional provision.

### Notes: Classical language status: facts and traps

- Created by a Government of India decision in 2004; criteria laid down in 2006 (Laxmikanth; official background dates the antiquity criterion to 2005; verify).
- Criteria as printed by Laxmikanth (2019; reportedly revised in 2024, see module 7; verify): 1,500-2,000 years of early texts or recorded history; a body of ancient literature valued as heritage; an original literary tradition; distinction between classical and modern forms.
- Benefits: centre of excellence, two awards for eminent scholars, UGC professional chairs (to begin with in Central Universities).
- Laxmikanth's list (2019): Tamil 2004, Sanskrit 2005, Telugu 2008, Kannada 2008, Malayalam 2013, Odia 2014.
- Reported 2024 additions: Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, Bengali (uncertain; verify).
- Trap: classical status is not an Eighth Schedule entry and not a constitutional provision.

### Notes: Frequently confused

- Official language versus national language: Part XVII uses the former only.
- Article 345 versus Article 347: State legislature's choice versus President's recognition on demand.
- Article 344 Committee versus the 1976 Committee on Official Language.
- Eighth Schedule versus State official languages versus classical languages: three different lists.
- High Court proceedings versus High Court judgments: different rules and different consent requirements.
- Article 350A (mother-tongue instruction for linguistic minority children at primary stage) versus Article 29 (right to conserve language) and Article 30 (minority educational institutions).
