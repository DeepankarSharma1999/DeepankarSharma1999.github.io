### Bridge: Consult, recommend, approve, control: words that decide how strong a power is

Law uses different verbs for how much say a body has, and an examiner tests the exact verb. Learn five rungs, from weakest to strongest.

1. **Inform**: the body must be told. It has no say in the decision.
2. **Consult**: the decision-maker must ask for the body's views and listen, but is free to decide otherwise. Consultation is not consent.
3. **Recommend**: the body gives a formal proposal. If the law says the recommendation is **mandatory** or **prior**, the decision-maker must obtain it before acting; whether the decision-maker must also follow it is a separate question that courts and commentators have argued about.
4. **Approve** (or consent): the body's agreement is needed for the action to go ahead. Without approval the action stops.
5. **Control** (or own, manage, enforce): the body itself holds the power and makes the decision.

A **Gram Sabha** is the assembly of all registered voters of a village (GS-195 bridge B2). It is different from the **Gram Panchayat**, the elected council of the village. When you read a clause, underline the verb and place it on the ladder before judging an option.

### Bridge: How a community becomes a Scheduled Tribe, and what PVTG means (flagged)

This bridge is standard knowledge, not in the extracts; verify each point at the official source before quoting.

A **Scheduled Tribe (ST)** is a community listed under **Article 342**: the **President**, by public notification and, for a State, after consulting the Governor, specifies the tribes or tribal communities (or parts of them) to be treated as Scheduled Tribes in relation to that State or Union Territory, and **Parliament** by law can include or exclude communities. Two consequences matter for Prelims. First, the declaring authority is the President, not the Governor; the Governor is consulted. Second, the lists are made **State by State**, so a community may be a Scheduled Tribe in one State and not in another (this is the idea tested in the 2024 PYQ).

A **PVTG** (Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group, earlier called Primitive Tribal Group) is a sub-category of Scheduled Tribes identified because the groups are especially vulnerable. Criteria commonly cited in Ministry of Tribal Affairs material: a pre-agricultural level of technology, a stagnant or declining population, extremely low literacy and a subsistence-level economy. The count commonly cited is **75 groups in 18 States and one Union Territory** (the Andaman and Nicobar Islands); Irular and Konda Reddi are in the commonly cited list. Verify the current list. Keep PVTG, Scheduled Tribe, Scheduled Area and tribal area (GS-198 bridge B1) as four separate ideas.

### 2. The two Schedules in short, and the Tribes Advisory Council in depth

*Objective:* Recall the Fifth and Sixth Schedules in eight lines and compare the Tribes Advisory Council, the Gram Sabha and the district council as three different bodies.

## Recall table (full treatment and edge cases: GS-198 module 8)

| Point | Fifth Schedule | Sixth Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Area | Scheduled Areas declared by the President, consulting the Governor | Tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, constituted as autonomous districts |
| Executive power | The State's extends; Governor reports annually to the President; Centre directs the State | Autonomous districts do not fall outside the State's executive authority |
| Special body | Tribes Advisory Council (advisory) | District and regional councils (elected; legislative, judicial, executive and financial powers) |
| Law | Governor directs on Acts and makes regulations; President assents to regulations | Council laws on specified matters need the Governor's assent |

## The Tribes Advisory Council (TAC) in depth

**From Laxmikanth (p. 898).** Each State having Scheduled Areas has to establish a TAC to advise on the welfare and advancement of Scheduled Tribes. Laxmikanth gives it **20 members, three-fourths of them representatives of Scheduled Tribes in the State legislative assembly**, which is a simplification: the Fifth Schedule (para 4(1); standard knowledge, verify) says **not more than twenty** members, of whom, as nearly as may be, three-fourths are ST representatives in the State legislative assembly. Learn the constitutional wording. A similar council can be established in a State having Scheduled Tribes but no Scheduled Areas **if the President so directs**. The Governor must **consult** it before making regulations for a Scheduled Area (p. 899).

**Flagged, standard knowledge from the text of the Fifth Schedule (verify):** the Schedule is commonly read as allowing the TAC to advise on matters **referred to it by the Governor**, with the Governor making rules about its composition and working. If that reading is right, the TAC has no power to set its own agenda or to bind anyone.

## Three bodies compared

| Body | Who sits | What it does | Strength on the ladder (bridge B1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tribes Advisory Council | Mostly ST members of the State assembly | Advises on ST welfare; consulted on regulations | Consult |
| Gram Sabha (under PESA) | All registered voters of the village | Approves plans, identifies beneficiaries, protects customs; consultation and recommendation on land and minor minerals | Consult to control (module 4) |
| District council (Sixth Schedule) | Not more than 30 members, not more than 4 of them nominated (Schedule text; Laxmikanth simplifies to 26 elected and 4 nominated; the Bodoland Territorial Council is larger, GS-198 module 6) | Makes laws, runs courts, manages services, collects revenue | Control, with the Governor's assent |

## Reading the debate

Analysis, not source: a council drawn from the State assembly is a body of legislators, not of villages, so its voice reaches the Governor at State level, while PESA created a body at the village. The TAC advises; it does not approve. A Prelims option that gives the TAC lawmaking power fails on this ground; an option that lets the TAC veto a Governor's regulation also fails, because the source says the Governor makes regulations **after consulting** it and the regulation is completed by the **President's assent**.

## Edge tests on 'the TAC is the tribal parliament'

Edge 1: it is advisory. Edge 2: it can exist in a State without Scheduled Areas by presidential direction. Edge 3: it is not a Sixth Schedule body; the Sixth Schedule has councils with powers.

### 3. PESA I: why an extension Act, and how it changes Part IX

*Objective:* Explain why Part IX needed an extension Act for Scheduled Areas and compare Part IX with PESA row by row.

## The problem

Part IX (Panchayats, inserted by the 73rd Amendment) builds a standard three-tier structure for every State. But Part IX **does not apply by its own force to Scheduled Areas** (Article 243M, listed in Laxmikanth's Table 38.2). The extract does not state a reason for the exemption. Analysis: Laxmikanth notes (p. 898) that the whole of the normal administrative machinery of a State is not extended to Scheduled Areas, so a uniform Part IX, designed for ordinary villages, would not fit communities with customary institutions. Parliament may extend Part IX to such areas with exceptions and modifications (the clause number, commonly Article 243M(4)(b), is standard knowledge: verify). It did so by the **Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996**, known as **PESA** or the **Extension Act**; assent and commencement were on **24 December 1996** (p. 855 and note 7). Laxmikanth (2019) says all ten States with Fifth Schedule areas enacted compliance legislation. Sixth Schedule areas are not covered: they are exempt from Part IX and have district councils.

## Seven objectives (p. 841)

Extend Part IX with modifications; give self-rule to the bulk of the tribal population; make the Gram Sabha the nucleus of village governance with participatory democracy; evolve an administrative framework consistent with traditional practices; safeguard tribal customs; empower panchayats with powers suited to tribal needs; and stop higher-level panchayats from taking the powers of the Gram Sabha or lower panchayats.

## Part IX against PESA

| Feature | Part IX (73rd Amendment) | PESA 1996 |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | General, but not Scheduled or tribal areas | Fifth Schedule areas only, by extension with modifications |
| Law's basis | State law within Part IX | State law must be in consonance with customary law, social and religious practices and traditional management of community resources |
| Village | A village specified by the Governor by public notification, or a group of villages (note 3) | A habitation or group of habitations, a hamlet or group of hamlets comprising a community that manages its affairs by traditions and customs |
| Gram Sabha | Exercises such powers as the State legislature determines (a voluntary provision) | Has defined competences: safeguard customs, approve plans, identify beneficiaries, and more (module 4) |
| Seat reservation | For SC and ST in proportion to population | In proportion to population, but ST seats not less than one-half |
| Chairperson posts | Reservation for SC and ST as State legislature provides; intermediate and district chairpersons elected indirectly | All chairperson seats at all levels reserved for ST |
| Nomination | Not a feature | State may nominate STs without representation at intermediate or district level, up to one-tenth of members to be elected |
| District level | Standard three-tier design | State to endeavour to follow the Sixth Schedule pattern |
| Inconsistent old laws | Continued for one year after commencement | Inconsistent provisions cease one year after assent; existing panchayats continue to the end of their term unless dissolved |

## What the table tells you

PESA changes **who** governs (ST majorities in seats and all chairs), **what law** governs (customary practice first), and **how strong** the village assembly is (module 4). The Gram Sabha moves from a body whose powers are left to the State to a body with named competences.

### 4. PESA II: a ladder of Gram Sabha powers, from consultation to control

*Objective:* Place each PESA power on the strength ladder and use it to reject options that overstate the Gram Sabha.

## Ladder

Use bridge GS-199-B1: inform, consult, recommend, approve, control. The PESA features (Laxmikanth pp. 841-843; full list in GS-195 module 7) fall on different rungs; where a feature is a competence rather than a rung, the right column gives the nearest rung.

| PESA provision (feature number) | Rung |
|---|---|
| Safeguard traditions, cultural identity, community resources and customary dispute resolution (4) | Control (protect customs and resources) |
| Approve plans, programmes and projects before the village panchayat implements them (5) | Approve |
| Identify beneficiaries of poverty and other programmes (5) | Approve (identify beneficiaries) |
| Certify utilisation of funds by the village panchayat (6) | Approve (certify use of funds) |
| Consultation before land acquisition for development projects and before resettlement and rehabilitation; planning and implementation coordinated at State level (9) | **Consult** |
| Planning and management of minor water bodies entrusted to panchayats at the appropriate level (10) | Control |
| Recommendations of the Gram Sabha or panchayat mandatory for prospecting licence or mining lease for minor minerals (11) | **Recommend (mandatory)** |
| Prior recommendation mandatory for grant of concession for exploitation of minor minerals by auction (12) | **Recommend (prior, mandatory)** |
| Specific powers the State must give: regulate intoxicants; own minor forest produce; prevent land alienation and restore unlawfully alienated tribal land; manage village markets; control money lending to STs; control institutions and functionaries in social sectors; control local plans and their resources including tribal sub-plans (13) | Control |
| Safeguards so higher-level panchayats do not take the powers of the Gram Sabha or lower panchayats (14) | Control (protect the Gram Sabha's own powers) |

## Four rules tested against the text

**Rule A: 'The Gram Sabha has a veto over land acquisition.'** The source says the Gram Sabha or panchayat shall be **consulted**, and project planning and implementation are coordinated at State level. Consultation is not consent. Edge: the 2013 land law requires prior consent in Scheduled Areas (module 6, flagged), but that is not the PESA clause. Verdict: false as a statement of PESA.

**Rule B: 'The Gram Sabha controls all minerals.'** The clauses speak of **minor** minerals only. Major minerals are governed by mining law (Union Entry 54 and State Entry 23 appear in Appendix II; the current law is to be verified). Verdict: false.

**Rule C: 'PESA gives ownership of forests to the Gram Sabha.'** The text says ownership of **minor forest produce** (item 7 of the Eleventh Schedule), not of forests as land. Verdict: false.

**Rule D: 'PESA powers operate automatically.'** The text says a State Legislature **shall ensure** that panchayats and Gram Sabhas are endowed with these powers; they take effect through State law, which is why compliance legislation was needed (module 5). Verdict: incomplete: the powers depend on State law.

A caution on 'mandatory recommendation': the extract says the recommendation is mandatory to be obtained; how far the State must follow it has been argued (verify).

### 5. PESA III: implementation, the gap and the debate

*Objective:* Explain why PESA's powers depend on State law and List entries, relate the general panchayat problems to Scheduled Areas, and list the debates with flagged items marked.

## What the sources say about compliance

Laxmikanth (p. 841, 2019) says all ten States with Fifth Schedule areas enacted compliance legislation by amending their Panchayati Raj Acts; State PESA rules were notified at different times, some only recently (verify the current status). PESA itself says inconsistent State law ceases one year after assent (feature 16), and asks States to follow the Sixth Schedule pattern at district level (feature 15). Compliance with the Act in law is not the same as the Gram Sabha holding the power in practice.

## Why State law and the Lists matter

Most PESA powers sit in State List entries or nearby ones (Appendix II): intoxicating liquors (State Entry 8, excise Entry 51), land (Entry 18), markets and fairs (Entry 28), money-lending (Entry 30), mines and minerals (State Entry 23, Union Entry 54) and forests (Concurrent Entry 17A). A State legislature can endow panchayats with powers in its own entries; where a Union law occupies the field, as with mining, the Gram Sabha's power depends on how State and Union laws fit (verify the present mining law). State land laws against alienation rest on the land entry (GS-198 module 3).

## The general panchayat diagnosis, applied with care

Laxmikanth reports the Second ARC and the Ministry of Panchayati Raj on why panchayats underperform. The extract does not separate Scheduled Areas, so applying it to PESA areas is analysis.

| Problem (p. 847-848) | Likely PESA meaning |
|---|---|
| Functions, funds and functionaries not devolved | Named powers on paper; staff and funds sit with the State |
| Excessive bureaucratic control; tied funds | Sarpanches wait for approvals; village plans bend to scheme rules |
| Gram Sabha status: many State Acts do not spell out powers, procedures or penalties | The core PESA weakness: a power without a procedure |
| Parallel bodies; poor infrastructure and training | Missions bypass the Gram Sabha; weak records |

## Debates (flagged, not in the extracts)

Commentary outside the extracts has argued that some State laws and rules restate PESA in words while leaving Gram Sabha powers subject to other State laws on land, forest, mining and excise, and that State rules were framed at different times and after long delays. Verify with Ministry of Panchayati Raj reports and academic studies before using any specific claim. Post-2024 developments (State rules, Ministry campaigns) are flagged verify.

## A balanced position for Mains

PESA is a rare Indian law that places a village assembly at the centre and gives customary law priority. Its limits are mostly implementation limits: dependence on State legislation, procedure, records and officials. Reform ideas that follow from the diagnosis: spell out Gram Sabha procedures, protect the Gram Sabha from higher-tier takeover (feature 14), train staff, and make the 'recommendation' clauses binding or reasoned (an idea to verify against current legal opinion).

### 6. Land, forests and displacement: the ladder of protections

*Objective:* Define alienation, displacement, resettlement and rehabilitation and list the protections for tribal land and forests, separating sourced rungs from flagged ones.

## Four words

**Alienation** means the transfer of land from tribal owners to others, often by sale, mortgage or fraud. **Displacement** means forced removal of people from their homes or lands, typically by projects such as dams, mines or roads. **Resettlement** is the physical relocation of the displaced; **rehabilitation** is restoring livelihoods, services and social life after relocation. The 2025 Mains question asks whether tribal development centres on displacement and rehabilitation; the answer begins by seeing that these are one axis among several.

## The ladder of protections

| Rung | What it does | Sourcing |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Governor's regulation (Fifth Schedule) | Prohibit or restrict transfer of land by or among STs, regulate allotment, regulate money-lending; President's assent needed | Laxmikanth p. 899; GS-198 module 3 |
| 2. Sixth Schedule council laws | Laws on land, forests, village administration; regulation of money-lending and trading by non-tribals; Governor's assent | Laxmikanth pp. 900-901 |
| 3. Article 371A and 371G | Acts of Parliament on customary law and ownership and transfer of land do not apply unless the State Assembly decides (Nagaland, Mizoram) | Laxmikanth pp. 811, 816; GS-200 |
| 4. PESA features 9, 11, 12, 13 | Consultation before acquisition; recommendation for minor minerals; power to prevent alienation and restore unlawfully alienated land | Laxmikanth pp. 842-843 |
| 5. Forest Rights Act 2006 (flagged) | Recognises individual and community forest rights, with a role for the Gram Sabha; the Ministry of Tribal Affairs is the nodal ministry (standard; 2021 PYQ) | Not in the extracts; verify |
| 6. Land acquisition law of 2013 (flagged) | Acquisition in Scheduled Areas only as a last resort; prior consent of the Gram Sabha, Panchayat or Autonomous District Council required (Section 41; verify) | Not in the extracts; verify |
| 7. Judicial decisions (flagged) | Samatha (1997) on transfer of land in Scheduled Areas to non-tribals; the Niyamgiri case (2013) on the Gram Sabha's role in a bauxite proposal | Not in the extracts; verify |

GS-198 module 3 also gives Article 19(5) on restrictions on movement and settlement for the protection of Scheduled Tribes.

## Where the ladder fails (analysis)

- **Enforcement**: protection on paper depends on records, officials and tribunals.
- **Diverging interests**: Scheduled Areas hold forests and minerals, so the same area is a rights zone and a resource zone. The Union and State gain revenue from projects while the affected community bears the cost.
- **Consultation without consent**: PESA's consultation clause (rung 4) may not prevent a project; the 2013 land law's prior-consent rule (rung 6, Section 41, flagged) is the stronger layer, and the forest and judicial rungs are flagged because the extracts do not cover them.

NCERT adds a caution: special provisions for north-eastern tribal communities have not been able to stem alienation and insurgency in parts of the region (p. 21). The honest reading is that constitutional design is necessary but not sufficient.

## Beyond displacement

Other axes of tribal development: identity and customs, political voice, resource rights, welfare delivery, protection of PVTGs, and conflict. A strong answer states why displacement matters, then shows what it leaves out.


### 7. Autonomous councils: powers, dependence and the live debates

*Objective:* Explain the delegated nature of Sixth Schedule autonomy and discuss finance, the Governor's reach, non-tribal safeguards and the flagged current debates.

## The structure and the Governor's reach (recap; details in GS-198 modules 5 to 7)

Tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram are autonomous districts with district councils: the Schedule text is commonly read as not more than thirty members, not more than four nominated (Laxmikanth's 26 plus 4 is a simplification), and the Bodoland Territorial Council is larger, up to 46 with 40 elected and 6 nominated (verify; GS-198 module 6). The Governor organises districts, nominates members, assents to council laws, may dissolve a council on a commission's recommendation and decides royalty disputes, which is why the autonomy is called delegated self-rule. The Sixth Schedule to the Constitution (Amendment) Act, 1995 (Parliament, for Assam) made it mandatory for the Governor to consult the North Cachar Hills and Karbi Anglong Autonomous Councils when using his discretionary powers.

## Money

Councils can raise land revenue and specified taxes, receive a share of mineral royalties (the 1988 Act set a time-limit for handing over the share) and benefit from Article 275(1) grants. Analysis: the own-revenue base is narrow, so councils depend on grants and on State releases. The Punchhi terms of reference (p. 362-363) asked the Commission to look at effective devolution to Panchayati Raj institutions and local bodies **including Autonomous Bodies under the Sixth Schedule** within a specified period; the problem was already on the national agenda.

## Live debates

1. **Real autonomy against Governor control.** NCERT's framing: demands for autonomy are political and are resolved by negotiation; autonomy is not secession.
2. **Non-tribals inside council areas.** The 2003 Bodoland Act provided safeguards for non-tribals in the area (GS-198 module 7). How to balance tribal protection with rights of other residents is a recurring tension.
3. **Settlements as a tool.** The 2003 Bodoland settlement followed a Memorandum of Settlement with the Bodo Liberation Tigers on 10 February 2003. Whether settlements deliver lasting peace and development is a Mains evaluation point.
4. **Other autonomous arrangements.** Part IX exempts the hill areas of Manipur (district councils) and Darjeeling district (Gorkha Hill Council) as well as Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram (p. 836). Article 371C gives Manipur's Hill Areas Committee a role (GS-200).

## Flagged post-extract items (verify each)

The 2020 Bodo accord; a constitutional amendment bill introduced in 2019 to strengthen autonomous councils with State Election Commission-type and finance provisions (status to be verified); a reported 2024 agreement involving Tripura's tribal council area; and the demand for Sixth Schedule status in Ladakh (GS-198 module 9). Do not give details beyond verified sources.

### 8. Design families, money and the tribal development debate

*Objective:* Compare the Constitution's designs for tribal self-governance and frame the development debate (equity, displacement, autonomy) with flagged policy items.

## Four design families

| Family | Idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised administration | The State administers, the Governor and the President adapt and supervise | Fifth Schedule, Tribes Advisory Council |
| Delegated self-government | Elected councils with law, courts and revenue | Sixth Schedule district councils |
| Customary-law shield | Parliamentary laws on customs, justice and land ownership apply only if the State Assembly agrees | Article 371A (Nagaland), 371G (Mizoram) |
| Village self-rule | A village assembly with named powers inside the panchayat system | PESA 1996 within Part IX |

Separate institutions also exist: a committee of tribal-area MLAs in Assam's Assembly (Article 371B) and a Hill Areas Committee in Manipur (Article 371C) (Laxmikanth ch. 37; GS-200).

## The money line

Article 275(1) provides **specific grants** for the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and for raising the level of administration of Scheduled Areas, on the Finance Commission's recommendation (GS-193 module 6). PESA feature 13 asks that panchayats control local plans and their resources, including tribal sub-plans. Flagged: the Tribal Sub-Plan strategy, PVTG schemes such as PM-JANMAN, and other tribal programmes are not in the extracts; verify names, years and outlays before using them.

## Framing the debate: five lenses

The 2026 Mains question asks whether tribal development policy reflects aspirations for socially grounded, equity-based governance. Use five lenses:

1. **Recognition**: status, customs and language (Article 342, customary law).
2. **Representation**: Tribes Advisory Council, district councils, reservation in panchayats.
3. **Resource rights**: land, forests, minerals (module 6).
4. **Delivery**: grants and schemes (Article 275(1), flagged schemes).
5. **Protection from harm**: displacement, alienation, conflict and the special case of PVTGs.

For each lens give one constitutional instrument, one gap and one reform.

## Two philosophies

Standard knowledge, flagged: the post-independence debate is often described as assimilation against isolation, with Nehru's tribal Panchsheel (articulated in the 1950s; dates vary, verify) proposing development along the community's own genius, with protection of land and forest rights. NCERT's conclusion supplies the modern view: national unity cannot be built by streamlining differences, and forced unity breeds alienation.

## Balanced judgement

Strengths: three distinct constitutional designs, village-level legal rights, special grants. Gaps: enforcement, State dependence, conflict between resource extraction and rights, uneven councils, thin data in the extracts. A fair answer says the policy framework **recognises** tribal aspirations in design but **realises** them unevenly.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Scheduled Area: an area declared by the President under the Fifth Schedule; Tribal area: an autonomous district under the Sixth Schedule in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura or Mizoram.
- Tribes Advisory Council: advisory body for ST welfare; not more than twenty members, about three-fourths ST members of the State assembly (Laxmikanth simplifies to 20; verify the Fifth Schedule text); consulted by the Governor before regulations.
- Gram Sabha: the assembly of all registered voters of a village; under PESA the nucleus of village governance in Scheduled Areas.
- PESA: the Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996, extending Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas with modifications.
- Alienation: transfer of tribal land to others; displacement: forced removal by projects; resettlement: relocation; rehabilitation: restoring livelihoods.
- Scheduled Tribe (flagged): a community notified under Article 342; PVTG (flagged): a vulnerability sub-category of Scheduled Tribes.
- Delegated autonomy: self-rule whose powers come from the Constitution and remain subject to the Governor and the State's executive authority.

### Notes: Part IX against PESA

- PESA modifies Part IX for Fifth Schedule areas: customary law first, a stronger Gram Sabha and ST control of seats and chairs.

### Notes: PESA powers on the strength ladder

- Read the verb: consult is weaker than recommend, which is weaker than approve, which is weaker than control.

### Notes: Three bodies compared

- Tribes Advisory Council, Gram Sabha and district council differ in membership, power and strength.

### Notes: Ladder of protections for tribal land and forests

- Governor's regulation (Fifth Schedule); President's assent.
- Sixth Schedule council laws and regulation of money-lending and trade by non-tribals; Governor's assent.
- Article 371A and 371G: customary law and land ownership protected unless the State Assembly decides otherwise.
- PESA features 9, 11, 12 and 13: consultation, recommendation and the power to prevent alienation and restore land.
- Land acquisition law of 2013, Section 41 (flagged): acquisition in Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas only as a last resort, with prior consent of the Gram Sabha, Panchayat or Autonomous District Council; stronger than PESA's consultation (verify).
- Flagged: Forest Rights Act 2006; land acquisition law of 2013; Samatha (1997) and Niyamgiri (2013): verify before quoting.
- Gaps: enforcement, State dependence, consultation without consent, project pressure on resource-rich areas.

- 1960-61: Dhebar Commission; 2002-04: Bhuria Commission (GS-198).

- 24 December 1996: PESA assent and commencement.
- 2006: Forest Rights Act (flagged).
- 2013: land acquisition law (flagged).

### Notes: Exceptions to neat rules

- PESA applies to Fifth Schedule areas and does not apply to Sixth Schedule tribal areas.
- Consultation under PESA is not consent; recommendation for minor minerals concerns only minor minerals.
- The Tribes Advisory Council can exist in a State without Scheduled Areas by presidential direction.
- A Scheduled Tribe may be listed in one State and not in another (flagged).
- Part IX is not applicable to Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram, the hill areas of Manipur with district councils, and Darjeeling district with its Gorkha Hill Council.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Gram Sabha versus Gram Panchayat.
- Consult versus consent versus recommend.
- Tribes Advisory Council (advisory) versus district council (legislative).
- Scheduled Tribe (status) versus Scheduled Area (place) versus PVTG (vulnerability category).
- Minor forest produce (PESA ownership) versus forest land and major minerals (not covered by PESA text).
- Fifth Schedule Governor's regulation (President assents) versus Sixth Schedule council law (Governor assents).
