### Bridge: What an Article, Part, Schedule and amendment are

## Four words you will meet in every line

The **Constitution** is one long document. It is divided into large sections called **Parts** (for example Part III is Fundamental Rights, Part IV is Directive Principles, Part IX is the Panchayats, Part XX is Amendment of the Constitution). Inside the Parts are numbered clauses called **Articles** (Article 21 is the right to life, Article 368 is the amending power). At the back are **Schedules**: lists and tables that the Articles refer to. The Eighth Schedule lists recognised languages, the Ninth protects certain laws from challenge, the Tenth holds the anti-defection rules, the Eleventh lists panchayat functions and the Twelfth lists municipal functions.

An **amendment** is a formal change to this text by addition, variation or repeal, made by the procedure the Constitution itself lays down. A successful change is passed as a **Constitutional Amendment Act** with an ordinal number and a year, such as the "Forty-Second Amendment Act, 1976". The numbers run in sequence, so a higher number is always later.

Do not confuse an amendment with **interpretation**: a court can change what a clause is understood to mean without changing a single word. NCERT calls this an informal amendment, and it matters later when you meet the basic structure doctrine.

### Bridge: How a Bill becomes law, very briefly

## How an ordinary Bill becomes law (very briefly)

A **Bill** is a draft law. An ordinary Bill is introduced in either House of Parliament (the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha), debated, and must be passed by both Houses by a **simple majority**, meaning more than half of the members present and voting. If the two Houses disagree, an ordinary Bill can go to a **joint sitting** of both Houses. Once passed, it goes to the President, who gives **assent**, and it becomes an **Act**. Money Bills and financial Bills follow special rules; GS-179 covers legislative procedure in depth, so do not try to master it today.

The reason to learn this much now is contrast. A constitutional amendment Bill follows the same broad route (introduced in Parliament, passed by both Houses, then presidential assent) but with a tougher voting rule, no joint sitting, and in some cases approval by state legislatures. Laxmikanth notes that, apart from the special majority, an amendment Bill is passed like an ordinary Bill: the ordinary route with extra locks added.

### 1. Orientation: why and how a living Constitution changes

*Objective:* Explain why the Constitution is both flexible and rigid, and map the topic.

## Why the Constitution must be changeable

NCERT starts from a puzzle. The Soviet Union had four constitutions in 74 years and France several republics, yet India still works under the Constitution adopted on 26 November 1949 and in force from 26 January 1950. The answer is not that nothing needed changing. The framers knew no document can foresee everything, so they allowed change, and the courts and political practice interpreted it flexibly. That is why the Constitution is called a **living document**.

## Flexible and rigid at once

A **flexible** constitution is easy to change; a **rigid** one is hard to change. Laxmikanth says the Indian Constitution is neither as easy to amend as Britain's nor as difficult as the American one: it is a synthesis. NCERT explains the design logic in four reasons:

- mistakes and gaps will appear and should be easy to fix;
- some provisions were temporary and could be altered once a new Parliament was elected;
- a federal polity cannot let the Centre alone change the rights and powers of states, so states must have a say on those matters;
- some features are so central that they must be harder to touch.

These reasons produced **three procedures** (module 3). Nehru, quoted by Laxmikanth, said a rigid and permanent constitution would stop the growth of a living people.

1. Article 368 and how a Bill amends the Constitution (module 2).

### 2. Article 368: the procedure step by step

*Objective:* List the eight steps of the Article 368 procedure and compute a special majority.

## Article 368 in one sentence

Article 368, in Part XX, says Parliament may, in exercise of its **constituent power**, amend any provision of the Constitution by addition, variation or repeal, following the procedure in that article. "Constituent power" means the power to make or change the Constitution itself, as opposed to ordinary law-making power.

## The procedure, step by step (Laxmikanth)

1. **Initiation**: only by introducing a Bill in either House of Parliament. State legislatures cannot initiate an amendment.
2. **Who introduces it**: a minister or a private member; no prior permission of the President is needed.
3. **Special majority in each House**: a majority of the *total membership* of the House **and** a majority of two-thirds of the members *present and voting*.
4. **Each House separately**: if the Houses disagree, there is **no joint sitting**; the Bill fails.
5. **Ratification, only if the Bill touches federal provisions**: legislatures of half of the states must approve it by a simple majority of members present and voting.
6. **Presidential assent**: after passage and any needed ratification the Bill goes to the President.
7. **The President must give assent**: he can neither withhold it nor return the Bill for reconsideration. This was made obligatory by the **24th Amendment (1971)**.
8. Once assented to, the Bill becomes an Act and the Constitution stands amended.

## Working out the special majority

NCERT gives a worked case. If 247 members are present and all vote, an ordinary Bill needs 124 votes. An amendment Bill in a House of 545 needs at least 273 votes (majority of total membership) *and* two-thirds of those voting. If 400 vote, two-thirds of 400 is about 267, so 273 is the binding figure; if 450 vote, two-thirds is 300, which becomes the binding figure. You must satisfy **both** tests, so the larger number applies.

"Total membership" counts all seats, including vacancies and absentees.

## Why this design

NCERT reads it as consensus-building: a party with a thin majority can pass ordinary laws but must win over some opposition parties to amend the Constitution. No outside body or referendum is needed, so amendment power rests with **elected representatives** (parliamentary sovereignty).

### 3. Three kinds of amendment and what needs the states

*Objective:* Classify a given constitutional change into simple majority, special majority, or special majority plus state ratification.

## The three kinds of amendment

Laxmikanth states that the Constitution can be amended in three ways.

| Kind | Rule | What it covers (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| By simple majority of Parliament | Same as an ordinary law; outside Article 368; not treated as an "amendment" under Article 368 | Admission, formation and boundary or name changes of states; creation or abolition of state legislative councils; Second Schedule salaries and allowances; quorum; rules of procedure; citizenship; elections to Parliament and legislatures; delimitation; Union territories; Fifth and Sixth Schedules |
| By special majority of Parliament | Majority of total membership plus two-thirds of those present and voting, in each House | Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and all other provisions not in the other two lists |
| By special majority plus ratification by half the states | As above, then half of the state legislatures approve by simple majority | See below |

## What needs ratification by the states

These are the federal provisions:

- election of the President and its manner;
- extent of the executive power of the Union and the states;
- the Supreme Court and High Courts;
- distribution of legislative powers between the Union and the states;
- the Goods and Services Tax Council (added by the 101st Amendment);
- any of the lists in the Seventh Schedule;
- representation of states in Parliament;
- Article 368 itself.

Notice the logic: each item protects the Union-state balance or the structure of the Union-level institutions. Once half the states consent, the formality is complete; silent states do not matter, and there is no time limit for the states to respond.

## Tests you can apply

NCERT's exercise asks you to tick the conditions for specific changes. Apply the lists: a change to citizenship rules or to state boundaries is a simple-majority matter; a change to freedom of religion needs a special majority; a change to the Union List needs special majority plus states. A provision about the Election Commission is not on the ratification list, so it falls in the residual special-majority category.

## Criticisms noted by Laxmikanth

No special amending body; states cannot initiate (except by a resolution asking Parliament to create or abolish a council); Parliament alone changes most of the Constitution; no time limit for states; no joint sitting; a sketchy procedure that invites court challenge. He still concludes, with K.C. Wheare, that it strikes a good balance.

### 4. Key amendments I: 1951 to 1976

*Objective:* State what the 1st, 7th, 24th, 25th and 42nd Amendments changed and why they were controversial.

## Block 1: 1951 to 1976, property, reorganisation and Parliament versus courts

**1st Amendment (1951).** Enacted by the Provisional Parliament (the Constituent Assembly sitting as Parliament before the first general elections of 1951-52) while Jawaharlal Nehru was Prime Minister, so the Ninth Schedule belongs to Nehru's prime ministership (the 2019 Prelims question Q47 depends on this; the Provisional Parliament and Nehru facts are standard knowledge rather than from the extract, so verify). It enabled special provisions for socially and economically backward classes; protected laws on acquiring estates; **added the Ninth Schedule** to shield land reform and other listed laws from judicial review; added three grounds for restricting freedom of speech and expression (public order, friendly relations with foreign states, incitement to an offence) and made restrictions "reasonable", hence open to court review; protected state trading and nationalisation from challenge under the right to trade. The 2023 PYQ asks which amendment was widely believed to overcome judicial interpretations of Fundamental Rights.

**7th Amendment (1956).** Scrapped the Part A, B, C and D classification of states and reorganised them into 14 states and 6 union territories; extended High Court jurisdiction to union territories; allowed a common High Court for two or more states.

**24th Amendment (1971).** Affirmed Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution including Fundamental Rights, and made presidential assent compulsory.

**25th Amendment (1971).** Curtailed the right to property; provided that laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) or (c) cannot be challenged for violating Articles 14, 19 and 31.

**38th and 39th (1975).** Made the emergency declaration and ordinances non-justiciable; placed disputes about the President, Vice-President, Prime Minister and Speaker beyond the courts. NCERT lists these with the 42nd as the most controversial amendments, made during the Emergency from June 1975.

**42nd Amendment (1976).** Called a "Mini-Constitution", it gave effect to the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendations. It added the words socialist, secular and integrity to the Preamble; added Fundamental Duties (Part IVA); made the President bound by Cabinet advice; added tribunals (Part XIVA); raised the term of the Lok Sabha and assemblies from 5 to 6 years; froze seats on the 1971 census till 2001; put constitutional amendments beyond judicial scrutiny; curtailed judicial review and writ jurisdiction; gave Directive Principles priority over some Fundamental Rights; changed the Directive Principles: three new Articles (39A, 43A, 48A) plus reworded Article 39(f); Laxmikanth's DPSP chapter counts four (the new Articles cover equal justice and free legal aid, workers' participation in management, and protection of environment, forests and wildlife); extended President's rule from six months to one year; shifted five subjects (including education and forests) from the State List to the Concurrent List. NCERT notes it changed the Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 articles.

### 5. Key amendments II: 1977 to 2003

*Objective:* State what the 44th, 52nd, 61st, 73rd, 74th, 86th and 91st Amendments changed.

## Block 2: 1977 to 2003, correction and consensus

**43rd and 44th (1977 to 1978).** Enacted by the Janata Government to undo distortions of the 42nd. The 43rd restored the Supreme Court's and High Courts' judicial review and writ jurisdiction and removed Parliament's special power over "anti-national activities". The 44th restored the five-year term of the Lok Sabha and assemblies and the quorum rules; allowed the President to send Cabinet advice back once for reconsideration (the second advice binds him); replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for national emergency; required the written recommendation of the Cabinet for an emergency; barred suspension of Articles 20 and 21 during an emergency; **deleted the right to property from the Fundamental Rights and made it only a legal right** (new Article 300A in Part XII: 'No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law'; the Article text is standard knowledge, not in the extract, so verify it; the word 'person' rather than 'citizen' shows it is available to any person, which is the point the 2021 Prelims question Q92 tests; compare Part III, where Article 19 speaks of citizens but Articles 14 and 21 speak of persons); and omitted the provisions that kept election disputes of the President, Vice-President, Prime Minister and Speaker away from the courts.

**52nd (1985).** The **anti-defection law**: disqualification of legislators for defection, with the new Tenth Schedule.

**61st (Act of 1988, in force from March 1989).** Voting age lowered from 21 to 18 for Lok Sabha and assembly elections. Laxmikanth's Appendix IV prints the year as 1989 (PDF p. 1398); the Act is dated 1988 and came into force in March 1989 (standard knowledge, verify). Treat both years as acceptable in an answer, but state which you mean.

**71st and 92nd.** 71st (1992) added Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali to the Eighth Schedule (18 languages); 92nd (2003) added Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali (22 languages).

**73rd and 74th (1992).** The 73rd gave constitutional status to panchayati raj by adding **Part IX** and the **Eleventh Schedule** (29 functional items); the 74th did the same for urban local bodies with **Part IX-A** and the **Twelfth Schedule** (18 items).

**77th, 81st, 82nd, 85th (1995 to 2001).** Reservation in promotion and related relaxations for SCs and STs, passed without difficulty (NCERT).

**86th (2002).** Added **Article 21A**: free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen as a fundamental right; changed Article 45 (early childhood care up to age six); added a fundamental duty for parents or guardians to provide educational opportunities.

**91st (2003).** Capped the Council of Ministers at 15 per cent of the strength of the House (with a state minimum of 12); disqualified defectors from becoming ministers or holding remunerative political posts; deleted the one-third split exemption in the Tenth Schedule.

NCERT groups the 52nd, 61st, 73rd, 74th and 91st as products of political consensus, passed even under coalition governments.

### 6. Key amendments III: 2011 to 2019

*Objective:* State what the 97th, 99th, 101st, 102nd and 103rd Amendments changed and note the status of the 104th to 106th.

## Block 3: 2011 to 2019, institutions and new schemes

**97th (2011).** Gave constitutional status to co-operative societies: the right to form them became a fundamental right, a new Directive Principle promoted them, and Part IX-B was added.

*Judicial note (standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify).* In Union of India v Rajendra N. Shah (2021) the Supreme Court struck down Part IX-B as it applies to cooperative societies in the States, because the Amendment affected a State subject but was not ratified by half the State legislatures as Article 368(2) requires; Part IX-B was left valid for multi-State cooperative societies, which fall within Union competence. This is the module 3 ratification rule at work: a federal change without ratification fails.

**99th (2014).** Replaced the collegium system with the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)**. In **2015 the Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional and void** as violating the independence of the judiciary, a part of the basic structure (the independence-of-the-judiciary ground is standard knowledge; verify), and the collegium returned. The 2019 PYQ (Q45) combined this with a claim about the 44th Amendment and itself uses the phrase 'violative of the independence of judiciary'.

**100th (2015).** Gave effect to the 1974 Land Boundary Agreement and 2011 Protocol with Bangladesh (exchange of enclaves) by amending the First Schedule.

**101st (2016).** Paved the way for the **goods and services tax (GST)**: concurrent taxing powers for Parliament and state legislatures on supply of goods or services, integrated GST on inter-state transactions, a **GST Council**, compensation to States for five years for loss of revenue (Laxmikanth Appendix IV, PDF p. 1407), and changes to Seventh Schedule entries. The detailed GST Council and compensation-cess mechanics are not in the extract and are unverified here. It is meant to remove the cascading effect of taxes and create a common national market. Both Mains PYQs on it ask for features plus an evaluation, including its fit with cooperative federalism.

**102nd (2018).** Gave constitutional status to the **National Commission for Backward Classes**, relieved the SC Commission of backward-class functions, and empowered the President to specify socially and educationally backward classes for a state or union territory.

**103rd (2019).** Empowered the state to make special provisions for **economically weaker sections**, with reservation of up to 10 per cent of seats in educational institutions (including private, except minority institutions) and of posts, in addition to existing reservations. Dated note (standard knowledge, verify): in Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India (November 2022) a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court upheld the 103rd Amendment by majority; the reservation's validity is therefore settled at that level, though the judgment's split and reasoning should be checked before use in a Mains answer.

## 104th, 105th and 106th: handle with care

Your Laxmikanth appendix extract ends at the 103rd. From standard knowledge, flagged for review against the Constitution text and the Gazette: the **104th (2019)** extended SC and ST reservation in legislatures for ten more years and ended the nominated Anglo-Indian seats; the **105th (2021)** restored to states the power to identify socially and educationally backward classes; the **106th (2023)**, the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, provides one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies, including within SC and ST seats, to take effect after delimitation following a census. NCERT's count of 106 amendments by January 2024 is consistent with this. Verify before quoting details.

### 7. Master view: basic structure and the sovereignty-versus-review debate

*Objective:* Explain the basic structure doctrine as a limit on Article 368 and argue both sides of the debate.

## The basic structure doctrine

Article 368 says Parliament may amend "any provision", but Laxmikanth records that Parliament **cannot amend the provisions that form the basic structure** of the Constitution, as ruled by the Supreme Court in **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**. NCERT states its three effects:

- it sets a specific limit on Parliament's amending power: no amendment may violate the basic structure;
- it still allows Parliament to amend any and all parts of the Constitution, within that limit;
- it makes the **judiciary the final authority** on whether an amendment violates the basic structure and on what the basic structure is.

The Constitution never uses the phrase "basic structure". NCERT calls the doctrine an invention of the judiciary born from the **spirit rather than the letter** of the Constitution, a judicial amendment without a formal one. The Court has not given a closed list; NCERT's own exercise warns that the claim "the Supreme Court has given a clear list" is wrong, and that Parliament still has power to amend after Kesavananda.

## How the conflict developed

NCERT places the clash in the years 1967 to 1973 and after: disputes about Fundamental Rights versus Directive Principles, about property, and about Parliament's power to amend. The 24th and 25th Amendments (1971) asserted Parliament's power; the Supreme Court gave the Kesavananda ruling in 1973; attempts to have the ruling reconsidered failed; the **42nd Amendment** asserted parliamentary supremacy and tried to place amendments beyond judicial scrutiny; and the Court repeated its stand in **Minerva Mills (1980)**. The 43rd and 44th restored the balance.

## Parliamentary sovereignty versus judicial review

- **Case for Parliament:** it represents the people and acts for the poor and backward; elected representatives should decide amendments.
- **Case for the courts:** democracy needs checks on arbitrary power; bypassing legal procedure, even with good intentions, excuses arbitrary action.
- **NCERT's resolution:** the basic structure doctrine keeps the Constitution both rigid (core cannot be touched) and flexible (everything else can be changed). Debate over what is basic is healthy, but the political class has accepted the doctrine.

A balanced answer states both sides, gives the 1973 to 1980 sequence, and concludes with checks and balances.

### 8. Examination application: shapes, traps and approach

*Objective:* Recognise the Prelims and Mains question shapes and avoid the standard traps.

## How UPSC asks Amendments

**Prelims shapes.** (1) *Which amendment did X?* The 2017 PYQ on a principle added to the Directive Principles by the 42nd, the 2023 PYQ on the amendment aimed at overcoming judicial interpretation of Fundamental Rights, and the 2024 PYQ on the languages added by the 71st. (2) *True or false pairs about two amendments*, such as the 2019 PYQ combining the 44th and 99th. (3) *Status of a right*, such as the 2021 PYQ on the right to property after the 44th. (4) *Current-law items*, such as the 2024 PYQ on women's reservation, which needs the 106th Amendment text.

**Mains shapes.** The 101st Amendment appears twice (2017 and 2023): features first, then an evaluation question, "efficacious enough" or "accommodative spirit of federalism". The 2026 question on women's reservation asks you to trace local bodies (73rd and 74th) to Parliament (106th) against the quoted statement about women as a class.

## Traps to expect

- The **Ninth Schedule** was added by the 1st Amendment (1951), not by the 24th or 42nd.
- Anti-defection: 52nd created the Tenth Schedule; 91st tightened it.
- The 42nd inserted three new Articles (39A, 43A, 48A) plus reworded Article 39(f); Laxmikanth's DPSP chapter counts four. "Right to work" and "living wage" are older ones.
- The 44th did **not** put the Prime Minister's election beyond court review; it removed such provisions, which the 39th had introduced.
- The 71st and 92nd both add languages; check which names belong to which.
- Right to property: deleted from Fundamental Rights by the 44th, remains a legal right.
- Ratification applies to federal items, not to all fundamental-rights changes.

## Approach

Anchor each amendment with a number, a year, and one noun (Ninth Schedule, Fundamental Duties, Tenth Schedule, Part IX, Article 21A, GST). For a Mains answer, give the problem the amendment tried to solve, its main change, and one evaluation line.

### Notes: Key definitions

- Constituent power: the power to make or change the Constitution itself; under Article 368 it is exercised by Parliament.
- Special majority: a majority of the total membership of a House and two-thirds of the members present and voting.
- Simple majority: more than half of the members present and voting.
- Ratification: approval by state legislatures, by simple majority, needed for amendments to federal provisions.
- Basic structure: the core features of the Constitution that Parliament cannot amend, as held in Kesavananda Bharati (1973); not defined in the text.
- Justiciable: open to examination by a court.

### Notes: Article 368 procedure

- Bill introduced in either House of Parliament only; minister or private member; no presidential permission.
- Passed in each House by special majority; no joint sitting on disagreement.
- Federal provisions: also ratified by legislatures of half of the states by simple majority; no time limit.
- President must assent (24th Amendment, 1971); cannot return the Bill.
- Assent makes it a Constitutional Amendment Act.

### Notes: Commonly confused

- 1st Amendment (Ninth Schedule) versus 52nd (Tenth Schedule).
- 73rd (Part IX, Eleventh Schedule) versus 74th (Part IX-A, Twelfth Schedule).
- 42nd (added Fundamental Duties) versus 86th (added one more duty for parents or guardians).
- 71st (Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali) versus 92nd (Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali).
- Simple-majority changes under other articles are not 'amendments' under Article 368.

### Notes: Exceptions

- States cannot initiate amendments except by a resolution asking Parliament to create or abolish a legislative council, which Parliament may ignore.
- No joint sitting for a deadlocked constitutional amendment Bill, though one exists for ordinary Bills.
- The President must assent to an amendment Bill, unlike an ordinary Bill where the President has options.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** What is a Schedule?
    - **a:** A list or table at the end of the Constitution referred to by Articles, such as the Eighth Schedule of languages.
  - - **q:** Who may introduce a constitutional amendment Bill?
    - **a:** A minister or a private member, in either House of Parliament; no prior presidential permission.
  - - **q:** How many ways can the Constitution be amended?
    - **a:** Three: simple majority, special majority, and special majority plus ratification by half the states.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is there no joint sitting for a constitutional amendment Bill?
    - **a:** Article 368 requires each House to pass it separately by special majority; a joint sitting is provided only for ordinary Bills.
  - - **q:** Why does the basic structure doctrine make the Constitution both rigid and flexible?
    - **a:** It places the core beyond amendment (rigid) while allowing Parliament to amend everything else (flexible).
  - - **q:** Why are changes to the Seventh Schedule lists subject to state ratification?
    - **a:** They alter the distribution of powers between the Union and the states, which a federal system should not let the Centre change alone.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A Bill seeks to change the manner of election of the President. Which procedure applies?
    - **a:** Special majority in each House plus ratification by half the state legislatures.
  - - **q:** A statement says the 42nd Amendment added the Tenth Schedule. Evaluate.
    - **a:** Incorrect: the 52nd Amendment added the Tenth Schedule; the 42nd added Fundamental Duties and other changes.

### Memory skeleton

- Article 368, Part XX: Parliament's constituent power
- Procedure: either House, special majority, no joint sitting, compulsory assent
- Three kinds: simple majority, special majority, special majority plus half the states
- Key amendments 1951-1976: 1st, 7th, 24th, 25th, 42nd
- Key amendments 1977-2019: 44th, 52nd, 61st, 73rd/74th, 86th, 91st, 99th, 101st

### Last-week summary

- Art 368 (Part XX): Bill in either House; special majority = majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting; no joint sitting; President must assent (24th, 1971).
- Federal provisions also need half the states by simple majority, no time limit; simple-majority changes (new states, councils, citizenship) are outside 368.
- 1st 1951 Ninth Schedule; 42nd Fundamental Duties; 44th property a legal right; 52nd Tenth Schedule; 61st vote at 18; 73rd Part IX, 74th Part IX-A.
- 86th Art 21A; 91st ministers capped at 15 per cent; 99th NJAC struck down 2015; 101st GST; Kesavananda 1973 basic structure, reaffirmed in Minerva Mills 1980.
