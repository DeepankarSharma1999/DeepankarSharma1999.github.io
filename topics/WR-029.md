## WR-029: Précis: essential ideas and suitable title

English Paper B (qualifying) · Writing/Language · Reading and writing

### 1. What a précis is, and what the English paper asks

*Objective:* Define a précis, state what the English qualifying paper asks about it (length, own words, title rule) and compute the target length for each of the ten official passages.

## What a précis is
A **précis** (say PRAY-see; the plural is spelt the same) is a short, faithful version of a longer passage. It keeps the author's central idea and the main supporting points, in the author's order, in your own words and in a fraction of the length. It adds nothing: no opinion, no new example, no comment on whether the author is right. Three terms you will use all lesson:
- **Essential idea**: a point without which the passage loses its meaning.
- **Gist**: the whole passage in one sentence.
- **Own words**: your sentences, not the author's; names, technical terms and numbers stay.

## What the paper asks
- English is **Paper B** of the Mains examination: 300 marks, matriculation standard, qualifying only; its marks are not counted for ranking, and 25 per cent is needed before the other papers are read (registry Plan-9, Plan-10; re-check each year's notification). The learner's Hindi qualifying paper (Paper A) is a separate set of lessons.
- The syllabus clause is "Precis Writing." (Mains English Paper B-2). The stated aim of the language papers is to read and understand serious discursive prose and to express ideas clearly and correctly (Plan-15).
- In the ten verified papers from 2017 to 2026 the précis is question 3. The project records list 75 marks for it; **verify on the printed paper**.
- Every one of the ten says "about one-third of its length" and asks for your own words or own language. The records give no tolerance, so "about" is not defined.

## The ten official tasks
Target = printed word count divided by 3 (python). The printed counts are 735 (2017), 795, 882, 792, 745, 750, 885, about 697, 863 and 773 (2026), so the targets run from 232 (2024) to 295 (2023): for 735 words, 245. The full table with the title instruction for each year is in the master notes.

## What the ten tasks teach
1. Passages run from about 700 to 890 words, so a précis is roughly 230 to 295 words; the practice passages here are shorter (270 to 350 words).
2. The title instruction **changes**: only 2022 asked for a title, and the last three papers forbid even suggesting one. Read the instruction line first (module 6).
3. The aim speaks of expressing ideas "clearly and correctly"; the marking scheme itself is not in the records. The passages are serious prose like the comprehension passages (Day 4 lesson WR-027).

### 2. Finding the essential ideas: read twice and write the gist

*Objective:* Read a passage twice, label what each paragraph does, and write the gist in one sentence using the formula topic + claim + reason.

## Two readings, two jobs
**First reading, no pencil.** Read straight through to learn what the passage is about and where it ends up. Do not stop for hard words. **Second reading, pencil in hand.** Now mark what each paragraph does. You cannot rank ideas you have not yet seen as a whole.

## What a paragraph can do
Label each paragraph with one of these roles:
- **Claim**: what the author wants you to accept.
- **Reason**: why the claim is true.
- **Example or illustration**: a case that makes a reason vivid. It supports, but is usually the first thing to go.
- **Contrast or concession**: "some argue ... but ...", where the author grants an opposing point and then answers it.
- **Definition**: what a term means; keep it only if the claim depends on it.
- **Conclusion or advice**: where the passage ends up. This is almost always essential.

## Signposts
*First, second, finally* count the reasons. *However, yet, but* turn towards the author's real point. *Thus, therefore, so* lead to a conclusion. *For example, such as* introduce a cuttable example. *In short* hands you the gist. The first and last sentences of a paragraph often carry its main idea.

## The four questions and the gist
Ask: What is the passage **about**? What does the author **say** about it? **Why**? And **so what** (the conclusion or advice)? Join the answers:

> The author argues (or explains) that **[topic] [claim]**, because **[main reason or reasons]**, and concludes **[so what]**.

**Illustration (analysis, in the lesson's own words).** The 2026 passage on reading history: reading history is worthwhile not only to learn what happened but because accounts of the past shape later action and show how people behave, so read several accounts and read for the present.

## Passages are not all alike
- **Argument** (a claim, reasons, a reply to objections): keep the claim, the reasons and the conclusion.
- **Exposition** (a topic explained by types, stages or features): keep the classification and the list of types, compressing detail.
- **Narrative or biography** (events in order): keep the turning points and the outcome.
- **Reflection** (ideas with personal remarks): keep the idea and drop the personal account unless the idea depends on it.

### 3. Ranking and cutting: what to keep, compress and drop

*Objective:* Sort a passage's ideas into keep, compress and cut, and compress a list of names or cases into one category phrase.

## Three levels
A précis is a ranking exercise. Give every idea a level before you write.

| Level | What it contains | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Essential | the central claim; the main reasons, stages or types; the author's conclusion or advice; a key contrast | always keep, in the author's order |
| 2. Supporting | secondary points; a definition the claim depends on; a cause or result that links two main points | keep in a few words if the length allows |
| 3. Disposable | examples and cases; anecdotes; most numbers and dates; direct quotations; rhetorical questions; repeated statements; the author's personal asides; most adjectives and adverbs | cut |

## Why examples go first
An example makes a claim easier to see but proves nothing new. Keep one only when it is the sole evidence for the claim, and then in a clause.

## Compress lists with a category word
A passage may name six reformers or five countries. You rarely have room to keep them all. Replace the list with its category: "several religious reform movements" instead of naming each; "a handful of countries" instead of four with percentages. Keep one or two names only if the question turns on them.

## Figures, quotations and questions
- **Numbers**: keep one only if it is the point ("a majority in three countries" rather than the three percentages). Treat figures with care; a passage may print an inconsistent one, and your précis should not repeat it.
- **Quotations**: turn the idea into your own sentence (module 4). Never copy a quoted sentence.
- **Rhetorical questions**: turn a question the author answers into a statement of the answer.

## What never to add
Your own opinion ("this is a wise view"), a fact from outside the passage, or an improvement on the argument. If a point is not in the passage, it is not in the précis.

## A short drill
"The town has a park, a library, a hospital, a bus stand and two schools" (15 words) compresses to "basic public facilities" (3 words).

## A final test for each kept sentence
Ask of each kept sentence: if it were deleted, would the author's argument still stand? If yes, it was level 3.

### 4. Own words, person, tense and reported speech

*Objective:* Rewrite sentences in your own words in the third person with consistent tense, turn quotations into reported speech, and join ideas with connectors that match their logic.

## What "own words" means
Own words means new sentences, not the author's with a few words swapped. Rebuild the sentence: change its order, merge two sentences, turn a verb into a noun. Keep names, technical terms and numbers. As a practice rule (not official), never copy more than three or four words in a row, apart from a name or term.

## Person: the précis is in the third person
The author may write "I", "we" or "you". The précis does not. Name the source: *the author*, *the writer*, *the speaker*, or just state the idea. The 2023 official passage is narrated by an "I" who describes his own anger; a précis writes *the author says his own anger is aroused mainly by ...*, never *my anger*. Do not write "in my opinion".

Example: "We must admit that our knowledge is slight" becomes "The author admits that knowledge is limited."

## Tense: choose and keep one
- What the author **argues** or explains: present tense (*argues, holds, concludes*).
- **Events in the past** that the passage tells of: past tense (*Ashoka sent missionaries*).
- **General truths**: present tense (*water boils at a fixed temperature*).
- A story is told in the past from start to finish. Do not slide between tenses without a reason (see WR-028, Tense, reporting verbs and reported speech).

## Quotations become reported speech
Remove the quotation marks and shift the pronouns. In a précis the author's view is reported in the present (*the author admits*), so replace a past reporting verb (*wrote, said*) with a present one rather than backshifting. Step the tense back one stage only when you keep a past reporting verb for a past event; a general truth stays in the present.

| Direct (in the passage) | Reported (in the précis) |
|---|---|
| The headmaster said, "I am tired." | The headmaster said that he was tired. (backshift, past reporting verb kept) |
| "We learn by trial and error," she wrote. | The author notes that people learn by trial and error. (present reporting verb; a general truth stays present) |

Choose the reporting verb that matches the force: *argues, states, warns, admits, urges, suggests* (WR-028, table of reporting verbs).

## Keep the hedge, drop the colour
Keep *may, perhaps, appears*; drop intensifiers (*extremely, deeply*).

## Connectors carry the logic
Use *because, so, although, but, however, thus, while, which* to show the relation between ideas (WR-028, Connectors and the logic they carry). A précis is a chain: each sentence hands over to the next.



### 5. Length: the one-third target, counting and compressing sentences

*Objective:* Set a word budget from the printed count, count words consistently, and shorten a draft by named moves without losing meaning.

## Set the target
**Target = printed word count ÷ 3.** If the paper prints 810 words, aim for 270. Because the instruction says "about" and the project records give no tolerance, treat a **band of 10 per cent either side** (243 to 297 for 270) as a practice limit, and aim inside 5 per cent. This band is Mission 90's practice rule, not an official one; verify against the paper's instruction. Far above the target shows weak cutting; far below it probably drops essentials.

## Count the same way every time
Count each word as written. A hyphenated compound (*self-control*) is one word; a number (*1909*) is one word; *don't* is one. Count in tens, marking every tenth word. Keep the title (if one is asked) outside the count, but check the paper. Printed counts can differ slightly from your own: the 2023 paper prints 885, and a raw split that counts the 13 stand-alone dashes gives 898. Counting methods differ, so use the printed number.

## Budget by paragraph
Divide the target by importance, not equally. For a 250-word précis: opening claim 50, two reasons 80 each, the reply to an objection 40. Note the budget beside the notes.

## Write long, then cut
Many first drafts run well over target. Cut by named moves:

| Move | Before | After | Saves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drop an example | The tax was resented, as in the case of the weavers of the coastal towns. | The tax was resented. | 11 words |
| Compress a list | schools, colleges, libraries and hospitals | public institutions | 3 words |
| Verb to noun | The committee decided to reject the proposal. | The committee rejected the proposal. | 2 words |
| Reduce a clause | the road, which was built in 1950 | the 1950 road | 4 words |
| Merge sentences | The river was foul. It killed the fish. | The foul river killed the fish. | 2 words |
| Remove padding | It is a fact that prices rose. | Prices rose. | 5 words |

The "Saves" column was counted by python. When trimming, remove the last-ranked idea first, then tighten sentences.

### 6. The title: when it is asked, when it is forbidden, and how to make a good one

*Objective:* Obey the title instruction printed on the paper, and when a title is asked write a short noun phrase that names the central idea.

## First, the instruction
Of the ten verified papers, **only 2022** says to give a title. Six papers say not to give one (2017 to 2021 and 2023), and the three most recent (2024, 2025, 2026) say not to give **or suggest** one. The rule is simple: do exactly what the paper's line says.
- **Title asked**: write a title above the précis.
- **Title forbidden**: write no title, heading, label or underlined summary line, and not even the word "Title". Mission 90 reading (the paper does not define 'suggest'): "suggest" covers a phrase such as "Précis on pollution" or an opening line that works as a heading. Start directly with the first sentence of the précis.

## What a good title is
A title names the **central idea** of the passage in a short phrase.
- **A noun phrase**, not a sentence: "The Case for Slow Reading", not "Slow reading is the best kind of reading."
- **Short**: as a practice rule, 3 to 8 words.
- **Right size**: not so broad that any passage on the subject would fit ("Reading"), and not so narrow that it names one detail ("A Poem Read for an Hour").
- **Faithful** to the précis, and not copied from the passage's first line. No full stop.

## A method: from the gist to the title
1. Write the gist sentence (module 2).
2. Underline the subject and the key idea of the claim.
3. Remove the verb and join: subject + angle.

| Gist | Title |
|---|---|
| Roadside trees give shade, clean air and flood protection, so towns should treat them as part of the road. | The Case for Roadside Trees |
| Noise harms health and cities should treat it as a public harm. | Noise: A Public Harm |

## Bad titles and their faults
"Trees" (too broad); "Neem trees need less repair" (too narrow, an example); "Trees are very useful for towns because they cool and clean" (a sentence).

## The one official passage that asked for a title (2022)
The 2022 passage moves from computer errors to human thought: mistakes are built into thinking, and discovery depends on using them. A title should name that link. Plausible titles (analysis, not an official key): "Error as the Root of Thought and Discovery" or "The Creative Side of Mistakes"; "Computer Errors" alone would be too narrow.

### 7. The method end to end: ten steps, a time plan and one worked passage

*Objective:* Carry out the ten-step précis method on a passage, from the instruction line to the fair copy, within a practice time plan of about 30 minutes.

## The ten steps
1. **Read the instruction line.** Title or no title? Own words? Printed word count? Compute the target (module 5).
2. **Read once, no pencil**, for the whole.
3. **Read again with a pencil.** Label paragraph roles; underline claim and conclusion; strike examples (modules 2, 3).
4. **Write the gist** in one sentence.
5. **Make notes**: five to eight numbered ideas, each in at most eight words, in the author's order.
6. **Rank and budget.** Give each idea a level and a word budget that sums to the target.
7. **Draft** in full sentences: own words, third person, one tense family, connectors (module 4).
8. **Count** and cut or add (module 5).
9. **Check** against the rubric in module 9: content, no additions, grammar, flow.
10. **Fair copy**, and the title only if the paper asks for one (module 6).

## A practice time plan
A practice target, not an official allotment; check the time the whole paper allows. About 30 minutes: steps 1 to 2, four; 3 to 4, six; 5 to 6, five; 7, eight; 8 to 9, five; 10, two. Untimed practice comes first.

The passage (roadside trees), the précis and the counts are in the master notes, "Worked passages 1 and 2". The working steps follow.

**Step 1.** Printed count 343; target 343 ÷ 3 = 114; band 103 to 126.

**Step 4, gist.** The author argues that towns should keep and plant roadside trees, because they give shade, cleaner air and flood protection at modest cost, and says the objections are answered by good choice and care.

**Step 5, notes** (level in brackets). 1 Towns sacrifice trees for roads (2). 2 Shade, cooler pavements, more walking (1). 3 Cleaner air (1); no substitute for vehicle controls (2). 4 Roots hold soil; less flooding (1). 5 Objection on maintenance, and the reply: right species, spacing, pruning (1). 6 Example of the old neem-shaded street (3, cut). 7 Planting must be sincere: protection, water, a responsible person (1). 8 Conclusion: trees are part of the road; modest cost (1).

**Step 7, the draft** follows the notes in order with the connectors *yet, but, also, so*; it runs to 111 words.

**Step 10, title** (if asked): "The Case for Roadside Trees" (5 words).

The example (the old street) was cut without loss, and the count of 111 words falls inside the band of 103 to 126.

## When time is short
Do not skip step 5: a rough list of five ideas prevents the commonest failure, writing the first half in detail and running out of space for the second.

### 8. Exam: what the ten official passages show, and the commonest errors

*Objective:* Recognise the type of an official précis passage, decide quickly what to keep and cut, and avoid the ten commonest errors.

## Reading the ten tasks as a set
The ten passages (2017 to 2026) are serious, unfamiliar prose of 700 to 890 words. They fall into types, and each type has a typical trap. This is analysis, not an official key.

The master notes hold the full table (year, type, what to keep, typical trap). Three examples show the pattern. The 2017 passage on work and happiness is an argument: keep its two sources of satisfaction and its conclusion, and do not list the examples of skilled work. The 2018 passage on a cultural renewal is an exposition with many names and quotations: keep the stages and the three aspects, compress the names. The 2023 passage on anger has a first-person narrator: keep the ideas, convert the voice.

## Four reading habits
1. **Find the turn.** Most passages change direction once ("however", "but", "yet"). The idea after the turn is usually the point.
2. **Compress names and numbers** (module 3).
3. **Keep the author's ending.** Passages end with advice or a conclusion; omit it and the précis is incomplete.
4. **Convert the voice.** First-person narrators, speeches and quotations must become third-person, reported statements (module 4).

## The ten commonest errors
1. Copying whole sentences.
2. Adding your own opinion or a fact from outside.
3. Keeping examples and dropping reasons.
4. Leaving out the conclusion.
5. Wrong length (over the band, or so short that a main point is lost).
6. Leaving direct speech or "I/we" in.
7. Switching tense without reason.
8. Writing a title when forbidden, or none when required (module 6).
9. A list of disconnected sentences instead of a chain with connectors.
10. Unclear pronouns and agreement slips (*it, they* without a clear referent).

The master notes pair each error with a repair. The self-check rubric in module 9 turns them into a score.

### 9. Self-check rubric and a timed practice routine

*Objective:* Score a précis on an eight-item rubric, log the lowest item, and follow a spaced routine on the ten official passages and the original practice passages.

## The self-check rubric (Mission 90's own, out of 16)
Score each item 0, 1 or 2 after drafting. This is a practice tool, not the examiner's marking scheme, which the project records do not give.

| # | Item | 2 points means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Central idea | the gist is clear in the first or second sentence |
| 2 | Coverage | every level 1 idea is present, in the author's order |
| 3 | Nothing added | no opinion, example or outside fact |
| 4 | Own words | no run of more than three or four copied words except names and terms (module 4) |
| 5 | Length | inside the 10 per cent band, ideally the 5 per cent aim (module 5) |
| 6 | Person and tense | third person, one consistent tense family, speech reported |
| 7 | Flow | connectors show the logic; no list of loose sentences |
| 8 | Title rule | obeyed exactly: title present and a good noun phrase if asked; nothing at all if forbidden (module 6) |

Total 13 to 16: ready for timed work. 10 to 12: one more untimed passage on the lowest item. Below 10: redo untimed. Always record the **lowest item**.

## The routine
1. **Untimed first** on one passage: all ten steps, no clock. Aim to score at least 12.
2. **Timed second** on another: the 30-minute plan from module 7.
3. **Compare** your ideas with the idea list for that passage (pyqNotes). Do not memorise any model; your sentences must be your own.
4. **Log**: date, passage, target, your count, score, lowest rubric item, one repair.
5. **Repeat** the lowest item on the next passage; **re-do the same passage after 7 days** without notes and compare.

### Notes: Definitions

- Precis: a short, faithful version of a passage that keeps the central idea and the main supporting points, in the writer's own words, at a stated fraction of the length.
- Essential idea: a point without which the passage would lose its meaning (level 1 in the ranking).
- Gist: the whole passage in one sentence, in the form topic + claim + reason.
- Own words: new sentences that restate the ideas; changing a few words of the author's sentence is not own words.
- Reported speech: the form in which a quotation or speech is retold without quotation marks, with person and tense adjusted (see WR-028).
- Title: a short noun phrase that names the central idea; written only when the paper asks for one.
- Target length: the printed word count divided by 3; band: Mission 90's practice limit of 10 per cent either side (not an official tolerance).

### Notes: Worked passages 1 and 2 (original)

- Worked passage 1 (original, invented for practice; 343 words). Target 343 ÷ 3 = 114.3; practice band 103 to 126; the precis below has 111 words.
- Passage: Most towns plan their roads for vehicles and forget the people who walk beside them. Trees are usually the first thing sacrificed when a road is widened. This is a mistake, because a line of mature trees does more for a town than its builders ordinarily admit. / The first benefit is shade. On a summer afternoon the surface of an open road becomes far hotter than the air above it, and pedestrians, cyclists and street vendors bear the heat directly. A canopy of leaves can cool the pavement and the people on it by several degrees, which makes walking a realistic choice for short trips and so reduces traffic. The second benefit is cleaner air. Leaves trap dust and absorb some gases, so a tree-lined street is less choking than a bare one, although no tree can replace controls on the vehicles themselves. The third benefit is less obvious. Roots hold the soil beside the road and let rain soak in, so that storms are less likely to flood the carriageway and the drains. / Some engineers object that trees are costly to maintain. Branches fall, roots lift paving stones, and fallen leaves block drains. These complaints deserve attention, but they argue for choosing the right species, planting at the right distance from buildings and pruning on a schedule; they do not argue for removing trees. A street in the old part of one city, shaded for a century by the same row of neem trees, needs less repair of its surface than a newer street across town that has never had a tree. / Planting must also be honest work. Saplings that are set out for a photograph and left without water die within a season, and the town is then poorer than before. A tree needs a protected pit, regular watering for the first few years and a person made responsible for it. / A town that treats trees as part of the road, and not as decoration along it, gets cooler streets, cleaner air and fewer floods for a modest and continuing cost.
- Ranking: Level 1: the claim that trees belong in road planning; the three benefits (shade and walking, cleaner air, flood protection); the objection on maintenance and its reply; sincere planting; the conclusion. Level 2: towns sacrifice trees; trees cannot replace vehicle controls. Level 3: the old street with neem trees, the several degrees, the summer afternoon, the photograph.
- Precis (111 words, original): Towns plan roads for vehicles and sacrifice trees, yet roadside trees give three benefits: shade that cools pavements and makes walking, and so less traffic, realistic; cleaner air through trapped dust and gases, though trees cannot replace vehicle controls; and protection against flooding because roots hold soil and let rain soak in. Engineers complain that branches fall, roots lift paving and leaves block drains, but such problems call for suitable species, careful spacing and regular pruning, not removal. Planting must also be sincere: saplings need protection, watering and a responsible person. Treating trees as part of the road gives cooler streets, cleaner air and fewer floods at a modest, continuing cost.
- Title if asked: The Case for Roadside Trees (5 words). Fits the central idea and is a noun phrase.
- Worked passage 2 (original, invented for practice; 350 words). Target 350 ÷ 3 = 116.7; practice band 105 to 128; the precis below has 116 words.
- Passage: We are told every day that readers have grown impatient. Screens offer a hundred headlines before breakfast, and few people finish a long article. Yet the most valuable kind of reading has never been quick. A page read slowly gives the mind time to question what the writer claims, to notice what has been left unsaid and to connect the new idea with older ones. Quick reading collects information; slow reading changes the reader. / A teacher of literature once asked her class to read a single poem of twelve lines for an entire hour. At first the students were restless and several thought the task absurd. By the end of the hour they had found meanings that no summary could have given them: a word repeated at the start and the close of the poem, a change of tone in the ninth line, a silence where an answer was expected. One student said that she had never before heard a poem. / Slow reading is not the same as slow thinking, and it does not mean reading everything slowly. A railway timetable should be scanned, and a novel read for pleasure may be read quickly. The skill lies in choosing. Material that will be used for an argument, an examination or a decision deserves a first reading to learn the shape of the whole, then a second reading with a pencil, marking the main claim of each paragraph and the reasons given for it. / Critics reply that nobody has the time. That is partly true, but the time lost to a careless reading is paid back when the reader must return to the text again and again because nothing was understood the first time. A candidate who reads twelve pages with attention often retains more than one who skims forty. / The habit can be built in small steps: ten minutes of undisturbed reading each day, a pencil in hand, and a single sentence written afterwards saying what the passage was about. The attention trained in this way does not stay in the library; it is carried into conversation, work and decisions.
- Ranking: Level 1: slow reading is the most valuable kind and changes the reader; it need not apply to everything; how to read twice; the time objection and its answer; the daily habit and its wider benefit. Level 3: the teacher and the twelve-line poem, the student's remark, the timetable and novel, twelve pages against forty.
- Precis (116 words, original): Although readers are said to be impatient, the most valuable reading has always been slow, because it lets the mind question the writer, notice what is unsaid and link new ideas to old ones; quick reading gathers information, slow reading changes the reader. This does not mean reading everything slowly: casual or reference material can be scanned, while matter needed for an argument, examination or decision deserves two readings, the second with a pencil. The objection that there is no time is weak, since careless reading wastes time through repeated returns to the text. A daily habit of ten minutes' attentive reading, ending with a one-sentence summary, builds an attention that serves conversation, work and decisions.
- Title if asked: The Case for Slow Reading (5 words). Names the central idea; the poem example is not in the title.

### Notes: Worked passages 3 and 4 (original)

- Worked passage 3 (original, invented for practice; 349 words). Target 349 ÷ 3 = 116.3; practice band 105 to 128; the precis below has 118 words.
- Passage: Few questions divide a staffroom as quickly as whether pupils should bring mobile phones into class. A senior teacher at one school put the objection plainly: "Every buzz of a phone pulls a child's mind out of the room. I cannot teach fractions to someone who is waiting for a message." Supporters of a ban add that phones encourage copying in tests, and that pupils who spend breaks on screens talk to each other less. / Defenders of phones answer that the device is also a dictionary, a calculator, a camera for recording experiments and a way to reach a parent in an emergency. A student who was asked for her view said, "If I am told to put it away, I lose all of that and I still have no more self-control than before." Some teachers have gone further and built phone use into lessons, asking pupils to look up the meaning of a word or to photograph a plant and identify it. / Both sides, however, take for granted that the phone is the problem. Research on attention, which is still young and sometimes contradictory, suggests something more careful. The harm seems to come not from the device being present in a bag but from its being at hand and switched on, for the habit of checking it is strong and is rewarded by every new notification. A phone that is silent and out of reach affects concentration much less than one lying open on the desk. / A sensible rule should therefore be specific rather than absolute. Phones may be kept in bags on silent during lessons and brought out only when the teacher asks for them. The rule should be the same for teachers as for pupils, since a teacher who answers calls during class cannot expect restraint from the room. Schools should review the rule after a term, with pupils and parents consulted, and change it if it is not working. / Neither prohibition nor free use serves education well. What serves it is a clear rule, fairly applied, that treats attention as something a school must protect.

### Notes: Tense and speech conversions

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Precis versus summary versus paraphrase: a precis is short and ranked; a paraphrase keeps the length and restates everything; a summary is any brief account, but a precis also keeps the author's emphasis and order.
- Precis versus comprehension answer: comprehension answers a question about the passage (WR-027, WR-028); a precis reports the whole passage's ideas.
- Own words versus synonym swapping: changing words one by one is still copying the sentence.
- Title versus heading versus label: a title is a noun phrase naming the central idea; 'Precis:' or an underlined summary line is a label and is forbidden where the paper forbids a title.
- About one-third versus exactly one-third: the instruction says 'about'; the project records give no tolerance, so Mission 90 uses a 10 per cent practice band.

### Core concepts

- A precis keeps the central idea and level 1 ideas of a passage, in the author's order, in your own words, at about one-third of the printed length.
- Rank every idea: keep the claim, reasons and conclusion; compress lists and numbers; cut examples, quotations and asides.
- Write in the third person with one tense family; turn quotations and speeches into reported speech; add nothing of your own.
- Read the title instruction on the paper first: only 2022 asked for a title, and the last three papers forbid giving or suggesting one.

Passage (302 words)

City noise is usually treated as a nuisance, like a leaking tap: irritating but harmless. The evidence against this view is gathering. Noise raises the level of stress hormones in the body even during sleep, and people exposed to it for years report more headaches, high blood pressure and fatigue than those who live in quiet areas. Passage (272 words)

Many people are proud of their ability to do several things at once. They answer messages while listening to a lecture, read while watching television and plan the next task while performing the present one. The belief behind this pride is that the mind, like a computer, can run several programs together. Careful experiments on attention do not support it.

What the mind actually does is switch. It leaves one task, loads another and returns, and each switch costs a little time and a little accuracy. The cost is small when one of the tasks is automatic, such as walking while talking. It is large when both tasks need thought, such as writing a report while following a conversation. In that case the person does not perform both tasks at once; he performs each badly and in turn, and often fails to notice how many mistakes he has made.

Habit makes the problem worse. A person who is interrupted often becomes used to interruption and begins to interrupt himself, glancing at the screen when nothing has called him. Concentration, which depends on the ability to stay with a difficult thing through its dull stretches, weakens as it is not used.

The remedy is not to refuse every distraction but to arrange work so that the important task gets uninterrupted periods. Switching off notifications for forty minutes, keeping a list of small matters to be dealt with together afterwards, and finishing one job before beginning another cost very little. Those who adopt the practice usually find that their work, though it seems slower at each step, is finished earlier and contains fewer errors.
