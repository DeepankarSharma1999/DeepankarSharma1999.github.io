## GS-010: Early medieval land grants and polity

Prelims GS I (Mains culture cross-links) · History · Ancient India

*Goal:* Explain Early medieval land grants and polity and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### What a grant actually transferred

A grant handed over far more than soil. In her Danguna charter, Prabhavati Gupta gives the village to a teacher, Chanalasvamin, with the standard agrahara privileges: soldiers and police may not enter, villagers are released from supplying grass, hides and charcoal to touring officials, and the donee receives hidden treasures along with major and minor taxes. Villagers are told to obey their new master. A Tamil section of a Chola grant lists trees, wells, pasture, canals and deep lakes as part of the gift and allows the recipient to collect taxes, dig wells and build irrigation channels. Each grant therefore created an intermediary claiming dues and authority over the people who actually worked the land.

### Polity: subordinate chiefs and the historians' debate

By the seventh century regional landlords and warrior chiefs served kings as subordinates, bringing gifts, attending court and supplying troops; some later broke free, as when the Rashtrakuta chief Dantidurga overthrew his Chalukya overlord in the mid-eighth century. Revenue officials came from influential families and their posts were often hereditary, so power was shared rather than centralised. Grants fit this setting. One group of historians treats them as a deliberate strategy to push farming into new tracts, since gifts ranged from small plots to large uncultivated stretches. Another sees them as evidence of weakening kings trying to win allies among samantas, with grand royal self-portraits in prashastis serving as a façade of power.

### Evidence limits and exam angles

Copper plates were given to the recipient as a record; one ninth-century Sanskrit–Tamil set is held together by a ring carrying the royal seal to prove authenticity. Yet inscriptions express the view of whoever commissioned them. Routine farming rarely appears, and pastoralists, fisherfolk, hunter-gatherers, shifting cultivators and mobile artisans often stayed outside the reach of officials and samantas. Prelims distinctions: agrahara and brahmadeya both denote land for Brahmanas, while devadana is temple land and pallichchhandam is Jaina land; grants predate the Guptas. Mains dimensions: agrarian expansion, new rural elites, gender and property through Prabhavati Gupta, and the limits of epigraphic evidence.
