### Bridge: Federalism in five lines: two governments, one Constitution

A **federation** is a country where power is shared between a national government and regional governments, and both are created and protected by one written Constitution. NCERT (Indian Constitution at Work, chapter 7) describes federalism as an institutional mechanism that accommodates two sets of government, one at the regional level and one at the national level, each autonomous in its own sphere. In India these are the **Union** (or Centre) and the **States**.

Five lines to hold on to:

1. India has two sets of governments: the Union government and a government for each State. Both have constitutional status and a clearly identified area of work.
2. The Constitution divides subjects through lists (Union, State and Concurrent), which you will meet again in topics on Centre-State relations. If there is a dispute over which list a power belongs to, the judiciary resolves it on the basis of the Constitution.
3. NCERT adds that real politics, culture, history and a culture of trust and cooperation decide how a federation actually works. Constitutions give a framework; politics gives it life.
4. Disputes come in two kinds. *Legal* disputes (what does the law say?) can be settled by courts. *Political* disputes (we want more autonomy, more money, a different border) are settled by negotiation. NCERT says exactly this: legal disputes between the Centre and the States, or between States, can be resolved by the judiciary, while demands for autonomy are political and need negotiation.
5. This lesson is about **inter-State** relations: how State meets State, and how the Constitution gives forums and referees for them.

You do not need the Union/State/Concurrent list details now. You only need the idea that there are several governments inside one country, and that they can disagree about water, borders, trade and money.

### Bridge: River basins and State boundaries: why water crosses borders

State boundaries in India were drawn mainly on language, history and administrative convenience; NCERT's federalism chapter says boundaries have been reorganised many times and that language is the basis for defining State boundaries. A river does not respect those lines.

A **river basin** (or river valley) is the whole area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. Rain falls anywhere in that area, runs downhill into streams, and finally reaches the main river. One basin may therefore lie across several States. The Krishna, Godavari, Narmada, Cauvery and Mahanadi are examples that appear among the tribunals you will study.

Three simple ideas follow.

- **Upper riparian and lower riparian.** The State where the river enters or originates is the *upper* riparian State; States further downstream are *lower* riparian States. An upstream State can store or divert water before it reaches the lower States. This is the root of many disputes. These two terms are standard vocabulary and are not defined in the Laxmikanth extract.
- **Shared use.** Water serves drinking, farming, industry and power. NCERT notes that river-water disputes are even more serious than border disputes because they affect drinking water and agriculture of the States concerned.
- **Why courts alone struggle.** Laxmikanth quotes a long-standing observation that rules based on private property in water do not give a satisfactory basis for settling disputes between States, where the public's interest in the proper use of water is involved. That is the argument for a special machinery, which Article 262 provides.

Keep this picture in mind: the river is one natural unit, the States are many political units, and the Constitution has to give them a referee.

### 1. Why States need rules for dealing with each other: the toolbox preview

*Objective:* Name the constitutional provisions on inter-State comity and the extra-constitutional bodies, and preview the dispute-resolution toolbox this lesson builds.

Laxmikanth opens chapter 15 with a simple idea: the success of the federal system depends not only on harmony between the Centre and the States but also between the States themselves. To help, the Constitution makes four provisions for **inter-State comity** (a word meaning friendly mutual respect and cooperation):

1. Adjudication of inter-State water disputes (Article 262).
2. Coordination through inter-State councils (Article 263).
3. Mutual recognition of public acts, records and judicial proceedings (Article 261).
4. Freedom of inter-State trade, commerce and intercourse (Articles 301 to 307).

In addition, **Zonal Councils** have been set up by Parliament to promote inter-State cooperation. Notice the difference in status: the first four sit in the Constitution; the Zonal Councils do not. UPSC loves that difference.

**The toolbox idea.** Think of disputes and coordination needs as problems, and the provisions as tools in a toolbox.

| Problem | Tool | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Dispute over river water | Article 262 and ad hoc tribunals | Special adjudication, outside ordinary courts |
| Legal dispute between governments | Supreme Court, Article 131 (see GS-186) | Judicial |
| Common-interest policy and non-legal disputes | Inter-State Council, Article 263; Zonal Councils | Advisory forum |
| Recognition of another State's records and judgments | Article 261 | Rule of law |
| Free trade across State lines | Articles 301 to 307 | Constitutional guarantee with limits |
| Tax policy that needs Centre and States together | GST Council, Article 279A | Constitutional cooperative forum |

At the end of the lesson (module 9) you will have this table filled in with who decides, whether the decision binds, and which Article applies.

**Where this fits.** The earlier topic GS-193 covers how money is shared between the Centre and the States through the Finance Commission. GS-186 covers the Supreme Court and its original jurisdiction under Article 131. This topic stands between them: the forums and mechanisms through which States and the Centre talk, argue and settle.

**Exam preview.** UPSC asked in 2025 how many of three bodies (the Inter-State Council, the National Security Council and Zonal Councils) were established under the Constitution. The Mains in 2026 asked how effective India's federal framework has been in accommodating regional and cultural diversity, with a stress on asymmetric federalism and better dispute-resolution mechanisms. Both reward knowing the *status* of each body and the *limits* of each tool.

### 2. Article 262 and inter-State river water disputes

*Objective:* State what Article 262 allows, describe the two 1956 Acts and the tribunal system, and recall the nine tribunals.

**Article 262** provides for the adjudication of inter-State water disputes. It makes two provisions (Laxmikanth, p. 373):

1. Parliament may by law provide for the adjudication of any dispute or complaint with respect to the use, distribution and control of the waters of any inter-State river or river valley.
2. Parliament may also provide that neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction in respect of any such dispute.

Notice that Article 262 does not itself create a tribunal. It *empowers Parliament* to make a law. That is a common examination point.

**The two laws of 1956.** Using that power, Parliament enacted two laws.

- The **River Boards Act, 1956** provides for river boards for the regulation and development of inter-State rivers and valleys. The Central government sets up a board on the request of the State governments concerned, to advise them.
- The **Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956** (often called the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act) empowers the Central government to set up an **ad hoc tribunal** to adjudicate a dispute between two or more States about the waters of an inter-State river or river valley. *Ad hoc* means set up for one specific dispute. The tribunal's decision is **final and binding** on the parties, and neither the Supreme Court nor any other court has jurisdiction over a dispute referred to the tribunal under the Act. Flag (verify): this bar is not absolute in practice; in the 2018 Cauvery judgment the Supreme Court heard appeals against the tribunal's award under Article 136 and modified it.

**Why an extra-judicial machinery?** Laxmikanth quotes the reasoning: the Supreme Court would indeed have jurisdiction to decide disputes between States on water supplies where legal rights or interests are involved, but experience shows that rules modelled on private property in water do not give a satisfactory basis for settling disputes where the public's interest in water use is at stake.

So a tribunal does not guarantee quick or accepted outcomes.

### 3. Article 263 and the Inter-State Council of 1990

*Objective:* State what Article 263 permits, describe the 1990 Inter-State Council's origin, composition and functioning, and explain why it is optional and advisory.

**Article 263** contemplates an Inter-State Council to coordinate between the States and between the Centre and the States. The President *can* establish such a council if at any time it appears to him that the public interest would be served. He may define its duties, organisation and procedure.

Article 263 specifies the kinds of duty a council may be given:

- (a) enquiring into and advising upon disputes that may arise between States;
- (b) investigating and discussing subjects in which the States or the Centre and the States have a common interest; and
- (c) making recommendations on any such subject, and particularly for the better coordination of policy and action on it.

Laxmikanth quotes a jurist: the council's function on disputes is *complementary* to the Supreme Court's jurisdiction under Article 131 to decide a legal controversy between governments. The council can deal with any controversy, legal or non-legal, but it is advisory, unlike the court, which gives a binding decision. (Article 131 is covered in GS-186.)

**Not a standing constitutional body.** Read the Article again: a council *may* be established. It is enabling, not mandatory. Under Article 263 the President has set up several councils over time: the Central Council of Health and Family Welfare, the Central Council of Local Government, and four Regional Councils for Sales Tax for the Northern, Eastern, Western and Southern zones. These are different from the general Inter-State Council.

**The 1990 Inter-State Council.** The **Sarkaria Commission** on Centre-State relations (1983-88) made a strong case for a permanent Inter-State Council under Article 263 and recommended it be called the Inter-Governmental Council, to distinguish it from other bodies set up under the same Article; it recommended that the Council be charged with duties (b) and (c). Acting on this, the Janata Dal government headed by V. P. Singh established the **Inter-State Council in 1990** by a presidential order (Laxmikanth's note gives the date of the order as 28 May 1990).

**Composition.**
- Prime Minister: Chairman.
- Chief Ministers of all States.
- Chief Ministers of Union Territories with legislative assemblies.
- Administrators of Union Territories without legislatures.
- Governors of States under President's rule.
- Six Central Cabinet Ministers, including the Home Minister, nominated by the Prime Minister.
- Five Ministers of Cabinet rank or Ministers of State (Independent Charge) nominated by the Chairman, as permanent invitees.

**Functions.** It is a *recommendatory* body on inter-State, Centre-State and Centre-Union Territory matters. Its duties as set out for the 1990 Council are: investigating and discussing subjects of common interest; making recommendations for better coordination of policy and action; and deliberating on other matters of general interest referred by the Chairman. Compare this with Article 263's list: the dispute-inquiry duty (a) is not part of the 1990 Council's stated duties in the extract.

**Working.** It is to meet at least thrice a year; meetings are held in camera; all questions are decided by consensus. Flag (verify): the rule is 'at least thrice a year', but the Council has not in practice met that often; check the Inter-State Council Secretariat's records for actual meeting dates before quoting any frequency.

**Supporting bodies.** A **Standing Committee** was set up in 1996 for continuous consultation and processing of matters for the Council: the Union Home Minister (chairman), five Union Cabinet Ministers and nine Chief Ministers. The **Inter-State Council Secretariat** was set up in 1991, headed by a Secretary to the Government of India, and since 2011 also acts as the secretariat of the Zonal Councils.

**For the 2025 Prelims.** The Inter-State Council exists because the Constitution permits it under Article 263 and the President established it by order. It is therefore an Article 263 body in a way a Zonal Council (created by statute) is not. The National Security Council is not an Article 263 creation (standard knowledge, not in the extracts: it was set up by executive decision in 1998; verify).

### 4. What the commissions said, and the Zonal Councils

*Objective:* Trace the demand for a permanent Inter-State Council through the commissions, and describe the five Zonal Councils and the North-Eastern Council.

**A demand that kept coming back.** Laxmikanth's chapter on trends in Centre-State relations shows how the Inter-State Council was recommended again and again.

- **Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC), 1966; report 1969.** Chaired first by Morarji Desai and later by K. Hanumanthayya. Among its recommendations: an Inter-State Council under Article 263. The Central government took no action on the ARC's recommendations.
- **Rajamannar Committee, 1969-71.** Appointed by the Tamil Nadu government; recommended among other things that an Inter-State Council be set up immediately. The Centre ignored its report.
- **Sarkaria Commission, appointed 1983; report 1988.** Made 247 recommendations. Among them: a permanent Inter-State Council (the Inter-Governmental Council) under Article 263; reactivation of Zonal Councils; sparing use of Article 356. The Centre implemented 180 of the 247, and the establishment of the Inter-State Council in 1990 is described as the most important.
- **Punchhi Commission, 2007; report 2010.** Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi's commission made over 310 recommendations and concluded that cooperative federalism would be the key to India's unity and development. On this topic it said: suitable amendments to Article 263 are needed to make the Inter-State Council a credible, powerful and fair mechanism; the Council should have a continuing auditing role in concurrent matters; Zonal Councils should meet at least twice a year, with the Council's secretariat as their secretariat; and an Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission should be set up (see module 6).

**Zonal Councils: statutory, not constitutional.** They were established by the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**, which divided the country into five zones and provided a council for each. Parliament made them; the Constitution did not. The zones took account of natural divisions, river systems and means of communication, cultural and linguistic affinity, and the requirements of economic development, security and law and order.

| Zonal Council | Members (as printed in the 6th edition) | Headquarters |
|---|---|---|
| Northern | Himachal Pradesh, Haryana, Punjab, Rajasthan, Delhi, Chandigarh, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh | New Delhi |
| Central | Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh | Allahabad (now Prayagraj) |
| Eastern | Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha | Kolkata |
| Western | Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu | Mumbai |
| Southern | Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Puducherry | Chennai |

**Composition of each Zonal Council.** The Union Home Minister; the Chief Ministers of all States in the zone; two other Ministers from each State; and the Administrator of each Union Territory in the zone. Advisers without a vote may include a person nominated by the Planning Commission (a body since replaced by NITI Aayog; standard knowledge), and the chief secretary and development commissioner of each State. The Home Minister is the common chairman of all five Councils, and each Chief Minister serves as vice-chairman by rotation for one year.

**Functions.** They are only deliberative and advisory. They discuss matters such as economic and social planning, linguistic minorities, border disputes and inter-State transport. Their objectives include achieving emotional integration, arresting acute State-consciousness, regionalism and linguism, helping remove the after-effects of separation in some cases, securing Centre-State cooperation, speedy execution of major development projects, and securing political equilibrium between regions.

**North-Eastern Council.** In addition, a North-Eastern Council was created by a separate Act of Parliament, the North-Eastern Council Act, 1971 (it came into existence on 8 August 1972). Its members are Assam, Manipur, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Meghalaya, Tripura and Sikkim, the last added in 2002. It formulates a unified regional plan and reviews the member States' measures for security and public order.

### 5. Article 261 (full faith and credit), the Supreme Court link and what happens to criminals across State lines

*Objective:* Explain Article 261 clause by clause, link Article 131 to GS-186, and state the limits of the extracts on extradition between States.

**Article 261: full faith and credit.** Each State's jurisdiction is confined to its own territory, so the acts and records of one State might not be recognised in another. The Constitution therefore contains the 'Full Faith and Credit' clause (Laxmikanth, p. 378). It lays down three things:

1. **Full faith and credit is to be given throughout India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Centre and every State.** The expression 'public acts' covers legislative and executive acts of government. 'Public record' includes any official book, register or record made by a public servant in discharging official duties.
2. **Parliament decides how such acts, records and proceedings are to be proved and what effect they have.** So the general rule is subject to Parliament's power to lay down the mode of proof and the effect.
3. **Final judgments and orders of civil courts in any part of India can be executed anywhere in India without a fresh suit.** This applies only to civil judgments and not to criminal ones. In other words, it does not require the courts of a State to enforce the penal laws of another State.

A simple example: a certificate issued by an official in one State is accepted as genuine by another State's offices; a civil court decree for money from one State can be put into execution in another.

**Why only civil?** Penal law is closely tied to each State's own territory and policing. A judgment that someone owes money is portable; a conviction is enforced by the State that tried the accused. This explanation of purpose is mine, not a quotation.

**The Supreme Court link (Article 131; see GS-186).** The Constitution gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over disputes between governments. As stated in the standard accounts (not in the chapter 15 extract; verify against GS-186), these include disputes between the Union and one or more States, between the Union and States on one side and other States on the other, and between two or more States, where the dispute involves a question on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends. The Inter-State Council's dispute-inquiry function is complementary to this: the Council advises on any controversy, legal or not, while the court decides legal ones with a binding judgment. Note the contrast with Article 262: the power to exclude courts from jurisdiction is given to Parliament for *water* disputes only.

**Extradition between States: a flag.** The brief for this topic mentions inter-State extradition. The extracts do not cover it. In standard usage, 'extradition' means surrender of an accused between *countries*. Within India, the movement of an accused between States is handled through the ordinary criminal procedure on arrest and warrants issued by courts in one State and executed in another, and not by Article 261, which expressly does not require enforcement of another State's penal laws. This statement rests on standard knowledge, not on the extract, so it is marked for verification.

**Article 261 in the toolbox.** It is not a dispute-settlement body. It is a background rule that prevents disputes from arising by making each State recognise the others' official acts and civil judgments.

### 6. Articles 301 to 307: free trade between States, with limits

*Objective:* State what Article 301 guarantees, the restrictions allowed by Articles 302 to 305, the status of Articles 306 and 307, and why the Inter-State Trade Commission matters.

Articles 301 to 307 in Part XIII of the Constitution deal with trade, commerce and intercourse within the territory of India (Laxmikanth, p. 379).

**Article 301.** Trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free. The object is to break down border barriers between States and make the country one unit for the free flow of trade. Two points trip students:

- The freedom is **not limited to inter-State trade**. It extends to intra-State trade and commerce as well. So Article 301 is violated whether restrictions are imposed at the frontier of a State or at any earlier or later stage.
- It is a freedom from restrictions **except** those that Part XIII itself allows (Articles 302 to 305).

**What restrictions are allowed?**

1. **Parliament** may impose restrictions on trade, commerce and intercourse between States or within a State in the public interest (Article 302). But Parliament **cannot give preference to one State over another or discriminate between States**, except in the case of scarcity of goods in any part of India (Article 303). An example of a Parliamentary law that controls supply is the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, which enables the Centre to control the production, supply and distribution of certain essential commodities.
2. A **State legislature** may impose *reasonable* restrictions in the public interest on trade with or within that State, but a Bill for the purpose can be introduced only with the **previous sanction of the President**. The State also cannot give preference to one State over another or discriminate between States (Article 304).
3. A State legislature may impose on goods imported from other States or Union Territories any tax to which similar goods manufactured in that State are subject. This stops discriminatory taxes against outside goods.
4. The freedom is subject to **nationalisation laws**, that is, laws providing monopolies in favour of the Centre or the States (Article 305). Parliament or a State legislature may make laws for the government to carry on any trade, business, industry or service, to the exclusion of citizens in whole or in part.

(The Article numbers 302 to 305 in this list follow Laxmikanth's Table 15.3 titles: 302 power of Parliament to impose restrictions; 303 restrictions on legislative powers of the Union and States; 304 restrictions among States; 305 saving of existing laws and State monopolies. Assigning each rule to a precise sub-clause is standard text, verify at legislative.gov.in.)

**Articles 306 and 307.** Article 306 (power of certain Part B States to impose restrictions) has been **repealed**. Article 307 lets Parliament **appoint an authority** to carry out the purposes of Articles 301 to 304 and give it the necessary powers and duties. Laxmikanth says that **no such authority has been appointed so far**; in the USA the equivalent is called the Inter-State Commerce Commission.

**Why does it matter now?** The **Punchhi Commission** (2010) recommended steps to set up an **Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission** under Article 307, read with Entry 42 of the Union List. It should have both advisory and executive roles with decision-making powers; as a constitutional body its decisions would be final and binding on all States and the Union, and an aggrieved party could appeal to the Supreme Court. The commission's terms of reference also asked about the need for freeing inter-State trade to build a unified domestic market. The GST regime (101st Amendment 2016; GST in force from 1 July 2017; see module 8) supplies a different kind of institution for the tax side of the same goal: a single national market.

**Quick contrast to remember.** Article 262 handles water disputes through tribunals. Article 263 handles coordination through a council. Articles 301-307 handle trade by a guarantee plus allowed restrictions, with a commission still unbuilt. Three different tools for three different problems.

### 7. Border disputes, political settlement and asymmetric federalism

*Objective:* Distinguish legal from political disputes, describe the recurring border and water disputes in NCERT, and define asymmetric federalism with India's examples.

NCERT's federalism chapter (chapter 7) separates two kinds of friction.

**Centre-State and State-State friction.** NCERT says that while the judiciary acts as the arbitration mechanism on disputes of a legal nature, inter-State disputes are in reality not merely legal; they have political implications and are best resolved through negotiation and mutual understanding. That is why the toolbox contains political forums as well as courts.

**Border disputes.** States claim territories belonging to neighbours. Language is the basis for defining State boundaries, but border areas often have populations speaking more than one language, so it is not easy to settle on the basis of a linguistic majority. NCERT's examples:

- **Maharashtra and Karnataka over the city of Belgaum** (a long-standing dispute).
- **Manipur and Nagaland**, a long-standing border dispute.
- **Punjab and Haryana over Chandigarh**, after Haryana was carved out of Punjab. The city houses the capital of both States. In 1985 the then Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi reached an understanding with Punjab's leadership that Chandigarh would be handed to Punjab, but NCERT notes that this has not happened yet.

Border disputes are described as more about sentiment. Where does a border dispute go? Parliament's power to form new States and alter the boundaries or names of any State rests with it, subject to securing the views of the State legislature concerned (NCERT, p. 161). So a boundary change is a legislative and political decision, not one for a tribunal. The Zonal Councils also list border disputes among the matters they can discuss.

**River waters** are called even more serious because of their link with drinking water and agriculture. NCERT cites the Cauvery dispute between Tamil Nadu and Karnataka (which reached the Supreme Court despite a tribunal) and the Narmada dispute among Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra.

**Asymmetric federalism: meaning.** In a symmetric federation every unit has the same powers and status. In an **asymmetric** federation some units have different arrangements because of their special history or social composition. India is asymmetric in several ways. NCERT's section on special provisions says:

- Representation in the Rajya Sabha is asymmetrical, because States differ in size and population (larger States get more seats).
- The Constitution has special provisions for some States because of their peculiar social and historical circumstances. Most pertain to the **north-eastern States** (Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram and others), because of their sizeable indigenous tribal populations, though NCERT notes these provisions have not stemmed alienation and insurgency in parts of the region. Special provisions also exist for hill States like Himachal Pradesh and for States such as Andhra Pradesh, Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Sikkim and Telangana.
- **Jammu and Kashmir** had special status under Article 370. NCERT records that this status no longer exists: by the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 the State was bifurcated into two Union Territories (Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh) with effect from 31 October 2019.

(Details of Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas and Articles 371 to 371J are standard syllabus content but are not in these extracts; they are covered under other topics.)

### 8. The GST Council (Article 279A): a constitutional forum for cooperative federalism

*Objective:* Describe the GST Council's origin, composition, voting rule, functions and dispute mechanism, and compute why the Centre holds an effective veto.

The 101st Constitution Amendment Act, 2016 paved the way for the Goods and Services Tax. Because running one national tax needs cooperation between the Centre and the States, the amendment inserted **Article 279A**, empowering the President to constitute a **GST Council** by order. The order was issued on 15 September 2016. The Secretariat is in New Delhi, and the Union Revenue Secretary is its ex-officio Secretary (Laxmikanth, chapter 46).

**Vision and mission.** The Council's vision is to establish the highest standards of cooperative federation; the textbook describes it as the first constitutional federal body vested with powers to take all major decisions relating to GST. It is to be guided by the need for a harmonised GST structure and a harmonised national market.

**Composition.** It is a joint forum of the Centre and the States:
- The Union Finance Minister as Chairperson;
- the Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance;
- the Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation, or any other Minister nominated, by each State government.

The State members choose one among themselves as Vice-Chairperson, and decide his term. The Chairperson of the Central Board of Excise and Customs (renamed the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs, CBIC, in 2018) is a permanent invitee without a vote.

**Voting.** One-half of the total members form the quorum. Every decision needs a majority of not less than **three-fourths** of the **weighted votes** of the members present and voting. The Central government's vote has a weight of **one-third** of the total votes cast; all the State governments combined carry **two-thirds**.

**What this means, in arithmetic** (my calculation from the source's rule, with all members voting). Three-fourths of all the weighted votes is 0.75. If the Centre votes against, the maximum 'yes' is the States' two-thirds, which is 0.667. That is less than 0.75, so no decision can pass without the Centre. If the Centre votes yes (0.333), the States must add at least 0.75 minus 0.333, which is 0.417, out of their combined 0.667. That is about 62.5% of the States' weight, so the Centre cannot pass anything alone either. Thus each side needs the other: that is the heart of cooperative federalism, and also the reason for tension when interests differ.

**Functions.** The Council recommends to the Centre and the States on: taxes, cesses and surcharges to be merged into GST; goods and services to be subjected to or exempted from GST; model GST laws and principles for levy, apportionment on inter-State supplies and place of supply; the turnover threshold for exemption; rates including floor rates and bands; special rates for a specified period during a natural calamity; special provisions for certain States (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Jammu and Kashmir (now a Union Territory; the list is as printed in the source), Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, as listed in the source); and any other GST matter.

**Other functions.** It recommends the date on which GST is levied on petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, petrol, natural gas and aviation turbine fuel; it **must establish a mechanism to adjudicate disputes** about its recommendations or their implementation (between the Centre and one or more States, between the Centre with some States and other States, or between two or more States); and it recommended compensation to States for revenue loss for a period of five years, on which Parliament enacted the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017.

**Validity safeguard.** Proceedings of the Council are not invalid merely because of a vacancy or defect in its constitution or in a member's appointment, or any procedural irregularity that does not affect the merits.

**Contrast with the Inter-State Council.** The GST Council is created by the Constitution itself (Article 279A), while the Inter-State Council is optional under Article 263. The GST Council takes decisions by a weighted-vote rule; the Inter-State Council decides by consensus and only recommends. Note that many GST recommendations are recommendations in form, and in Mohit Minerals (2022): the Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding on Parliament or State legislatures, a point central to the 2023 Mains question (verify). Also, later developments, such as the compensation period and the treatment of fuels, should be verified against current sources.

### Notes: Definitions for inter-State relations

- Inter-State comity: friendly mutual respect and cooperation between States, promoted by the Constitution through Articles 261, 262, 263 and 301-307.
- Ad hoc tribunal: a body set up for one specific dispute, here for a particular inter-State river dispute under the Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956.
- Inter-State Council: a body the President may establish under Article 263 to coordinate between States and between the Centre and States; the 1990 Council is recommendatory.
- Zonal Council: a statutory body for each of five zones created by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956; deliberative and advisory.
- Full faith and credit: the rule that public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Centre and every State are recognised throughout India (Article 261).
- Asymmetric federalism: a federal arrangement where units do not all have identical powers or status, because of history or social composition.
- Cooperative federalism: Centre and States working together through joint forums, as in the GST Council.

### Notes: Articles at a glance

- Article 261: full faith and credit to public acts, records and judicial proceedings.
- Article 262: adjudication of disputes relating to waters of inter-State rivers or river valleys; Parliament may exclude court jurisdiction.
- Article 263: provisions with respect to an Inter-State Council.
- Article 279A: GST Council, inserted by the 101st Amendment Act, 2016.
- Article 301: freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India.
- Article 302: power of Parliament to impose restrictions in the public interest.
- Article 303: restrictions on legislative powers of the Union and States regarding trade and commerce (no discrimination, scarcity exception).
- Article 304: restrictions on trade, commerce and intercourse among States.
- Article 305: saving of existing laws and laws providing for State monopolies.
- Article 306: repealed.
- Article 307: appointment of an authority to carry out the purposes of Articles 301-304; none appointed so far per Laxmikanth.
- Article 131: Supreme Court's original jurisdiction over disputes between governments (see GS-186).

### Notes: Nine inter-State water dispute tribunals (as printed, up to 2019)

- Three were set up in 1969 (Krishna-I, Godavari, Narmada); the latest in the list is Mahanadi (2018).
- Check for tribunals set up after the source's date.

### Notes: Inter-State Council at a glance

- Basis: Article 263; established by presidential order dated 28 May 1990 (Janata Dal government of V. P. Singh), following the Sarkaria Commission.
- Chair: Prime Minister. Members: Chief Ministers of all States and of Union Territories with legislatures, Administrators of other Union Territories, Governors of States under President's rule, six Union Cabinet Ministers including the Home Minister.
- Permanent invitees: five Ministers of Cabinet rank or Ministers of State (Independent Charge) nominated by the Chairman.
- Meetings: at least three a year; in camera; decisions by consensus.
- Standing Committee (1996): Home Minister as chairman, five Union Cabinet Ministers, nine Chief Ministers.
- Secretariat (1991): headed by a Secretary to the Government of India; also secretariat of the Zonal Councils since 2011.
- Other Article 263 bodies: Central Council of Health and Family Welfare, Central Council of Local Government, four Regional Councils for Sales Tax.

### Notes: Zonal Councils and the North-Eastern Council

- Zonal Councils are statutory (States Reorganisation Act, 1956), advisory and deliberative; the Home Minister is common chairman.
- The North-Eastern Council arises from a separate Act of 1971; Sikkim became the eighth member in 2002.

### Notes: Commissions and their recommendations on inter-State forums

- Administrative Reforms Commission (1966; report 1969): recommended an Inter-State Council under Article 263; no action was taken.
- Rajamannar Committee (Tamil Nadu, 1969; report 1971): recommended an Inter-State Council immediately; ignored by the Centre.
- Sarkaria Commission (1983; report 1988; 247 recommendations, 180 implemented): permanent Inter-State Council; reactivate Zonal Councils.
- Punchhi Commission (2007; report 2010; over 310 recommendations): amend Article 263 to strengthen the Council; auditing role over concurrent matters; Zonal Councils to meet at least twice a year; Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission under Article 307.

### Notes: Dispute-resolution toolbox: political/administrative versus judicial

- Judicial tools give binding answers to legal questions; political and administrative forums build agreement but only recommend.
- Water disputes are a special case: adjudication by an ad hoc tribunal with courts barred.

### Notes: Article 261 full faith and credit

- Public acts include legislative and executive acts; public records include official books, registers and records made by public servants.
- Parliament may lay down the manner of proof and the effect of such acts, records and proceedings.
- Final civil judgments and orders can be executed anywhere in India without a fresh suit; criminal judgments are not covered; penal laws of one State are not enforced by another State's courts under the clause.

### Notes: Articles 301-307 in one view

- The freedom of Article 301 covers intra-State as well as inter-State trade, and is subject to Part XIII restrictions.
- Parliament may restrict in the public interest but cannot discriminate between States, except for scarcity of goods.
- A State legislature may impose reasonable restrictions in the public interest; the Bill needs the President's previous sanction; it cannot discriminate.
- A State may tax imported goods in the same way as similar local goods; nationalisation laws are saved.
- Article 306 is repealed. Article 307 authority has not been appointed per Laxmikanth; the Punchhi Commission urged an Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission.

### Notes: GST Council key facts

- Created under Article 279A inserted by the 101st Amendment Act, 2016; constituted by presidential order of 15 September 2016.
- Chairperson: Union Finance Minister. Quorum: one-half of members. Decision: not less than three-fourths of weighted votes of members present and voting.
- Weights: Centre one-third; all States together two-thirds. Centre has an effective veto; States need about 62.5% of their combined weight with the Centre in favour.
- Functions include rates, exemptions, thresholds, model laws, compensation recommendation for five years (Act of 2017) and a mechanism to adjudicate GST disputes.
- Secretariat in New Delhi; Union Revenue Secretary is ex-officio Secretary.

### Notes: Frequently confused pairs

- Statutory (Zonal Councils, River Boards, tribunals under the 1956 Act) versus constitutional (GST Council, Article 131 jurisdiction) versus enabling (Inter-State Council).
- Article 262 (water) versus Article 263 (council) versus Article 261 (recognition of acts).
- Inter-State Council (political forum) versus Supreme Court (binding legal decision).
- Inter-State Council of 1990 versus Article 263 councils such as the Central Council of Health.
- Zonal Councils (five) versus the North-Eastern Council (separate Act of 1971).
- Article 301 inter-State only (wrong) versus inter-State and intra-State (right).
