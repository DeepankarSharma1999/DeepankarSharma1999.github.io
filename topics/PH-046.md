### Bridge: Bridge: similar triangles and small slopes

A ray in this lesson is a straight line described by two numbers: its **height** h above the axis (a length, positive above) and its **slope** theta (a pure number, positive if the ray rises as it travels to the right). Paraxial means the heights are small compared with the focal lengths and the slopes are so small that tan theta = theta. After travelling a horizontal distance z the height changes by z times the slope, so h_new = h + z theta. That single line is the whole 'free flight' rule.

Similar triangles say: if two triangles have the same angles, their sides are in the same ratio. Take a ray that, after leaving a lens at height h, is heading for a point on the axis a distance f beyond the lens. Its slope is -h/f (the minus sign says it falls). If the ray travels a further distance z, its height is h(1 - z/f); at z = f it reaches the axis. This 'height shrinks linearly to zero at the focus' picture is exactly what a similar-triangle statement says.

Why this matters: every derivation in this lesson is an exercise in updating (h, theta) at each lens and each gap. A thin lens changes only the slope (by -h/f) and leaves h alone; a gap changes only the height. Nothing else is needed.

### 1. Orientation: why two lenses can be replaced by one 'equivalent' system

*Objective:* State what the two-lens problem asks, the result in one line, and the plan of the lesson.

## The question

A camera, a microscope or a telescope never uses one lens. Two thin lenses on a common axis, with a gap d between them, form the smallest 'system'. The Mains question is always the same: **replace the pair by one equivalent lens and say where it sits**. Day 18 lesson PH-044 answered this with the matrix method (module 8, Core: two thin lenses, the focal length and the unit planes). This lesson answers it with **ray geometry**, the route that Brij Lal and Subrahmanyam use in Chapter 4 (PDF pp. 102 to 105) and Chapter 5 (PDF pp. 119 to 124), and then checks the two routes against each other. Having two independent derivations is the best protection against a sign slip in the exam.

## The result you are heading for

Lens 1 has focal length f1, lens 2 has f2, both in air, separated by d. Distances are Cartesian: light travels left to right and a distance to the right of a lens is positive.

$$ 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(f1 f2) = (f1 + f2 − d)/(f1 f2) $$

The pair has two **principal planes** (the planes of unit lateral magnification, met in Day 19 lesson PH-045). The first, H1, lies at a distance alpha = F d / f2 to the right of lens 1. The second, H2, lies at beta = −F d / f1 from lens 2 (negative means to the left of lens 2). Measured from H1 and H2 the ordinary Gauss formula 1/v − 1/u = 1/F works.

## A first picture

For the Day 18 numbers f1 = +10 cm, f2 = +20 cm, d = 25 cm the formula gives F = 40 cm, alpha = +50 cm and beta = −100 cm. The planes are **crossed**: H2 lies to the left of H1. That looks strange, but a principal plane is only a reference plane, not a physical surface, and the book remarks on this reversed order (PDF p. 121, the note after Eq. 5.36).

### 2. Foundation: the words, the sign convention and the ray-update rule

*Objective:* Define the terms of the lesson and use the (height, slope) update rule for a thin lens and for a gap.

## Words defined once

- **Coaxial system**: lenses whose centres lie on one straight line, the axis.
- **Thin lens**: a lens whose thickness is negligible, so a ray enters and leaves at the same height. Its **focal length** f is positive for a converging (convex) lens and negative for a diverging (concave) lens. Its **power** is P = 1/f (dioptre if f is in metres).
- **Equivalent focal length F** of a system: the focal length of the single thin lens that would bend a parallel ray by the same amount. It is measured from a principal plane, not from a lens.
- **Principal planes H1, H2**: a ray that enters the system at height h on the object side and a ray that leaves at height h on the image side look as if one single bend happened between the two planes. They are conjugate planes of lateral magnification +1 (Day 19 lesson PH-045).
- **Focal points**: F2 is where an incoming parallel beam is brought together; F1 is the point whose rays leave the system parallel.
- **Optical interval** (the book's symbol Δ): Δ = f1 + f2 − d with the sign used in this lesson; Brij Lal prints Δ = d − (f1 + f2) in Eq. 4.32 and gets F = −f1 f2 / Δ, the same thing (PDF p. 103). Here we use Δ = f1 + f2 − d, so F = f1 f2 / Δ, to avoid a minus sign.

## Sign convention (Cartesian)

Light travels from left to right. Positions are measured from the lens or plane named: to the right positive, to the left negative. Heights above the axis are positive. A slope theta is positive for a ray that rises to the right. The thin lens formula is 1/v − 1/u = 1/f with u negative for a real object. Brij Lal's Chapter 4 treats the signs with a different footnote convention (footnote, PDF p. 96, as recorded in Day 19 lesson PH-045); that lesson compares the two. We use Cartesian throughout and say so.

## The update rule

State of a ray = (h, theta). Two moves are enough.

$$ Gap of length z (in air):  h → h + z theta,   theta unchanged $$

$$ Thin lens f:  theta → theta − h/f,   h unchanged $$

Check the lens rule: a ray parallel to the axis (theta = 0) at height h > 0 leaves with theta = −h/f, falling towards the axis and reaching it after a distance f. That is the definition of the focal length, so the rule is right. A ray through the centre has h = 0 and is not bent. These two moves are exactly the matrices T = [1 z; 0 1] and L = [1 0; −1/f 1] of Day 18 lesson PH-044, acting on the column (h, theta).

## What the book calls deviation

The book writes the bend of a thin lens as delta = h/f (a size, with direction implied; PDF p. 101, Eq. 4.24 shows it is independent of where the object is). In our signed rule that is theta changing by −h/f. The book's 'deviation produced by the first lens' delta1 = h1/f1 and 'by the second' delta2 = h2/f2 are therefore the two terms of our update rule, and the total deviation is their sum: delta = delta1 + delta2.

## Quick numbers

For f = 20 cm the power is 1/0.20 = +5 D. For f = −50 cm it is −2 D. For lenses in contact (d = 0) the powers simply add, P = P1 + P2 (Brij Lal Eq. 4.26). Separation breaks that simple addition, and the rest of the lesson is about how.

### 3. Core: the equivalent focal length by the deviation method

*Objective:* Derive 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(f1 f2) by following one parallel ray through two lenses.

## Setup

A ray parallel to the axis hits lens 1 at height h1 (theta = 0). We follow it through the pair and ask for the single slope it ends with. Brij Lal uses this 'deviation' argument in section 4.17.1 (PDF pp. 102 to 103, Eqs. 4.27 to 4.32); the steps below are the same logic written with the signed update rule of module 2.

## Derivation, every step

**Step 1: lens 1.** Height h1, slope before = 0. After the lens, slope theta1 = −h1/f1. (Book: delta1 = h1/f1.)

**Step 2: the gap.** The ray travels the distance d, so the new height is

$$ h2 = h1 + d theta1 = h1 (1 − d/f1) $$

In the book this is the similar-triangle statement 'triangle A L1 F1 is similar to triangle B L2 F1', which gives h2 / h1 = (f1 − d)/f1 (PDF p. 103, Eq. 4.28). Here F1 of the book is the second focal point of lens 1, a distance f1 from lens 1.

**Step 3: lens 2.** Slope before = theta1. After the lens,

$$ theta2 = theta1 − h2/f2 = −h1/f1 − h1 (1 − d/f1)/f2 $$

**Step 4: collect.** Factor −h1:

$$ theta2 = −h1 [ 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(f1 f2) ] $$

**Step 5: compare with one lens.** A single thin lens of focal length F, struck by the same parallel ray at height h1, would leave it with slope −h1/F. The pair gives the same bend, so

$$ 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(f1 f2) $$

This is the book's Eq. 4.30 and 4.31. Combining over a common denominator, F = f1 f2 / (f1 + f2 − d).

## Where the ray crosses the axis

After lens 2 the ray has height h2 and slope theta2. It reaches the axis after a distance L2F2 = h2 / (−theta2) = F (1 − d/f1). This is the **back focal distance** (from the second lens, not from a plane). It is positive when the focus is real (to the right of lens 2) and negative when the emerging ray is still diverging so its extension crosses the axis to the left.

## Dimensional check and limits

- Units: each term is 1/length, so the equation is consistent.
- d = 0: 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2, the powers add (the book's Eq. 4.26).
- d = f1 + f2: 1/F = 0, the pair is **afocal** (module 6).
- d > f1 + f2 with both lenses convex: F is negative, the pair diverges (the book's note 1, PDF p. 104).
- Swap the lenses: the formula is symmetric in f1 and f2, so F does not depend on the direction of travel (the book states this, PDF p. 104). The principal-plane positions do change (module 4).

## Medium between the lenses

If the gap holds a medium of index mu, the book's note 2 (PDF p. 104; the extracted formula is garbled, so verify) corresponds to 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(mu f1 f2): the gap acts like an air gap of reduced length d/mu. For f1 = 10, f2 = 20, d = 5 cm and mu = 1.5 this gives F = 7.5 cm (air: 8 cm). Here f1 and f2 are the focal lengths in the actual surroundings (they differ from the in-air values if the gap medium wets the inner faces).

## Numbers for the Day 18 data

With f1 = 10, f2 = 20, d = 25: 1/F = 0.1 + 0.05 − 25/200 = 0.025 cm⁻¹, F = 40 cm. In dioptres: 10 D + 5 D − 0.25 m × 10 × 5 = 2.5 D, F = 0.4 m. Back focal distance L2F2 = 40 (1 − 25/10) = −60 cm: the focus is virtual, 60 cm to the left of lens 2.

### 4. Core: the principal planes and focal points from the same ray

*Objective:* Find alpha, beta and the focal points of the pair from the parallel ray and from its reverse, and check them with book examples.

## The second principal plane H2 (parallel ray from the left)

Brij Lal (section 5.10.1, PDF pp. 119 to 121, Figs. 5.12 and 5.13) extends the incoming ray forwards and the final ray backwards until they meet at height h1. The meeting plane is the second principal plane H2. In signed form: after lens 2 the ray is the line

$$ height(z) = h2 + theta2 z,   z measured from lens 2 $$

It is at the incoming height h1 when z = (h1 − h2)/theta2. Insert h1 − h2 = h1 d / f1 and theta2 = −h1/F:

$$ beta = (h1 − h2)/theta2 = −F d / f1 $$

This is Eq. 5.34 (PDF p. 121) of the book. beta is the distance of H2 from **lens 2**; with d, F and f1 positive it is negative, so H2 lies to the left of lens 2. Check with the equivalent form beta = −f2 d / Δ (this lesson's Δ = f1 + f2 − d; with the book's Δ = d − (f1 + f2) the sign flips): since F = f1 f2 / Δ, F d / f1 = f2 d / Δ. Same.

## The first principal plane H1 (ray from the right)

Reverse the system: light now enters lens 2 first. The same derivation with the roles of the two lenses exchanged gives the distance of the first plane from the **nearer** lens (lens 2 now), −F d / f2 measured leftwards from lens 2 in the reversed picture; turning back to the original frame that plane is at

$$ alpha = + F d / f2   (to the right of lens 1) $$

The book obtains Eq. 5.35 and 5.36 (PDF p. 121) by this symmetry argument. The matrix route of PH-044 gives the same pair (alpha = (D − 1)/C and beta = (1 − A)/C).

## Order of the planes

The sign of alpha and beta follows the sign of F and of the lenses. For two convex lenses with F > 0, alpha = F d/f2 > 0 (H1 to the right of lens 1) and beta = −F d/f1 < 0 (H2 to the left of lens 2). H2 is therefore to the left of H1 exactly when alpha − (d + beta) > 0, that is when F d (1/f2 + 1/f1) > d, i.e. when F (f1 + f2)/(f1 f2) > 1, i.e. when (f1 + f2)/(f1 + f2 − d) > 1, which is true for every 0 < d < f1 + f2. So for two convex lenses that form a converging pair the planes are **always in reversed order**: the book notes this contrast with a thick lens (PDF p. 121, 'Note'). In the 2024 case H2 is 125 cm to the left of H1.

## Focal points

The second focal point F2 is at L2F2 = F (1 − d/f1) from lens 2 (module 3). The first focal point F1 follows by symmetry: its distance from lens 1 is

$$ L1F1 = −F (1 − d/f2) $$

(a negative number means F1 lies to the left of lens 1). The book writes these as Eqs. 5.37 and 5.38. In terms of the planes, F2 is a distance F to the right of H2 and F1 a distance F to the left of H1.

## Checks on book examples

Examples 5.2, 5.10 to 5.12 and the 2024 data are tabulated in the master notes (all recomputed).

## Read-off for the 2024 data

H1 is 50 cm right of lens 1; H2 is 100 cm left of lens 2, i.e. at −75 cm from lens 1; F2 = H2 + 40 = −35 cm from lens 1 (virtual, 60 cm left of lens 2); F1 = H1 − 40 = +10 cm.

### 5. Core: the finite-object proof - the same planes for every object position

*Objective:* Show algebraically that sequential imaging through two lenses is the Gauss formula measured from H1 and H2, with the same alpha, beta and F.

## Why a second proof

Modules 3 and 4 used an object at infinity; a principal plane must work for **every** object. Brij Lal (section 5.10.2, PDF pp. 122 to 124, Eqs. 5.39 to 5.54) therefore images an object at a finite distance through both lenses and compares the result with the Gauss formula. The extract loses many symbols, so the algebra below is rebuilt and checked by computer algebra.

## Step 1: image by lens 1 (Cartesian, distances from each lens)

Object distance u (negative for a real object left of lens 1). Lens 1 gives an image at v1:

$$ 1/v1 − 1/u = 1/f1  ⟹  v1 = u f1 / (u + f1) $$

## Step 2: that image is the object for lens 2

Lens 2 is at distance d, so the object distance for lens 2 is u2 = v1 − d. The final image distance v (from lens 2) obeys 1/v − 1/u2 = 1/f2. Eliminating v1 and clearing denominators gives the book's Eq. 5.41:

$$ u v (d − f1 − f2) + u f2 (f1 − d) + v f1 (d − f2) − d f1 f2 = 0 $$

(The numerical check with the 2024 data is in derivation D2, practice section.)

## Step 3: the equivalent-system form

If the pair acts as one thin lens of focal length F between H1 and H2, the Gauss formula applies with the **reduced distances** U = u − alpha (object distance from H1) and V = v − beta (image distance from H2):

$$ 1/V − 1/U = 1/F $$

Multiply by U V F: F U − F V = U V. With U = u − alpha, V = v − beta:

$$ u v − u (beta + F) + v (F − alpha) + (alpha beta + F alpha − F beta) = 0 $$

(This is Eq. 5.45 of the book.)

## Step 4: compare the coefficients

Divide Eq. 5.41 by (d − f1 − f2) = −Δ with Δ = f1 + f2 − d, so that the u v term has coefficient 1:

$$ u v − u f2 (f1 − d)/Δ − v f1 (d − f2)/Δ + d f1 f2/Δ = 0 $$

Match term by term, using F = f1 f2 / Δ:

- Coefficient of u: beta + F = f2 (f1 − d)/Δ. So beta = [f1 f2 − d f2 − f1 f2]/Δ = −d f2/Δ = −F d / f1.
- Coefficient of v: F − alpha = f1 (f2 − d)/Δ. So alpha = [f1 f2 − f1 f2 + d f1]/Δ = d f1/Δ = F d / f2.
- Constant: alpha beta + F alpha − F beta = [−d² f1 f2 + d f1 f2 (f1 + f2)]/Δ² = d f1 f2 (f1 + f2 − d)/Δ² = d f1 f2/Δ ✓ (it matches automatically; it is a consistency check).

So the **same** alpha and beta as module 4 come out, and the same F = f1 f2 / Δ. The principal planes of the pair are independent of the object: they are properties of the lenses and the gap.

## The practical recipe

Compute F, alpha, beta; take U = u − alpha; solve 1/V = 1/F + 1/U; then v = V + beta and m = V/U. Cross-check lens by lens.

## Check on a book example

Brij Lal Example 5.6 (f1 = +20, f2 = −10, d = 8 cm, object at 50 cm from the convex lens) gives F = −100, alpha = +80, beta = +40, U = −130, V = −56.52, v = −16.52 cm from lens 2 (8.52 cm left of lens 1) and m = 0.435, all as printed; lens by lens, v1 = 33.33, u2 = 25.33 and v = −16.52 ✓.

### 6. Core: special cases - from contact to the telescope

*Objective:* Describe how F, alpha and beta change as d varies, and derive the afocal (telescopic) pair and its angular magnification.

## Sweeping the gap

Keep f1 = +10 and f2 = +20 cm and change d only (all recomputed):

| d (cm) | F (cm) | alpha (cm) | beta (cm) | L2F2 (cm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (contact) | 6.667 | 0 | 0 | 6.667 |
| 10 (= f1) | 10 | 5 | −10 | 0 |
| 15 | 13.33 | 10 | −20 | −6.667 |
| 25 | 40 | 50 | −100 | −60 |
| 29 | 200 | 290 | −580 | −380 |
| 30 (= f1 + f2) | infinite | not defined | not defined | not defined |
| 35 | −40 | −70 | 140 | 100 |

As d rises towards f1 + f2 the pair becomes weaker (F rises) and the planes run far away; at d = f1 + f2 the power is zero; beyond it the pair diverges.

## d = f1 + f2: the afocal pair

Put d = f1 + f2 in the update rule. A parallel ray at height h1 leaves lens 1 with slope −h1/f1, reaches lens 2 at h2 = h1 (1 − d/f1) = −h1 f2/f1, and leaves with slope theta2 = −h1/f1 − h2/f2 = −h1/f1 + h1/f1 = 0. A parallel ray stays parallel, so no focus exists: the power C is zero. The system matrix of Day 18 lesson PH-044 becomes [A B; 0 D] with

$$ A = 1 − d/f1 = −f2/f1,   D = 1 − d/f2 = −f1/f2,   A D = 1 $$

The first says the **height** of a parallel beam is multiplied by −f2/f1 (a beam compressed or widened). The second says the **slope** of any ray is multiplied by D = −f1/f2: the **angular magnification** of the pair is

$$ M = −f1/f2 $$

(sign: negative means the output ray tilts the other way, an inverted image). This is a telescope: lens 1 is the objective (large f1), lens 2 the eyepiece (small f2). Example: f1 = 60, f2 = 5, d = 65 cm: A = −1/12, D = −12, determinant 1 ✓, M = −12. If lens 2 is concave, f2 = −5 cm, then d = f1 + f2 = 55 cm, A = 1 − 55/60 = 0.0833, D = 1 + 11 = 12: M = +12, an erect image (the Galilean arrangement). The optical interval is zero; Brij Lal's Δ is the distance between F2 of lens 1 and F1 of lens 2 (PDF p. 103), so an afocal pair has the focus of the first lens exactly on the focus of the second.

The book itself does not name the telescope case in the extracts; it only remarks that a telescope uses a group of lenses (PDF p. 108). The afocal case here is standard result, derived from the formula the book proves (verify with your class text).

## Other cases worth knowing

- **d = f1** (table row 10): the first lens's focus lands on lens 2, so L2F2 = 0.
- **Convex plus concave:** Brij Lal Example 5.5 (f1 = +50, f2 = −50, d = 10 cm) gives F = +250 cm, alpha = beta = −50 cm: a weak converging pair, although at d = 0 the powers would cancel.

## Cross-check with the matrix

From PH-044, F = −1/C, alpha = (D − 1)/C, beta = (1 − A)/C. For f1 = 10, f2 = 20, d = 25 the matrix is A = −1.5, B = 25, C = −0.025, D = −0.25: F = 40, alpha = (−1.25)/(−0.025) = 50, beta = (2.5)/(−0.025) = −100 ✓. The ray method and the matrix agree.

### 7. Core: the Huygens and Ramsden eyepieces as two-lens systems

*Objective:* Compute the focal length and cardinal points of the Huygens and Ramsden eyepieces from the two-lens formulae.

## What an eyepiece is

An **eyepiece** (ocular) is the lens system a person looks through in a microscope or telescope. Two common designs use two plano-convex lenses of the same glass. Brij Lal treats them as two-lens problems by the matrix method (Examples 7.6 and 7.7, PDF pp. 175 to 177) and asks for their cardinal points in the questions (PDF pp. 132 and 177). The geometry details (which face is turned where) matter for aberrations; those belong to Day 21 lesson PH-047, and here we need only f1, f2 and d.

## Huygens eyepiece (book Example 7.7)

Focal lengths 3f and f, separation 2f.

$$ 1/F = 1/(3f) + 1/f − 2f/(3f · f) = (1 + 3 − 2)/(3f) = 2/(3f)  ⟹  F = 3f/2 $$

$$ alpha = F d / f2 = (3f/2)(2f)/f = 3f,   beta = −F d / f1 = −(3f/2)(2f)/(3f) = −f $$

So H1 is 3f to the right of lens 1 and H2 is f to the left of lens 2, i.e. at 2f − f = f from lens 1. H1 is at 3f from lens 1, beyond lens 2 (which is at 2f), so H1 lies **outside** the pair, to the right of lens 2; H2 is at f from lens 1, between the lenses. Nodal planes coincide with principal planes in air. The book's Example 5.1 is the same problem (3F, F, 2F, with F as the unit): it prints alpha = 3F, beta = −F ✓. Focal points: L2F2 = F(1 − d/f1) = (3f/2)(1/3) = f/2 (real, f/2 beyond lens 2) and L1F1 = −F(1 − d/f2) = −(3f/2)(1 − 2) = +3f/2. The first focal point lies **between** the lenses, at 3f/2 from lens 1. Light from the objective converges towards F1, but lens 1 intercepts it and forms the real intermediate image at f from lens 1 (1/v = 1/(3f) + 1/(1.5f) gives v = f), the first focal plane of lens 2, so the final image is at infinity. A cross-wire placed there would be seen through lens 2 alone, so the Huygens eyepiece is generally not used with cross-wires or scales (standard knowledge; verify).

## Ramsden eyepiece (book Example 7.6)

Two lenses of focal length f. The text layer splits 2f/3 into '2', '3', 'f'; the translation matrix, b = c = 1/3 and the book's Problem 5 (PDF p. 178: f = 4 cm, F = 3 cm, plane distances ±2 cm) all fit d = 2f/3 = 8/3 cm.

$$ 1/F = 2/f − (2f/3)/f² = 4/(3f)  ⟹  F = 3f/4 $$

$$ alpha = F d/f = (3f/4)(2f/3)/f = f/2,   beta = −f/2 $$

### 8. Master: achromatism of a separated pair (the 2023 and 2026 questions)

*Objective:* Derive the condition for an achromatic pair of lenses separated by d and solve the verified numerical.

## What 'achromatic' means here

A glass has slightly different refractive indices for blue (mu_B) and red (mu_R) light, so a lens has a slightly different focal length for each colour: a **chromatic aberration**. A pair is called **achromatic** (for the focal length) if its equivalent focal length F is the same for the two colours, so that a distant object gives equal-sized images in the two colours (see the assumptions below). The wider study of chromatic and spherical aberration is Day 21 lesson PH-047; here we need only the **condition**, which the book lists as questions on PDF p. 133 (Questions 15 to 18).

**Dispersive power** omega of a glass: omega = (mu_B − mu_R)/(mu_Y − 1), where mu_Y is the index for a mean (yellow) colour. This definition is standard (not in the supplied extract; verify). For a thin lens, power P = (mu − 1) K where K = 1/R1 − 1/R2 depends only on geometry. Hence the change of P between blue and red is dP = (mu_B − mu_R) K = omega P.

## Derivation for a separated pair

Start from P = P1 + P2 − d P1 P2, with d fixed (the lens mounts do not change with colour). Change colour; each lens has dP1 = omega1 P1, dP2 = omega2 P2:

$$ dP = omega1 P1 + omega2 P2 − d (P2 dP1 + P1 dP2) = omega1 P1 + omega2 P2 − d P1 P2 (omega1 + omega2) $$

Achromatism: dP = 0, so omega1 P1 + omega2 P2 = d P1 P2 (omega1 + omega2). Divide by P1 P2:

$$ omega1/P2 + omega2/P1 = d (omega1 + omega2) $$

With P = 1/f this reads omega1 f2 + omega2 f1 = d (omega1 + omega2):

$$ d = (omega1 f2 + omega2 f1)/(omega1 + omega2) $$

A common slip is the symmetric-looking form (omega1 f1 + omega2 f2)/(omega1 + omega2): with the labelling used here (omega1 belongs to the lens of focal length f1) it is wrong, as the numerical check below shows. If your class notes print a different arrangement, test it with that check.

**Same glass:** omega1 = omega2, so d = (f1 + f2)/2, the Huygens eyepiece condition (module 7).

**Lenses in contact (d = 0):** omega1 P1 + omega2 P2 = 0, i.e. omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0. One lens must be convex and one concave (Brij Lal Problems 3 and 4 on PDF p. 133).

## The 2023 numerical

omega1 = 0.020, omega2 = 0.028, f1 = 10 cm, f2 = 5 cm:

$$ d = (0.020 × 5 + 0.028 × 10)/(0.020 + 0.028) = (0.10 + 0.28)/0.048 = 7.917 cm $$

Check by perturbation: P1 = 0.1, P2 = 0.2 cm⁻¹; for the colour change s (small), P1(s) = 0.1(1 + 0.020 s), P2(s) = 0.2(1 + 0.028 s); P(s) = P1 + P2 − 7.917 P1 P2. dP/ds at s = 0 is 0 to 10⁻¹¹ with d = 7.917, and 8 × 10⁻⁴ (not zero) with d = 7.083, the value of the swapped form. At d = 7.917 cm the pair has F = 1/(0.3 − 7.917 × 0.02) = 7.06 cm.

## Assumptions and meaning

1. Thin lenses in air, with d fixed.
2. dP_i = omega_i P_i, with omega referred to the mean index (a first-order statement).
3. Only F is made colour-independent. A distant object therefore gives images of equal angular size (no lateral colour), but the back focal distance F(1 − d/f1) still varies with colour, so the coloured images are not coincident; this is why the separated condition suits eyepieces. Spherical aberration is untouched (PH-047).

### Notes: Definitions

- Coaxial thin-lens pair: two thin lenses on one axis, focal lengths f1 and f2, separated by d, both in air.
- Equivalent focal length F: focal length of the single thin lens (placed between the principal planes) that bends a parallel ray exactly as the pair does.
- Principal planes H1, H2: conjugate planes of lateral magnification +1; alpha locates H1 from lens 1, beta locates H2 from lens 2.
- Focal points: F2 (image-side) is at L2F2 = F(1 − d/f1) from lens 2; F1 (object-side) is at L1F1 = −F(1 − d/f2) from lens 1.
- Afocal pair (telescopic): d = f1 + f2, power zero, a parallel beam leaves parallel.
- Optical interval (this lesson): Δ = f1 + f2 − d, so F = f1 f2 / Δ (the book writes Δ = d − (f1 + f2) and F = −f1 f2/Δ).
- Dispersive power omega = (mu_B − mu_R)/(mu_Y − 1) (standard definition; verify): the fractional change of a thin lens's power between blue and red.

### Notes: Equations to know (Cartesian convention)

- Ray update: gap z: h → h + z theta; thin lens: theta → theta − h/f.
- 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(f1 f2) = (f1 + f2 − d)/(f1 f2); in dioptres P = P1 + P2 − d P1 P2 (d in metres).
- alpha = F d / f2 (H1 from lens 1); beta = −F d / f1 (H2 from lens 2).
- L2F2 = F (1 − d/f1); L1F1 = −F (1 − d/f2).
- Equivalent-system imaging: U = u − alpha, 1/V − 1/U = 1/F, v = V + beta, m = V/U.
- Afocal: d = f1 + f2, A = −f2/f1, D = −f1/f2, angular magnification M = −f1/f2.
- With a medium of index mu in the gap: 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(mu f1 f2) (verify; the extract is garbled).
- Achromatism of a separated pair: d = (omega1 f2 + omega2 f1)/(omega1 + omega2); same glass d = (f1 + f2)/2; contact omega1/f1 + omega2/f2 = 0.
- Matrix cross-check: F = −1/C, alpha = (D − 1)/C, beta = (1 − A)/C with A = 1 − d/f1, B = d, C = −(1/f1 + 1/f2 − d/(f1 f2)), D = 1 − d/f2.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- F is measured from the principal planes; the distances from the lenses are the back and front focal distances (F(1 − d/f1) and −F(1 − d/f2)).
- alpha is measured from lens 1; beta from lens 2: never from the same lens.
- A positive F does not mean a real focus: in the 2024 data F > 0 and L2F2 = −60 cm (virtual).
- The 'equivalent lens' position depends on the direction of the light (book, PDF p. 104); F does not.
- Achromatism equalises F for two colours; it does not remove spherical aberration (Day 21 lesson PH-047).
- Afocal does not mean no image: it means the image of a distant object is also at infinity (telescope).

      - Lens 1: v1 = u f1/(u + f1). Gap: u2 = v1 − d. Lens 2: 1/v − 1/u2 = 1/f2.

- Ray state (h, θ), Cartesian: gap h → h + zθ; thin lens θ → θ − h/f
- Deviation method: one parallel ray through both lenses gives 1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − d/(f₁f₂)
