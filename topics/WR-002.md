### Bridge: Six words for brainstorming and thesis

WR-001-B2 defined stance, thesis, counter-view and dimension. Six more words appear here.

- **Diverge**: to produce many ideas without judging them.
- **Converge**: to sort and select among those ideas.
- **Central question (CQ)**: one sentence asking what the essay must settle; the thesis answers it.
- **Concession**: a paragraph or sentence that fairly states the strongest counter-view before explaining why your thesis still holds.
- **Truism**: a statement so obviously true that nobody would argue it, such as 'education is important'. A truism is not a thesis.
- **Fence-sitting**: refusing to take a position, for example 'there are advantages and disadvantages'.

A tiny illustration. Topic: 'Silence is a form of speech.' Central question: when is silence a form of speech? Truism: 'Silence is common.' Fence-sitting: 'Silence can both help and hurt.' A thesis: 'Silence communicates only where both people share a code that lets them read it; elsewhere it is mere absence.'

### 1. Where brainstorming and thesis fit in the Essay paper

*Objective:* Place brainstorming and thesis choice in the essay-writing chain, and state what each produces in ten to fifteen minutes.

## The chain

An essay is written in steps. WR-001 taught the first: **decode** the topic (paraphrase, key terms, demand, scope, stance options, dimensions, counter-view). This lesson teaches the next two.

1. **Decode** the topic (WR-001).
2. **Brainstorm**: generate material and tensions about the topic. *Output: a grid of angles, examples and objections.*
3. **Choose a thesis**: decide what the essay will argue. *Output: one sentence.*
4. Outline, write, check: later Essay topics in the registry (not yet written at this point).

Four terms, used from now on.

- **Brainstorm**: to produce many ideas quickly before judging any of them.
- **Dimension**: an angle from which the topic can be examined (social, economic and so on; WR-001-B2).
- **Thesis**: your stance written as one clear sentence that the whole essay defends.
- **Reject**: to throw out a weak thesis deliberately, with a reason.

## Why a thesis matters here

The official Essay clause (registry entry Mains Essay-1) asks candidates to keep closely to the subject, to arrange ideas in orderly fashion, to write concisely and to express themselves effectively and exactly. A thesis serves the first three at once: it fixes what the essay is about, gives every paragraph a job, and tells you what to leave out. An essay without a thesis tends to become a list of loosely related points.

## Time and facts to verify

The project's summary of the paper is two essays of about 1,000 to 1,200 words in three hours, carrying 250 marks (WR-001 module 1). These are planning figures; confirm them against the instructions printed on the paper. If the three hours were split evenly, each essay would get 90 minutes, so ten to fifteen minutes of planning is roughly one-ninth to one-sixth of that. This is a practice target, not an official rule.

UPSC publishes no model essay and no marking scheme that this project has found. Nothing here is an examiner expectation; the method is a way to be relevant and exact.

### 2. Brainstorming with a dimension grid

*Objective:* Run a four-minute brainstorm that first diverges (free list) and then fills a grid of dimensions and probe questions.

## Diverge, then converge

Good brainstorming has two phases. **Diverge**: for about one minute write whatever comes to mind (words, cases, names) without judging it. **Converge**: for about three minutes sort that material into a grid, and add what the grid shows is missing. The grid stops you from writing only about what you happen to know.

## The grid

Rows are dimensions. Use WR-001's nine lenses (WR-001 module 5): social, economic, political, ethical, environmental, historical, technological, international and gender; add psychological, legal or cultural if the topic needs one. WR-001 shows how to choose among them; here the aim is to *generate*, not choose.

Columns are the four probe questions below, with one short phrase per cell.

| Probe | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Who | Who gains or loses, who decides, who is left out? |
| When | What changed over time, or what will change? |
| Example | One concrete case, place or event (mark *verify* if unsure) |
| Against | What would a thoughtful opponent say from this dimension? |

## A partial grid

Topic: 'A city should be built for walking before it is built for driving.'

| Dimension | Who | When | Example | Against |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Social | Children, the elderly, people without vehicles | Streets were shared before motor traffic | Streets as shared space | Long distances tire the elderly |
| Economic | Street vendors, shops, road builders | Footfall may rise after pedestrianisation (verify) | Footfall and parking cost (verify any figures) | Delivery vehicles need access |
| Environmental | Residents breathing traffic air | (blank) | Heat, noise and air quality | Heat and rain make walking hard |
| Ethical | Everyone has an equal claim to public space | (blank) | Is road space a public good? | Emergency services need speed |

## Rules for a useful grid

1. **One phrase per cell**; write words, not sentences.
2. **Blanks are information.** An empty cell means that dimension is weak for this topic; do not force it.
3. **At least one 'Against' cell must be filled** in some dimension (the Against column is the probe that tests your own view); otherwise you have not tested your own view.
4. **Mark every uncertain fact** with *verify*, so you do not write it as certain.
5. **Stop at four minutes.** The grid feeds the thesis; it is not the essay.

## Two extra axes

If you are stuck, ask the same questions at different levels (individual, community, state, world) or at different times (past, present, future). A level or time shift often shows a tension that a single frame hides.

For a figurative topic, write the literal and figurative senses first (WR-001-B1), then apply Who and Example to the figurative sense. For 'A lamp is judged by the dark it enters' (Example 5, module 7), Who asks who stands in the dark, and Example asks for a case where a programme reached the hardest place.

### 3. From the topic words to the central question

*Objective:* Turn a decoded topic into one central question that a thesis can answer.

## Why a question

A topic is a prompt; a thesis is an answer. The step between them is a **central question** (CQ): one sentence that asks what the essay must settle. Without it, brainstormed material has nothing to answer.

WR-001 modules 3 and 4 explain how to read key terms, qualifiers and the implicit command. Take the result of that step as given and ask: *what is the real question behind these words?*

## Four patterns

| Topic type | Typical CQ |
|---|---|
| Claim ('X is Y') | How far, and under what conditions, is the claim true? |
| Comparison or order ('A before B', 'more A than B') | Which should come first or count more, and when does the order reverse? |
| Concept or aphorism | What does it mean, and what follows if it is true? |
| Question topic | What is your answer, and what would change it? |

For a quotation or metaphor, first write the literal sense and then the figurative sense in plain words (WR-001-B1), and form the CQ from the figurative sense.

## Three tests for a CQ

1. **Key-word test.** It uses the topic's key words (or exact equivalents) and keeps its qualifier ('before', 'more ... than', 'can', 'least').
2. **Two-answer test.** A thoughtful person could answer it differently. If everyone answers the same, it is a topic and not a question.
3. **Size test.** It could be answered in 1,000 to 1,200 words (the project's planning guide). 'What is the future of cities?' fails this test.

## Examples

- Topic: 'A city should be built for walking before it is built for driving.' CQ: *Should a city be built for walking before driving, and when should that order give way?*
- Topic: 'Rainwater harvesting is more a habit than a technology.' CQ: *Is rainwater harvesting more a habit than a technology: does it succeed because of its equipment or because people keep using and maintaining it?*
- Topic: 'Silence is a form of speech.' CQ: *When is silence a form of speech, and when is it mere absence?*

## A common error

Writing a CQ about the *theme* ('How can cities become sustainable?') instead of the subject. Each of the three examples keeps the topic's own operative words: 'walking before driving', 'more a habit than a technology', 'a form of speech'. If the CQ could be asked of ten different topics, it is too general.

### 4. Choosing a stance and writing the thesis

*Objective:* Choose among four stances, write a thesis with claim, reason and boundary, and test it against an eight-item checklist.

## Four stances

After the grid and the CQ, choose a **stance**.

1. **Agree**: the claim is true as a general rule, with its scope stated (checklist item 4).
2. **Agree with conditions**: true only when named conditions hold.
3. **Disagree**: false or misleading, with a reason.
4. **Reframe**: the claim answers the wrong question; a better question is offered and answered.

'Agree with conditions' is not fence-sitting. The conditions must be *named* and they must carry the argument. 'Both sides have merits' names nothing.

## The thesis formula

A usable thesis has three parts: **claim, because, boundary**.

> *Claim* (what you hold), *because* (the main reason), *boundary* (when it holds or fails).

Example: 'Walking-first planning should be the default for city streets, because street space is shared public property and walking is the one mode everyone uses; cars deserve priority only where distance or need leaves no alternative.'

## The thesis-quality checklist

Test each candidate against these eight items. A good thesis passes all of them.

1. **On subject**: uses the topic's key words or close equivalents (walking-first for 'walking before driving') and keeps its qualifier.
2. **Debatable**: a thoughtful person could argue the opposite.
3. **Specific**: says something that would not fit ten other topics.
4. **Bounded**: names a condition, scope or limit.
5. **Supportable**: you can name at least three distinct dimensions, each with a concrete example.
6. **Fair**: leaves room for the strongest counter-view as a concession.
7. **One sentence**: if it needs two, the idea is not yet clear.
8. **Doable**: can be argued within 1,000 to 1,200 words.

## Choosing between candidates

Write two or three candidate theses and score each 0, 1 or 2 on each item. Total them and prefer the highest. If two tie, prefer the one with more *ready examples*. You may adopt a stance you hold only mildly if you can support it better; an essay is judged on reasoning and exactness, not on private belief. Choose the stance *after* the grid, not before it: an early stance makes the grid a hunt for supporting points.

### 5. Rejecting weak theses: seven patterns and repairs

*Objective:* Recognise seven kinds of weak thesis, name the defect, and repair each.

## Seven weak patterns

Topic for all examples: 'A city should be built for walking before it is built for driving.'

| Pattern | Example | Defect |
|---|---|---|
| Restated topic | 'Cities should be built for walking before driving.' | Repeats the prompt; no reason, no boundary |
| Truism | 'Cities are important for development.' | Nobody disagrees; not about the subject |
| Fence-sitting | 'Walking and driving both have advantages and disadvantages.' | Takes no position |
| List of points | 'Walking is healthy, cheap and clean.' | A list is not a claim; no order or tension |
| Absolute | 'Cars must be banned from all cities.' | Too extreme to defend; ignores the word 'before' |
| Drift | 'Pollution is the biggest threat to human health.' | Not the subject; this is a theme |
| Slogan | 'Humanity must change its ways.' | Too grand; no reason or limit |

## Three rejection tests

1. **Opponent test.** Can a thoughtful person argue the opposite? If not, reject it.
2. **So-what test.** If you wrote the thesis and stopped, would the reader learn what you will prove? If not, reject it.
3. **Paragraph test.** Can you name three paragraphs that would defend it, each from a different dimension? If you can name only one kind of paragraph, reject it.

The opponent and paragraph tests are the quick forms of checklist items 2 and 5, so you hold the eight items, not eleven separate tests.

## Repair

Repairs add what is missing: a position, a reason, a boundary.

- Fence-sitting repaired: 'Walking-first should be the default, because street space is shared and walking is the one mode everyone uses; but it fails unless streets are safe, lit and connected to transit.'
- List of points repaired: 'Walking-first planning matters less for what it gives pedestrians than for who it includes: children, the elderly and those without vehicles.'
- Absolute repaired: 'Cars should lose priority in dense centres, not in the whole city, because distance and goods movement still need roads.'
- Drift repaired: return to the subject, 'who gets priority in street design?', before any pollution argument.

## Rejection is not failure

Rejecting two weak theses in the first minutes is part of the method. A rejected thesis is often a source: the fence-sitting thesis usually contains your conditions, and the list of points usually contains your dimensions. Keep the useful pieces and discard the sentence.

### 6. Worked examples 1 and 2

*Objective:* Follow the whole chain (grid, central question, candidate theses, rejection, final thesis) on two original topics.

These are original topics and one reasonable reading of each. Examples marked *verify* must be checked before use.

## Example 1: 'A city should be built for walking before it is built for driving.'

- **Decode (short).** 'Built for' means design priority; 'before' means order of priority, not exclusion.
- **Grid highlights.** Social (children, elderly), economic (vendors, footfall), environmental (air, heat), ethical (equal claim to public space), historical (cities were walkable before motor vehicles), against (goods movement, long distances, safety after dark).
- **CQ.** Should a city be built for walking before driving, and when should that order give way?
- **Candidates.** (a) 'Walking is better than driving.' It changes the qualifier ('before' to 'better than') and gives no reason: rejected. (b) 'Walking and driving both matter.' Fence-sitting: rejected. (c) 'Walking-first should be the default for city streets, because street space is shared and walking is the one mode everyone uses; priority passes to vehicles where distance or need leaves no alternative, and walking-first fails unless streets are safe and connected to transit.'
- **Choice.** (c) passes the checklist on its wording (items 1 to 4, 6 and 7); item 5 needs a concrete example for each dimension, such as a named pedestrian-priority street and a market with vendors (verify any case before use); item 8 is a practice judgement. Dimensions: ethical, social, economic, environmental, with safety as the complicating one.
- **Counter-view for a concession paragraph.** Cities with long commutes and heavy goods traffic cannot be walked end to end.

## Example 2: 'Rainwater harvesting is more a habit than a technology.'

- **Decode (short).** 'More ... than' sets a comparison; *habit* means a routine that people sustain; *technology* means equipment and design. The topic does not ask how to build a tank.
- **Grid highlights.** Social (household and community upkeep), economic (cheap hardware, recurring maintenance), environmental (recharge of groundwater), technological (pits, tanks, filters), political (rules and subsidies), historical (older water-storage systems; verify examples), against (dense cities with little space need engineering).
- **CQ.** Is rainwater harvesting more a habit than a technology: does it succeed because of its equipment or because people keep using and maintaining it?
- **Candidates.** (a) 'Technology is crucial to rainwater harvesting.' Absolute (it drops the comparison and ignores 'habit'): rejected. (b) 'Both habit and technology are important.' Fence-sitting: rejected. (c) 'Harvesting depends more on habit than technology, because the equipment is simple and old while neglected pits and tanks are a frequent cause of failure (verify with an example); so schemes that pay for hardware without building upkeep habits waste money, though dense cities need real engineering.'
- **Choice.** (c): conditional, specific and bounded. The three dimensions are social, economic and political, with technological as the complicating one.

## What to notice

In both examples, the chosen thesis is the one that keeps the topic's own comparison ('before'; 'more ... than') and adds a boundary. The rejected theses lost the comparison.

### 7. Worked examples 3 to 5

*Objective:* Apply the chain to a policy claim with a modal word and to an abstract inverse-relationship topic, and to a figurative topic.

## Example 3: 'Social audits can make governments honest.'

- **Decode (short).** A *social audit* is a public review of a programme's records and delivery by the people it serves, usually in an open meeting (the MGNREGA Act, 2005, provides for social audit of the scheme in Section 17; verify before citing). *Honest*: truthful and free of fraud. *Can* is a modal word: the claim is about possibility, so conditions decide it.
- **Grid highlights.** Political (who acts on the findings), ethical (truth against retaliation), social (participation of beneficiaries), economic (money recovered or not; verify), technological (open data), against (audits catch small officials, not the powerful).
- **CQ.** Can social audits make governments honest, and under what conditions?
- **Candidates.** (a) 'Social audits are good for transparency.' Truism: rejected. (b) 'Corruption is a major problem in India.' Drift: rejected. (c) 'Social audits can make governments honest only when auditors can reach the records, are protected from retaliation, and findings lead to action; without these they become ritual.'
- **Choice.** (c). It turns the modal 'can' into three named conditions. Dimensions: political, ethical, social, technological.
- **Concession.** Audits may reach local staff more easily than the senior officials who benefit.

## Example 4: 'The more connected we are, the less we listen.'

- **Decode (short).** *The more ... the less* is an inverse relationship. *Connected* may mean technological links or social ties; *listen* means attending with openness, not merely hearing. Decide which meanings you will use.
- **Grid highlights.** Psychological (attention is limited), social (shallow versus deep ties), technological (constant notifications), ethical (a duty to hear others), political (public debate and echo chambers), economic (attention-selling business models), against (helplines, community meetings and classrooms use connection to improve listening).
- **CQ.** Do we listen less the more connected we are, or only when connection is constant and shallow?
- **Candidates.** (a) 'Technology has destroyed listening.' Absolute: rejected. (b) 'Connection has pros and cons.' Fence-sitting: rejected. (c) 'Connection lowers listening when it is constant and shallow, but where it is purposeful it can raise it; so the problem lies in how connection is designed, not in being connected.'
- **Choice.** (c) *reframes* the cause from connection to design. Dimensions: psychological, social, technological, ethical.
- **Concession.** Even purposeful connection runs against a limit: no one can listen to everyone.

## Example 5: 'A lamp is judged by the dark it enters.' (figurative)

- **Literal sense.** A lamp's worth is measured by how dark the place is where it is lit.
- **Figurative sense.** An institution should be judged by the hardest conditions it reaches, not by results where things are already easy. 'Judged' implies a standard.
- **Grid highlights.** Who stands in the dark: the remote and the unserved. Ethical (a duty to reach the last person), economic (cost per person rises in the dark), political (targets reward easy places). Example: a health scheme judged by reach in remote hamlets (verify any case). Against: averages are also fair measures, and chasing hard cases can neglect easy gains.
- **CQ.** Should an institution be judged by the difficulty of the conditions it enters, and when is that the wrong test?
- **Candidates.** (a) 'Institutions should be judged fairly.' Truism: rejected. (b) 'Results and effort both matter.' Fence-sitting: rejected. (c) 'An institution should be judged by the dark it enters, meaning how well it serves the hardest to reach, because averages hide neglect; but difficulty is no excuse, so the test must also ask whether the lamp lights the room.'
- **Choice.** (c) turns the metaphor into a standard and a boundary. Dimensions: ethical, social, economic, political.

## What to notice

Example 3 turned a modal word into conditions; Example 4 split a general claim into two cases. Example 5 read a metaphor literally and then figuratively. All three avoided the absolute and the fence.

### 8. Timed practice, the self-check and the common mistakes

*Objective:* Run a twelve-minute planning drill, score it with the thesis-quality checklist, and name the three commonest mistakes.

## The twelve-minute drill

A practice target, not an official norm. Use ten minutes for a short drill and fifteen for a hard topic.

| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 2 | Decode (WR-001): paraphrase, key terms, qualifier |
| 4 | Brainstorm: one minute free list, three minutes grid |
| 2 | Central question and its three tests |
| 3 | Two or three candidate theses; score; choose |
| 1 | Check against the eight items; write the counter-view in one line |

If you reach minute twelve with the thesis unchosen, choose the best candidate and move on. A sound thesis chosen at minute twelve beats a perfect one at minute twenty.

## With the WR-001 sheet

In the exam, fill only the Topic, Key terms, Demand and Scope fields of the WR-001 module 6 sheet, in about two minutes (the 'Decode' row). This lesson's grid replaces the sheet's Dimensions, Counter-view and Examples fields, and 'Reframe' is a fourth stance added to WR-001-B2's three. Choosing the topic inside each section (WR-001 module 6 triage) also uses part of the 90 minutes; these are practice figures, not official.

## Self-check

Score the final thesis 0, 1 or 2 on each of the eight checklist items (module 4) and note the total out of 16. This is a self-assessment tool made for this lesson, not an official scale. Record the *lowest-scoring item*; that is your next drill.

## The three commonest mistakes

1. **Topic drift.** The essay answers the theme, not the subject. Check: can you point to the topic's own words in the thesis? Repair: re-read the key terms and the qualifier.
2. **Vagueness.** The thesis uses words such as 'important', 'complex' or 'both sides'. Repair: add a position, a reason and a boundary (module 4).
3. **List of points.** The brainstorm becomes the outline directly, with no claim ordering the points. Repair: write the thesis first, then keep only the points that defend it.

Two further mistakes appear often: choosing the stance before the grid, and listing seven dimensions where three would do (WR-001 suggests three to five).

## After the drill

Wait a day, then look again at your thesis. Ask whether you would still defend it in front of a sceptic. Then write the first two sentences of the essay. Planning that never becomes a first paragraph is rehearsal only. Completing drills is not mastery; scoring and repairing them is the evidence.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Theme versus subject (WR-001): the theme is the broad area, the subject is the topic's own claim.
- Dimension versus stance: 'social' is an angle; 'social media harms the young' is a stance.
- Brainstorm versus outline: a brainstorm is material, an outline is ordered argument.
- Agree with conditions versus fence-sitting: the first names conditions, the second names nothing.
- Counter-view versus concession: the counter-view is the opposing argument; the concession is how you handle it in the essay.

  - Writing a CQ about the theme.
  - Calling a list of points a thesis.
  - Using 'important', 'complex' or 'both sides' as a thesis.
  - Using seven dimensions where three would do.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** In one sentence, what is a thesis?
    - **a:** Your stance on the topic written as one clear sentence that the whole essay will defend.
  - - **q:** What is the difference between a topic and a thesis?
    - **a:** A topic is the prompt set by the paper; a thesis is your answer to it, with a reason and a boundary.
  - - **q:** What do 'diverge' and 'converge' mean in brainstorming?
    - **a:** Diverge: produce many ideas without judging; converge: sort and select among them, here on a grid.
  - - **q:** Name the nine lenses of the grid in this lesson (WR-001 module 5).
    - **a:** Social, economic, political, ethical, environmental, historical, technological, international, gender (psychological, legal or cultural may be added).
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is a list of points not a thesis?
    - **a:** A list names attributes but makes no claim and gives no order or tension. A thesis claims something that the points defend.
  - - **q:** Why is 'agree with conditions' not fence-sitting?
    - **a:** It names the conditions and takes a position that depends on them, whereas fence-sitting names nothing.
  - - **q:** Why should the stance be chosen after the grid and not before?
    - **a:** An early stance turns the grid into a hunt for supporting points and hides the dimensions that complicate it.
  - - **q:** Why must the thesis keep the topic's qualifier ('before', 'more than', 'can')?
    - **a:** The qualifier carries the topic's exact demand; dropping it moves the essay to the theme.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** Score these three candidate theses on 'Social audits can make governments honest': (i) 'Social audits are good.' (ii) 'Corruption is widespread.' (iii) 'Social audits can make governments honest only if auditors reach records, are protected from retaliation and findings lead to action.' Which passes the checklist?
    - **a:** (iii) passes. (i) is a truism (the thesis takes a position nobody disputes and says nothing specific); (ii) is drift; (iii) is on subject, debatable, bounded and supportable. Indicative scores out of 16 (self-assessment, one reasonable reading): (i) about 4, (ii) about 3, (iii) about 14.
  - - **q:** Rewrite 'Technology has pros and cons' as a conditional thesis on 'Technology will repair the harm technology has caused.'
    - **a:** One reasonable rewrite: 'Technology can repair some harm technology has caused only where policy and funding direct it to the harm, and not where its use is driven by profit or neglect.' Check it against the eight items.
  - - **q:** Name the pattern of 'Pollution is the biggest threat to human health' as a thesis on 'A city should be built for walking before driving', and say how to repair it.
    - **a:** Drift. Return to the topic's subject, who gets priority in street design, before making any pollution argument.
- **essay:**
  - - **q:** Twelve-minute planning drill. Topic: 'Cheap food is expensive.' Decode, brainstorm on a grid, write a central question, write two candidate theses, reject one with a named pattern, and choose.
    - **a:** Indicative self-check points (one reasonable reading, not an official expectation): 'cheap' and 'expensive' use different standards (price at the till versus full cost); dimensions: economic (hidden costs), health, environmental, social (those who produce), ethical; counter-view: cheap food prevents hunger for the poor, so a thesis should say who benefits and who pays. A thesis such as 'Cheap food is expensive when its price leaves out the cost to health, land and labour, but it remains a lifeline for poor households' passes the checklist if it can be supported with examples marked verify.
    - **id:** WR-002-E1
  - - **q:** Twelve-minute planning drill. Topic: 'A language dies when its songs are no longer sung.' Decode the metaphor, build the grid, write the central question and choose a thesis.
    - **a:** Indicative points: literal (songs) versus figurative (living cultural use); dimensions: cultural, social, educational, technological (recording), historical; counter-view: languages also die when economic pressure removes need, even if songs are archived. A conditional thesis names when songs are a sign and when a cause. These are self-check points, not an official expectation.
    - **id:** WR-002-E2
  - - **q:** Twelve-minute planning drill. Topic: 'Technology will repair the harm that technology has caused.' Identify the modal and prediction words, write the central question, reject one weak thesis, and choose.
    - **a:** Indicative points: 'will' is a prediction, so decide how confident to be and under what conditions; dimensions: environmental, technological, political (governance), ethical, economic; counter-view: harms arise from use and incentives, not tools alone. A thesis with named conditions (policy, funding, restraint) passes where an absolute ('it will') does not. Self-check points only.
    - **id:** WR-002-E3
  - - **q:** Twelve-minute planning drill. Topic: 'Trust is the cheapest infrastructure a country can build.' Define 'infrastructure' and 'cheapest', make the grid, and choose a thesis. Then score it out of 16.
    - **a:** Indicative points: 'infrastructure' as roads and as institutions that let strangers cooperate; 'cheapest' invites a comparison of cost; dimensions: economic, political, social, ethical, historical; counter-view: trust cannot be built by spending, and it can be slow to build. A thesis may say that trust is cheap to spend and costly to earn, and name what builds it. Self-check points only.
    - **id:** WR-002-E4
  - - **q:** Timed rejection drill (five minutes). For each of these candidate theses on 'A city should be built for walking before it is built for driving', name the weak pattern: (i) 'Cities need transport.' (ii) 'Both walking and driving have a role.' (iii) 'Cars are the root of every urban problem.' (iv) 'Walking is healthy and cheap.'
    - **a:** (i) truism (accept drift); (ii) fence-sitting; (iii) absolute (accept slogan); (iv) list of points. Repairs: fence-sitting, list of points, absolute and drift are repaired in module 5; the truism repair ('make a claim someone could dispute') is in masterNotes, Seven weak thesis patterns.
    - **id:** WR-002-E5

    - **a:** Restated topic, truism, fence-sitting, list of points, absolute, drift, slogan.

### Memory skeleton

- Stance: agree, agree with conditions, disagree, reframe
- Thesis = claim + because + boundary; reject by opponent, so-what, paragraph tests
- Score on the eight-item checklist; drill the lowest item next
