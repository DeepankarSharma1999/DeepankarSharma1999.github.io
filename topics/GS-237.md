## GS-237: Inclusive growth and development indicators

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Growth, public finance and infrastructure

*Goal:* Explain Inclusive growth and development indicators and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Why averages mislead

To compare countries we divide total income by population, giving per capita income. NCERT shows its limit with two imaginary countries that have the same average income: in one, all citizens earn similar amounts; in the other, four are poor and one is extremely rich. Most people would choose the first, which shows distribution matters. A rising average can also coexist with stagnant incomes for large groups. Development goals extend beyond income to security, equal treatment and freedom, which are hard to measure but still important. Inclusive growth asks whether opportunities and outcomes improve broadly, so analysts must look past the average to who gains.

### Non-income indicators and public provision

NCERT's comparison of Haryana, Kerala and Bihar is the classic exam example. Haryana had the highest per capita income of the three, yet Kerala recorded an IMR of 6 against Haryana's 28 (2020) and a higher literacy rate, 94 against 82 per cent (2017-18). The explanation is that money in a household's pocket cannot buy everything: clean environments, safe medicines and protection from infection often depend on collective, usually public, provision. Kerala's adequate basic health and education facilities explain its lead, and a working Public Distribution System improves nutrition. UNDP's Human Development Index combines education, health and income; in HDR 2025, India ranked 130 of 193 while Sri Lanka ranked 89, a reminder that income alone does not set rank.

### Current measures of inclusion

The Economic Survey 2025-26 tracks poverty by both money and deprivation. In June 2025 the World Bank lifted its extreme poverty line to USD 3.00 a day at 2021 prices; on that basis India's 2022-23 extreme poverty rate is 5.3 per cent. NITI Aayog's Multidimensional Poverty Index, based on education, health and living standards, fell from 55.3 per cent in 2005-06 to 14.96 per cent in 2019-21 and is estimated at 11.28 per cent in 2022-23, with states that were poorer in 2015-16 reducing it faster. Social protection systems now reach 64.3 per cent of the population (2025), against 22 per cent in 2016. Present both income and non-income evidence in answers.

### Worked example

Question: 'Higher per capita income does not guarantee higher development.' Frame: define per capita income; give the two-country distribution example; cite Haryana vs Kerala on IMR; explain collective provision of health and education; add HDI and MPI as alternatives with figures (India HDI rank 130/193; MPI 55.3% → 11.28%); conclude that inclusive growth needs income growth plus public services.

### Recall

- - **q:** Define Infant Mortality Rate.
  - **a:** Number of children who die before completing one year of age per 1,000 live births in that year.
- - **q:** Which three dimensions does UNDP's HDI combine?
  - **a:** Education, health (life expectancy) and per capita income.
- - **q:** What did NITI Aayog's MPI show for 2005-06 and 2022-23?
  - **a:** About 55.3 per cent multidimensionally poor in 2005-06, falling to an estimated 11.28 per cent in 2022-23.

### Common trap

Concluding that a state with higher per capita income must be more developed: Haryana out-earns Kerala on average yet has much worse infant mortality.
