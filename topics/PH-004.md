### Bridge: Cross product recap: magnitude, direction and the product rule

A **vector** has a size and a direction. The **cross product** of two vectors a and b is a third vector a × b defined by two facts. Its **size** is |a × b| = a b sin θ, where θ is the angle between a and b (0 to 180 degrees); this equals the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span. Its **direction** is perpendicular to both a and b, given by the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers of your right hand from a towards b through the smaller angle; the thumb points along a × b.

Three properties matter in this lesson.

1. **Order matters**: b × a = −(a × b). Swapping the factors flips the sign.
2. **Parallel vectors give zero**: if a is parallel (or anti-parallel) to b then sin θ = 0, so a × b = 0. In particular a × a = 0.
3. **Component form**: for a = (a_x, a_y, a_z) and b = (b_x, b_y, b_z), (a × b)_x = a_y b_z − a_z b_y, and the y and z components follow by cycling x → y → z → x. With unit vectors i, j, k along x, y, z: i × j = k, j × k = i, k × i = j, and j × i = −k.

**Product rule.** If a and b change with time, d(a × b)/dt = (da/dt) × b + a × (db/dt). The order of the factors must be kept. The rule holds because every component is a sum of products; for example the x component a_y b_z − a_z b_y has derivative (ȧ_y b_z + a_y ḃ_z) − (ȧ_z b_y + a_z ḃ_y), which regroups into the x components of ȧ × b and a × ḃ.

Worked check. a = (2, 0, 0) and b = (0, 3, 0): size 2 × 3 × sin 90° = 6, direction by the right-hand rule along +z, so a × b = (0, 0, 6). Component formula: (a × b)_z = a_x b_y − a_y b_x = 6. Reversing the order gives (0, 0, −6).

### Bridge: Rotational kinematics recap: angle, angular velocity and v = ω × r

An angle is measured in **radians**: the angle in radians equals arc length divided by radius, so one full turn is 2π rad (360 degrees). A radian is a ratio of two lengths, so it is dimensionless.

For a point moving on a circle of radius r, the **angular velocity** is ω = dθ/dt (rad s⁻¹) and the **angular acceleration** is α = dω/dt (rad s⁻²). The speed along the circle is v = ω r. If you know revolutions per minute (rpm), ω = 2π × (rpm)/60.

The table shows the one-to-one translation from straight-line motion to rotation about a fixed axis; it is used all through this lesson.

| Straight line | Rotation about a fixed axis |
|---|---|
| displacement x | angle θ |
| velocity v = dx/dt | angular velocity ω = dθ/dt |
| acceleration a | angular acceleration α |
| mass m | moment of inertia I |
| force F | torque τ |
| momentum p = m v | angular momentum L = I ω |

**Vector form.** The direction of the angular velocity vector ω is along the axis, given by the right-hand rule (curl the fingers the way the body turns; the thumb gives ω). For a point at position r measured from any point on the axis, its velocity is v = ω × r. Check: the magnitude is ω r sin θ, where r sin θ is the perpendicular distance from the axis, so |v| = ω × (distance from axis), as expected. NCERT Class XI chapter 6 uses exactly this relation.

Worked check. A wheel turns at 120 rpm: ω = 2π × 120/60 = 12.57 rad s⁻¹. A point 0.50 m from the axis moves at v = 12.57 × 0.50 = 6.28 m s⁻¹.

### Bridge: Torque as a moment of force: lever arm and direction

A force pushing on a door does more good the farther it is from the hinge and the more squarely it pushes. That 'turning effect' is the **torque** (moment of force). For a force F applied at the point with position vector r measured from a chosen point O (the **origin** or reference point),

$$ τ = r × F,   |τ| = r F sin θ = r_⊥ F $$

where θ is the angle between r and F and r_⊥ = r sin θ is the **lever arm**, the perpendicular distance from O to the **line of action** of F (the straight line along which the force acts).

Direction: perpendicular to the plane of r and F, by the right-hand rule. A torque that tends to turn things anticlockwise as seen from above points up (+z); clockwise points down.

Unit and dimensions: newton metre (N m); dimensions M L² T⁻², the same as energy, but torque is a vector and energy is a scalar, and a torque is never quoted in joules.

Three facts to remember. First, torque depends on the chosen point O; the same force has different torques about different points. Second, the torque is zero if r = 0 (force applied at O), if F = 0, or if the line of action passes through O (sin θ = 0). Third, sliding a force along its own line of action does not change its torque, because r_⊥ stays the same.

Worked check. A 20 N force acts perpendicular to a door 0.40 m from the hinge: τ = 0.40 × 20 = 8.0 N m. If the same force makes 30° with the line from the hinge, τ = 20 × 0.40 × sin 30° = 4.0 N m.

### 1. Orientation: what 'conservation of angular momentum' asks of you

*Objective:* State what the topic covers, which past questions test it and how the ten modules are ordered.

## What this topic is

**Angular momentum** is the quantity that measures 'how much rotation' a body or system has about a chosen point. For a single particle it is L = r × p, where r is the particle's position from the point and p = m v is its momentum. The central law of this topic is one sentence: **if the total external torque on a system about a point is zero, the total angular momentum of the system about that point does not change**. The rest of the lesson explains where that sentence comes from, how far it reaches and where it fails.

### 2. Foundation: angular momentum and torque of a particle

*Objective:* Define L = r × p and τ = r × F, compute them from components and from the lever arm, and state their units and origin dependence.

## The two definitions

Choose an origin O in an inertial frame. A particle of mass m at position r with velocity v has momentum p = m v. Its **angular momentum about O** is

$$ L = r × p = m (r × v),   |L| = m v r sin θ = m v r_⊥ $$

where θ is the angle between r and v, and r_⊥ is the perpendicular distance from O to the straight line of the motion. A force F acting on the particle at position r has **torque about O**

$$ τ = r × F,   |τ| = F r_⊥ $$

Both are vectors perpendicular to the plane of the two vectors in the product (right-hand rule). **Units**: L in kg m² s⁻¹ (equivalently J s, the same units as Planck's constant); τ in N m. **Dimensions**: [L] = M L² T⁻¹ and [τ] = M L² T⁻².

## Four things beginners often miss

1. **Straight-line motion has angular momentum.** A ball flying in a straight line has L = m v r_⊥ about any point not on its line, and L = 0 about points on its line. 'Angular' refers to the geometry of r, not to circular motion.
2. **Both depend on the origin.** Change O and r changes, so L and τ change. A statement about angular momentum is incomplete without 'about the point O'. Always write the origin.
3. **L = 0 does not mean the particle is at rest.** It only means r and v are parallel.
4. **Direction is not along the velocity.** L is perpendicular to the plane of r and v.

## Worked example 1 (components)

A particle of mass 0.50 kg is at r = (3, 4, 0) m with velocity v = (2, −1, 0) m s⁻¹. Then p = (1.0, −0.5, 0) kg m s⁻¹ and L_z = x p_y − y p_x = 3(−0.5) − 4(1.0) = −5.5 kg m² s⁻¹, so L = (0, 0, −5.5), clockwise as seen from +z. About the point A = (0, 6, 0) the position is r − A = (3, −2, 0) and L_z = 3(−0.5) − (−2)(1.0) = +0.5. Same motion, two points, two answers (different even in sign). A python evaluation of both cross products agrees.

## Worked example 2 (lever arm, a sliding block)

A block of mass m slides along the x axis at speed v. About a point on the x axis, r_⊥ = 0 and L = 0. About a point A on the y axis at height a above the axis, r_⊥ = a, so |L_A| = m v a. For motion in the +x direction with A above the line, the position from A to the block is (x, −a, 0) and (x i − a j) × (m v i) = + a m v k, so L_A points along +z. Past question 2018 Q1(b) has this structure; the official diagram fixes the positions, so read the figure before fixing signs.

## Why define torque this way

The next module shows that the time derivative of r × p is exactly r × F. That is the reason for these two particular definitions: they make rotation obey a law shaped exactly like F = dp/dt.

### 3. Core: dL/dt = τ (recap), angular impulse, partial conservation and the moving origin

*Objective:* Recall dL/dt = τ (derived in PH-001 module 7), state the angular impulse, the component-wise conservation rule and the extra term when the origin moves.

## Recap (full derivation in PH-001 module 7)

Assumptions: the origin O is a fixed point of an inertial frame; the mass m is constant; speeds are non-relativistic. Right-handed axes; a rotation anticlockwise as seen from +z gives positive L_z. Differentiating L = r × p with the order of the factors kept (bridge B1):

$$ dL/dt = (dr/dt) × p + r × (dp/dt) = v × (m v) + r × F = r × F = τ $$

The first term vanishes because v is parallel to p (write that reason in an exam: it carries a mark). PH-001 module 7 gives the steps and the component check; they are not repeated here. Dimensions: [dL/dt] = M L² T⁻² = [τ].

**Angular impulse.** Integrating in time, ΔL = ∫ τ dt, the angular impulse about O. It is the rotational partner of the linear impulse ∫ F dt = Δp (PH-001 module 3), and it is the tool of module 8 (impacts) and module 9 (a gyroscope resists a short torque). Because L is a vector, ΔL has the direction of the (time-averaged) torque.

## When is L constant?

The torque r × F is zero in exactly three situations: (i) r = 0 at that instant; (ii) F = 0; (iii) F is parallel or anti-parallel to r, so the line of action passes through O. Then dL/dt = 0 and L is constant in **both magnitude and direction** (every component is fixed). Condition (iii) is the central-force case of module 4. The free particle is the check: r(t) = r₀ + v t gives r × v = r₀ × v, constant.

**Component form (partial conservation).** Each component obeys its own equation, dL_x/dt = τ_x and so on, so if only one component of the torque vanishes only that component of L is conserved. A ball under gravity (force along −z) has τ_z = x F_y − y F_x = 0 about any point, so L_z is conserved while L_x and L_y change. This is what the top in module 9 uses. The argument is for components along fixed axes; a component along a moving axis needs care (module 9).

## The moving origin

Suppose the reference point O′ moves with velocity V_O. With r′ = r − R_O (R_O the position of O′) and L′ = r′ × p,

$$ dL′/dt = (v − V_O) × p + r′ × F = τ′ − V_O × p $$

because v × p = 0. So dL′/dt = τ′ holds about a moving point only if V_O × p = 0: the point is fixed, or V_O is parallel to p, or p = 0. For a system the same steps give dL′/dt = τ′ − V_O × P; the choice V_O = V_cm = P/M removes the extra term, which is why the centre of mass is a legitimate origin (module 6).

## A trap examiners use

Do not drop (dr/dt) × p without a reason, and do not quote τ = dL/dt about an accelerating point without stating the extra term.


### 4. Core: central forces conserve L and force the motion into a plane (recap)

*Objective:* Recall (from PH-003 module 2) that a central force gives τ = 0, constant L and planar motion; write the polar form J = m r² θ̇ and avoid the traps.

## Definition

A **central force** is F = f(r) r̂, where r̂ = r/r points from a fixed centre O to the particle and f depends only on the distance r. It points always along the line to O: toward O if f < 0, away if f > 0. Examples: gravity, the Coulomb force and the spring force F = −k r (f = −k r). The centre is assumed fixed (for two comparable masses use the reduced-mass reduction of PH-009).

## Recap of the two theorems (proved in full in PH-003 module 2)

1. **L is conserved.** τ = r × f(r) r̂ = (f/r)(r × r) = 0, so dL/dt = 0 by module 3. This answers 2019 Q1(a)(ii) and 2026 Q4(c)(i): for F = −k r the force is central and J = m r × v is conserved.
2. **The path is planar.** L · r = (r × p) · r = 0 (a vector repeated in a triple product), so r stays in the plane through O perpendicular to the constant L; v lies there too (2018 Q1(a)). If L = 0 the motion is along a straight line through O.

These questions (2018 Q1(a), 2019 Q1(a), 2026 Q4(c)) are shared with PH-003; use PH-003 module 2 for the full write-up including constant areal velocity.

## Polar form (two dimensions)

Choose the plane as the x-y plane, so L = (0, 0, J) with J = m (x ẏ − y ẋ). With x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ: x ẏ − y ẋ = r² θ̇, so

$$ J = m r² θ̇ $$

Check by torque: τ_z = r F_θ, where F_θ is the force component along increasing θ; dJ/dt = m (2 r ṙ θ̇ + r² θ̈) = r × [m (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇)] = r F_θ, which is zero for a central force. The areal velocity dA/dt = J/(2m) and the effective potential U_eff = J²/(2 m r²) + U(r) are in PH-003; the graph of U_eff for the spring force is in the diagram of this module. Newton argued the converse: constant areal velocity about a point means the force is central (Mathur section 6.15).

## Traps

1. Central means 'always along the line to a fixed centre'. L is conserved as soon as the force direction is along ±r̂, **even if its magnitude depends on direction** (τ = 0 needs only the direction). The extra requirement that the magnitude depends on r alone (f(r) only) is what makes the force conservative (U = −∫ f dr); a force along r̂ whose magnitude varies with direction conserves L but is not conservative in general.
2. 'Central' (direction) is not 'conservative' (work); J is conserved because τ = 0, not because the force is conservative.
3. Conservation is of the vector L about the centre, not about other points.


### 5. Core: a system of particles - the weak and strong third law (recap of internal torques)

*Objective:* Recall dL/dt = τ_ext for a system (PH-001 module 7) and state exactly which form of Newton's third law is needed.

## Defining the total, and the recap

For N particles the **total angular momentum about O** is L = Σ r_i × p_i. Write F_i = F_i^ext + Σ_{j≠i} F_ij. As in module 3 the v_i × p_i terms vanish, so dL/dt = Σ r_i × F_i^ext + Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} r_i × F_ij. Grouping the double sum in pairs gives r_i × F_ij + r_j × F_ji = (r_i − r_j) × F_ij, which is zero when F_ij lies along the line joining the two particles, hence dL/dt = τ_ext. This pair cancellation and the couple example are worked in full in PH-001 module 7 and are not repeated. If τ_ext = 0 the total L is conserved even though individual L_i change.

## Which third law? (new here)

The **weak form** (F_ji = −F_ij, equal and opposite) is enough for the total force, so linear momentum obeys dP/dt = F_ext. The **strong form** (also along the line of centres) is needed for the torques: with the weak form alone the pair term (r_i − r_j) × F_ij need not vanish. The strong form holds for gravity, Coulomb forces, springs and, in the usual idealisation, contact forces inside a rigid body. For forces between moving charges, the magnetic part need not be along the line of centres, and the angular momentum of the electromagnetic field must then be included (standard remark, outside the supplied extracts; verify). A common slip is to say 'internal forces cancel by the third law' without 'along the line joining'.

## Independence of the two laws

Dimensions: every term has dimensions M L² T⁻². Limiting case: N = 1 gives module 3. A system can have zero net force but nonzero torque (a couple: two equal and opposite forces along different lines, ΣF = 0, τ_ext = (r₁ − r₂) × F ≠ 0), and a single force through O has no torque yet accelerates the system, so conservation of P and of L are independent laws.

Sources: NCERT Class XI chapter 6 (page 17) states τ_int = 0 under exactly these two assumptions, and Mathur (section 6.13) says the internal forces form collinear action-reaction pairs.


### 6. Core: the split L = R × P + L_cm, orbital and spin parts

*Objective:* Derive the decomposition of the angular momentum of a system into the angular momentum of the centre of mass and the angular momentum about the centre of mass, and the torque law for each part.

## Why split it

A planet both circles the Sun and spins on its axis. The total angular momentum about the Sun is the sum of two physically different pieces. To prove that, use the centre of mass (CM; PH-009 module 2).

## Definitions and two identities

Let the system have total mass M = Σ m_i. The CM position is R = Σ m_i r_i / M, and its velocity is V = Σ m_i v_i / M. Write each position and velocity as the CM value plus a value measured from the CM: r_i = R + r_i′ and v_i = V + v_i′, where r_i′ is the position of particle i **relative to the CM**. Then

$$ Σ m_i r_i′ = Σ m_i (r_i − R) = M R − M R = 0 $$

Differentiating in time, Σ m_i v_i′ = 0: **the momentum of the system relative to the CM is zero.** Also the total momentum is P = Σ m_i v_i = M V.

## The split, step by step

$$ L = Σ r_i × m_i v_i = Σ (R + r_i′) × m_i (V + v_i′) $$

Expand into four sums:

1. R × V Σ m_i = R × M V = R × P.
2. R × Σ m_i v_i′ = R × 0 = 0.
3. (Σ m_i r_i′) × V = 0 × V = 0.
4. Σ r_i′ × m_i v_i′ ≡ L_cm, the angular momentum **about the CM**.

$$ L = R × P + L_cm $$

**Interpretation.** R × P is the angular momentum of the whole mass M placed at the CM and moving with it (the **orbital** part, Mathur's term; it depends on the origin O). L_cm is the **spin** part, about the CM. The spin part does not depend on the origin O, since it is built from relative positions and velocities only. Mathur (section 6.13) says the same, noting that for the Earth the two parts are inclined at 23.5 degrees.

## Torque law for the spin part

Let A = dV/dt be the CM acceleration (the CM frame is non-inertial if A ≠ 0). Differentiate L_cm = Σ r_i′ × m_i v_i′, drop the v′ × v′ term and use m_i (A + a_i′) = F_i:

$$ dL_cm/dt = Σ r_i′ × m_i a_i′ = Σ r_i′ × (F_i − m_i A) = Σ r_i′ × F_i − (Σ m_i r_i′) × A = Σ r_i′ × F_i $$

The last sum is the torque of all forces about the CM; the internal ones cancel by module 5, so **dL_cm/dt = τ_cm,ext**. It holds even when the CM accelerates. Combining with d(R × P)/dt = R × F_ext gives back dL/dt = τ_ext, consistent with module 5.

## Consequence

If the torque about the CM vanishes, the spin is conserved no matter how the CM moves. A spinning ball thrown through uniform gravity keeps constant spin: the weight acts as if at the CM, so its torque about the CM is zero.

## Numbers

Earth's orbital angular momentum about the Sun is M r² (2π/T) with M = 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg, r = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m, T = 365.25 days: 2.66 × 10⁴⁰ kg m² s⁻¹. The spin part is I ω with I ≈ 0.331 M R² and sidereal rotation period 86164 s: 5.85 × 10³³ kg m² s⁻¹. The orbital part is about 4.6 million times larger (constants are standard values, outside the supplied extracts; verify).

### 7. Core: rotation about a fixed axis and variable moment of inertia

*Objective:* Use L_z = I ω to solve skater, platform and collapsing-sphere problems, and account for the energy.

## From particles to L_z = I ω

For a rigid body turning about a fixed axis (call it z) with angular speed ω, each particle moves in a circle of radius ρ_i (its perpendicular distance from the axis) at speed ρ_i ω. The component of its angular momentum along the axis is m_i ρ_i (ρ_i ω). Summing,

$$ L_z = (Σ m_i ρ_i²) ω = I ω $$

where I is the **moment of inertia** about the axis. The torque law for the z component is τ_z = dL_z/dt = I α (when I is constant). For bodies symmetric about the axis the whole vector L lies along the axis; for lopsided bodies L can have components off the axis (PH-020). Upadhyaya (section 3.4) and NCERT (section 6.12) present these results.

## Conservation with changing I

If the external torque about the axis is zero, I ω is constant. A **system that is not rigid** can change I by moving mass toward or away from the axis. Then

$$ I₁ ω₁ = I₂ ω₂,   ω₂ = ω₁ (I₁/I₂) $$

**Rotational kinetic energy.** K = ½ I ω² = L²/(2I). With L fixed, K₂/K₁ = I₁/I₂: when I shrinks K rises. The energy comes from the internal agent (the skater's muscles; gravity for a collapsing star), which does positive work on the system. So angular momentum is conserved and rotational energy is not.

## Worked example 1: skater

I₁ = 4.0 kg m² (arms out) and ω₁ = 2π rad s⁻¹ (1 rev/s). Arms in: I₂ = 1.2 kg m². Then ω₂ = 4.0 × 2π/1.2 = 20.9 rad s⁻¹ (3.33 rev/s). K rises from 78.96 J to 263.2 J, a gain of 184.2 J supplied by the skater.

## Worked example 2: person on a platform

A frictionless platform (I = 120 kg m²) carries a 60 kg person at the rim, radius 2.0 m, rotating at 1.0 rad s⁻¹. The person walks to the centre (the person's I → 0). Initial I = 120 + 60 × 4 = 360 kg m². Final I = 120. So ω₂ = 360 × 1.0/120 = 3.0 rad s⁻¹. K goes from 180 J to 540 J.

## Worked example 3: shrinking sphere

For a uniform sphere I = (2/5) M R² (standard result, Mathur chapter 11). With M fixed, I ∝ R², so ω ∝ 1/R² and the period T ∝ R². If the Earth's diameter halved, the day would be 24 × (1/2)² = 6 h (a problem of this kind is set in Upadhyaya, page 214). A rotating core of radius 1.0 × 10⁷ m and period 10 days that collapses to 1.0 × 10⁴ m has period 10 d × 10⁻⁶ = 0.864 s. This is an idealisation: uniform density, no mass loss, no angular momentum shed, non-relativistic.

## Checks

Dimensions: I ω is M L² T⁻¹. Limiting case: I₂ = I₁ gives ω₂ = ω₁. Trap: 'the platform's angular momentum is conserved' is wrong; the **system** (platform plus person) is.

### 8. Core: impulsive collisions about a pivot - the rod and the ballistic pendulum

*Objective:* Use conservation of angular momentum about the pivot during a short impact, then energy conservation after it, to solve rod and ballistic-pendulum problems.

## The idea

An **impulsive** force is a very large force acting for a very short time. During it, the other finite forces (such as gravity) change the angular momentum negligibly, because their angular impulse is tiny. If the body is hinged at a point P, the hinge force acts at P, has zero lever arm about P, and so exerts **no torque about P**. Hence for the short impact

$$ L_P(before) = L_P(just after) $$

about the pivot P. **Linear momentum is not conserved** in the impact, because the hinge supplies an unknown impulse. Choosing P as the origin eliminates that unknown. This is the central skill of the module.

## Ballistic rod pendulum

A uniform rod (mass M, length ℓ) hangs from a frictionless hinge at one end. Its moment of inertia about the hinge is ∫₀^ℓ (M/ℓ) x² dx = M ℓ²/3. A bullet (mass m, speed v, horizontal) hits and sticks at distance x below the hinge.

Step 1 (impact, angular momentum about the hinge): m v x = I_tot ω, with I_tot = M ℓ²/3 + m x², so ω = m v x/(M ℓ²/3 + m x²).

Step 2 (swing, energy conservation, the hinge does no work): ½ I_tot ω² = (M ℓ/2 + m x) g (1 − cos φ), where φ is the maximum angle.

Numbers: M = 2.0 kg, ℓ = 1.0 m, m = 0.020 kg, x = 1.0 m, v = 100 m s⁻¹. I_tot = 0.6667 + 0.020 = 0.6867 kg m², ω = 2.0/0.6867 = 2.913 rad s⁻¹, kinetic energy 2.913 J, (M ℓ/2 + m x) g = 1.02 × 9.8 = 9.996 J, 1 − cos φ = 0.2914, φ = 44.9°. Check by linear momentum: just after impact the momentum is (M ℓ/2 + m x) ω = 2.97 kg m/s against 2.00 before; the 0.97 kg m/s difference is the hinge's impulse, which shows linear momentum is not conserved.

(Symbol note: Π denotes linear impulse in this module; J is reserved for angular momentum, as in module 4.)

## A free rod struck by an impulse

A rod lying on a smooth table (mass M, length ℓ) receives impulse Π perpendicular to it at distance d from the CM. No hinge exists. The linear impulse gives v_cm = Π/M. The angular impulse about the CM gives I_cm ω = Π d with I_cm = M ℓ²/12. With M = 0.60 kg, ℓ = 1.2 m, Π = 3.0 N s and d = 0.30 m: v_cm = 5.0 m s⁻¹, I_cm = 0.072 kg m², ω = 12.5 rad s⁻¹. The point on the rod momentarily at rest lies at distance s with ω s = v_cm, so s = 0.40 m on the side of the CM opposite to the blow. In general s = I_cm/(M d).

## Centre of percussion

For the hanging rod struck at distance x from the hinge, the hinge impulse is zero if the rod's CM momentum is exactly the impulse: Π = M ω ℓ/2 together with Π x = (M ℓ²/3) ω. Dividing, x = 2ℓ/3. A blow there causes no jolt at the hinge (the sweet-spot idea of a cricket bat).

## Traps

Conserving linear momentum for a hinged system; forgetting to use I_tot including the bullet; using energy conservation through the impact (it is a perfectly inelastic event, so mechanical energy is lost).

### 9. Master: direction preserved, symmetry, and when conservation fails (the top)

*Objective:* Explain gyroscopic stability and the Earth's fixed axis, state the symmetry link, and derive the precession rate of a spinning top as a flagged forward link.

## L is a vector: its direction is also conserved

If τ = 0 then every component of L is fixed, so the **direction** is fixed as well as the size. A bicycle wheel spun fast keeps its plane while you walk around with it; NCERT's bicycle-rim demonstration (page 16) shows this. A rotor with large L needs a large angular impulse to change direction, since ΔL = ∫ τ dt; this is the gyroscope idea. The Earth's spin axis keeps pointing near the same stars because L is huge and the torques of the Sun and Moon on the equatorial bulge are small, so the direction of L wanders only slowly (about 26,000 years for a full circle; standard value, outside the extracts; verify).

Vector demonstration: a person on a frictionless swivel chair holds a wheel (I_w = 0.50 kg m², ω = 30 rad s⁻¹, so L_w = 15 kg m² s⁻¹ upward) and flips it upside down. Vertical L is conserved: 15 = −15 + I_p ω_p, so ω_p = 30/I_p; for I_p = 6.0 kg m² (person plus chair), ω_p = 5.0 rad s⁻¹.

## Symmetry behind the law (one paragraph)

Conservation of linear momentum reflects that physics is unchanged by shifting the whole system; conservation of angular momentum reflects that physics is unchanged by rotating the whole system about an axis. Mathur (section 6.16) argues that if the potential energy of a system does not change under rotation, no external torque acts, and L is conserved: the potential must depend on the distances between particles only. The general statement, that each continuous symmetry gives a conserved quantity, is Noether's theorem (standard; not in the supplied extracts; verify the historical attribution).

## When there is an external torque: the top

A symmetric top spins with angular speed ω about its axis (moment of inertia I about it), its pivot on the floor, its CM at distance ℓ along the axis, axis inclined at θ to the vertical. Gravity M g acts at the CM and gives a torque τ = r_cm × M g of size M g ℓ sin θ. The vector r_cm is along the axis (the same direction as L when the spin is fast) and the weight is vertical, so τ is **horizontal and perpendicular to L**.

For a short time dt: dL = τ dt. Because τ ⟂ L, the length |L| does not change; only its direction does. The tip of L moves around a horizontal circle of radius L sin θ at rate Ω, so |dL| = L sin θ Ω dt. Equating,

$$ L sin θ Ω = M g ℓ sin θ,   Ω = M g ℓ/(I ω) $$

**Fast-spin approximation**: this assumes L ≈ I ω along the axis and ω ≫ Ω; the exact treatment (Euler angles, nutation, a Lagrangian) is a forward link to PH-020. Numbers: M = 0.20 kg, ℓ = 0.040 m, I = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ kg m², ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad s⁻¹ give Ω = 4.99 rad s⁻¹ (ω/Ω is about 63, so the approximation is acceptable).

## Which angular momenta are conserved for the top? (symmetric top only)

Gravity's torque about the vertical axis is zero (the weight is parallel to it), so the **vertical component L_z is conserved**. The torque of gravity also has no component along the symmetry axis e₃ (τ = r_cm × M g is perpendicular to r_cm, which lies along e₃). But τ · e₃ = 0 is not by itself enough, because e₃ moves with the body: d(L · e₃)/dt = τ · e₃ + L · (ω × e₃) = τ₃ + (I₁ − I₂) ω₁ ω₂ (this is Euler's equation, I₃ ω̇₃ = (I₁ − I₂) ω₁ ω₂ + τ₃). The extra term vanishes only for a **symmetric top (I₁ = I₂)**. Then ω₃, and hence **L₃ = I₃ ω₃, is conserved**; for an asymmetric body it is not.

**Lagrangian (forward link to PH-020; standard result, verify against that lesson).** With Euler angles (θ, φ, ψ), the tip fixed and I₁ = I₂ about the tip, the Lagrangian is

$$ ℒ = ½ I₁ (θ̇² + φ̇² sin²θ) + ½ I₃ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ)² − M g ℓ cos θ $$

φ and ψ do not appear in it (they are cyclic), so their conjugate momenta are constants:

$$ p_ψ = I₃ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ) = I₃ ω₃,   p_φ = I₁ sin²θ φ̇ + I₃ cos θ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ) $$

p_ψ is the component along the symmetry axis and p_φ is the vertical component L_z. The two partial derivatives were checked symbolically with sympy. The derivation of the kinetic energy in Euler angles and the equation for θ belong to PH-020, which is not yet written; treat these expressions as a flagged forward link.

### Notes: Definitions

- Angular momentum of a particle about O: L = r x p = m (r x v); |L| = m v r_perp; units kg m^2 s^-1 (J s); dimensions M L^2 T^-1.
- Torque (moment of force) about O: tau = r x F; |tau| = F r_perp; units N m; dimensions M L^2 T^-2.
- Central force: F = f(r) r-hat toward or away from a fixed centre with f a function of r alone.
- Strong third law: internal forces are equal, opposite and along the line joining the two particles.
- Orbital angular momentum R x P (depends on the origin) and spin angular momentum L_cm = sum r_i' x m_i v_i' (independent of the origin).
- Angular impulse: the integral of tau dt, equal to the change in L.
- Moment of inertia about an axis: I = sum m_i rho_i^2, with rho_i the perpendicular distance from the axis.

### Notes: Governing equations

- dL/dt = tau for a particle about a fixed point of an inertial frame.
- System: dL/dt = tau_ext (internal torques cancel by the strong third law); dP/dt = F_ext.
- L = R x P + L_cm; dL_cm/dt = tau_cm,ext even when the CM accelerates.
- Central force: tau = 0, so L is constant; L . r = 0 so the path is planar; J = m r^2 theta-dot.
- Fixed axis: L_z = I omega, tau_z = I alpha; if tau_z = 0 and I changes, I1 omega1 = I2 omega2 and K = L^2/(2I).
- Impulsive impact about a hinge: angular momentum about the hinge is conserved; linear momentum is not.
- Hanging rod: I_hinge = M l^2/3; centre of percussion at x = 2 l/3 from the hinge.
- Top (fast spin, flagged forward link): Omega = M g l/(I omega).

### Notes: Derivation chain to be able to write closed-book

- 1. dL/dt = v x p + r x F = tau (v parallel to p; product rule keeps the order).
- 2. Central force: tau = (f/r)(r x r) = 0, L constant; L . r = 0 gives a plane.
- 3. Pair of internal forces: r_i x F_ij + r_j x F_ji = (r_i - r_j) x F_ij = 0 (strong third law); so dL/dt = tau_ext.
- 4. r_i = R + r_i', sum m_i r_i' = 0, expand four terms, L = R x P + L_cm.
- 5. Fixed axis: L_z = I omega; no torque, I omega constant; K = L^2/(2I) changes because internal work is done.
- 6. Pivoted impact: angular momentum about the hinge during the impact, energy during the swing.
- 7. Top: dL = tau dt with tau perpendicular to L gives Omega = M g l/(I omega).

### Notes: Which conservation law needs which condition

- Conservation of linear momentum, angular momentum and mechanical energy are independent laws with different conditions.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- No force versus no torque: a central force is non-zero, yet its torque about the centre is zero.
- Conserved quantity of the system versus of a part: the platform alone does not conserve L; the platform plus the person does.
- Angular momentum conserved versus kinetic energy conserved: a skater drawing in her arms changes K.
- Orbital versus spin angular momentum: only the spin part is independent of the origin.
- Linear momentum in a hinged collision: not conserved (hinge impulse); angular momentum about the hinge is.
- Torque versus work: both have dimensions of energy, but torque is a vector and is not measured in joules.

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- dL/dt = tau needs a fixed (or CM) origin in an inertial frame; for an accelerating origin an extra term appears.
- The internal-torque cancellation needs the strong third law; for moving charges the field carries angular momentum (standard remark; verify).
- L = I omega along the axis holds for symmetric bodies; asymmetric bodies have L not parallel to omega (PH-020).
- Variable-mass systems (rocket) need the extra momentum flow terms (PH-001 module 4); this lesson uses constant mass.
- The precession formula is the fast-spin approximation; nutation and the exact Euler-angle treatment are forward links.
- Relativistic and quantum corrections are outside this syllabus line: spin angular momentum of particles is quantised (Mathur section 6.18 appears only as a contents-list entry in the supplied extract; the section text is not extracted or used).

  - Using energy or linear momentum through an impulsive hinged impact
- **distractorAnalysis:**
  - A claim that L = 0 implies the particle is at rest is false: it implies only that r and v are parallel.
  - A claim that kinetic energy is conserved when the skater changes her moment of inertia is false: internal work is done.
  - A claim that angular momentum is conserved because the central force is conservative mixes two different properties: the cause is zero torque about the centre.
  - A claim that the hinge conserves linear momentum in the ballistic rod is false: the hinge supplies an impulse.
- **prelimsElimination:** - Not a Prelims topic; the original self-check items use elimination by dimensions, by the zero-torque condition and by the origin rule.

- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is it incorrect to say that angular momentum is conserved because the net force is zero?
    - **a:** The condition is zero net torque; a central force is non-zero but has zero torque, and a couple has zero net force but non-zero torque.
  - - **q:** Explain why internal torques cancel in a system, and state what could prevent it.
    - **a:** Each pair gives (r_i - r_j) x F_ij = 0 when F_ij is along the line joining the particles (strong third law); for forces not along that line a net internal torque can appear.

    - **a:** L is very large and the external torque on the equatorial bulge is small, so dL = tau dt changes the direction only very slowly (standard period about 26000 years; verify).

### Memory skeleton

- L = r × p, τ = r × F, both about a named point
- dL/dt = τ; central force gives L constant and a plane
- System: internal torques cancel (strong third law), dL/dt = τ_ext
- Split L = R × P + L_cm (orbital plus spin)
- Fixed axis: L_z = I ω; I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂, K = L²/(2I) changes
- Pivoted impact: L about hinge in the impact, energy in the swing
- Which law needs which condition: force, torque, work

### Last-week summary

- dL/dt = v × p + r × F = τ; v × p = 0 since v ∥ p; origin fixed or CM, inertial frame.
- Pair (r_i − r_j) × F_ij = 0 needs the strong third law; then dL/dt = τ_ext; conservation needs zero torque, not zero force.
- L = R × P + L_cm; spin part origin independent; dL_cm/dt = τ_cm,ext even if the CM accelerates.
- No torque: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂, K = L²/(2I) rises as I falls (internal work).
- Hinge impact: L about hinge conserved, P not; rod I = M l²/3, centre of percussion 2l/3.
