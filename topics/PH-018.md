### Bridge: Matrix times vector, and symmetric matrices

A 3×3 matrix acting on a column vector produces a new column vector: each output component is a row of the matrix multiplied term by term with the input and added. For the matrix with rows (0.17, −0.08, 0), (−0.08, 0.17, 0), (0, 0, 0.16) and the vector (0, 10, 0), the first output is 0.17×0 + (−0.08)×10 + 0 = −0.8, the second is 0.17×10 = 1.7 and the third is 0. So the output (−0.8, 1.7, 0) points in a different direction from the input (0, 10, 0). That is exactly what L = I ω does: the output L need not be parallel to ω.

A matrix is **symmetric** when swapping rows and columns changes nothing (the entry in row 1 column 2 equals that in row 2 column 1). The inertia tensor is symmetric because I_xy = −Σ m x y is the same as I_yx. A real symmetric matrix always has three perpendicular directions along which it only stretches the vector (output parallel to input). These are the **eigenvectors**, the stretch factors are the **eigenvalues**, and for the inertia tensor they are the principal axes and principal moments. In the example, the vector (1, 1, 0) gives output 0.09 × (1, 1, 0): eigenvalue 0.09.

### Bridge: Why the components of L change in a turning frame

Take an arrow glued to a turntable. Someone riding the turntable describes the arrow by two numbers measured along two marks painted on the table: these numbers never change, because the arrow and the marks turn together. Someone standing beside the turntable measures the arrow along fixed east and north directions: those numbers change continuously, going up and down as the arrow swings round. It is the same arrow described in two coordinate systems.

Angular momentum of a spinning rigid body works the same way. If L is attached to the body's turning (as for the tilted dumbbell of Module 2), its components in the body's own axes are constant while its components in space axes keep changing, and so the space observer must see a torque. The inertia tensor behaves like the arrow too: constant in body axes, time-dependent in space axes. That is why we move to body axes: the tensor stops depending on time, and we pay for it with one correction term, ω × L, which accounts for the turning of the axes themselves.

### Bridge: Rate of change of a vector in a rotating frame

Start with a unit vector e glued to a body that turns with angular velocity ω. In a short time dt the body turns through angle |ω| dt about the direction of ω, and the tip of e moves a small distance perpendicular both to ω and to e. That small displacement is (ω × e) dt, because the cross product has magnitude |ω||e| sin(angle) and points perpendicular to both. So for a vector glued to the body, de/dt = ω × e.

Now take a general vector A = A₁e₁ + A₂e₂ + A₃e₃ written with body axes. The product rule gives two kinds of terms: the components can change (dA₁/dt and so on, the body observer's rate) and the axes can turn (Aᵢ deᵢ/dt = Aᵢ ω × eᵢ). Adding, (dA/dt)_space = (dA/dt)_body + ω × A. PH-002 Module 3 proves this in full and PH-002 bridge B2 gives the derivative of the rotating unit vector; this lesson uses the result as a tool.

### 1. Where this topic sits and what the examiner wants

*Objective:* Place 'equation of motion for rotation' on the map: what PH-015 and PH-017 already gave you, what is new here, and which nine verified questions this lesson prepares you for.

## What you already have, and what is missing

PH-015 told you what a rigid body is, gave it six degrees of freedom, defined angular velocity **ω**, and showed that angular momentum is **L** = **I** **ω** with the inertia tensor (Module 6 there). PH-017 then worked out the *fixed-axis* machinery: τ = Iα, rotational energy, rolling down an incline, the compound pendulum, the Atwood machine with a massive pulley, the turntable and the tipping rod. Every one of those problems let you keep one axis fixed in space and treat the moment of inertia as a single number.

That shortcut fails when the body is free to turn about a point, or when it spins about an axis that is not a symmetry axis. Then **L is not parallel to ω**, the direction of **L** can change even when the rate of spin does not, and a torque is needed just to keep the spin steady. This lesson builds the *general* equation of motion for rotation and uses it on the two famous cases: a body with no torque on it (the Earth, a thrown racket) and, as a bridge, a top under gravity (the full treatment is in PH-020).

## The one idea

The rotational law is **τ = dL/dt**, with torque and angular momentum taken about the same point, and that point is either fixed in an inertial frame or is the centre of mass. Everything else is bookkeeping for how to evaluate dL/dt when **L** = **I** **ω** and the body is turning. The bookkeeping gives three results:

1. In the body's own principal axes: **τ = I α + ω × (I ω)**.
2. Written component by component: **Euler's dynamical equations**.
3. With the torque set to zero, or set by gravity: the behaviour of the free body and of the heavy top.

### 2. L = Iω in the space frame: why L is not parallel to ω, and a spinning dumbbell

*Objective:* Use the components L_i = Σ I_ij ω_j, see why the axis and the angular momentum point in different directions, and compute the torque a tilted spinning dumbbell exerts on its bearings.

## The tensor (link, not re-derived)

L_i = Σ_j I_ij ω_j with a symmetric matrix I_ij (I_xx = Σ m (y² + z²), I_xy = −Σ m x y): derived in PH-015 Module 6, elements in PH-017 Module 9 and PH-015 Module 7 (recap there, not repeated). Carry forward only this: state the origin and axes, K = ½ ω · L, and along the three principal axes I = diag(I₁, I₂, I₃) (a symmetry axis is always one of them; matrix tools: bridge B1).

## Why L is not parallel to ω

Write **L** = Σ m [ r² **ω** − (**r · ω**) **r** ]. The first term is along **ω**; the second points along each particle's own position vector. Only when the body is symmetric about the spin axis do the sideways pieces cancel in pairs. Examination sentence: *the mass is not symmetric about the spin axis, so the sideways parts of the particles' angular momenta do not cancel.*

## The space-frame equation, and why it is awkward

In an inertial frame the law is **τ = dL/dt**. But the components I_ij depend on the axes you choose. If the axes are fixed in space, the body turns through them, the coordinates x, y, z of its particles change, and every I_ij changes with time: **I**(t) = R **I**_body Rᵀ, where R(t) is the rotation matrix of PH-016. So even with **ω** constant, **L** = **I**(t) **ω** can change. The next example shows it numerically.

## Worked example: the tilted dumbbell

Two point masses m = 0.50 kg sit at the ends of a light rod of half-length a = 0.30 m, the rod centre on a vertical axle through O. The rod makes angle θ = 60° with the axle, and the assembly turns with constant ω = 20 rad/s about the axle (z-axis). The bearings are d = 0.40 m apart.

1. Position of one mass: r = a (sinθ, 0, cosθ). Velocity v = **ω** × r, magnitude ω a sinθ, perpendicular to the plane of the axle and the rod.
2. Each mass has angular momentum m r × v of magnitude m a (ω a sinθ), since r ⟂ v, and the two are equal. So |**L**| = 2 m a² ω sinθ = 2(0.50)(0.09)(20)(0.866) = 1.559 kg·m²/s.
3. **L** is perpendicular to the rod, so it makes 90° − θ = 30° with the axle. Its axial part is L_z = |L| sinθ = 2 m a² ω sin²θ = 1.35 kg·m²/s; its sideways part is 0.779 kg·m²/s and rotates with the dumbbell.
4. Because the sideways part rotates at ω, **L** sweeps a cone about the axle: dL/dt = **ω** × **L**, magnitude ω |L| sin30° = (20)(1.559)(0.5) = 15.59 N·m.
5. The bearings must therefore supply a torque of 15.59 N·m, as a pair of forces F = 15.59/0.40 = 38.97 N, although ω never changes.

**Independent check (forces only):** each mass needs centripetal force m ω² (a sinθ) = 0.50 × 400 × 0.2598 = 51.96 N toward the axle. The two forces are equal and opposite, but their lines of action are separated along the axle by 2a cosθ = 0.30 m, so they form a couple of 51.96 × 0.30 = 15.59 N·m. ✓

**Limits:** the torque 2 m a² ω² sinθ cosθ is zero at θ = 90° (L ∥ ω) and at θ = 0 (rod along the axle), and largest at 45°. **Dimension:** kg·m²·s⁻² = N·m. ✓

### 3. The body frame: dL/dt (space) = dL/dt (body) + ω × L

*Objective:* Convert the space-frame law τ = dL/dt into an equation written in axes fixed in the body, obtaining τ = I α + ω × (I ω), and interpret each term.

## The idea

The trouble in Module 2 was that **I**(t) changes in space axes. In axes **fixed in the body** the inertia tensor is a constant matrix, because the particles do not move relative to the axes. So write **L** in those axes. The price: the axes themselves rotate, so you must correct the time derivative.

## Rule for the derivative (link PH-002; one-line recap)

For any vector **A**, if (dA/dt)_space is its rate of change seen by the inertial observer and (dA/dt)_body is the rate seen by an observer who rotates with the body (watching only the components along the body axes), then

$$ (dA/dt)_space = (dA/dt)_body + ω × A $$

PH-002 Module 3 proves it from d**e**ᵢ/dt = **ω** × **e**ᵢ for each body axis. In short: the components change at the body rate, and each axis vector adds its own turning, ω × A. Bridge B3 repeats the unit-vector argument.

## Applying it to L

The origin is a fixed point O (or the centre of mass), the axes **e**₁, **e**₂, **e**₃ are fixed in the body, and the torque is taken about the same point. The space-frame law gives

$$ τ = (dL/dt)_space = (dL/dt)_body + ω × L $$

Now use **L** = **I** **ω**. In body axes **I** is constant, so (dL/dt)_body = **I** (dω/dt)_body. Next, **ω** × **ω** = 0, so applying the rule to **A** = **ω** gives (dω/dt)_space = (dω/dt)_body + ω × ω = (dω/dt)_body. The angular acceleration **α** is therefore the *same* in both frames, and we may write it without a label. Hence

$$ τ = I α + ω × (I ω) $$

with every quantity evaluated in the body axes.

## Reading the two terms

- **I α** is the part you already knew: torque equals inertia times angular acceleration.
- **ω × (I ω)** = **ω × L** is new. It is the torque needed just to turn the direction of **L** along with the body. It vanishes when **L** is parallel to **ω**, which happens when ω is along a principal axis, or when I is a multiple of the unit matrix (sphere, cube about the centre).
- **Check with Module 2:** for the dumbbell, α = 0, so τ = **ω** × **L**, exactly what was found.

## Conditions to state in an answer

1. The body is rigid, so **I** is constant in body axes.
2. The reference point O is either fixed in an inertial frame, or is the centre of mass (L = R × P + L_cm splits into a centre-of-mass part and a part about the CM, Mathur Section 6.13, proved in PH-004 Module 6, and the part about the CM obeys the same law even when the CM accelerates; this is how rolling is handled in Module 9).
3. τ and L are about the same point; components are along body axes, not space axes.
4. If the body axes are not principal, **I** is a full constant matrix but still constant; the equation is valid but the component form is untidy. Principal axes make it neat.

## Limiting cases and checks

- **Fixed principal axis:** take **ω** = ω **e**₃ with I₃ the moment about it. Then **ω** × **L** = 0 and τ₃ = I₃ α, which is PH-017's τ = Iα.
- **Sphere:** **I** = I **1**, so **I** **ω** ∥ **ω**; τ = I α in every direction.
- **Dimensions:** both terms have dimension M L² T⁻², the dimension of torque. ✓
- **Sign convention:** all vectors are right-handed; **ω** × **L** follows the right-hand rule.

Mathur (Section 11.13) writes the same step as τ_j = δJ_j/δt + ω × J, with J_j = Σ I_jk ω_k, and then restricts to the principal axes. The next module does that restriction component by component.

### 4. Euler's dynamical equations: full derivation

*Objective:* Derive I₁ω₁′ − (I₂ − I₃)ω₂ω₃ = τ₁ and its two cyclic partners from τ = Iα + ω × (Iω), and use them correctly in a problem.

## Setting

Origin O: a fixed point of the body, or its centre of mass. Axes **e**₁, **e**₂, **e**₃: principal axes at O, fixed in the body (right-handed). Then **I** = diag(I₁, I₂, I₃). Write ω₁, ω₂, ω₃ for the components of **ω** along them and ω₁′ = dω₁/dt, the rate of change of the component; by Module 3 this is also the component of **α**.

## Derivation, step by step

**Step 1: the components of L.** Since **I** is diagonal, L₁ = I₁ω₁, L₂ = I₂ω₂, L₃ = I₃ω₃.

**Step 2: the body-frame derivative.** The axes look fixed to the body observer, so (dL/dt)_body has components (I₁ω₁′, I₂ω₂′, I₃ω₃′), because I₁, I₂, I₃ are constants.

**Step 3: the cross product.** For vectors **a** and **b**, (a × b)₁ = a₂b₃ − a₃b₂, and cyclic. With **a** = **ω** and **b** = **L**:

- (ω × L)₁ = ω₂L₃ − ω₃L₂ = ω₂(I₃ω₃) − ω₃(I₂ω₂) = (I₃ − I₂) ω₂ω₃
- (ω × L)₂ = ω₃L₁ − ω₁L₃ = ω₃(I₁ω₁) − ω₁(I₃ω₃) = (I₁ − I₃) ω₃ω₁
- (ω × L)₃ = ω₁L₂ − ω₂L₁ = ω₁(I₂ω₂) − ω₂(I₁ω₁) = (I₂ − I₁) ω₁ω₂

**Step 4: assemble** τ = (dL/dt)_body + ω × L, component by component:

$$ τ₁ = I₁ω₁′ + (I₃ − I₂) ω₂ω₃ = I₁ω₁′ − (I₂ − I₃) ω₂ω₃ $$

$$ τ₂ = I₂ω₂′ + (I₁ − I₃) ω₃ω₁ = I₂ω₂′ − (I₃ − I₁) ω₃ω₁ $$

$$ τ₃ = I₃ω₃′ + (I₂ − I₁) ω₁ω₂ = I₃ω₃′ − (I₁ − I₂) ω₁ω₂ $$

These are **Euler's equations** (Mathur Eq. xiii, Section 11.13; Upadhyaya Eq. 8, Section 3.14, where the first appears as τ₁ = I₁ω₁′ + (I₃ − I₂)ω₂ω₃, the same thing).

## How to remember and read them

The pattern is cyclic: 1 → 2 → 3 → 1. In equation *i*, the product on the right involves the *other two* components, and its coefficient is the difference of the *other two* moments, ordered (j − k) with (i, j, k) cyclic. Equation 1 has (I₂ − I₃) with ω₂ω₃; equation 2 has (I₃ − I₁) with ω₃ω₁; equation 3 has (I₁ − I₂) with ω₁ω₂. If the differences are zero (sphere), the coupling vanishes.

## Assumptions and traps

- τᵢ are components of the torque **along the body axes**, not along fixed space directions. A fixed gravitational torque therefore has *time-dependent* components τᵢ(t) in these equations.
- The equations need principal axes. If you use any other body axes, keep the matrix form of Module 3.
- The equations are three coupled non-linear first-order equations: ω's change shape, not just size, because the body axes rotate.

## Checks

1. **Dimensions:** I ω′ and Δ(I) ω ω both have dimension M L² T⁻². ✓
2. **Fixed axis:** if the body can turn only about e₃ (a constraint that makes ω₁ = ω₂ = 0 at all times), then τ₃ = I₃ω₃′, which is τ = Iα, and the net τ₁ = τ₂ = 0 (the bearings cancel any applied perpendicular torque; Upadhyaya Eqs. 9 to 11).
3. **Sphere** (I₁ = I₂ = I₃): the coupling terms disappear; τ = Iα.
4. **Symmetric body** (I₁ = I₂): the third equation becomes I₃ω₃′ = τ₃, so ω₃ is constant in the torque-free case; this is the key to Module 6.

## Example of use

A body with I₁ = 3, I₂ = 5, I₃ = 6 (units of A) has ω = (n, 0, n) at t = 0 and no torque. Then ω₁′ = (5 − 6)(0)(n)/3 = 0, ω₃′ = 0, but ω₂′ = (6 − 3)(n)(n)/5 = 0.6 n² ≠ 0. So even though ω₂ starts at zero it immediately grows: the body does not keep spinning about a fixed direction in its own axes. Module 7 follows this through.

### 5. Torque-free motion: kinetic energy, |L| and the ellipsoids

*Objective:* Show from Euler's equations that K and |L|² are constants when τ = 0, derive the power relation dK/dt = τ·ω, and picture the tip of ω moving on the intersection of two ellipsoids.

## Setting

Set τ = 0 and use the equations of Module 4:

$$ I₁ω₁′ = (I₂ − I₃) ω₂ω₃,   I₂ω₂′ = (I₃ − I₁) ω₃ω₁,   I₃ω₃′ = (I₁ − I₂) ω₁ω₂ $$

The body is a free rigid body (Earth, a satellite, a thrown racket) with the origin at its centre of mass. Because the CM moves uniformly when no force acts, the CM frame is inertial, so the equations apply (Upadhyaya, Section 3.14).

## Derivation 1: kinetic energy is constant

K = ½ (I₁ω₁² + I₂ω₂² + I₃ω₃²). Differentiate:

dK/dt = I₁ω₁ω₁′ + I₂ω₂ω₂′ + I₃ω₃ω₃′

Multiply the first Euler equation by ω₁, the second by ω₂, the third by ω₃, and substitute:

dK/dt = ω₁ω₂ω₃ [ (I₂ − I₃) + (I₃ − I₁) + (I₁ − I₂) ] = ω₁ω₂ω₃ × 0 = 0

So **K is constant**. This is the result asked for in the 2019 question. With a torque kept in the equations, the same steps give the **power law**

$$ dK/dt = τ₁ω₁ + τ₂ω₂ + τ₃ω₃ = τ · ω $$

That is, P = τ · ω holds in general, and there is no extra law to remember (Module 9 uses it).

## Derivation 2: |L|² is constant

The magnitude of **L** is the same in any frame, so in body axes L² = I₁²ω₁² + I₂²ω₂² + I₃²ω₃². Differentiate and multiply each Euler equation by Iᵢωᵢ instead:

½ d(L²)/dt = I₁²ω₁ω₁′ + I₂²ω₂ω₂′ + I₃²ω₃ω₃′ = ω₁ω₂ω₃ [ I₁(I₂ − I₃) + I₂(I₃ − I₁) + I₃(I₁ − I₂) ]

Expand the bracket: I₁I₂ − I₁I₃ + I₂I₃ − I₁I₂ + I₃I₁ − I₂I₃ = 0. So L² is constant. This agrees with the vector argument: with τ = 0, **L** itself is a *fixed* vector in space; its components along the *moving* body axes change but its length does not.

## The two ellipsoids (a verbal picture)

In ω-space (axes ω₁, ω₂, ω₃) the two constants define two surfaces:

- **Energy ellipsoid:** I₁ω₁² + I₂ω₂² + I₃ω₃² = 2K, with semi-axes √(2K/Iᵢ).
- **Momentum ellipsoid:** I₁²ω₁² + I₂²ω₂² + I₃²ω₃² = L², with semi-axes L/Iᵢ.

The tip of **ω**, seen in the body axes, must lie on both, so it moves along their curve of intersection, called a **polhode**. For a given L and K the possible curves are closed loops around the axis of the largest moment and around the axis of the smallest; near the intermediate axis the polhodes (separatrices) cross at ±ω₂, which signals instability (Module 7).

## A bound that sets the picture

Because each Iᵢ lies between I_min and I_max:

L² = Σ Iᵢ²ωᵢ² ≤ I_max Σ Iᵢωᵢ² = 2K I_max, and L² ≥ 2K I_min.

So **I_min ≤ L²/(2K) ≤ I_max**. Three regimes follow: L²/2K between I_min and I₂ gives loops around the smallest-I axis; between I₂ and I_max gives loops around the largest-I axis; exactly L²/2K = I₂ gives the separating curves through the intermediate axis, the case of the 2025 question.

## Checks

1. **Symmetric case:** I₁ = I₂ gives constant ω₃ and constant ω₁² + ω₂² (Module 6): circles, so the polhode is a circle about the symmetry axis. ✓
2. **Energy accounting:** a true rigid body cannot dissipate; real flexible bodies lose K at fixed L and drift to the largest-I axis, since K = L²/(2 I_eff) is smallest there.
3. **Numerical check (Python):** with I = (1, 2, 3) and ω(0) = (0.3, 1.0, 0.2), integrating Euler's equations to t = 50 keeps K at 1.1050 and L² at 4.4500 to nine digits. ✓

### 6. Torque-free symmetric top: ω precesses at Ω = (I₃ − I₁)ω₃/I₁, and the Earth

*Objective:* Derive the body-frame precession of ω for I₁ = I₂, find the space-frame motion of the symmetry axis, and apply the result to the Earth's wobble with an honest comparison to observation.

## Setting

A symmetric top has I₁ = I₂ ≠ I₃ about the centre of mass; the e₃ axis is the figure (symmetry) axis. Torque zero. Euler's equations from Module 5 become

$$ I₁ω₁′ = (I₁ − I₃) ω₂ω₃,   I₁ω₂′ = (I₃ − I₁) ω₃ω₁,   I₃ω₃′ = 0 $$

## Derivation

**Step 1.** The third equation gives ω₃ = constant. (Upadhyaya, Section 3.16.)

**Step 2.** Define the constant Ω = (I₃ − I₁) ω₃ / I₁. Then ω₁′ = −Ω ω₂ and ω₂′ = +Ω ω₁.

**Step 3.** Differentiate the first: ω₁″ = −Ω ω₂′ = −Ω² ω₁. This is simple harmonic motion: ω₁ = A cos(Ωt + φ₀), and from ω₂ = −ω₁′/Ω, ω₂ = A sin(Ωt + φ₀).

**Step 4.** So the part of **ω** perpendicular to the figure axis has constant length A and turns round e₃ at angular speed Ω, while ω₃ stays fixed. The vector **ω** sweeps a cone about the figure axis, seen from the body.

**Interpretation and sign.** If I₃ > I₁ (flattened, disc-like, oblate) then Ω has the same sign as ω₃: the cone is traced in the same sense as the spin. If I₃ < I₁ (elongated, rod-like, prolate) the sense is opposite. If I₃ ≈ I₁ the precession is very slow compared with the spin.

## The picture in space

Since I₁ = I₂, **L** = I₁(ω₁e₁ + ω₂e₂) + I₃ω₃e₃ = I₁**ω** + (I₃ − I₁)ω₃ **e**₃, so

$$ ω = L/I₁ − Ω e₃ $$

The space derivative of the axis vector is d**e**₃/dt = **ω** × **e**₃ = (**L**/I₁) × **e**₃, because **e**₃ × **e**₃ = 0. But **L** is constant. Hence **the figure axis rotates about the fixed vector L at angular speed L/I₁**, keeping a constant angle θ with it, where tanθ = I₁ω⊥/(I₃ω₃). The three vectors **L**, **ω**, **e**₃ stay in one plane and rotate together about **L**.

**Consistency check.** The plane containing **e**₃ and **L** turns at L/I₁. Relative to that plane, the body spins at ω₃ − (L/I₁)cosθ = ω₃ − I₃ω₃/I₁ = −Ω, using L cosθ = I₃ω₃. So the space picture (precession L/I₁, spin −Ω) agrees with the body picture (Ω).

**Wobbling coin.** A thin disc has I₃ = 2I₁. Then Ω = ω₃ and, for small wobble, L/I₁ ≈ 2ω₃: a wobbling plate shows its axis circling about twice per spin. Python integration with I = (1, 1, 2), ω₃ = 10 and ω⊥ = 0.1 gives Ω = 10.0000 and an axis rate 20.00025, and tanθ matches 0.005. ✓

## The Earth as a torque-free symmetric top

The Earth is flattened at the poles, so its polar moment C = I₃ is slightly larger than the equatorial moment A = I₁. External torques are small, so Euler's equations give Ω = (C − A) ω₃ / A. Upadhyaya (Section 3.16) uses (C − A)/A ≈ 1/306 and obtains a period T = 2π/Ω = 306 days, since ω₃ is one rotation per day. With the commonly quoted figure (C − A)/A ≈ 1/305 (verify), T ≈ 305 sidereal days (about 304 mean solar days): this is Euler's prediction for how a displaced rotation axis would circle the figure axis.

**Observation.** The measured motion of the pole (the Chandler wobble) has a period of about 433 days (reported; verify), and Upadhyaya prints 440 days with a wobble radius of about 10 m at the pole (about 0.32 arcsecond). The source attributes the gap to the Earth not being a perfectly rigid, perfect oblate spheroid. **Do not write that the Chandler wobble is 305 days; 305 days is the rigid-Earth prediction.**

**Limits:** (C − A) → 0 gives Ω → 0, no precession (sphere). ω₃ → 0: no precession (Ω → 0). **Dimension:** [Ω] = [ω₃] since (I₃ − I₁)/I₁ is dimensionless. ✓

### 7. Three different moments: the intermediate-axis instability and the 2025 question

*Objective:* Derive the linearised stability of spin about each principal axis, state which axes are stable, and recognise the 2025 problem as the borderline (separatrix) case.

## Setting

Torque-free body with I₁, I₂, I₃ all different, spinning about principal axis 1 with large rate Ω₀. Perturb it slightly: ω = (Ω₀, ε₂, ε₃) with ε₂, ε₃ small. We follow ε₂ and ε₃ to first order, throwing away products of two small quantities.

## Derivation (linear stability about axis 1)

From Module 5:

- I₁ω₁′ = (I₂ − I₃) ε₂ε₃, which is second order, so ω₁ ≈ Ω₀ stays constant to first order.
- I₂ε₂′ = (I₃ − I₁) ε₃ Ω₀
- I₃ε₃′ = (I₁ − I₂) Ω₀ ε₂

Differentiate the second and substitute the third:

$$ ε₂″ = (I₃ − I₁)Ω₀ ε₃′ / I₂ = (I₃ − I₁)(I₁ − I₂) Ω₀² ε₂ / (I₂I₃) = −κ ε₂,   κ = (I₂ − I₁)(I₃ − I₁) Ω₀² / (I₂I₃) $$

The same equation holds for ε₃.

- **κ > 0** (I₁ is the smallest or the largest moment: the two factors I₂ − I₁ and I₃ − I₁ have the same sign): ε₂ oscillates at √κ. The perturbation stays small: **stable**.
- **κ < 0** (I₁ is the intermediate moment: the factors I₂ − I₁ and I₃ − I₁ have opposite signs): ε₂″ = +|κ| ε₂, so ε₂ grows as e^{λt} and the perturbation does not stay small: **unstable**. For a spin axis i whose moment lies between the other two moments I_a < I_i < I_b,

$$ λ = Ω₀ √[ (I_i − I_a)(I_b − I_i) / (I_a I_b) ] $$

For the labelled intermediate axis 2 this is λ = Ω₀ √[(I₂ − I₁)(I₃ − I₂)/(I₁I₃)].

**Rule:** rotation about the axes of smallest and largest moment is stable; about the intermediate axis it is unstable. Warning: this is stability against *small* disturbances for a rigid body. For a body that can dissipate energy at fixed L, only the largest-moment axis is a final resting state (Module 5).

## Numbers (checked by Python)

A thick racket-like body (not a lamina, since I₁ + I₂ ≠ I₃) with I = (0.010, 0.020, 0.025) kg·m² spinning at 10 rad/s:

- About axis 1 (smallest): κ = 0.30 Ω₀², frequency 5.48 rad/s, period 1.147 s (integration: 1.1472 s).
- About axis 3 (largest): κ = 0.375 Ω₀², frequency 6.12 rad/s, period 1.026 s (integration: 1.0260 s).
- About axis 2 (intermediate): λ = 0.4472 × 10 = 4.47 s⁻¹, e-folding time 0.224 s (fitted growth rate 4.475 s⁻¹). For the nonlinear flip the disturbance must be stated: with ω₁ = ω₃ = 0.1 rad/s (one per cent of the spin in each transverse component) the integration shows ω₂ passing through zero at 1.77 s, after which the body has flipped over (the tennis-racket or book-toss effect); with 0.1 rad/s in only one transverse component the first zero comes at 1.27 s (ω₃ only) or 1.29 s (ω₁ only). The flip is periodic: ω₂ swings between about +10 and −10 rad/s and crosses zero at about 1.77, 4.67, 7.57 and 10.47 s (returning through zero at about 7.6 s). Linear theory fails once the disturbance is large, so these times are numerical, not linear, results and depend on the disturbance.

## The borderline case: 2025 Q2(a)

Principal moments 3A, 5A, 6A, ω(0) = (n, 0, n). The intermediate axis is axis 2 (5A). Module 5's bound: 2K = A n²(3 + 6) = 9A n², L² = A²n²(9 + 36) = 45 A²n², so L²/(2K) = 5A = I₂ exactly: the motion lies *on the separating curve*. It approaches the intermediate axis only asymptotically. Using the two constants (full derivation in practice item D5; PH-015 Module 8 also solves this question) one finds ω₁ = ω₃ = n sech(nt/√5) and ω₂ = (3n/√5) tanh(nt/√5). For large t: **ω₁ → 0, ω₃ → 0, ω₂ → 3n/√5 ≈ 1.342 n**. The Python integration of Euler's equations agrees with these closed forms to 10⁻¹¹.

### 8. Bridge to PH-020: applying tau = dL/dt to the heavy symmetric top (steady precession)

*Objective:* Apply Euler's equations and tau = dL/dt to the heavy top: derive the steady-precession condition mgl = Ω(I₃ω₃ − I₁Ω cosθ) and the slow-precession result Ω ≈ mgl/(I₃ω₃), state their limits; the full treatment of the top and gyroscope is PH-020.

This module is a bridge: it applies τ = dL/dt and Euler's equations to the heavy top. The full treatment (nutation, the Euler-angle Lagrangian, gyroscope applications) is PH-020.

## Setting and sign conventions

A symmetric top has one point O fixed (its tip, on the ground). I₃ is the moment about the figure axis **e**₃; I₁ = I₂ is the moment about any axis through O perpendicular to **e**₃ (computed *about O*, so the parallel-axis theorem is already included). The centre of mass is at distance l from O along **e**₃, the axis makes angle θ with the upward vertical **z**, and the top has mass m. Gravity −mg**z**.

## The torque

Torque about O: **τ** = l**e**₃ × (−mg**z**) = mgl **z** × **e**₃. Two consequences: **τ** ⟂ **e**₃, so Euler's third equation gives I₃ω₃′ = 0 and **ω₃ is constant**; and **τ** ⟂ **z**, so the vertical component of **L** is constant.

## Simple version: fast spin

If the spin is fast, **L** ≈ I₃ω₃ **e**₃. The torque is perpendicular to **L**, so by d**L** = **τ** dt the vector **L** changes direction, not size. The tip of **L** moves on a horizontal circle of radius L sinθ. In time dt it turns through dφ: |d**L**| = L sinθ dφ = mgl sinθ dt. Cancel sinθ:

$$ Ω_p = dφ/dt = mgl / (I₃ω₃) $$

This is the Mathur and Upadhyaya result (Sections 11.15, 3.17); the same fast-spin derivation is given in PH-004 Module 9, which also lists which components of L are conserved. The sense is that of the spin when the top stands above its pivot.

## Exact steady precession (derivation)

Steady precession: θ constant, **e**₃ turning about **z** at constant rate Ω. The angular velocity is **ω** = Ω**z** + s**e**₃ (s = spin relative to the precessing frame), so ω₃ = s + Ω cosθ.

1. The part of **ω** perpendicular to **e**₃ is Ω(**z** − cosθ **e**₃).
2. Therefore **L** = I₁Ω(**z** − cosθ **e**₃) + I₃ω₃**e**₃ = I₁Ω**z** + (I₃ω₃ − I₁Ω cosθ)**e**₃.
3. **z** and Ω are constant, and d**e**₃/dt = Ω**z** × **e**₃, so d**L**/dt = (I₃ω₃ − I₁Ω cosθ) Ω **z** × **e**₃.
4. Equate to **τ** = mgl **z** × **e**₃ and cancel **z** × **e**₃ (valid for sinθ ≠ 0):

$$ mgl = Ω (I₃ω₃ − I₁ Ω cosθ),   i.e.   I₁ cosθ Ω² − I₃ω₃ Ω + mgl = 0 $$

5. Roots: Ω = [ I₃ω₃ ∓ √(I₃²ω₃² − 4 I₁ mgl cosθ) ] / (2 I₁ cosθ).

**Slow root (the usual precession).** For I₃²ω₃² ≫ 4I₁mgl cosθ, expand the root: Ω ≈ mgl/(I₃ω₃) · [1 + I₁Ω₀ cosθ/(I₃ω₃)], where Ω₀ = mgl/(I₃ω₃). **At θ = 90°** (horizontal gyroscope) cosθ = 0 and Ω = mgl/(I₃ω₃) *exactly*.

## Conditions, limits, consequences

- **Real roots need** ω₃ ≥ 2√(I₁ m g l cosθ)/I₃ (for cosθ > 0; a top hanging below its pivot, cosθ < 0, always has real roots). Below this a steady cone is impossible: the top is not spinning fast enough and falls or nods.
- **Fast spin:** ω₃ → ∞ gives Ω → 0: the axis holds its direction (gyroscopic stability, Mathur Section 11.16).

## Numbers (checked by Python)

Gyroscope wheel: m = 0.50 kg, l = 0.12 m, I₃ = 2.0×10⁻³ kg·m², I₁ about the pivot 8.2×10⁻³ kg·m², spin 3000 rpm (314.16 rad/s). Slow approximation: Ω₀ = 0.588/0.6283 = 0.936 rad/s. At θ = 60° the exact slow root is 0.9416 rad/s (+0.61 per cent); minimum spin for steady precession there is 49.1 rad/s (469 rpm).

**Nutation.** If the top is not launched with exactly the steady rate, θ oscillates between limits while the axis precesses. That needs the Euler-angle energy equation and is a forward link to PH-020 (not treated here).

**Application:** the Sun and Moon pull on the Earth's equatorial bulge; the axis precesses once in about 26,000 years (verify; Mathur, Section 11.17, gives 25,800), about 50 arcseconds a year (360° × 3600/25,800).

### 9. Applications: rolling, the yo-yo, energy and power

*Objective:* Choose the point about which to take torques, solve rolling and the yo-yo with the general law, cross-check against PH-017, and use K and P = τ·ω in the energy method.

## Which point for the torque?

The law τ = dL/dt is guaranteed for (a) a point fixed in an inertial frame and (b) the centre of mass. A third point is safe only in special cases. The **contact point P** of a body that rolls without slipping on a plane is momentarily at rest and its acceleration points toward the centre of the body (only the centripetal part ω²R survives, since a − αR = 0). The extra term in the law is M (r_cm − r_P) × a_P, which vanishes because both vectors lie along the same line. So **τ_P = I_P α holds (with I_P = I_cm + MR² for a round body of radius R)**, valid for a round body with its CM at the geometric centre, rolling without slipping on a fixed plane or fixed curved surface: the contact point's acceleration lies along the common normal through the CM, and |r_cm − r_P| is constant. The incline below and the cylinder rolling inside a fixed cylinder (2025 Q1(a)) are both such cases.

## Rolling down an incline: general law and the PH-017 check

Down-slope positive, incline angle β, body mass M, radius R, I_cm = MK², no slipping.

- **About P:** the only torque about P comes from the weight component M g sinβ acting at the centre, arm R (normal force and friction pass through P). So M g sinβ R = (MK² + MR²) α and a = αR:

$$ a = g sinβ / (1 + K²/R²) $$

- **About the CM** (PH-017, Module 7): M a = Mg sinβ − f and I_cm α = fR give the same a; for a solid sphere (K² = 2R²/5), a = (5/7) g sinβ.

## The yo-yo

A yo-yo of mass m, moment I about its axle, axle radius r hangs on a string wound on the axle; the free end is held fixed. Down is positive. The string is vertical and leaves the axle at the point P, a horizontal distance r from the centre.

**CM method.** m a = m g − T, and torque about the centre: T r = I α, with a = α r (no slipping on the string). Eliminate T:

$$ a = g / (1 + I/(m r²)),   T = m g (I/(m r²)) / (1 + I/(m r²)) $$

**Contact-point method.** About P the weight has arm r, and the rotation is about P: m g r = (I + m r²) α, so a = α r gives the same a. ✓ (P is at rest instantaneously and its acceleration is horizontal, toward the centre.)

**Numbers:** m = 0.20 kg, R = 0.040 m (I = ½mR² = 1.6×10⁻⁴ kg·m²), r = 0.008 m: I/(m r²) = 12.5, a = 9.8/13.5 = 0.726 m/s², T = 1.815 N; 0.80 m takes 1.48 s; 92.6 per cent of the energy is rotational. **Limits:** r → R gives a = 2g/3; r → 0 gives a → 0.

## Energy method and power

Work done by a torque: dW = τ·dθ, and power P = τ·ω, which is exactly dK/dt (Module 5). For a rigid body rolling, K = ½Mv²(1 + K²/R²) with K here the radius of gyration. The worked energy examples (sphere, disc, flywheel) and the translation-rotation dictionary table (the table sought in 2023 Q2(c)(i)) are in the master notes ('Translation-rotation dictionary and energy examples'); numericals N9 to N11 practise them.

## Decision guide: which equation?

### Notes: The general equations in one place

- Space frame, point O fixed in an inertial frame or the centre of mass: τ = dL/dt.
- Angular momentum: L = I ω, with L_i = Σ_j I_ij ω_j, I_xx = Σ m(y² + z²), I_xy = −Σ m x y; the matrix is symmetric.
- Frame rule: (dA/dt)_space = (dA/dt)_body + ω × A.
- Body frame with body-fixed axes: τ = I α + ω × (I ω), where α = dω/dt is the same in both frames.
- Euler's equations (principal axes): I₁ω₁′ − (I₂ − I₃)ω₂ω₃ = τ₁; I₂ω₂′ − (I₃ − I₁)ω₃ω₁ = τ₂; I₃ω₃′ − (I₁ − I₂)ω₁ω₂ = τ₃.
- Kinetic energy K = ½ ω·L = ½ Σ Iᵢωᵢ²; power P = τ·ω = dK/dt.
- Torque-free: K and L² constant; L constant as a vector in space.

### Notes: Derivation chain: what follows from what

- τ = dL/dt (space) → frame rule → τ = Iα + ω × (Iω) in body axes (Module 3).
- Principal axes and the cross product expanded → Euler's equations (Module 4).
- Euler's equations × ωᵢ, summed → dK/dt = τ·ω (Module 5).
- Euler's equations × Iᵢωᵢ, summed, τ = 0 → L² constant (Module 5).
- I₁ = I₂ → ω₃ constant → ω₁″ = −Ω²ω₁ with Ω = (I₃ − I₁)ω₃/I₁ (Module 6).
- Small perturbation of spin about axis i → ε″ = −κε, sign of κ decides stability (Module 7).
- Torque mgl z × e₃ on a symmetric top, steady cone → mgl = Ω(I₃ω₃ − I₁Ω cosθ) (Module 8).

### Notes: Three torque-free cases compared

- Which axis a free rigid body spins about decides everything; the polhode shape follows from Module 5.

### Notes: Heavy top and gyroscope formulae

- Torque about the pivot: τ = mgl z × e₃, perpendicular to e₃ and to the vertical.
- Fast-spin precession: Ω = mgl/(I₃ω₃); exact at θ = 90°.
- Exact steady precession: mgl = Ω(I₃ω₃ − I₁Ω cosθ).
- Roots: Ω = [I₃ω₃ ∓ √(I₃²ω₃² − 4I₁mgl cosθ)]/(2I₁cosθ); slow root is the ordinary precession.
- Real roots require I₃²ω₃² ≥ 4I₁mgl cosθ (for cosθ > 0; a top hanging below its pivot, cosθ < 0, always has real roots).
- Slow-root correction: Ω ≈ Ω₀[1 + I₁Ω₀cosθ/(I₃ω₃)], Ω₀ = mgl/(I₃ω₃).

### Notes: Key terms

- Principal axes: the three perpendicular axes through a point along which the inertia tensor is diagonal.
- Torque-free motion: motion of a rigid body with no external torque about its centre of mass.
- Polhode: the path traced by the tip of ω as seen in the body axes.
- Precession: turning of the direction of an axis (here the figure axis or the angular momentum) about another direction. (slow for a fast top; torque-free coin precession runs at about 2ω₃)
- Nutation: an additional nodding of the top's axis; flagged, not derived here.
- Figure axis: the symmetry axis e₃ of a symmetric top.
- Gyroscopic term: ω × (I ω), the torque needed to turn L along with the body.

### Notes: Standard moments used in examples

- Thin disc about the symmetry axis I₃ = MR²/2; about a diameter I₁ = MR²/4, so I₃ = 2I₁.
- Sphere: I = 2MR²/5 for every axis through the centre, so Ω = 0 in the torque-free case.
- Cube about an edge through a corner: I_XX = 2Ma²/3, I_YX = I_ZX = −Ma²/4 (as given in the 2025 question; PH-017 Module 9).
- Two point masses m at ±a on a light rod: I = 2ma² about an axis perpendicular to the rod through its centre, 0 about the rod.
- Earth: (C − A)/A about 1/305 (verify); rigid-body period 305 days.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Euler's theorem (any displacement about a fixed point is a rotation), Euler angles (three orientation coordinates) and Euler's equations (dynamics in the body frame) are three different things.
- ω (instantaneous angular velocity) and L (angular momentum) are parallel only along a principal axis.
- Ω in Module 6 is the turning rate of ω relative to the body; L/I₁ is the turning rate of the figure axis relative to space.
- 305 days is the rigid-Earth prediction; the observed Chandler wobble is about 433 days (verify).
- Precession of the heavy top is driven by external torque; torque-free precession needs none.

### Notes: When the equations need care

- Euler's equations in the stated form need principal axes at the reference point; otherwise use the matrix form.
- The reference point must be fixed or the centre of mass; an accelerating arbitrary point adds a term M(r_cm − r_P) × a_P.
- Contact-point torque for rolling is valid for a round body with its CM at the geometric centre, rolling without slipping on a fixed plane or fixed curved surface: the contact point's acceleration lies along the common normal through the CM, and |r_cm − r_P| is constant; it holds on an incline and for a cylinder rolling inside a fixed cylinder.
- Slow-precession formula fails when the spin is not fast: check I₃²ω₃² against 4I₁mgl cosθ (for cosθ > 0; a top hanging below its pivot, cosθ < 0, always has real roots).
- The 'flip' time of the intermediate axis is a nonlinear result and depends on the size of the disturbance.
