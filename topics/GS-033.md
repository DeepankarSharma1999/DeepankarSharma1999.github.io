## GS-033: Administrative changes after 1858

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS1 · History · Modern India / Freedom

### Bridge: Words of government: Crown, Parliament, Viceroy, province, district, covenant

- **Crown**: the British monarch as the holder of sovereignty. After 1858 the Crown, not the East India Company, ruled India.
- **Parliament**: Britain's law-making body. It passed every Act named in this topic.
- **Secretary of State for India**: a British cabinet minister, answerable to Parliament, who headed Indian affairs from London.
- **Viceroy**: the Crown's personal representative in India, the former Governor-General with an added title.
- **Presidency and province**: a large territorial unit of British India. The three presidencies were Bengal (Calcutta), Madras and Bombay; other provinces were smaller or newer.
- **District**: the basic unit of administration inside a province, headed by an official (the collector or district magistrate).
- **Covenanted service**: the top civil posts, held by officials who signed a covenant (formal contract); 'uncovenanted' posts were lower.
- **Resolution**: a formal government policy statement. It is *not* an Act of Parliament; Mayo's 1870 and Ripon's 1882 measures were resolutions.

The trick is to place each person or event on a ladder: Parliament (London) at the top, the Secretary of State below it, the Viceroy in India, then provinces, districts and local bodies. Most questions ask at which rung something happened.

### 1. Orientation: from Company to Crown, the machine in one page

*Objective:* State in your own words what 'administrative changes after 1858' covers and name the nine parts of the machine that changed.

## What this topic is

*Administration* means the day-to-day running of a state: who takes decisions, who collects revenue, who enforces the law, who is recruited into office. It differs from *constitutional* change, which is about Acts of Parliament that redraw powers (GS-158 gives that sequence). This topic asks a narrower question: after the British Crown took over from the East India Company in 1858, how did the working machinery of government change?

## Why 1858 is the hinge

The Revolt of 1857 (GS-032) showed that an organised rising could threaten British rule. Spectrum says the British learned fast. They wanted to narrow the gap between ruler and ruled, to bring some Indians into administration so that rulers would understand local customs, and to protect British commercial interests, which by now included large British capital in railways and loans to the Government of India. Spectrum calls the result a *new stage of colonialism*: the machinery was repaired and rebuilt, but real power stayed in British hands.

### 2. Foundation: the top of the pyramid, Secretary of State, Viceroy and councils

*Objective:* Draw the chain of authority from Parliament to the Viceroy after 1858 and list the weaknesses of the councils of 1861.

## The chain of authority

GS-032 (module 9) and GS-158 (module 4) give the text of the Act of 1858. Here the aim is to read it as a *structure*.

1. **British Parliament**: the ultimate power over India stayed with it (Spectrum).
2. **Secretary of State for India**: a member of the British cabinet, answerable to Parliament, who took over the work earlier done by the Company's Directors and the Board of Control.
3. **Council of India** (15 members): only advisory. The initiative and the final decision rested with the Secretary of State. Spectrum says this ended the dual system of Pitt's India Act of 1784, in which two bodies had controlled the Company.
4. **Viceroy** (the Governor-General with a new title): carried on the government in India with an **executive council** whose members headed departments and advised him.

## The paradox

Spectrum argues that concentrating authority in the Secretary of State in London gradually reduced the Viceroy to a subordinate position, kept Indian opinion further away from policy-making, and increased the influence of British industrialists, merchants and bankers. It adds that the Crown's assumption of power was one of formality rather than substance. So the top of the machine looked more accountable (to Parliament) but was further from India.

## What the Indian Councils Act of 1861 added (context only)

- A fifth member, a jurist, joined the Viceroy's executive council.
- For making laws the Viceroy could add six to twelve extra members, at least half of them non-officials, Indian or English.
- The Viceroy could issue **ordinances** (emergency orders that bypass the council) valid for six months.

Spectrum lists the weaknesses of this law-making council: it could not discuss important matters, and no financial matter without prior approval; it had no control over the budget and could not discuss executive action; a bill needed the Viceroy's approval and could still be disallowed by the Secretary of State; and the Indian non-officials came from elite groups (princes, landlords, diwans), not from popular opinion. Its main use was to give official measures the look of having passed a legislature. The 1892 and 1909 Acts widened these councils; GS-158 module 4 has the points, and GS-050 (Day 14, not yet written) will return to them.

## Source differences to remember

GS-032 notes a related difference (Spectrum against NCERT); teach 'Viceroy from 1858' and name both instruments only if the question asks.
- Long title: 'An Act for the better Government of India' (Government of India Act 1858; standard, verify). Spectrum's 'Better Government' is right; GS-158 module 4 had printed 'Good Government', a textbook paraphrase, now corrected there.

### 3. Core: why the machine was rebuilt, and the policy mood after 1857

*Objective:* Explain the two causes Spectrum gives for a 'new stage of colonialism' and describe five policies that follow from it, with the dated labour and press laws.

## Two causes

1. **The lesson of 1857.** Organised mass action could threaten British rule, so the ruler-subject gap was to be narrowed and some natives associated with administration to learn local customs (Spectrum).
2. **A changing world economy.** In the later nineteenth century new industrial powers (the USA, Japan, European states) competed for colonies, raw materials and markets, and British supremacy in finance and trade ended. British capital flowed into Indian railways, loans, tea, coal, jute, shipping and banking, and needed political security.

Spectrum places the 'reactionary' vice-royalties of Lytton, Dufferin, Lansdowne, Elgin and, above all, Curzon in this stage.

## Five policies that followed (Spectrum, chapter 27)

- **Divide and rule**: after a spell of repression of Muslims following 1857, the authorities after 1870 used middle and upper educated Muslims against rising nationalism; princes were set against subjects, and caste and region were played off.
- **Hostility to educated Indians**: the new nationalism, with the Congress founded in 1885, was read as a challenge.
- **Alliance with zamindars and princes** as a counterweight; lands of most Awadh taluqdars confiscated before 1857 were restored.
- **Withdrawal of support for social reform**, which had angered the orthodox.
- **Neglect of social services**: heavy spending on army, administration and wars left little for education, health and sanitation.

Alongside this runs an *ideology*: Spectrum (chapter 28) says the aim was redefined as permanent 'trusteeship' over Indians, who were called permanently immature.

## Labour law: the Factory Acts

The first demand for factory regulation came from the Lancashire textile lobby, which feared cheap Indian labour. A commission was appointed in 1875; the first Act came in 1881.

| Act | Main provisions (Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| Factory Act 1881 | Children under 7 barred; children's hours limited to 9 a day; four holidays a month; dangerous machinery fenced |
| Factory Act 1891 | Minimum age raised 7 to 9, maximum 12 to 14; children's hours 7; women's hours 11 a day with a 1.5 hour interval; weekly holiday for all |

Neither Act applied to British-owned tea and coffee plantations, where laws made breach of a labour contract a criminal offence. Note what the 1881 Act does *not* contain: no wage fixing and no right to form unions. That is the point behind the 2017 PYQ in module 10.

## Press law

Metcalfe lifted press restrictions in 1835. Lytton's Vernacular Press Act of 1878 restricted the Indian-language press; it was repealed in 1882 after protest. Restrictions returned in 1908 and 1910 during the swadeshi agitation (GS-038).

## Racial exclusion

Indians were kept out of the higher civil and military posts and out of many public places. Association was meant to bring cooperation and information, not to share power.

### 4. Core: provinces and finance, from Mayo to the 1882 revenue split

*Objective:* Describe the structure of the provinces after 1858 and sequence the financial decentralisation steps of 1870, 1877 and 1882.

## Two kinds of province

Spectrum describes a split. Spectrum says the three **presidencies** of Bombay, Madras and Calcutta (Bengal) had more rights and powers and were each governed by a **governor** with an executive council of three, all appointed by the Crown. This fits Madras and Bombay; Bengal was under a Lieutenant-Governor from 1854 until 1912, when it got a governor-in-council (standard; verify). The other provinces were governed by **lieutenant governors** and **chief commissioners** appointed by the Governor-General (Viceroy). The Indian Councils Act of 1861 returned law-making powers to Madras and Bombay (taken away in 1833, GS-158 module 3) and allowed councils in other provinces. This is the beginning of **legislative devolution**: power moved down from the Governor-General's council to the provinces.

## Why money is the key

Spectrum calls the grants of 1870 the first step towards bifurcating (splitting) central and provincial finances, which implies that finances had been run as one pool until then. Control of money was therefore the real test of provincial freedom. Spectrum says that the steps taken in this period were administrative reorganisation to raise revenue and cut expenditure, not progress towards provincial autonomy.

## The three steps

| Year | Viceroy | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1870 | Mayo | Fixed sums from central revenue given to provinces to run police, jails, education, medical services and roads; provinces to manage these as they liked. Spectrum calls it the first step to separate central and provincial finance |
| 1877 | Lytton | Land revenue, excise, general administration, and law and justice transferred to provinces; plus a fixed share of income from stamps, excise and income tax collected in the province |
| 1882 | (Ripon's period) | All revenue sources divided into three groups: general (centre), provincial (provinces) and divided |

Spectrum's verdict: the central government stayed supreme and kept detailed control, because both centre and provinces were fully subordinate to the Secretary of State and the British government.

## Where the story goes next

- The Act of 1919 separated provincial and central budgets for the first time and let provincial legislatures make their budgets (GS-158 module 5; Spectrum chapter 26).
- Under the 1935 Act provinces got independent financial powers and could borrow on their own security (GS-047 module 6, later today).

## Source slips

Spectrum's end-of-chapter summary says 'Mayo's Resolution of 1878' and 'Indian Councils Act of 1882'. The chapter text gives Mayo's resolution as 1870, and the Councils Act that followed the Congress demand as 1892. Treat the text as correct and the summary as a printing slip.

### 5. Core: local government from Mayo to Ripon and the 1935 Act

*Objective:* State why the British created local bodies, what Ripon's 1882 resolution said, and why the bodies stayed weak until 1935.

## What local bodies are

A **municipality** governs a town; a **district board** governs a rural district (the 1908 commission, below, also proposed sub-district boards at taluka or tehsil level); a **panchayat** is the village-level body. They were meant to run education, health, sanitation, water supply and roads from local taxes.

## Why the British wanted them (Spectrum's five reasons)

1. Financial difficulties of over-centralisation.
2. Modern civic amenities seen in Europe had to be transplanted as India's economic contacts grew.
3. The rising nationalist movement put better basic facilities on its agenda.
4. Some policy-makers saw Indian association at local level as a way to check politicisation without weakening British supremacy.
5. Local taxes could pay for local welfare, avoiding a burden on the treasury or the rich.

## The stages

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1864 to 1868 | Local bodies first formed; mostly nominated members, headed by district magistrates; seen as extra tax-collecting devices |
| 1870, Mayo | Local taxation allowed so provinces could balance budgets; provinces passed municipal acts |
| 1882, Ripon | Resolution to apply Mayo's decentralisation principle to local bodies; Ripon is called the father of local self-government |
| 1908 (Spectrum; standard accounts say 1907 to 1909, verify) | Royal Commission on Decentralisation: more powers for village panchayats, sub-district boards, freer taxation |
| 1915 and 1918 | Official resolution mostly left the report on paper; the 1918 resolution tied local self-government to the August 1917 promise of responsible government |
| 1919 | Local self-government made a transferred subject, but finance was reserved, so ministers lacked funds |
| 1930 | Simon Commission found little progress and proposed more provincial control |
| 1935 | Provincial autonomy put finance under popular ministries; new local acts; powers of taxation little improved |

## Ripon's resolution in five points

Non-officials in a majority; non-officials as chairpersons; official interference reduced to revising and checking, not dictating; local bodies given definite duties and revenue; official sanction retained for loans, new taxes, sale of property and large works. Acts between 1883 and 1885 followed.

## Why it stayed weak

Elected members were a minority in all district boards and in many municipalities; the franchise was narrow; district boards were still headed by officials; government could suspend or supersede bodies at will. Spectrum adds that the bureaucracy did not share Ripon's liberal views and that Curzon actually increased official control.

## Link forward

Article 40 of the Constitution directs states to organise village panchayats; the 73rd and 74th Amendments strengthened rural and urban bodies (GS-195, Day 3, and GS-196, Day 4). Those are the modern answers to the weaknesses listed here.

### 6. Core: the civil services, from Cornwallis to the Lee Commission

*Objective:* Sequence the main steps in the evolution of the civil service and explain how Indians were kept out of high posts.

## Meaning

The **civil service** is the body of salaried officials who administer a state. Under the Company the term separated *civil* servants (running trade and revenue) from military and naval servants. *Covenanted* servants were those bound by a written covenant (contract) with the Company; the posts reserved for them were the top ones (Spectrum notes a reservation of posts worth over 500 pounds a year in 1793).

## Timeline

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1786 to 1793 | Cornwallis organises the service: higher pay, strict rules against private trade, ban on presents and bribes, promotion by seniority |
| 1800 | Wellesley sets up Fort William College, Calcutta, to train new recruits |
| 1806 | Court of Directors disapproves it; East India College set up at Haileybury in England with two years' training |
| 1853 | Charter Act ends Company patronage; recruitment by open competition |
| 1858 | Queen's Proclamation promises equal opportunity irrespective of race or creed |
| 1861 | Indian Civil Service Act reserves certain posts for covenanted servants; exam held in England, in English, based on classical learning (Greek and Latin) |
| 1863 | Satyendra Nath Tagore becomes the first Indian to qualify |
| 1878 to 1879 | Lytton's Statutory Civil Service: one-sixth of covenanted posts for Indians of high families, nominated by local governments; failed and abolished |
| 1886 | Aitchison Committee (set up by Dufferin): drop covenanted and uncovenanted; Imperial (exam in England), Provincial and Subordinate (exams in India) services; age back to 23 |
| 1893 | House of Commons resolution for simultaneous exams in India and England, never implemented |
| 1919 | Montford reforms: one-third of recruitment in India, rising by 1.5 per cent annually; simultaneous exams |
| 1924 | Lee Commission: 50:50 parity of Europeans and Indians in direct ICS recruitment within 15 years; Public Service Commission |
| 1935 | Federal and provincial Public Service Commissions provided |

## The age-limit squeeze

The maximum age for the exam was 23 in 1859, 22 in 1860, 21 in 1866 and 19 in 1878 (Lytton). Lowering it by four years in nineteen years, with the exam in London, made competition very hard for young Indians.

## Why Indians were excluded (Spectrum)

British belief that only the English could build a service serving British interests; belief that Indians were untrustworthy and incapable; and competition for lucrative posts among the Europeans themselves. The Charter Act of 1833 had *declared* Indians eligible, but Spectrum says this was never really carried out.

## Evaluation

Two methods kept European supremacy: the hard entry route, and the fact that key and well-paid posts stayed European. Indianisation after 1918 under nationalist pressure did not transfer effective power, since Indian officers served imperial interests. Recruitment stayed British-controlled, which helps explain why the Congress, once founded in 1885, demanded a lower age limit and simultaneous exams in India and England.

### 7. Core: the army and the police after 1857

*Objective:* Describe the post-1857 army reorganisation with its figures and the evolution of the police up to the Frazer Commission.

## The army: purpose

Spectrum gives two aims: to prevent another revolt (Dufferin warned in 1888 that the lessons of 1857 must not be forgotten), and to guard the Indian empire against rival powers such as Russia, Germany and France. The Indian branch was used for expansion in Asia and Africa; the British section acted as an army of occupation. GS-032 module 9 covers the 1861 Army Amalgamation Scheme and the 'White Mutiny'; those are not repeated.

## Three methods

1. **Raise the European share.** The commissions of 1859 and 1879 insisted on a one-third white army (14 per cent before 1857). The ratio of Europeans to Indians was fixed at one to two in the Bengal army and two to five in the Madras and Bombay armies. Check: 1 in 3 is 33 per cent; 2 in 7 is about 29 per cent. Artillery, tanks and armed corps were reserved for Europeans (Spectrum's 'tanks' is a loose label for heavy arms across the whole period up to the Second World War; tanks proper date only from the First World War, so verify before quoting it).
2. **Keep Indians out of command.** No Indian could rise above subedar until 1914; Indians entered commissioned ranks only from 1918 (Spectrum). The Indian Sandhurst Committee of 1926 imagined a 50 per cent Indianised officer cadre only by 1952.
3. **Divide and counterpoise.** The 1879 Army Commission described a counterpoise of 'natives against natives'. Lord Roberts (commander-in-chief; Spectrum prints 1887 to 1892, standard accounts 1885 to 1893, verify) developed the idea of 'martial' and 'non-martial' races: Sikhs, Gurkhas and Pathans, who had helped suppress the revolt, were preferred; soldiers from Awadh, Bihar, central India and south India were declared non-martial. Regiments mixed caste and community companies; newspapers and nationalist writings were kept from soldiers.

## The police: before the Act

Under the Mughals faujdars kept order beyond towns and the kotwal in cities. During the dual rule of 1765 to 1772 zamindars were expected to keep police staff and often neglected it; Warren Hastings restored the faujdar in 1774.

## The police: British reform

- **1791, Cornwallis**: modernised the *thana* (circle) system under a *daroga* (an Indian), with a superintendent of police (SP) at the district head, and relieved zamindars of police duties.
- Bentinck abolished the SP post: the collector-magistrate headed the police and the commissioner acted as SP. Spectrum says this burdened the collector.
- **1860 Police Commission**, then the **Indian Police Act, 1861**: a civil constabulary; inspector-general for a province, deputy inspector-general for a range, SP for a district. The British did not create an All-India Police; the Act set a police pattern for provinces, with uniform ranks.
- **1902 to 1903, Curzon: Frazer Commission**: direct recruitment of senior officers, training schools, more police and pay, village inquiries allowed, a Criminal Intelligence Department. The DCI became the central domestic and foreign intelligence body; CIDs were set up in provinces (split in 1929).

## Evaluation

Police curbed dacoity and thugee. But they were unsympathetic to the public and were used against the national movement, losing the people's empathy.

## Source slip

Spectrum's police chronology says '1808 Mayo appointed an SP for each division'. Lord Mayo was Viceroy decades later, so the name cannot be right; 1808 falls in Lord Minto's governor-generalship (1807 to 1813); teach the reform without the name and verify. Spectrum's end-of-chapter summary also lists a police landmark in '1818' for which the chapter text gives no event (verify).

### 8. Core: the judiciary and law, from Mayor's Courts to the Federal Court

*Objective:* Sequence the main judicial reforms and give two positive and two negative effects of the British legal system.

## Idea: rule of law

*Rule of law* means everyone, including officials, is answerable to law administered by courts. Spectrum credits British reforms with establishing it, but with racial exceptions.

## Before the British system

Spectrum says pre-colonial justice had no regular gradation of courts: Hindu disputes often went to caste elders, panchayats or zamindars; for Muslims the qazi was the unit; rulers were the fountainhead of justice, and justice could be arbitrary.

## Timeline

| Date | Reform |
|---|---|
| 1726 | Mayor's Courts at Madras, Bombay and Calcutta: start of a common-law system based on precedents |
| 1772 to 1785, Hastings | District Diwani Adalats (civil, under the collector) and Fauzdari Adalats (criminal), with Sadar courts above; Hindu and Muslim law for the respective communities |
| 1773 | Regulating Act: Supreme Court at Calcutta (original and appellate powers); frequent clashes with other courts |
| 1786 to 1793, Cornwallis | Separation of powers: collector confined to revenue; district judge for civil cases; European circuit courts; a graded system from Munsiff to King-in-Council; Cornwallis Code |
| 1828 to 1833, Bentinck | Circuit courts abolished; Sadar courts at Allahabad; vernacular allowed in lower courts; English replaces Persian in the Supreme Court (as Spectrum says; the Calcutta Supreme Court used English from 1774, verify) |
| 1833 | Law Commission under Macaulay to codify laws |
| 1859 to 1861 | Civil Procedure Code (1859), Indian Penal Code (1860), Criminal Procedure Code (1861) |
| 1860 | Europeans: no special privileges except in criminal cases, where no Indian-origin judge could try them |
| 1861 to 1862 | Indian High Courts Act 1861; Supreme Courts and Sadar Adalats merged into high courts at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1862); Spectrum prints 1865 (slip, verify) |
| 1935 | Federal Court provided for; set up 1937 |

## The Cornwallis Code in four ideas

Revenue and justice separated; Europeans under the courts' jurisdiction; officials answerable to civil courts for official acts; sovereignty of law.

## Evaluation (Spectrum)

**Positive**: rule of law; codified laws replaced religious and personal laws of rulers; Europeans brought under jurisdiction; government servants made answerable to civil courts.

**Negative**: the system became complicated and expensive, so the rich could manipulate it; false evidence and deceit; delayed justice; overburdened courts; European judges unfamiliar with Indian usage; and in criminal cases Europeans could be tried only by European judges.

## Source differences

- High courts: the standard fact is the Indian High Courts Act of 1861, with the courts at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras opening in 1862. Spectrum prints 1865, which is a slip; use 1861 to 1862 and verify before using the year in an exam answer.

Personal law was largely left alone, while criminal law, contract law and procedure were overhauled (Spectrum, chapter 28). For the Supreme Court of India see GS-186 (Day 1).

### 9. Master: princely states and the viceroys' record

*Objective:* Explain the two-point policy toward princely states and match each viceroy to one measure from the extracts.

## The policy toward princely states

Princely states were territories ruled by Indian rulers under British overlordship. After 1858 Spectrum describes a *two-point policy*: use the states as a bulwark (buffer) of empire, and subordinate them completely. GS-032 module 9 covers the Proclamation promises; see GS-028 (Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse) for the earlier tools.

- Annexation stopped; the new rule was to depose or punish but not annex.
- Territorial integrity was guaranteed and the right to adopt an heir respected.
- In 1876 the Queen took the title *Kaiser-i-Hind* (Queen Empress), ending the 'fiction' that states stood as equals of the Crown.
- Curzon said princes ruled merely as agents of the Crown.
- **Paramountcy**: the right to interfere in internal affairs through residents or by appointing and dismissing ministers and officials.
- Railways, roads and telegraphs made interference easier; rising democratic feeling in the states gave a motive to suppress it.
- Positive side: British helped some states adopt modern administrative institutions.

## The viceroys in the extracts

| Viceroy or officer | Measures in Spectrum |
|---|---|
| Canning | Allahabad durbar announcing the Crown's rule (1858); portfolio system, giving each branch of the executive council a head |
| Mayo | Fixed provincial grants (1870); local taxation; municipal acts |
| Lytton | 1877 transfer of revenue heads; Statutory Civil Service (1878 to 1879); Vernacular Press Act (1878) |
| Ripon | Local self-government resolution (1882); Vernacular Press Act repealed 1882 |
| Dufferin | Aitchison Committee 1886; 1888 warning on 1857 |
| Curzon | Frazer Commission on police 1902 to 1903; more official control of local bodies; princes as agents |

Lytton's Kaiser-i-Hind year (1876) and the Delhi durbar of 1877 are standard facts linked to his viceroyalty; the extract names the 1876 title only (verify). Roberts, as commander-in-chief, not Viceroy, built the martial-races doctrine. Elgin is quoted on racial dominance; Lansdowne has no measure in the extracts.

Spectrum treats Ripon as a liberal exception in a stage of 'reactionary' vice-royalties; link each viceroy to one measure rather than labelling a whole career.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Administration: the day-to-day running of government, as distinct from the Acts that fix constitutional powers.
- Paramountcy: the Crown's claim to overriding authority over princely states, including the right to interfere in their internal affairs.
- Divide and counterpoise: the army policy of balancing European troops against Indian ones and Indian groups against each other.
- Covenanted civil service: top civil posts held under a formal contract; the Indian Civil Service Act 1861 reserved certain offices for it.
- Legislative devolution: moving law-making powers from the centre to provinces, begun by the Indian Councils Act 1861.
- Trusteeship: the claim, in the later stage of colonial rule, that Indians were permanently immature and in need of British guidance.

### Notes: Chronology after 1858

- 2 Aug 1858: Act for the Better Government of India; 1 Nov 1858: Queen's Proclamation read at Allahabad.
- 1860: Indian Penal Code and Police Commission; 1861: Councils Act, ICS Act, Police Act, Criminal Procedure Code, Army Amalgamation Scheme (per GS-032).
- 1863: Satyendra Nath Tagore first Indian to qualify for the ICS.
- 1870: Mayo's financial decentralisation and resolution; 1877: Lytton's transfer of heads; 1882: Ripon's resolution and the three-way revenue split.
- 1878: Vernacular Press Act (repealed 1882); 1878 to 1879: Statutory Civil Service.
- 1881: first Factory Act; 1891: second Factory Act.
- 1886: Aitchison Committee; Roberts as commander-in-chief (Spectrum 1887 to 1892; standard 1885 to 1893, verify); 1902 to 1903: Frazer Police Commission.
- 1919: Montford reforms and the one-third Indian recruitment proposal; 1924: Lee Commission; 1935: Public Service Commissions provided; Federal Court set up 1937.

### Notes: Finance: the three steps compared

- Each row names the viceroy, the year and the heads concerned; use it to answer 'which came first'.

### Notes: Civil-service age limit and recruitment proposals

- Age limit fell from 23 to 19 in nineteen years; later proposals tried to widen entry.

### Notes: Army and police numbers

- White share of the army: 14 per cent before 1857; one-third after the commissions of 1859 and 1879.
- Ratio of Europeans to Indians: one to two in the Bengal army; two to five in the Madras and Bombay armies.
- Indians barred from the officer rank; highest rank subedar until 1914; commissioned ranks opened from 1918.
- Police Act 1861: inspector-general (province), deputy inspector-general (range), superintendent (district); no All-India Police.
- Frazer Commission 1902 to 1903 led to the Department of Criminal Intelligence and provincial CIDs.

### Notes: Cause and effect of the post-1857 policy

- Lesson of 1857: organised action can threaten rule, so the army was rebalanced and association with some Indians was tried.
- New industrial rivals and British capital in India: rulers wanted security and consolidated control.
- Congress founded 1885: the educated were met with hostility rather than partnership.
- Association without power: Indianisation did not transfer effective power since Indian officials served imperial interests.

### Notes: Institutions at a glance

- Secretary of State for India and the 15-member Council of India: London control after 1858.
- Viceroy and executive council: department heads advise; a fifth, jurist member added in 1861.
- Presidencies of Madras and Bombay (governor plus council of three; Spectrum generalises this to Bengal, which was under a Lieutenant-Governor from 1854 to 1912, verify) and provinces under lieutenant governors or chief commissioners.
- Municipalities, district boards and panchayats: local bodies of Mayo, Ripon and later.
- Federal Court (1937), provided for by the 1935 Act: handled disputes and limited appeals; the Privy Council in London dominated it.

### Notes: Commissions and committees

- Aitchison Committee on Public Services 1886: classified services as Imperial, Provincial and Subordinate.
- Army commissions of 1859 and 1879: one-third white army; counterpoise of natives against natives.
- Police Commission 1860: led to the Police Act 1861; Frazer Commission 1902 to 1903: criminal intelligence and training.
- Royal Commission on Decentralisation (dated 1908 by Spectrum; standard accounts 1907 to 1909): panchayats and sub-district boards.
- Lee Commission 1924: parity in ICS recruitment and a Public Service Commission.

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Viceroy versus Governor-General: same person after 1858; 'Viceroy' is the Crown's representative title.
- Mayo (1870 finance, resolution) versus Ripon (1882 local bodies).
- Charter Act of 1853 (open competition) versus ICS Act 1861 (reserved posts, London exam).
- Police Act 1861 (provincial forces) versus an All-India Police (not created by the British).
- Factory Act 1881 (child labour) versus a wage or union law (no such provision).

### Notes: Source slips and differences

- Spectrum summary says 'Mayo's Resolution of 1878' and 'Indian Councils Act of 1882'; the text says 1870 and 1892.
- '1808 Mayo' in the police chronology cannot be Lord Mayo; 1808 falls in Lord Minto's governor-generalship (1807 to 1813); verify the officeholder.
- High courts: standard fact is the Indian High Courts Act 1861, courts opening 1862 (verify); Spectrum's 1865 is a slip.
- Royal Commission on Decentralisation: 1908 in Spectrum; standard accounts 1907 to 1909.
- Title of the 1858 Act: long title 'An Act for the better Government of India' (standard; verify); GS-158 had printed 'Good Government', a textbook paraphrase, now corrected.
- Viceroy title: Proclamation (chapter 7) versus Act (chapter 26) in Spectrum itself.
- Presidencies: Spectrum says each was run by a governor-in-council; Bengal was under a Lieutenant-Governor from 1854 to 1912 (standard; verify).
- Roberts as commander-in-chief: Spectrum 1887 to 1892; standard accounts 1885 to 1893 (verify).

### Core concepts

- After 1858 the machinery of government was rebuilt to prevent another 1857 while keeping power British.
- Authority ran from Parliament to the Secretary of State to the Viceroy; the Council of India only advised.
- Association of Indians was used to gain cooperation and information, not to share real power.
