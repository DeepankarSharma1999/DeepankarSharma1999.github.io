### Bridge: Words you need: federation, autonomy, dyarchy, reserved and transferred subjects

- **Federation**: a state in which power is divided between a central government and units (provinces or states), each with its own sphere. The opposite is a *unitary* state, where the centre holds the power.
- **Provincial autonomy**: provinces governing their own subjects with their own money and ministers answerable to their own legislatures, free from day-to-day control by the centre.
- **Dyarchy** (double government): a system in which some subjects were run by elected ministers (*transferred*) and others by the Governor with his executive council, not answerable to the legislature (*reserved*).
- **Responsible government**: ministers must answer to the legislature and resign if they lose its confidence.
- **Safeguards and special responsibilities**: powers kept by the Governor-General and the Governors to override ministers in named areas such as minorities or security.
- **Electorate and franchise**: the *franchise* is the right to vote; an *electorate* is the group of voters who choose a representative. A *separate* (communal) electorate means that a community votes only for its own candidates.
- **Weightage**: extra seats for a group beyond its population share.

The trick: ask three questions of any provision. Who decides (minister or governor)? Who answers (legislature or nobody)? Who votes (which electorate)?

### Bridge: Section, Article, List and Schedule: reading a statute and a Constitution

The Government of India Act 1935 is a *statute* (an Act of the British Parliament). Its numbered units are **sections**, and its tables at the end are **schedules**. The Constitution of India has numbered **Articles**, grouped into **Parts**, with **Schedules**. Examination questions mix the two, so the first habit is to ask which document a number belongs to.

A **List** is a list of subjects (such as defence or police) that fixes which legislature may make laws on them. The 1935 Act had three: Federal, Provincial and Concurrent (concurrent means both may legislate). The Constitution later uses the Union, State and Concurrent Lists in the Seventh Schedule.

**Residuary powers** are powers over any subject that no list mentions. Who holds them is a telling test of how federal a system is: under the 1935 Act they were subject to the Governor-General's discretion, but in the Constitution they lie with Parliament (Article 248; verify in the Constitution's text).

One more word: **Instrument of accession**, a document by which a ruler joined a larger political unit.

### 1. Orientation: the 1935 Act in one page

*Objective:* State what the Government of India Act 1935 planned, what actually came into force, and when.

## The Act in one sentence

The Government of India Act, 1935 was a long Act of the British Parliament that tried to give India a federal system and responsible government in the provinces. Part of it worked; the central, federal part never did.

## On paper and in practice

| Feature | On paper | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| All-India Federation | Provinces, chief commissioners' provinces and princely states in one federation | Never came into being |
| Dyarchy at the centre | Reserved and transferred subjects in the federal executive | Never operated |
| Provincial autonomy | Dyarchy ended in the provinces; ministers answerable to legislatures | In force from 1 April 1937 |
| Federal Court | Court to interpret the Act and settle disputes | Set up in 1937 |
| Electorates and franchise | Separate electorates extended; wider vote | Used in the elections of early 1937 |
| Centre during the period | Federal scheme | The central government continued under the 1919 Act with minor amendments |

Spectrum (ch26, PDF p562) adds that the operative part of the Act stayed in force until 15 August 1947. That is a slip; see the source cautions below. Standard fact: the Act, as adapted, served as the Dominion of India's constitution until 26 January 1950 (verify).

## Why a student should care

- It is the last big step of the constitutional story that began in 1773 (GS-158 module 6 gives a short summary; this lesson goes deeper).
- It is the source of many institutions and ideas that the Constitution of India later reshaped (module 9).
- It is a frequent Prelims subject: three official questions (2018, 2021, 2024) are verified for this topic.

## Three things to hold in mind

1. **Two halves**: a provincial half that worked and a federal half that did not.
2. **Two kinds of dyarchy**: removed in the provinces, planned for the centre.
3. **Safeguards everywhere**: governors and the Governor-General kept special powers.

## Two source cautions

- Spectrum says the Act had 321 'articles'; an Act of Parliament has *sections*, and *Articles* belong to the Constitution. GS-158 says 321 sections and 10 schedules; teach sections and verify.
- Spectrum's chapter on the national movement says the central government carried on until 1946 under the 1919 Act. Spectrum (ch26, PDF p562) says the operative part of the Act remained in force till 15 August 1947. That is a slip. Under the Indian Independence Act 1947, the Act, as adapted, served as the Dominion of India's constitution until 26 January 1950, when Article 395 of the Constitution repealed it (standard; verify). A safe answer avoids both '1946' and '15 August 1947'.

### 2. Foundation: where the 1919 Act left things

*Objective:* Summarise the 1919 position (dyarchy, a bicameral centre, restricted franchise) and explain why it was judged to have failed.

## The starting point

GS-158 module 5 gives the 1919 Act in outline. In short, the Act of 1919 introduced **dyarchy** (double government) in the provinces, replaced the central council with a two-house legislature (a Council of State and a Legislative Assembly), introduced direct election with a restricted franchise tied to property, tax or education, extended communal electorates to Sikhs, Christians and Anglo-Indians besides Muslims (and to Europeans, standard; verify), and for the first time separated provincial and central budgets (Spectrum).

## Why Spectrum calls it a failure

- The structure stayed unitary and centralised, though some power moved to provinces.
- The central legislature could vote supplies but could not remove the government, and the Governor-General had overriding powers: veto, reservation for the King's pleasure, and the power to enact laws he thought essential for safety, tranquillity or interests of British India.
- Dyarchy failed in the provinces. A concrete example from Spectrum: local self-government was a transferred subject under ministers, but finance was reserved under an executive councillor, so ministers lacked funds to do much.
- Subhash Kashyap, quoted by Spectrum, calls the legislature a non-sovereign law-making body powerless before the executive.

Indian leaders did get administrative experience, but not responsible government.

## The trigger for change

The 1919 Act required a Royal Commission after ten years. The British government appointed it early, in November 1927 (the Simon Commission, module 3). Three demands then shaped the outcome:

1. End dyarchy in the provinces.
2. Bring the princely states into a larger federal structure.
3. Provide safeguards for minorities and British interests.

## Three questions to hold

- **Who rules?** Under dyarchy ministers and executive councillors shared the provincial government; the 1935 Act aimed at ministers alone in provinces.
- **Who pays?** Reserved finance defeated transferred subjects.
- **Who votes?** A small electorate chose representatives in separate communal groups.

## Why this matters for 1935

Every major 1935 feature answers a failure listed above: provincial autonomy answers the dyarchy failure; the federation tried to bind the states into the structure; the safeguards tried to answer the fear that responsible government would damage British interests.

| 1919 problem | 1935 answer |
|---|---|
| Reserved finance starved transferred subjects | All provincial subjects under ministers |
| Centre unitary and centralised | All-India Federation with three lists |
| Centre legislature without power over the executive | Dyarchy at the centre, with vote of no confidence in the lower house |
| Restricted franchise | Franchise extended (module 7) |

See the vocabulary bridge modules if terms such as federation, autonomy or reserved subject are new.

### 3. Core: the road to the Act, 1927 to 1935

*Objective:* Sequence the steps from the Simon Commission to the Act and compare Simon's proposals with what the Act did.

## Timeline

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 8 Nov 1927 | Indian Statutory Commission (Simon Commission) appointed: all-white, seven members; boycotted by major Indian parties (GS-044 to come on Day 15, not yet written) |
| May 1930 | Two-volume report |
| Nov 1930 to Dec 1932 | Three Round Table Conferences (GS-046) |
| 16 Aug 1932 | Communal Award; 24 Sep 1932 Poona Pact (GS-052 module 6) |
| March 1933 | White Paper on Constitutional Reforms: federal set-up and provincial autonomy |
| 1934 | Joint committee of both Houses (Spectrum names Lord Linlithgow as chairman) reports |
| Early 1935 | Bill prepared; GS-046 gives February 1935 for the Joint Select Committee's draft Bill |
| August 1935 | Act passed by Parliament |
| 1 April 1937 | Provincial autonomy begins |

## What Simon proposed

Abolition of dyarchy and representative government in the provinces with autonomy; discretionary powers for the governor on internal security and protection of communities; no parliamentary responsibility at the centre, with the Governor-General appointing cabinet members; federalism accepted only for the future, with a consultative council of greater India including British provinces and princely states; local legislatures for NWFP and Baluchistan; separation of Sindh from Bombay (Spectrum; verify) and of Burma from India; communal electorates continued. By the time it appeared, Spectrum says, events had overtaken it.

## Simon compared with the Act

| Issue | Simon (1930) | Act (1935) |
|---|---|---|
| Provincial dyarchy | Abolish | Abolished; autonomy |
| Dyarchy at centre | Rejected (Spectrum notes this) | Provided for in the federal executive |
| Federation | Not in the near future | Planned, conditional on the states |
| Communal electorates | Continue | Continued and extended |
| Sindh and Burma | Separate (Sindh: Spectrum, verify) | Separated (module 7) |

## The joint committee's federation test

Spectrum says the committee's report of 1934 held that the federation would be set up only if at least 50 per cent of the princely states were ready to join. Spectrum's other chapter states the conditions differently: states entitled to 52 seats in the Council of States must agree, and states with half of all states' population must join (module 4).

## The Congress and the Act

The Congress did not attend the third conference. After the Act, Spectrum says it was condemned by nearly all sections and rejected unanimously by the Congress, which demanded instead a Constituent Assembly elected on adult franchise. The Hindu Mahasabha and the National Liberal Federation favoured working the Act at both levels.

## Source differences

- GS-046 notes Spectrum's two chapters differ on the month of enactment (July versus August 1935). Use August 1935 for passing; the date of operation is 1 April 1937 for the provincial part.
- Simon on federation: Spectrum ch26 (p561) says Simon recommended that a federation 'be established', while ch18 says federation was not for the near future. The table follows ch18; verify the wording before quoting.
- The Joint Select Committee is dated 1934 (Spectrum chapter 26) and February 1935 for its draft Bill (chapter 19), which can be reconciled as report then draft Bill.

### 4. Core: the All-India Federation and why it never came into being

*Objective:* Describe the proposed federation, its conditions, and the reasons it failed.

## The plan

Spectrum describes an All-India Federation consisting of the **Governors' provinces**, the **Chief Commissioners' provinces** and the **Indian (princely) states** that chose to accede. Each ruler willing to join would sign an **instrument of accession** (a formal document) stating how much authority he surrendered to the federal government. Provinces were therefore in the federation by the Act; states were in it only by choice.

## The conditions

Spectrum states two conditions in one chapter:

1. States with 52 seats allotted in the proposed Council of States must agree to join.
2. The aggregate population of those states must be 50 per cent of the total population of all Indian states.

The arithmetic fits: the Council had 260 seats, of which 40 per cent (104) were to be nominated by the princes; half of 104 is 52. Another chapter gives the committee's looser statement: at least half of the princely states.

## Why it never came

Spectrum gives: the conditions were not fulfilled; the scheme was opposed by various parties of India. GS-046 module 9 adds that the federation depended on the princes, who lost interest once Congress power at the centre seemed less likely. The extracts do not give each prince's reasons, so a safe answer names the unmet conditions and the opposition.

The result: the central government continued under the 1919 Act with minor amendments; the provinces moved to autonomy on 1 April 1937.

## Why the princes mattered

- States made up a large part of the subcontinent; a federation without them could not be 'all-India'.
- Their rulers were not elected; the Act gave them a third to 40 per cent of the seats in the federal houses by nomination.
- They were bound to the Crown by paramountcy (GS-033 module 9), not to a legislature.

## The lists

Powers were divided in three lists: Federal, Provincial and Concurrent. The commonly cited numbers of items, Federal 59, Provincial 54 and Concurrent 36, are standard textbook figures that GS-158 also gives; they are not in this lesson's extracts, so verify. Residuary legislative powers (subjects on no list) did not go to the federal or provincial legislatures but were subject to the discretion of the Governor-General.

## Federation and the Constitution

Spectrum describes the scheme as a rigid constitution amendable only by the British Parliament, with a very strong Governor-General, reserved subjects and nominated state members. For the Constitution, see module 9.

## The PYQ link

The 2024 Prelims statement that the Act provided for a federation of provinces and states describes the plan, not its working. The word 'provided for' fits; 'established' would not have worked in practice. Even so, UPSC itself used 'established' loosely in the 2018 question ('the Federation established by the Government of India Act of 1935'). Judge a statement by what it claims actually operated, not by one verb.

### 5. Core: the federal executive and legislature on paper

*Objective:* Describe dyarchy at the centre, the two federal houses and the Governor-General's powers, including the residuary power.

## The pivot

Spectrum calls the Governor-General the pivot of the entire constitution. The federal executive was split in two.

## Dyarchy at the centre

| Group | Subjects | Who administered | Responsible to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reserved | Foreign affairs, defence, tribal areas, ecclesiastical affairs | Governor-General, advised by executive councillors | Not to the legislature |
| Transferred | All others | Governor-General, advised by ministers elected by the legislature | Federal legislature; ministers must resign if confidence is lost |

The Governor-General could also act in his individual judgement for his special responsibilities, such as the security and tranquillity of India. Spectrum notes that dyarchy, rejected by Simon, was provided for here.

## The two houses

| House | Seats | Composition | Term |
|---|---|---|---|
| Council of States (upper) | 260 | Partly elected directly from British India; 40 per cent nominated by princes | Permanent; one-third retire every third year |
| Federal Assembly (lower) | 375 | Partly indirectly elected from British India; one-third nominated by princes | Five years |

Arithmetic from those shares: 40 per cent of 260 is 104 and 260 minus 104 is 156; one-third of 375 is 125 and 375 minus 125 is 250. Spectrum calls it odd that the upper house was directly elected and the lower indirectly. A joint sitting broke deadlocks. Only the Assembly could move a vote of no confidence against ministers.

## The Governor-General's powers

- 80 per cent of the budget was non-votable.
- He could restore cuts in grants, certify bills rejected by the legislature, issue ordinances and exercise his veto.
- Even an Act he assented to could be disallowed by the King-in-Council.
- **Residuary powers** (legislation on subjects in no list) were subject to his discretion.

## The three lists and the residuary trap

The Federal, Provincial and Concurrent lists divided subjects. The Constitution later gave the residuary power to Parliament; under the 1935 Act it was the Governor-General's discretion. This is the answer to the 2018 PYQ.

## Why it never operated

None of this operated, because no federation was formed (module 4). The rules for the federal legislature remain important as a statement of what the British intended and as examination content.

## The PYQ link

The 2024 Prelims option 'defence and foreign affairs under the federal legislature' conflicts with the reserved list: those subjects were with the Governor-General, not the legislature.

### 6. Core: provincial autonomy and the end of provincial dyarchy

*Objective:* List the features of provincial autonomy, the Governor's special powers and the bicameral provinces.

## What autonomy meant

Spectrum says the distinction between reserved and transferred subjects was abolished and responsible government was established in provinces, subject to safeguards. Key features:

- Provinces gained a separate legal identity and were freed from the superintendence and direction of the Secretary of State and the Governor-General; they derived authority directly from the British Crown.
- Provinces had independent financial powers and resources and could borrow on their own security.
- Ministers, in a council headed by a premier, administered all provincial subjects and were answerable to, and removable by, the legislature.
- The provincial legislature could legislate on the provincial and concurrent lists.
- All members were directly elected, with an extended franchise (module 7).

## Provincial 1919 versus 1935

| Feature | 1919 | 1935 |
|---|---|---|
| Subjects | Reserved and transferred | Single group under ministers |
| Authority | Under the Secretary of State and Governor-General's control | Directly from the Crown |
| Finance | Reserved, with a separate budget | Independent powers; borrowing |
| Legislature | One house | Two houses in six provinces |

## The Governor

The Governor was the Crown's nominee and representative. He had special powers regarding minorities, the rights of civil servants, law and order, British business interests, partially excluded areas and princely states; 40 per cent of the budget was non-votable. He could refuse assent to a bill, promulgate ordinances and enact Governor's Acts, and could take over administration indefinitely. The commonly cited legal basis for the takeover is Section 93 of the Act, a standard-knowledge point not in the extracts (GS-158 module 6 also flags it; verify).

## Bicameral provinces

Spectrum lists bicameral legislatures in Madras, Bombay, Bengal, the United Provinces, Bihar and Assam, with the other five unicameral. The eleven provinces at the 1937 elections were Madras, Central Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, United Provinces, Bombay, Assam, NWFP, Bengal, Punjab and Sindh, so the five unicameral ones are Punjab, Central Provinces, NWFP, Orissa and Sindh (by elimination). Spectrum's chapter on Congress rule describes the upper chambers in five provinces as dominated by landlords, moneylenders and capitalists, so ministries had to conciliate them.

## What autonomy could and could not do

- It enabled ministers to run all provincial subjects and gave them funds, for instance for local bodies (GS-033 module 5).
- It could not end the Governor's overriding powers, so ministers governed within brakes.
- The central government remained British-controlled (module 1).

## A comparison to remember

Dyarchy was *removed where it had been tried* (provinces) and *planned where it had not* (the centre). Students often swap these: provincial dyarchy ended in 1937; federal dyarchy never began.

## The PYQ link

Statements such as 'provinces remained under the superintendence of the Secretary of State' conflict with the Spectrum text above.

### 7. Core: franchise, electorates and the new provinces

*Objective:* State the franchise figures and their source discrepancy, the extension of communal electorates to women, labour and depressed classes, and the territorial changes.

## Franchise: how many could vote

Spectrum gives different figures. Chapter 26 says about 10 per cent of the total population got the vote. The chapter on the Congress campaign says an estimated 30.1 million persons, including 4.25 million women, were enfranchised (14 per cent of the population) and that 15.5 million, including 917,000 women, voted. Another page says 14 per cent of the British Indian population. The two percentages use different bases, so teach 'roughly one in ten to one in seven' and avoid claiming a single exact share.

Arithmetic from the Spectrum figures: women were 4.25 divided by 30.1, about 14 per cent of those enfranchised; the voters were 15.5 out of 30.1, about 51 per cent; 917,000 of 4.25 million women, about 22 per cent, voted.

## Separate electorates and weightage

Spectrum says the principles of communal electorates and *weightage* (extra representation beyond population share) were extended to depressed classes, women and labour, and that separate electorates based on the Communal Award were made operational. For the Award and the Poona Pact see GS-052 module 6. GS-052 notes that, per Spectrum, 3 per cent of seats were reserved for women in all provinces except NWFP under the Award.

## Women

Spectrum says women got the right on the same basis as men, but qualifications for the vote in practice differed; standard accounts mention property, education or marriage-based qualifications and reserved seats for women (verify details). The 2021 PYQ asks about reserved seats for women in the 1935 Act: the lesson's sources support reserved seats through the extension of the system to women.

## Territorial changes

- **Sindh** separated from Bombay and **Burma** separated from India: Spectrum lists both as Simon proposals (Sindh: Spectrum, verify; Burma is firm).
- **Orissa** appears as a province at the 1937 election.
- **Aden**: standard knowledge says it was detached from British India in 1937 (verify; not in the extracts).
- Standard dates: Orissa and Sind became provinces on 1 April 1936, Burma was separated on 1 April 1937 (verify).
- Eleven provinces voted in 1937; Baluchistan remained a Chief Commissioner's province (verify).

For the PYQ 2022 link to territorial reorganisation see GS-033 module 10.

## Table: electorate features

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Provincial members | All directly elected |
| Communal electorates | Extended to depressed classes, women, labour |
| Weightage | Added to the same groups |
| Franchise | About 10 to 14 per cent (sources differ) |
| Reserved women's seats | Yes (3 per cent in the Award, per Spectrum) |

## A comparison

The 1919 Act's franchise rested on property, tax or education; the Spectrum extract says 1935 extended it but the vote stayed far from universal. The Constitution's adult franchise is a break from this, not a continuation.

### 8. Core: Federal Court, services and the working of the Act, 1937 to 1939

*Objective:* Describe the Federal Court and the other institutions of the Act and summarise the 1937 elections and Congress ministries.

## Institutions

- **Federal Court**: provided for by the Act and established in 1937; original and appellate powers to interpret the Act and settle disputes between governments, with limited appeals from high courts (GS-033 module 8). Spectrum says the Privy Council in London dominated it.
- **Public Service Commissions**: the Act provided for a Federal Public Service Commission and Provincial Public Service Commissions (GS-033 module 6); GS-158 also names a Joint Public Service Commission.
- **India Council abolished**: the Council of the Secretary of State was abolished.
- **Reserve Bank of India**: GS-158 lists the RBI among the Act's provisions. Standard accounts say the RBI was set up under a separate Reserve Bank of India Act of 1934 and began work on 1 April 1935; treat the link to the 1935 Act cautiously and verify.

## The Congress debate

Spectrum describes a two-stage debate. After the Civil Disobedience Movement, Congress argued over constructive work, council entry and continued struggle. After the Act, the left (Nehru, Bose, socialists) opposed office acceptance as responsibility without power; others held that the administrative field should not be left to pro-government forces, and Gandhi, who opposed it at first, agreed by early 1936 to give a trial. The Congress decided at Lucknow in early 1936 to fight the elections and postpone the decision on office. Office acceptance was decided only after the elections, by the AICC in March 1937 and the CWC in July 1937 (standard; verify). Spectrum places Faizpur in late 1937; the Faizpur session came in December 1936 before the elections (standard knowledge; verify).

## The 1937 elections

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Provinces voting | 11 (February 1937) |
| Seats in the provincial assemblies | 1,585 |
| Seats contested by the Congress | 1,161 |
| Seats won | 716 |

Arithmetic: 716 of 1,161 is about 62 per cent of contested seats and about 45 per cent of all 1,585 seats. The Congress won a majority in all provinces except Bengal, Assam, Punjab, Sindh and NWFP, and was the largest party in Bengal, Assam and NWFP.

## The ministries

Ministries were formed in Bombay, Madras, Central Provinces, Orissa, United Provinces and Bihar, later in NWFP and Assam (Spectrum chapter 21). GS-158 gives 'seven of eleven'; counts differ by date. They resigned in October 1939 after the Second World War began and lasted 28 months. Gandhi told them to hold office lightly, as 'crowns of thorns'. They eased civil-liberty curbs and passed land and debt measures but could not change the imperial structure.

## Why the 1937 experience mattered

It showed the Act's limits (safeguards, Governor's powers) and that Indians could run provincial administration. This points to the Constituent Assembly demand in module 9.

## Why not more detail

The Congress ministries and the Muslim League response belong to GS-050 (Constitutional developments 1909 to 1947, Day 14, not yet written); GS-048 (Quit India, Day 12) touches the 1939 resignations only as background.

### 9. Master: criticism, strategy and what the Constitution kept and changed

*Objective:* Evaluate the Act using the sourced criticisms and connect it to the Constitution with certain Article-level links.

## The critics

Spectrum says the Act was condemned by nearly all sections. Nehru's remark that India was given a car with all brakes and no engine summarises the main complaint: the Governor-General and Governors retained extensive safeguards and special responsibilities, so elected ministers worked inside brakes.

## Weaknesses

- Extensive safeguards and special powers (module 5 and 6).
- A rigid constitution with no possibility of internal growth; the right of amendment was reserved to the British Parliament.
- Extension of communal electorates promoted separatist tendencies that, Spectrum argues, culminated in Partition.
- Indians took no part in making it.
- Franchise stayed narrow (module 7).

## The British long-term strategy (Spectrum)

Suppression could only be short-term; the plan was to integrate large sections of the movement into the constitutional structure: revive moderate liberals, convince Congressmen that extra-legal struggle was ineffective, divide right from left, and, by creating strong provincial leaders, provincialise the Congress. Linlithgow is quoted as saying the Act was framed to maintain British influence; the historian Tomlinson, quoted by Spectrum, says constitutional advance was determined by the need to attract Indian collaborators.

## What the Act achieved

A step towards responsible government and a written constitution, in Spectrum's words, though not made by Indians; real provincial power; and a training in administration (module 8).

## Legacy: the Constitution

The Act is the largest structural source of the Constitution (GS-158 module 7 lists points from Laxmikanth, flagged for review). Article-level links that are standard and reliable:

| 1935 Act | Constitution of India |
|---|---|
| Federal, Provincial and Concurrent lists | Union, State and Concurrent Lists in the Seventh Schedule (Article 246) |
| Residuary power at the Governor-General's discretion | Residuary power with Parliament (Article 248) |
| Federal Court | Supreme Court (Article 124) |
| Public Service Commissions | Article 315 |
| Governor's emergency takeover (Section 93) | President's Rule (Article 356); modelled on it, standard, verify |
| Restricted franchise, separate electorates | Adult franchise (Article 326) and joint electorates |

Spectrum observes that the Congress demanded instead a Constituent Assembly elected on adult franchise. GS-158 module 7 notes, citing NCERT, that the Assembly was itself chosen by provincial assemblies elected under the 1935 system (verify).

## A balanced answer

The Act was both a constraint and a bridge: it limited Indian power, yet gave Indians experience and many structures later reused.

## Interpretations

The extracts let a student argue three lines: a genuine step (autonomy), a manoeuvre (collaboration strategy), or a blueprint (structures reused). Good answers use evidence for each.

### 10. Exam: three verified PYQs, answer frames and traps

*Objective:* Reason through the three verified PYQs and plan 150-word and 250-word answers.

## The three verified PYQs

1. **2018 Prelims Q38**: in the 1935 federation, residuary powers went to which authority (Federal Legislature, Governor-General, Provincial Legislature or Provincial Governors). Module 5: residuary legislative powers were subject to the Governor-General's discretion. Trap: the Constitution (Article 248) gives residuary power to Parliament.
2. **2021 Prelims Q42**: statements on women: the 1919 reforms recommending votes for all women above 21; the 1935 Act giving reserved seats to women. 3. **2024 Prelims Q62**: statements on the federation and the control of defence and foreign affairs. Statement 1 matches module 4 (a federation planned of provinces and princely states); statement 2 conflicts with module 5: those subjects were reserved to the Governor-General.

## Two small checks

The 2021 question combines 1919 and 1935; learn the one-line difference: restricted franchise in 1919; reserved seats extended to women in 1935.

### Notes: Core definitions

- Federation: division of power between a centre and units; the 1935 federation was planned but never formed.
- Provincial autonomy: responsible government in provinces under safeguards; began 1 April 1937.
- Dyarchy: double government by ministers (transferred) and executive councillors (reserved); abolished in provinces, planned for the federal centre.
- Instrument of accession: the document by which a ruler would join the federation and state the authority he surrendered.
- Residuary powers: powers over subjects in no list; at the Governor-General's discretion under the Act.
- Weightage: extra seats beyond population share, extended to depressed classes, women and labour.

### Notes: Chronology to 1937

- 8 Nov 1927: Simon Commission appointed; May 1930: report.
- Nov 1930 to Dec 1932: three Round Table Conferences (GS-046).
- 16 Aug 1932: Communal Award; 24 Sep 1932: Poona Pact (GS-052 module 6).
- March 1933: White Paper; 1934: joint committee under Linlithgow reports; early 1935: Bill.
- August 1935: Act passed; Feb 1937: provincial elections in 11 provinces.
- 1 April 1937: provincial autonomy begins; 1937: Federal Court set up.
- Oct 1939: Congress ministries resign after the Second World War begins; 1947 to 1950: Act as adapted is the Dominion's constitution; 26 Jan 1950: repealed (Art. 395); Spectrum's '15 Aug 1947' end date is a slip (verify).

- Governor: special powers on minorities, civil servants' rights, law and order, British business interests, excluded areas and princely states; refuse assent; ordinances; Governor's Acts; indefinite takeover; 40 per cent of the provincial budget non-votable.

- Provincial autonomy (operated) versus federation (never formed).

- Residuary power: Governor-General's discretion (1935) versus Parliament (Constitution).

### Last-week summary

- Federation of provinces and states planned, never formed; dyarchy shifted to the centre: foreign affairs, defence, tribal areas, ecclesiastical affairs reserved to the Governor-General.
- Council of States 260, Federal Assembly 375; residuary powers at the Governor-General's discretion (Constitution: Parliament, Art. 248).
- Provincial autonomy 1 April 1937: all subjects under ministers, authority direct from the Crown; bicameral in six of eleven provinces (Madras, Bombay, Bengal, UP, Bihar, Assam).
- Separate electorates extended to depressed classes, women, labour; Federal Court 1937; 1937 elections: Congress won 716 of 1,161 contested; Congress demanded a Constituent Assembly on adult franchise.
