### 2. Tense, reporting verbs and reported speech

*Objective:* Report a passage in the correct tense, choose a reporting verb that matches how firmly the author speaks, and convert a quotation into reported speech.

## Why grammar matters here

In a comprehension answer a wrong tense, a lost hedge or a muddled attribution changes what the passage seems to say, so a grammar slip is also a meaning slip.

## Report the passage in the present tense

A passage exists now, so what it says is reported in the present: *The author argues that ...*; *The passage gives three reasons.* Events the passage tells of stay in the past: *Pierre Ceresole organised the first international work-camp after the war.* General truths stay in the present. Do not drift between tenses in one answer without a reason.

Time words are anchors. A passage that says an assumption held *not so long ago* but *we now know* otherwise (2022) needs both tenses: *It was assumed that ...; scientists now know that ...*

## Match the reporting verb to the author's firmness

| Verb | What it signals |
|---|---|
| states, notes, observes | the author presents a fact |
| argues, maintains, contends | a position others may reject |
| suggests, implies | an indirect or tentative point |
| claims, alleges | a view the writer does not endorse |
| concedes, admits | a point granted to the other side |
| warns, cautions | a risk |

Use *claims* or *believed* for a view the author reports but does not share (the British self-image in 2024). Use *shows* or *proves* only when the passage reports a settled result; this is the trap in 2022 (e).

## Reported speech

Passages quote people (Alberuni in 2018, Pierre in 2020). To report a quotation, change the pronouns, and if the reporting verb is in the past, step the tense back one stage.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| The principal said, "We collect rain from every roof." | The principal said that the school collected rain from every roof. |
| She said, "The tanks have cut our bill." | She said that the tanks had cut the school's bill. |

If the reporting verb is present (*says, argues*), do not step back. A general truth may stay present: *She said that water is scarce in summer.* Time and place words shift too: now becomes then, here becomes there, this becomes that.

## Hedges survive the retelling

*Perhaps the need for defence was not given priority* becomes *The author suggests that defence may not have been a priority.* Never delete *perhaps, may, appears* when you paraphrase.

### 3. Agreement, voice, parallel lists and clear pronouns

*Objective:* Fix the four sentence faults that most often damage a comprehension answer: agreement, muddled voice, broken lists and vague pronouns.

## Subject-verb agreement

Find the true subject, then match the verb. Words between subject and verb do not change it.

- *The number of trees that survive **is** small.* (the number, singular) but *A number of trees **were** lost.* (several trees)
- *One of the factors **is** education.* (one, singular)
- *Each of the points **needs** support.*
- *Neither the state nor the citizens **are** free of duty.* With *neither ... nor*, the verb follows the nearer subject.
- *Evidence, information, advice* and *knowledge* are uncountable and take singular verbs: *The evidence **is** weak.*
- Collective nouns (*government, committee*): singular is the safe choice in exam English; British usage also allows a plural verb when the members act separately. Either way, stay consistent. *Data* may be singular or plural in modern usage; choose one and stay consistent.

## Active and passive voice

Use active voice when the doer matters: *The British claimed to embody civilisation.* Use passive when the doer is unknown or unimportant: *Tourism was viewed as an engine of growth.* The trap in answers is the **hiding passive**: *It is believed that ...* leaves out who believes. In a comprehension answer name the believer: *The author reports that the British believed ...*. If the passage itself uses a passive without an agent (*the potentialities of invasions were recognised only in hindsight*), your explanation should supply the missing agent: *later observers, looking back.*

## Parallel lists

An enumeration answer needs items in the same grammatical form. Faulty: *The state should provide security, enforce the law and to protect the poor.* Fixed: *provide security, enforce the law and protect the poor.* Keep a list all nouns, all *-ing* forms or all verbs after *should*. A numbered list is cleanest.

## Pronouns

*They* and *it* must point to one clear noun. In *The council met the residents, and they refused*, who refused? Repeat the noun: *the residents refused.*

## Concision

One idea per sentence, about twenty-five words at most. Cut *it is a fact that*, *in the passage it is mentioned that*. Compare *According to the passage, it is mentioned that the author feels that rights are important* with *The author argues that rights carry duties.*

### 4. Connectors and the logic they carry

*Objective:* Read a passage's connectors as signposts of logic, and use the right connector in your own answer.

## A connector is a signpost

Words such as *however*, *because* and *therefore* tell you how two sentences relate. Reading them correctly shows what the author really thinks; using them correctly makes your answer reason rather than list.

| Relation | Connectors | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | and, moreover, in addition, also | a further point of the same kind |
| Contrast | but, yet, however, whereas, on the other hand; even so, nevertheless, still | a point against the last one (even so, nevertheless and still introduce the real point after a concession) |
| Concession | although, though, admittedly, granted, despite (+ noun) | the author grants a point, then makes a stronger one |
| Cause | because, since, as, for, owing to | the reason follows |
| Result | so, therefore, hence, thus, consequently | the conclusion follows |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that | the claim holds only on a condition |
| Example | for instance, such as | support for the last claim |
| Sequence | first, second, lastly, then | the order or count of points |

## Four reading rules

1. **After a concession, the author's real point comes next**, often opening with *yet*, *but* or *even so*. In *The suggestion is sound. Yet a tree planted without thought can fail* (an original sentence), the author's weight is on the failure.
2. **Sequence words give the count.** The list in 2017 (e) runs on *First, Second, Third, Fourth, And lastly*; five markers mean five reasons, but check whether the long first reason holds several points (more leisure time, paid holidays, younger travellers).
3. **Because points back, therefore points forward.** The sentence with *because* is a premise; the sentence with *therefore* is a conclusion.
4. **Some words have two jobs.** *Since* can mean from a time or because; *while* can mean at the same time or whereas; *as* can mean because or when. Decide from the sense.

## Using connectors in your answer

For a cause question, write *X happened because Y* and then, if useful, *so Z*. For a comparison, use *whereas* or *by contrast*. For a hedge, use *possibly* or *it may be that*. Punctuation trap: *however* joining two clauses needs a semicolon or full stop before it, not a comma alone: *The plan was cheap; however, it failed.*

### 5. The parts of an argument

*Objective:* Name the parts of an argument and map a short passage into claim, premises, assumption, counter-claim and conclusion.

## The vocabulary

An **argument** is a set of statements in which some (the **premises**, reasons) are offered to support another (the **conclusion**, or main **claim**). Not every passage argues: a description or a story does not. A passage that says *you should*, *therefore* or *because* usually does, but a *because* can also merely explain a fact. An argument tries to persuade you to accept a claim; an explanation only accounts for something already accepted.

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Claim / conclusion | what the author wants you to accept |
| Premise | a reason given for the claim |
| Evidence | facts, figures, examples or expert statements offered for a premise |
| Assumption | an unstated belief that the reasoning needs |
| Counter-claim | the opposing view the author mentions |
| Rebuttal | the author's reply to the counter-claim |

Premise indicators are *because, since, given that*. Conclusion indicators are *therefore, so, hence, it follows*.

## A model mapping (original)

*The village hall should open in the evening. Most adults work until six. A hall that shuts at five serves only children and pensioners. Some say evening staff cost too much, but a trial of three evenings would show whether people come.*

- Claim: the hall should open in the evening.
- Premise 1: most adults work until six. Premise 2: a hall that closes at five serves only some groups.
- Assumption (unstated): adults would use the hall if it were open.
- Counter-claim: evening staff cost too much. Rebuttal: a cheap trial would test the claim.

The assumption is what an examiner calls the hidden step. Test it: *if this belief were false, would the argument collapse?* If yes, it is an assumption (WR-027 module 5 separates it from inference).

## Real passages argue too

The 2025 passage is itself about argument: find the reasons, the conclusion and what the author assumes. The 2024 passage can be mapped: the British needed to justify their rule (aim); civilisation was a gift universally desirable (premise); India lacked it and could not acquire it unaided (premise); therefore they had a precious gift to offer (conclusion). The author's own critical point is that the British defined civilisation to match their own ways, so, as the author argues, they turned their own facts (habits) into values (norms) for others, a move the author calls *illicit*.

### 6. Strong and weak support, and hedging language

*Objective:* Judge how well a claim is supported and keep the author's level of certainty in your own sentence.

## Strong and weak support

| Stronger support | Weaker support |
|---|---|
| specific, checkable facts or figures | vague phrases such as *many people say* |
| several independent examples | one story or one example |
| a source with expertise on this very point | a famous person speaking outside their field |
| a fair comparison of like with like | two things that happened together, taken as cause and effect |
| a claim limited to what the evidence covers | words like *all, always, never* from a few cases |
| reasons | loaded words and emotion in place of reasons |

Two traps recur: confusing **correlation** (two things occur together) with **cause**, and **over-generalising**. When Alberuni, quoted in 2018, writes that Indians are *by nature* secretive, the *by nature* generalises about a whole people and invites the answer that such a claim needs evidence. The author's own phrase *which one may or may not agree with* signals that the passage leaves room for that judgement.

## The hedge ladder

Authors show how sure they are. Your sentence must show the same.

| Level | Words |
|---|---|
| Certain | proves, is certain, undoubtedly |
| Strong | shows, demonstrates, establishes |
| Moderate | suggests, indicates, is likely, tends to |
| Tentative | may, might, perhaps, possibly, appears, seems |
| Doubtful | is unlikely, could hardly |

Examples from the verified papers: 2018 uses *perhaps* and *alternatively* for the undefended passes; 2021 says food shortages *could occur* and security *may become* the major challenge; 2022 (e) asks how research *proves* a relationship, but the passage reports scientists who *concluded* that the relationship is close. Your answer should say *the research provides strong evidence*, not *proves*, unless the passage uses the strong word.

## Rule

Never move a statement up or down the ladder. A hedge turned into a fact, or a fact weakened into a maybe, is an accuracy error, the criterion learners most often mark themselves generously on (WR-027 module 10).

### 7. Tone and attitude with textual evidence

*Objective:* Name the tone of a passage in precise words and justify it with a quoted clue.

## Three words to keep apart

**Subject** is what the passage is about. **Attitude** is what the author thinks of it. **Tone** is how that attitude sounds, carried by word choice and sentence moves. WR-027 (bridge module 2, master note on tone words) gives the basic tone list. Add these:

| Tone | Sounds like | Do not confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| polemical | argues forcefully against a view, taking a side | critical (which can be calm) |
| ironic | the words say one thing, the meaning is opposite | sarcastic (ironic but cruel) |
| didactic | teaches, advises, instructs | preachy |
| ambivalent | holds two feelings at once | balanced (which weighs fairly) |
| detached | no emotion, distant | objective (neutral and fair) |
| laudatory | praises warmly | optimistic (about the future) |
| resigned | accepts something unwelcome | pessimistic |

## Four-step method

1. **Collect the colour words**: adjectives, adverbs and verbs that judge (*enslaved, frantic, marvels*).
2. **Watch the moves**: a concession followed by a *but*, a warning, a question to an imagined objector.
3. **Check who speaks**: a harsh word inside a quotation or a reported view may not be the author's.
4. **Name it**: one or two words, one clue quoted (a short phrase), and the part of the passage it covers. A mixed tone is allowed: *analytical, with sharp criticism in the last paragraph.*

## Readings of the official passages (Mission 90 analysis, arguable)

- **2019**: critical, then guardedly hopeful. History is *enslaved and distorted* for selfish purposes; yet the author sees a trend towards a broader view and cautions against the dangers of the "new history".
- **2024**: analytical and critical. The British are said to have *wrapped* their gifts in the language of civilisation, and the author calls the move from fact to value *illicit*. There is irony in the image of a gift whose shape matches the giver's own habits.
- **2025**: didactic and conversational. The writer imagines an objector (*you may object*) and replies; the contempt for tautologies belongs to those whom the writer describes.
- **2026**: confident and celebratory, with one note of caution (*much more needs to be done*).
- **2018**: tentative and analytical (*perhaps, appears*).

Another reader may label them differently; a quoted clue is the test.

### 8. Writing the answer: a six-step recipe and three worked analyses

*Objective:* Draft a correct, concise, own-words answer by following six steps, and see the steps on three verified questions that WR-027 does not analyse.

## The recipe

1. **Verb and limit**: mark the directive and the scope (WR-027 module 2).
2. **Find the paragraph** with your paragraph map.
3. **Draft the answer sentence in the present tense**, using your own words.
4. **Add one or two supports** joined by the right connector.
5. **Sweep for TAPS**: Tense, Agreement, Parallel lists, clear Subjects (pronouns).
6. **Trim** to what the question needs.

All sample answers below are original Mission 90 analysis. They are **not** official answers and no official key exists in the project. These three questions are different from those analysed in WR-027 modules 8 and 9; attempt each first on your own copy of the paper.

## Worked analysis 1: 2019 (d), a list after a concession

*What are the dangers associated with "new history"?* The paragraph opens with a concession (*dangers, as well as virtues*), so the virtues are not asked for; the dangers follow. **Trap**: giving the advantages of the broad scope, or turning *tend to* and *some of the historians* into *all historians always*. Sample: *The passage names three dangers. (1) Overpopularisation and underspecialisation, which tend to cheapen history and weaken it as a sober study. (2) The sacrifice of important facts by some of its historians, who prefer a chronicle of heroic persons and romantic events. (3) The blurring of the line between history and fiction.* Note the list: three noun phrases, one point per number, the hedges kept.

## Worked analysis 2: 2021 (e), a cause chain with hedges

*How is environmental breakdown related to probable food shortage?* The passage reports a prediction, so the answer is a possible effect, not a fact. **Trap**: *will* and *is* where the passage has *could* and *may become*. Sample: *The passage links them as cause and possible effect. Experts predict that serious food shortages could occur this decade, because soil erosion, unsustainable use of groundwater and deforestation together reduce the world's potential food production by nearly 14 million tonnes a year. New technologies are unlikely to make up the loss soon, so access to food may become the most important food-security challenge of the century.* Note: *because* gives the cause, *so* the result, and each hedge stays in place.

## Worked analysis 3: 2022 (e), a strong verb in the question

*In what way does the latest research prove the relationship between apes and humans?* Two wording mismatches: the passage says the researchers *concluded*, not *proved*, and the molecular work it describes dates from the 1960s and 1970s, so *latest* is loose. **Trap**: writing *proves* or inventing newer studies. Sample: *The passage reports that molecular studies support a close relationship rather than proving it outright. Zuckerkandl found the peptide patterns from the haemoglobin of a human, a chimpanzee and a gorilla indistinguishable; Goodman concluded that human and chimpanzee albumins are too alike to tell apart; and Sarich and Wilson concluded that humans and the African apes are very closely related.* Note: past tense for what each researcher did, present tense for what the passage reports, and *support* in place of *prove*.

### 9. Practice protocol and the error log

*Objective:* Use the original passages and drills with a repeatable routine and track your own error types.

## Routine for each passage

1. **Read once for gist (3 minutes), once with a paragraph map (5 minutes).**
2. **Attempt every question** without looking at the model answer. Keep within the question's scope (no official word limit is shown in the records).

4. **Compare by idea** with the model answer, not by wording.

6. **Log each error** with a category.

Passage (147 words)

The town library closes at five in the afternoon, and the council has refused to extend its hours. Its reason is cost: opening until nine would need two more staff members. This reasoning is incomplete. Most adults in the town work until at least six, so a library that closes at five is open mainly to children, pensioners and the unemployed. A survey of 200 residents, carried out by the library's own volunteers, found that 140 would visit more often if evening hours were available. Admittedly, a survey of this kind may overstate the interest, since people who answer questions about libraries tend to like libraries. Even so, the council has offered no figures of its own. Until it does, its refusal rests on an assumption about cost rather than on evidence about use, and a trial of three evenings a week would settle the matter cheaply.

(a) State the author's claim and two premises.
(b) Name one assumption the author's argument needs.
(c) Which sentence is a concession, and what does the author do next?
(d) How strong is the survey as support? Answer in one sentence that keeps the author's hedge.

**A.** Original Mission 90 sample answers (not official; there is more than one correct way to write them):
(a) Claim: the council's refusal is not supported by evidence, and a three-evening trial should be run to settle the question. Premises: (1) most adults work until six, so the library serves only some groups after hours; (2) a survey found that 140 of 200 residents would visit more with evening hours. A third: the council has given no figures of its own.
(b) That people who say they would visit more would actually come, and that the extra cost would be repaid in use. If this were false, the call for a trial would lose its force.
(c) 'Admittedly, a survey of this kind may overstate the interest ...' grants a point to the other side. The author then replies with 'Even so', that the council has given no figures, so the real point lies after the concession.
(d) It is moderate support: it is specific, but since it was run by volunteers who favour the library, it may overstate the interest, as the author concedes. (A hedge, 'may overstate', is kept; do not write 'the survey is false'.)

Passage (127 words)

The city transport department has announced, with evident pride, that its new bus timetable is the most comprehensive in the region. It certainly is thick. At sixty-four pages, it lists every route, every stop and, in a charming departure from convention, no times for the three busiest services. Commuters who hoped to learn when the number 12 might arrive are invited to consult the depot. The depot, according to a notice pinned to its gate, is open on weekdays between ten and one. One must admire the confidence of a department that expects people who work from nine to five to reach a depot that closes at one. Perhaps the intention is to teach patience, a virtue the city's passengers have been given ample chance to practise.

(a) Name the tone in one or two words and quote one clue.
(b) What does 'comprehensive' mean in the department's mouth, and why does the author seem to doubt it?
(c) Rewrite 'One must admire the confidence of a department ...' without irony, in one sentence.
(d) Is the author's real attitude stated directly? Explain in two sentences.

**A.** Original Mission 90 sample answers (not official; there is more than one correct way to write them):
(a) Ironic and critical. Clue: 'in a charming departure from convention, no times for the three busiest services', where 'charming' praises what the sentence shows to be a failure.
(b) For the department it means covering everything. The author doubts it because the timetable is thick yet leaves out the times for the busiest services, so it is not complete where it matters.
(c) The department has little reason for confidence, since it expects working people to reach a depot that is closed when they are free.
(d) No. The author never says 'this is badly run'; the meaning comes from the gap between the praise words (admire, charming) and the facts. The reader must infer the criticism from the contrast.

Passage (128 words)

At the annual meeting on Saturday, the school principal said that the rainwater tanks installed two years earlier had cut the school's water bill by almost a third. "We collect rain from the roof of every building," she said, "and we use it for the gardens and the toilets." (b) Change her direct quotation 'We collect rain from the roof of every building' into reported speech beginning 'She said that'.

(d) Correct the agreement: 'The cost of the pits and the tanks were less than expected.'

**A.** Original Mission 90 sample answers (not official; there is more than one correct way to write them):
(a) 'Warned' signals a caution; 'added' introduces a further point.
(b) She said that the school collected rain from the roof of every building. (The reporting verb is past, so 'collect' steps back to 'collected'; 'we' becomes 'the school'.)

Recorded lessons are often praised because students can pause, rewind and repeat them. The praise is deserved, but it describes only half of learning. (b) What does 'Although' signal, and what does 'therefore' signal?

**A.** Original Mission 90 sample answers (not official; there is more than one correct way to write them):

Passage (139 words)

A stationery company has advertised that students who use its FocusPlus notebooks score higher marks. The advertisement cites a school where, after the notebooks were introduced in July, the average mark in the December test rose from 58 to 64. It also quotes a retired cricketer, who says that he has never seen a better notebook. These claims prove less than they seem to. The school's marks may have risen for other reasons: a new teacher, an easier test, or students simply having studied for another term. A cricketer's liking for stationery says nothing about learning. All the advertisement establishes is that two things happened in the same year. A fair test would compare two similar groups of students over the same period, one using the notebooks and one not, and the company has not reported such a comparison.

(a) Classify the two pieces of evidence and say why each is weak.
(b) Which phrase in the passage is a hedge, and what does it do?
(c) What would make the evidence stronger?
(d) State the author's conclusion in one sentence, using a verb of the right strength.

**A.** Original Mission 90 sample answers (not official; there is more than one correct way to write them):
(a) The school's marks are a before-and-after figure: they show correlation, not cause, since other factors could explain it. The cricketer is an authority speaking outside his field, so his opinion says nothing about learning.
(b) 'may have risen for other reasons' is the hedge. It keeps the author modest: the author does not say the notebooks have no effect, only that the evidence does not show one. ('Prove less than they seem to' is an understatement, not a hedge.)
(c) A fair comparison of two similar groups over the same period, one using the notebooks and one not.
(d) The author concludes that the advertisement does not show that the notebooks improve marks (not 'proves that they do not'). The verb should be 'does not show' or 'establishes nothing more than', not 'disproves'.
