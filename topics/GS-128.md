### Bridge: Bridge: settlement words (hamlet, village, town, city, hinterland, node, function)

A **settlement** is a place where people have built houses and live together. A **hamlet** is the smallest cluster, a few houses. A **village** is a larger cluster whose people mostly earn a living from the land: farming, grazing, fishing, forestry. A **town** or **city** is a larger and more tightly packed cluster whose people mostly earn a living from making things (**secondary activity**) or from services such as trade, transport, teaching and government (**tertiary activity**). In NCERT's words the sparse small settlements are villages and the fewer larger ones are urban settlements. The word **rural** means 'of the countryside' and **urban** means 'of towns and cities'.

The **hinterland** of a town is the surrounding area that sends it food and raw materials and receives its goods and services. A **node** is a meeting point of many routes; NCERT calls cities nodes of economic growth. A **function** is the main job a town does for the region: governing, manufacturing, trading, hosting pilgrims, housing a university, guarding a border. A town can have one dominant function and several smaller ones.

Two more words help. The **built-up area** is the land covered by houses and roads (NCERT uses it, with the distance between houses, to judge a village type). **Primary activity** means work that takes from nature directly: farming, mining, fishing. This lesson uses these words in the NCERT sense unless it says otherwise.

### Bridge: Bridge: share, growth rate, percentage points and thousands

Table 2.1 in NCERT gives two kinds of per cent, and mixing them is the commonest error. The first is a **share**: the urban population as a per cent of the total population of the country in that Census year (31.16 per cent in 2011). The second is a **decadal growth rate**: how much the urban population itself grew over the ten years since the previous Census, as a per cent of its earlier size. In the formula the extract uses for population (growth = (later number minus earlier number) divided by the earlier number, times 100), the earlier number is the base.

A share can rise while a growth rate falls. A simple case: a country has 100 urban and 400 rural people. Next decade the urban group grows by 30 per cent to 130 and the rural group by 10 per cent to 440. The urban growth rate is 30 per cent. The urban share moves from 100/500 = 20 per cent to 130/570 = 22.8 per cent. If the urban growth rate then drops to 25 per cent while the rural rate falls to 5 per cent, the share still rises. The gap between two per cents (22.8 and 20) is stated in **percentage points** (2.8), not 'per cent'.

The table prints urban population '(in thousands)', so 285,355 means 285,355 thousand, which is about 285 million. The year marked with an asterisk, 2011, was the provisional count. Check the unit before comparing with another table.

### 1. Orientation: what a settlement is and how rural and urban differ

*Objective:* Define a human settlement in your own words and name the three headline differences between rural and urban settlements.

## What this topic asks

People do not live spread evenly like butter on bread. They live in clusters of houses, and the clusters range from a handful of huts to a metropolitan city. This lesson asks four questions about those clusters in India. What is a settlement? What shapes do villages take, and why? What makes a place a town, and how did Indian towns arise? How fast is India urbanising? (Day 21 lesson GS-127 already taught where people live, density, growth and migration; this lesson builds on it.)

## The NCERT definition

NCERT Class XII defines a **human settlement** as a cluster of dwellings of any type or size where human beings live. The people may build houses and other structures and command some area or territory as their economic support-base. So settling inherently involves two things: grouping of people and apportioning of territory as a resource base.

Settlements vary in size and type, from a **hamlet** to a **metropolitan city**. As size changes, the economic character, the social structure, the ecology and the technology of the settlement change too. NCERT calls the sparsely located small settlements **villages**, specialising in agriculture or other primary activities. The fewer, larger settlements are **urban settlements**, specialising in secondary and tertiary activities.

## The three headline differences

1. **Economic base.** Rural settlements live from land-based primary activity. Urban settlements live from processing raw materials, making finished goods and a variety of services.
2. **Link.** Cities are nodes of economic growth and serve the people of the rural hinterland, receiving food and raw materials in return, through a transport and communication network.
3. **Social life.** Rural people are less mobile and relations are intimate. Urban life is complex and fast and relations are formal.

## The map of this lesson

Module 8 admits what the extracts leave out, module 9 answers the nine official questions, and module 10 is a drill. A good first pass is modules 1 to 7 and 9.

### 2. Foundation: the rural-urban contrast and the city-village chain

*Objective:* Explain the three rural-urban differences with reasons and describe how a city and its hinterland exchange goods through a chain of towns.

## The economic difference, with the reason

A village is small and spread out because farming and grazing need land. Villages are spaced far apart because each needs a large farmed area around it; inside a village the houses may still be tightly packed (module 3: the clustered type is the commonest in India). A city is large and compact because making goods and offering services need workers close together, near markets, roads and each other. This is why NCERT can say rural settlements are sparse and urban settlements are generally compact and larger (the reasoning is a reading; the sentences are NCERT's).

## The link: hinterland, node, network

Cities provide goods and services not only to urban dwellers but also to people of the rural settlements in their **hinterlands**. In return they receive food and raw materials. This functional relationship runs through **transport and communication networks** (roads and railways from Day 20 lesson GS-126 are the physical links).

NCERT adds that the exchange of goods and services is performed sometimes directly and sometimes through a series of **market towns** and cities. So cities are connected directly and indirectly with villages, and with each other.


## The social difference, without caricature

NCERT says rural people are less mobile, so relations are intimate; in urban areas the way of life is complex and fast, and relations are formal. These are broad tendencies, not laws. They matter later (module 9) because an examiner can ask whether caste or community ties vanish in cities, and the NCERT sentence alone does not settle that.

## A comparison to keep

| Point | Rural settlement | Urban settlement |
|---|---|---|
| Spacing | Sparse, small, many | Compact, larger, fewer |
| Main work | Land-based primary activity | Processing, manufacturing, services |
| Role | Send food and raw materials | Node of growth, send goods and services |
| People | Less mobile, intimate | Complex, fast, formal |

## The idea of size change

NCERT notes that with size the economic character, social structure, ecology and technology change. A hamlet of ten houses needs no municipality; a city of millions needs water supply, drains and transport. This is the seed of the urban-problems theme in module 8.

### 3. Core: the four rural settlement types and the factors behind them

*Objective:* Name the four rural types with their NCERT regions, give the three groups of factors, and match a description to the right type.

## What decides the type

NCERT says the type of settlement is determined by the extent of the **built-up area** and the **inter-house distance**. In India the compact or clustered village of a few hundred houses is a rather universal feature, particularly in the northern plains, but other forms exist. Three groups of factors act together.

- **Physical features:** nature of terrain, altitude, climate, availability of water.
- **Cultural and ethnic factors:** social structure, caste and religion.
- **Security factors:** defence against thefts and robberies.

NCERT puts rural settlements into four types.

## 1. Clustered (agglomerated, nucleated)

A compact, closely built-up area of houses. The living area is distinct and separate from farms, barns and pastures. The streets show a recognisable pattern such as rectangular, radial or linear (street shapes inside the clustered type, not separate types; for settlement patterns on a map see Day 17 lesson GS-095, Master: drainage patterns, conventional signs and settlement patterns). Found generally in **fertile alluvial plains and the north-eastern states**. Security reasons produce compact villages in the **Bundelkhand** region of central India and in **Nagaland**. In **Rajasthan** scarcity of water necessitates compact settlement for maximum use of the available water.

## 2. Semi-clustered (fragmented)

Arises from a tendency of clustering in a restricted area of dispersed settlement, or from the **segregation or fragmentation of a large compact village**. One or more sections of village society choose, or are forced, to live a little away from the main cluster. Generally the land-owning dominant community holds the central part and people of lower strata and menial workers settle on the outer flanks. Widespread in the **Gujarat plain and parts of Rajasthan**.

## 3. Hamleted

The settlement is fragmented into several physically separate units bearing a common name, locally called **panna, para, palli, nagla, dhani** in various parts of the country. The segmentation of a large village is often motivated by social and ethnic factors. Found more frequently in the **middle and lower Ganga plain, Chhattisgarh and the lower valleys of the Himalayas**.

## 4. Dispersed (isolated)

Isolated huts or hamlets of a few huts in remote jungles or on small hills, with farms or pasture on the slopes. Extreme dispersion is caused by the extremely fragmented terrain and land resource base of the habitable area. Seen in many areas of **Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh and Kerala**.

## Text sketches

A hand-drawn figure helps: four small panels (tight block of houses; a tight block with a few houses set apart on one flank; two or three separate groups sharing one name; single huts scattered on slopes). The sketch is in the diagram box of this module.


## Reading the examples carefully

Rajasthan appears under clustered (water scarcity) and under semi-clustered (parts of Rajasthan): the same state can hold two types, so learn the reason with the region. Nagaland is listed in the text under compact settlement for defence, yet the caption of NCERT Fig. 2.3 says 'Dispersed settlements in Nagaland'. Treat this as a 'check': learn the text and note the caption.

### 4. Core: urban settlements and the Census definition of a town

*Objective:* State the Census urban definition as NCERT prints it, apply its tests to a made-up place, and name the points to verify.

## General features

NCERT says urban settlements are generally compact and larger in size, engaged in a variety of non-agricultural, economic and administrative functions. They are functionally linked to rural areas around them (module 2).

## The definition the Census uses

The Census of India counts a place as urban in one of two ways (standard Census definition, not in the extracts; verify against the current Census text).

1. **Statutory town:** any place with a municipality, corporation, cantonment board or notified town area committee. It is urban whatever its size, work pattern or density.
2. **Census town:** any other place that passes **all three** tests: at least **5,000 persons**; at least **75 per cent of male main workers** in non-agriculture pursuits; a density of at least **400 persons per sq km**.

## What NCERT prints

NCERT quotes the Census of India, **1991** definition: all places which have municipality, corporation, cantonment board or notified town area committee and have a minimum population of 5,000 persons, at least 75 per cent of male workers engaged in non-agriculture pursuits and a density of population of at least 400 persons per square kilometre are urban.

Read literally, the 'and' joins civic status to the three numbers as four conditions. That is a paraphrase slip (legy202 p. 3): the Census treats civic status and the three numbers as separate routes. Learn the two routes. A statement such as 'a place with a municipal body is urban irrespective of population' is correct.

## Four worked checks (original examples)

| Place | Population | Male workers in non-agriculture | Density | Civic body | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 6,000 | 80 per cent | 350 per sq km | none | Not urban: fails density as a census town |
| Q | 4,800 | 90 per cent | 600 per sq km | none | Not urban: fails size |
| R | 7,000 | 82 per cent | 450 per sq km | none | Urban: a census town |
| S | 3,000 | 40 per cent | 150 per sq km | municipality | Urban: a statutory town, whatever the numbers |

## Edge cases to remember

- **Non-agriculture is not the same as non-primary.** Mining is primary but it is not agriculture, so a mining town (module 6 names Raniganj and Jharia) can pass the 75 per cent test.
- **The work test names male main workers,** not all workers and not the whole population. Class XII Exercise 1(ii) option (c), '75% of the population engaged in primary sector', inverts the test on both counts: it is the option that is not part of the definition.
- **An urban agglomeration** is a town together with its adjoining built-up outgrowths, counted as one unit (standard; verify).

## Why the count of towns jumps

Table 2.1 (module 7) shows towns fall from 2,843 in 1951 to 2,365 in 1961 although urban population rose. The extract does not explain this. A change in the definition is the usual explanation: the 1961 Census applied the uniform criteria above (standard knowledge; verify).


### 5. Core: the evolution of towns in India, ancient to post-independence

*Objective:* Sort Indian towns into ancient, medieval, modern and post-independence origins using the NCERT examples and explain what drove each stage.

## One long story in four stages

NCERT says towns flourished in India since prehistoric times; even during the Indus valley civilisation there were towns such as Harappa and Mohanjodaro (as the extract spells it). Evolution continued with periodic ups and downs until Europeans arrived. NCERT classifies Indian towns by their period of evolution as ancient, medieval and modern, and then describes the changes after independence.

## Stage 1: ancient towns

There are a number of towns with a historical background spanning **over 2,000 years**. Most developed as **religious and cultural centres**. Examples: **Varanasi** (one of the important ones), **Prayag (Prayagraj), Pataliputra (Patna), Madurai**.

## Stage 2: medieval towns

About **100** of the existing towns have their roots in the medieval period. Most developed as **headquarters of principalities and kingdoms**: fort towns which came up on the ruins of ancient towns. Examples: **Delhi, Hyderabad, Jaipur, Lucknow, Agra, Nagpur**.

## Stage 3: modern towns

The British and other Europeans developed a number of towns. They began with **trading ports on coastal locations**: Surat, Daman, Goa, Pondicherry. The British later consolidated their hold around three principal nodes, **Mumbai (Bombay), Chennai (Madras) and Kolkata (Calcutta)**, and built them in the British style. As they extended domination, directly or through control over princely states, they set up **administrative centres, hill-towns as summer resorts**, and added new civil, administrative and military areas. **Towns based on modern industries** also evolved after 1850; **Jamshedpur** is NCERT's example.

## Stage 4: after independence

- **Administrative headquarters:** Chandigarh, Bhubaneswar, Gandhinagar, Dispur.
- **Industrial centres:** Durgapur, Bhilai, Sindri, Barauni.
- **Satellite towns** around metropolises: Ghaziabad, Rohtak and Gurugram around Delhi.
- With increasing investment in rural areas, a large number of **medium and small towns** developed all over the country.


## Reading cautions

A town can belong to more than one lens: Varanasi is ancient and also appears in module 6 as religious-cultural and educational; Delhi appears as medieval here and, as New Delhi, as an administrative city in module 6. Check one sentence: NCERT says the evolution continued until the arrival of Europeans in India 'in the eighteenth century', but the same page lists Portuguese-period ports (Daman, Goa). Teach the ports as the first European stage and treat the century as a 'check' (standard accounts place first European arrivals earlier: Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498 and the Portuguese took Goa in 1510; verify).

### 6. Core: the nine functional classes of towns and why big cities become multi-functional

*Objective:* Name the nine NCERT functional classes with examples, and explain why a growing city stops fitting one class.

## Central place and specialist

Apart from their role as central or nodal places, many towns and cities perform specialised services. They are known for some specific activity, product or service. NCERT stresses that each town performs a number of functions; the classes below name the **dominant or specialised** function.

## The nine classes, with NCERT's examples

1. **Administrative towns and cities:** support administrative headquarters of higher order. Chandigarh, New Delhi, Bhopal, Shillong, Guwahati, Imphal, Srinagar, Gandhinagar, Jaipur, Chennai.
2. **Industrial towns:** industries are the prime motive force. Mumbai, Salem, Coimbatore, Modinagar, Jamshedpur, Hugli, Bhilai.
3. **Transport cities:** either **ports** engaged in export and import (Kandla, Cochin, Kozhikode, Vishakhapatnam) or **hubs of inland transport** (Agra, Dhulia, Pandit Deendayal Upadhyay Nagar, Itarsi, Katni).
4. **Commercial towns:** specialise in trade and commerce. Kolkata, Saharanpur, Satna.
5. **Mining towns:** in mineral-rich areas. Raniganj, Jharia, Digboi, Ankaleshwar, Singrauli.
6. **Garrison cantonment towns:** emerged as garrison towns. Ambala, Jalandhar, Mhow, Babina, Udhampur.
7. **Educational towns:** began as centres of education and grew into campus towns. Roorki, Varanasi, Aligarh, Pilani, Prayagraj.
8. **Religious and cultural towns:** prominent for religious or cultural significance. Varanasi, Mathura, Amritsar, Madurai, Puri, Ajmer, Pushkar, Tirupati, Kurukshetra, Haridwar, Ujjain.
9. **Tourist towns:** Nainital, Mussoorie, Shimla, Pachmarhi, Jodhpur, Jaisalmer, Udagamandalam (Ooty), Mount Abu.

(Spellings follow the extract; modern spellings differ, such as Roorkee, Ankleshwar and Visakhapatnam; verify before writing in an answer.)

## Same town, more than one class

Varanasi appears under educational and under religious and cultural. Mumbai is industrial here but is also named as a British-built node in module 5. Jaipur is listed administrative while module 5 puts it among medieval fort towns. These are not contradictions: the classes ask 'what is the town known for now?', the evolution stages ask 'in which period's story does NCERT place it?'.

## Why cities change

NCERT: the cities are not static in their function; the functions change because of their dynamic nature. **Even specialised cities, as they grow into metropolises, become multi-functional**: industry, business, administration, transport and other functions become important, so intertwined that the city cannot be placed in one functional class.

A clear reasoning chain (a reading, not an NCERT sentence): a factory town attracts workers, workers need shops, schools and transport, these create commerce and administration, and soon the original function is only one among many.

## A rule tested on edge cases

Rule to test: 'a town's class tells you its only function.' Edge 1: Mumbai is industrial yet also a port and commercial centre. Edge 2: Varanasi sits in three lenses (ancient, educational, religious). Edge 3: Agra is a transport hub in this list but is also a medieval town. The rule fails; the safe rule is 'the class names the dominant function'.

### 7. Core: urbanisation in India, Table 2.1 and the rural-urban figures

*Objective:* Read Table 2.1 for share, growth rate and number of towns, and state the NCERT urbanisation claims with the checks on them.

## What is measured

NCERT: the **level of urbanisation** is measured as the percentage of urban population to total population. In 2011 it was **31.16 per cent**, quite low compared with developed countries. Total urban population increased **eleven-fold** during the twentieth century. Enlargement of urban centres and the emergence of new towns have played a significant role in the growth of urban population. NCERT says the growth rate of urbanisation has slowed down during the last two decades.

## Table 2.1 in one glance (as printed; urban population in thousands)

| Year | Towns/UAs | Urban population (thousand) | Share of total (per cent) | Decadal growth (per cent) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1901 | 1,827 | 25,851.9 | 10.84 | none |
| 1951 | 2,843 | 62,443.7 | 17.29 | 41.42 |
| 1961 | 2,365 | 78,936.6 | 17.97 | 26.41 |
| 1981 | 3,378 | 1,59,463 | 23.34 | 46.14 |
| 1991 | 4,689 | 2,17,611 | 25.71 | 36.47 |
| 2001 | 5,161 | 2,85,355 | 27.78 | 31.13 |
| 2011 | 6,171 | 3,77,000 | 31.16 | 31.08 |

(The other decades are in the extract. 2011 is provisional.)

## Reading the table (recomputed)

- **Share:** 10.84 to 31.16, a rise of 20.32 percentage points over 110 years.
- **Eleven-fold:** 285,355 divided by 25,851.9 is 11.04 for 1901 to 2001, so the NCERT sentence refers to the twentieth century.
- **Towns/UAs** (the column is headed Number of Towns/UAs; an agglomeration counts once): from 1,827 to 6,171, about 3.4 times. Do not quote it as the count of towns: Census 2011 is usually given as 7,935 towns (4,041 statutory, 3,894 census) plus 475 agglomerations (standard knowledge; verify).
- **Highest decadal urban growth in the table:** 46.14 per cent for 1971 to 1981. After it the rate falls to 36.47 and 31.13; the printed 2011 figure, 31.08, is flagged under 'Checks to remember'.
- **Share and rate are different things.** The share rose 2.07 percentage points in 1991 to 2001 and 3.38 in 2001 to 2011, even though the urban growth rate fell from 36.47 to 31.13 in the decade to 2001. Reason by arithmetic: total population growth fell (21.54 to 17.64 per cent, legy201 Table 1.1), so a similar urban growth took a bigger bite of a more slowly growing total. This reading is not stated in the extract. Whether 2001-11 slowed further depends on the doubtful 31.08: on 32.12 (or the Census figure of about 31.8) it did not fall below 31.13. NCERT's slowing sentence is thus sourced to 1991-2001; the share rising 2.07 then 3.38 points shows faster urbanisation in level terms.

## Rural-urban composition (population chapter)

About **68.8 per cent** of Indians lived in villages in 2011 (urban 31.16, which makes 68.84), in 640,867 villages of which 597,608 (93.2 per cent) are inhabited. The agricultural workforce fell from 58.2 per cent (2001) to 54.6 per cent (2011). The causes and regions of rural-urban migration are taught in Day 21 lesson GS-127, Core: rural-urban migration in India and the pull of big cities.

## Checks to remember

- The printed 2011 growth, 31.08, does not follow from the printed 285,355 and 377,000 thousand, which give 32.12. Rounding cannot explain it (rounding 377,000 by up to 500 moves the result under 0.2), and 31.08 needs a 2001 base of about 287.6 million, which matches no Census figure. The Census 2011 provisional release gave about 31.8 per cent on a revised 2001 base of about 286.1 million, so 31.08 may be a misprint of 31.8 (standard knowledge; verify). Mark 'check'.
- Towns fall from 2,843 to 2,365 between 1951 and 1961 while urban population rises; the extract gives no reason (a definition change is the usual one; verify).
- The state-wise urban share Appendix is not in the extract, so the state ranking cannot be taught from it.

### 8. Master: the Smart Cities box and the urban issues the extracts leave out

*Objective:* State the Smart Cities box in NCERT's words, list urban problem themes with their flags, and use the hook-then-flag method for any urban question.

## What NCERT says about Smart Cities

The NCERT box (legy202, p. 5) says the **objective** of the Smart Cities Mission is to promote cities that provide core infrastructure, a clean and sustainable environment and a decent quality of life to citizens. One feature is to apply **smart solutions** to infrastructure and services to make them better: for example, making areas less vulnerable to disasters, using fewer resources and providing cheaper services. The focus is on **sustainable and inclusive development**, and the idea is to look at **compact areas** and create a **replicable model** that acts like a lighthouse to other aspiring cities.

Outside the extract (standard knowledge, verify): the Mission was launched in 2015 with a list of selected cities, uses an area-based approach plus some pan-city projects, and its scope, funding and end date have changed; check the latest status. Also verify any claim about outcomes.

## What the extracts do not cover

## The hook-then-flag method

For any urban question, build the answer in three layers.

1. **Sourced hook:** a statement the extracts give (a function, the node-hinterland link, Table 2.1, a Smart Cities phrase, a satellite town, a rural-urban figure).
2. **Reasoned link:** one sentence that joins the hook to the question (for example: more people packed in a node means more demand for transport).
3. **Outside point with a flag:** an example or fact from outside, written with 'verify' in your notes and with a cautious phrase in the answer (for example 'reported cases include...').

### Notes: Definitions to know cold

- Human settlement: a cluster of dwellings of any type or size where human beings live, with some area or territory commanded as an economic support-base (NCERT Class XII).
- Village (rural settlement): a sparsely located small settlement specialising in agriculture or other primary activities.
- Urban settlement: a fewer, larger settlement specialising in secondary and tertiary activities; generally compact and larger in size.
- Hinterland: the area around a city that receives its goods and services and sends it food and raw materials.
- Node of economic growth: NCERT's description of a city, linked to villages directly or through market towns.
- Built-up area and inter-house distance: the two things NCERT says decide the type of a rural settlement.
- Level of urbanisation: the percentage of urban population to total population (NCERT).
- Satellite town: a town developed around a metropolis; NCERT names Ghaziabad, Rohtak and Gurugram around Delhi.
- Statutory town, census town and urban agglomeration: standard Census terms not defined in the extracts (verify against the current Census definitions before use).

### Notes: Evolution of towns in India (NCERT)

- Indus valley civilisation: towns such as Harappa and Mohanjodaro (as spelled in the extract) already existed.

### Notes: The Census definition and its edge cases

### Notes: Confused pairs

- Urban share versus urban growth rate: the share is urban divided by total; the growth rate is the change in urban population between Censuses (module 7).
- Clustered versus semi-clustered: semi-clustered has a section living a little apart; the dominant community stays central.
- Hamleted versus dispersed: hamleted units share a common name and are social splits; dispersed huts are scattered by terrain.
- Evolution stage versus functional class: the stage says in which period's story NCERT places a town; the class says what it is known for now.
- Non-agricultural versus non-primary: mining is primary but not agricultural.
- Level of urbanisation (a percentage) versus number of towns (a count).
- Statutory town versus census town: statutory towns are urban by civic status whatever their size; census towns are urban by passing all three numeric tests.
