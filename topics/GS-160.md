## GS-160: Preamble and constitutional ideals

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Constitution fundamentals

### Bridge: What a preamble, an ideal and an ideology are

## Three everyday words

A **preamble** is a short opening statement. Think of the paragraph at the start of a school's rule book that says who made the rules and what the school stands for, before the numbered rules begin. A Constitution does the same: before the Articles, it says who made it, what kind of country it creates and what it hopes to achieve.

An **ideal** is a goal worth reaching even though it is never fully reached, such as "nobody should be treated as inferior because of birth". An ideal is not a rule you can be punished for breaking; it is a direction. A **value** is a belief about what is good (freedom, equality, dignity). An **ideology** is a connected set of ideas about how society and government should be organised, for example socialism or liberalism. Ideals and values are the parts; an ideology is the whole package.

## Why this matters for the Preamble

NCERT says a Constitution is not only a set of rules about who holds power. It also says what the society hopes to become, and it can give a people a shared identity. The Preamble is the place where those hopes are stated in the shortest form. When you read the Preamble, ask three questions of every word: what does it describe (the State), what does it promise (the aims), and does a court enforce it directly or does it guide the interpretation of other provisions?

Keep one distinction in mind from the start: **stating an ideal is different from enforcing a rule**. Much of this lesson is about how the Preamble's ideals reach daily life through other provisions.

### Bridge: The Preamble's key words as plain vocabulary

## A one-line glossary before the lesson

Read these as plain-language first meanings. The lesson will then add the constitutional meaning.

- **Sovereign**: nobody outside the country can give it orders. Like an adult who runs their own household.
- **Socialist**: concerned with reducing gross gaps between rich and poor, usually by giving the State a role in the economy.
- **Secular**: the State does not belong to one religion. It is not the same as being against religion.
- **Democratic**: the people have the final say, directly or through representatives they elect.
- **Republic**: the head of the State is chosen for a fixed time and does not inherit the post, unlike a king or queen.
- **Justice**: fair treatment and fair shares; the Preamble splits it into social, economic and political.
- **Liberty**: freedom to think, speak, believe and worship; freedom with limits, not licence.
- **Equality**: no special privileges by birth; equal chances to rise.
- **Fraternity**: a feeling of brotherhood and common belonging among citizens.

## A small test of the glossary

Britain has a king, so it is a monarchy; India's head of State is the President, elected for a fixed term, so it is a republic. A State that makes no religion its official faith and treats all faiths alike is secular. A country where officials are chosen by free elections is democratic. These everyday meanings are the first layer; modules 3 and 4 add the exact constitutional layer, which is richer. Do not try to memorise the glossary; use it as scaffolding while you read.

### 1. Orientation: the Preamble in one reading

*Objective:* Read the Preamble as one sentence with four parts and name its four ingredients.

## What the Preamble is

The **Preamble** is the introduction or preface to the Constitution. Laxmikanth says it contains the summary or essence of the Constitution, and that the American Constitution was the first to begin with a Preamble; many countries, including India, followed. N. A. Palkhivala, a jurist, called it the identity card of the Constitution.

## The text, as Laxmikanth prints it

> We, the people of India, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; in our Constituent Assembly this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution.

It is one long sentence. Learn its skeleton and the rest hangs on it.

## The four ingredients (Laxmikanth)

1. **Source of authority**: the Constitution derives its authority from the people of India ("We, the people").
2. **Nature of the Indian State**: sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic and republican.
3. **Objectives**: justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.
4. **Date of adoption**: 26 November 1949.

## A reading map

Read the sentence as WHO speaks (we, the people), WHAT they set up (a republic with five descriptors), WHY (four aims) and WHEN (26 November 1949). The rest of this lesson unpacks the second and third parts, then asks the legal questions: is the Preamble part of the Constitution, can it be amended, and what can a court do with it?

## Two facts to pin early

The Preamble is based on the **Objectives Resolution**, drafted and moved by Nehru and adopted by the Constituent Assembly. And it has been amended **once**: the 42nd Amendment Act (1976) added three words, **Socialist, Secular and Integrity**. Before 1976 the Preamble described India as a sovereign democratic republic and spoke of the "unity of the Nation".

### 2. Where the Preamble came from

*Objective:* Explain the Objectives Resolution as the source of the Preamble and why the Preamble was enacted last.

## The Objectives Resolution

Laxmikanth records that the Preamble is based on the **Objectives Resolution**, drafted and moved by Pandit Nehru on 13 December 1946 and adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 22 January 1947. NCERT calls it the resolution that defined the aims of the Assembly and the best summary of the principles the nationalist movement brought to the Assembly. For the meeting, the vote and the committees see GS-159.

NCERT lists its main points: India is an independent sovereign republic; a Union of British Indian territories, Indian States and other willing parts; territories are autonomous units with powers except those given to the Union; all power and authority flow from the people; social, economic and political justice, equality of status and opportunity, equality before law and fundamental freedoms (speech, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association, action), subject to law and public morality; adequate safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed and other backward classes; the territorial integrity of the Republic; and a contribution to world peace and the welfare of mankind.

## Spot the overlap

Lay the Preamble beside that list. Sovereign republic, power from the people, justice, equality and freedoms appear in both. The Preamble is shorter and drops the detail on units and safeguards, which the Articles later supply. That is why it is called a summary: it compresses the Resolution into the aims and the nature of the State.

## The inheritance behind the words

NCERT argues that no constitution is simply the product of its Assembly. The principles were forged during the freedom struggle, and the Assembly gave them a legal form. NCERT calls the Constitution a moral commitment to the promises of the nationalist movement. Laxmikanth adds a source note: the ideal of justice in the Preamble was taken from the Russian Revolution (1917), and the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity from the French Revolution (1789-1799). These are "ideals taken from" statements, not claims that those revolutions wrote the Indian text.

## The Preamble was enacted last

Like every part, the Preamble was enacted by the Constituent Assembly, but after the rest of the Constitution was already enacted. Laxmikanth gives the reason: to ensure it conformed to the Constitution as adopted. When the President of the Assembly put the question, it was whether the Preamble stands as part of the Constitution; the motion was adopted. Keep this fact; module 5 uses it.

## "We, the people"

NCERT reads the opening words as a humble claim: the Constitution is not given by great men but prepared and adopted by the people. It also says the Constitution was never put to a referendum, yet drew authority from the credibility of its makers and because the people adopted it by abiding by it. The Assembly itself was elected indirectly, not by universal suffrage, which is a point critics raise (module 6).

### 3. The State described: sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic

*Objective:* Explain each of the five descriptors of the Indian State with its constitutional meaning and one anchor fact.

## Sovereign

Laxmikanth: India is neither a dependency nor a dominion of any other nation but an independent State; no authority is above it and it is free in internal and external affairs. From 15 August 1947 to 26 January 1950 India was a dominion in the British Commonwealth and ceased to be one on 26 January 1950 by declaring itself a sovereign republic. In 1949 India continued its full membership of the Commonwealth and accepted the Crown as head of the Commonwealth, but this extra-constitutional declaration does not affect sovereignty; membership of the UN does not limit it either. Laxmikanth adds that a sovereign State can acquire or cede territory. [Standard knowledge, verify: in the Berubari reference (1960) the Court held that ceding Indian territory to another country needs a constitutional amendment under Article 368, not an ordinary law.]

## Socialist

Added by the 42nd Amendment (1976), but the Constitution already carried socialist content in certain Directive Principles; the amendment made explicit what was implicit. The Congress had adopted a resolution at Avadi (1955) for a socialistic pattern of society. Laxmikanth stresses that the Indian brand is **democratic socialism**, with a **mixed economy** where public and private sectors co-exist, and not the state socialism that nationalises all means of production and abolishes private property. He quotes the Supreme Court: it aims to end poverty, ignorance, disease and inequality of opportunity, and is a blend of Marxism and Gandhism leaning heavily towards Gandhian socialism. He adds that the 1991 economic reforms diluted the socialist credentials of the State; treat that as his reading, not a settled legal fact.

## Secular

Also added in 1976. The Supreme Court said in 1974 that although the words were not mentioned, the framers clearly wanted a secular State, which is why Articles 25 to 28 on freedom of religion were included. NCERT agrees the Constitution has always been secular. Laxmikanth says it embodies the **positive concept**: all religions have the same status and support from the State, unlike the Western concept of complete separation of church and State. NCERT calls the Indian approach **principled distance**: the State may help or hold back religious communities, depending on what better promotes liberty, equality and social justice (for example acting against untouchability, or aiding minority educational institutions).

## Democratic

Based on popular sovereignty. Laxmikanth separates **direct democracy** (Switzerland; devices are referendum, initiative, recall, plebiscite) from **indirect or representative democracy**, which is parliamentary or presidential. India has representative parliamentary democracy, where the executive answers to the legislature. Signs: universal adult franchise, periodic elections, rule of law, independent judiciary, no discrimination on certain grounds. The word is used broadly: **political, social and economic democracy** (module 4 carries Ambedkar's argument).

## Republic

A democratic polity is either a monarchy (hereditary head, for example Britain) or a republic (head elected directly or indirectly for a fixed period, for example the USA). India's President is indirectly elected for five years. Laxmikanth adds two meanings: political sovereignty vests in the people and not a single person, and there is no privileged class, so public offices are open to all without discrimination.

### 4. The aims: justice, liberty, equality, fraternity

*Objective:* Explain each of the four aims, the sub-types the Preamble names, and the Articles that give them force.

## Justice: social, economic, political

Laxmikanth: the Preamble's justice has three forms, secured through Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. **Social justice** is equal treatment without distinction of caste, colour, race, religion or sex, no special privileges, and improvement of the conditions of backward classes (SCs, STs, OBCs) and women. **Economic justice** is non-discrimination on economic grounds and the removal of glaring inequalities of wealth, income and property. Social plus economic justice is called **distributive justice**. **Political justice** means equal political rights, equal access to political offices and an equal voice in government.

## Liberty

Liberty means the absence of restraints on individuals **and** the provision of opportunities for developing personality. The Preamble names five liberties: thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; they come through Fundamental Rights that courts enforce. Laxmikanth stresses that liberty is not licence and is qualified by limits in the Constitution. Note what the list omits: **economic liberty is not named** as a liberty (economic justice is named under justice).

## Equality of status and of opportunity

No special privileges to any section and adequate opportunities for all. Laxmikanth names three dimensions. **Civic**: equality before law (Article 14), no discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth (Article 15), equal opportunity in public employment (Article 16), abolition of untouchability (Article 17), abolition of titles (Article 18). **Political**: no one ineligible for electoral rolls on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex (Article 325) and adult suffrage for Lok Sabha and Assembly elections (Article 326). **Economic**: Laxmikanth cites Article 39, which gives men and women an equal right to an adequate livelihood and equal pay for equal work.

## Fraternity

A sense of brotherhood, promoted by single citizenship and by the Fundamental Duty (Article 51A) to promote harmony and common brotherhood across religious, linguistic, regional or sectional diversities. The Preamble says fraternity must assure two things. First, **the dignity of the individual**; K. M. Munshi read this as recognising that every personality is sacred, and the duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women supports it. Second, **the unity and integrity of the Nation**, covering psychological and territorial national integration. Article 1 calls India a "Union of States", signalling that States cannot secede; the aim is to overcome communalism, regionalism, casteism, linguism and secessionism. "Integrity" was added in 1976.

## The trinity argument

In his concluding speech (25 November 1949) Dr Ambedkar argued that political democracy cannot last without social democracy, a way of life that recognises liberty, equality and fraternity as a union and not as separate items. Without equality, liberty gives the few supremacy over the many; equality without liberty kills individual initiative; and without fraternity, liberty and equality cannot become a natural course of things. NCERT prints the same passage. This is the best single quotation to use in a Mains answer on the Preamble.

### 5. Legal status: part of the Constitution, amendable, interpretive, non-justiciable

*Objective:* State the three Supreme Court positions on the Preamble and what the Preamble can and cannot do in law.

## The question

Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution, or only an introduction outside it? The answer decides whether it can be amended and how far courts may rely on it.

## Three cases, three positions (Laxmikanth)

| Case | What the Court said |
|---|---|
| Berubari Union reference (1960) | The Preamble shows the general purposes behind the provisions and is a key to the minds of the makers. If an Article is ambiguous, the Preamble can help interpret it. But the Court specifically opined that the Preamble is **not a part** of the Constitution. |
| Kesavananda Bharati (1973) | The Court rejected the earlier view and held the Preamble **is a part** of the Constitution, to be read and interpreted in the light of its vision. |
| LIC of India v Consumer Education and Research Centre (1995) | Again held the Preamble an **integral part** of the Constitution. |

Laxmikanth supports the later view from the Assembly record: the Preamble was put to the vote as part of the Constitution, so the present judicial view matches the founders' intention.

## What the Preamble is not

Two cautions from Laxmikanth. The Preamble is **neither a source of power to the legislature nor a prohibition upon its powers**, so a law cannot be struck down merely because it conflicts with the Preamble's wording. And it is **non-justiciable**: its provisions are not enforceable in courts. Its work is interpretive: it guides readers of vague Articles and states the vision, while enforceable rights live in Part III.

## Can it be amended?

The question first arose in Kesavananda Bharati. The petitioner argued that the Preamble could not be amended because it was not part of the Constitution and held basic elements. The Court held that the Preamble is a part of the Constitution and **can be amended under Article 368, but not so as to alter its basic features**. The Court said the Constitution's edifice rests on the basic elements in the Preamble; remove one and the structure cannot survive, and an amending power cannot be read to take such features away. The Preamble has been amended **only once**, by the 42nd Amendment (1976), adding Socialist, Secular and Integrity, and that amendment was held valid.

## Putting it together

Part of the Constitution: yes (since 1973, reaffirmed 1995). Amendable: yes, but not the basic features. Enforceable on its own: no. Used for interpretation: yes. For the basic structure doctrine see GS-173; for the amending procedure see GS-167.

### 6. Constitutional ideals: the vision of the Assembly, Nehru and Ambedkar

*Objective:* Describe the philosophy of the Constitution and apply it to the Preamble's ideals, including its critics and limits.

## What 'philosophy of the Constitution' means

NCERT asks us to read the Constitution as based on a moral vision, not only as laws. This means understanding what its key terms (rights, citizenship, minority, democracy) can mean, working out a coherent picture of society from them, and reading the text with the Constituent Assembly debates, which explain why the values were chosen. NCERT says the philosophy resists one label: it is liberal, democratic, egalitarian, secular and federal, open to community values, sensitive to minorities and disadvantaged groups, and committed to a common national identity, with democracy as the umpire.

## The core features NCERT lists

- **Individual freedom**: a liberal commitment with long roots, from Rammohan Roy's defence of press freedom to opposition to arbitrary arrest.
- **Social justice**: liberalism linked to justice, shown by reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- **Respect for diversity and minority rights**: community-based rights, such as running educational institutions.
- **Secularism**: principled distance, not strict separation (module 3).
- **Universal franchise**: accepted with faith in the common man, though hierarchies were deep.
- **Federalism**: including asymmetric provisions, for example Articles 371 and 371A for particular States.
- **National identity**: a common identity that does not deny religious or linguistic ones; separate electorates were rejected, and the aim was fraternity.

NCERT adds procedural achievements: faith in deliberation, and a spirit of compromise and accommodation.

## The Assembly's voices

Nehru described a Constituent Assembly as a nation on the move, shedding its past structures and fashioning a new garment; in NCERT's reading, a Constitution here is a tool of democratic transformation that also empowers those traditionally deprived of power. Ambedkar's social democracy argument is in module 4. Sardar Patel pressed the idea of evolving one community, free of majority-minority thinking.

## Criticisms and limits (NCERT)

Three criticisms: the Constitution is unwieldy, unrepresentative (the Assembly was not elected by universal franchise, though many opinions were voiced) and alien (borrowed). NCERT replies that borrowing was innovative selective adaptation. It then names real limits: a centralised idea of national unity; neglect of gender justice within the family; and socio-economic rights placed in Directive Principles instead of Fundamental Rights. NCERT concludes that these do not threaten the philosophy.

## Reading ideals correctly

An ideal gives direction; the work is done by Articles, laws and institutions. NCERT's closing point is that the shared commitment to live and prosper together on equality, liberty and fraternity is itself an outcome of the Constitution's working.

### 7. Preamble, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; the 42nd Amendment

*Objective:* Compare the Preamble with Part III and Part IV and explain the 1976 insertions and their context.

## Three layers of the same ideals

The Preamble states the aims; Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (Directive Principles) carry them out. Laxmikanth says the Preamble's justice is secured through both parts and its liberties through enforceable Fundamental Rights. The table in master notes compares them. The shortest comparison: the Preamble is a **statement of vision and a tool of interpretation**; Fundamental Rights are **enforceable claims** against the State; Directive Principles are **guidelines to the State** that courts do not enforce (see GS-165). In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court held harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles to be part of the basic structure (Laxmikanth, Table 11.1).

## The 42nd Amendment and the Preamble

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act (1976) added **Socialist, Secular and Integrity**. So the phrase became "sovereign socialist secular democratic republic" and "unity of the Nation" became "unity and integrity of the Nation". NCERT calls the 42nd the most wide-ranging and controversial amendment, passed during the Emergency; it changed the Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 Articles, and was seen as attempting to override Kesavananda. The 43rd and 44th amendments (1977-78) undid most of its disputed changes but did not remove the new Preamble words, and the Court held the Preamble amendment valid. GS-166 covers the Fundamental Duties added in the same amendment; GS-172 lists the amendment's other changes.

## Words that were implicit, now explicit

Laxmikanth treats 'socialist' as making explicit what the Directive Principles implied, and 'secular' as naming what Articles 25 to 28 already did. Laxmikanth's secular provisions list also includes Articles 14, 15, 16, 25 to 30 and the Directive on a Uniform Civil Code (Article 44). That is why a question "was India secular before 1976?" has a two-part answer: the word was absent, the substance was present.

## Another way to place the ideals

The Preamble's "sovereign, democratic, republic" and "unity and integrity" and "secular" also appear in Laxmikanth's list of basic-structure elements (Table 11.1 and the list of elements): sovereign democratic republic, secular character, unity and integrity of the nation, welfare state, freedom and dignity of the individual, equality, and more. So the Preamble's words are not only descriptions; many are also limits on the amending power.

### 8. Recent debate on 'socialist' and 'secular'; constitutional morality

*Objective:* Summarise the 2024-25 challenge to the 1976 insertions with a verification flag, and place constitutional morality in a Mains frame.

## The challenge to the 1976 words [verify: not in the textbook extracts]

Petitions were filed in the Supreme Court arguing that 'socialist' and 'secular' should not have been inserted into a Preamble adopted in 1949, partly because the Emergency-era Parliament had passed the amendment. As understood when this lesson was drafted, the Court decided the matter in November 2024 (the Balram Singh matter) and did not accept the challenge: it held that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 extends to the Preamble, that the date of adoption does not bar amendment, that secularism is part of the basic structure and that 'socialism' in India is best read as a welfare State rather than a ban on private enterprise. **Treat this paragraph as a flagged summary** and check the judgment text and later orders before quoting any detail in an answer. Laxmikanth's textbook position (module 5) is that the amendment was held valid earlier.

## How to write about it safely

Use verbs such as 'the Court reiterated' and 'in the recent challenge', and anchor your argument to safe facts: the words were added in 1976; the Preamble is part of the Constitution; it can be amended without altering basic features (Kesavananda); Articles 25 to 28 show secularism existed; the Directive Principles show socialist content existed. Do not claim more about the case than you can verify.

## Constitutional morality [not in the extracts; flagged]

Four verified PYQs ask about constitutional morality. The idea is that the values of the Constitution (justice, liberty, equality, fraternity, dignity) must guide officials and citizens, rather than the passing preferences of a majority or personal morality. Ambedkar used the phrase in the Constituent Assembly (1948), arguing that it is not a natural sentiment and must be cultivated [verify the exact speech]. Courts have invoked it in several modern judgments (for example the 2018 cases on the criminalisation of homosexuality and on the entry of women into the Sabarimala temple [verify]). For the 2025 Mains question, link it to judicial independence (a basic-structure element in Laxmikanth's list) and accountability.

## A three-part frame for any 'ideals' question

1. **Define** with the Preamble's words (the four aims, dignity, unity and integrity).
2. **Show the working**: which Articles, institutions and cases carry the ideal.
3. **Test it**: where practice falls short (NCERT's limits) and what restores balance (courts, voters, a culture of deliberation).

Nehru's remark that no Constitution should be permanent and rigid, quoted by Laxmikanth in the amendment chapter (see GS-167), shows the same balance: fixed ideals, flexible means.

### 9. Examination application: patterns, traps and elimination

*Objective:* Recognise the six question shapes on this topic and apply elimination to the verified PYQs.

## The verified PYQ set

Sixteen verified questions are attached: twelve Prelims (2017 to 2023) and four Mains (GS II 2021 and 2025; GS IV 2019 and 2022). Read the questions (the PYQ panel) before this module; the notes there give analysis, not an official key.

## Six question shapes

1. **Is it in the Preamble?** (2017 Q38; 2021 Q93). Know the text. Liberty has five named forms; economic liberty is not among them. On 26 January 1950 the Preamble read sovereign democratic republic: 'socialist' and 'secular' came in 1976.
2. **Legal status** (2020 Q16; 2017 Q48). Part of the Constitution, interpretive, non-justiciable, neither a source of power nor a limit.
3. **Concept definitions** (2017 Q5, 2019 Q85, 2018 Q40, 2018 Q45, 2020 Q7, 2023 Q33, 2017 Q42, 2021 Q87). These test political-theory understanding. Anchor on the source: equality means no special privileges; liberty is more than absence of restraint; a Constitution defines and limits government.
4. **Constitutional morality** (Mains). Define, give judicial examples, balance.
5. **Case-year pairs** (Berubari, Kesavananda, LIC).
6. **Amendment facts** (the three words, 1976, only one amendment).

## Prelims traps

- 'Preamble is not a part of the Constitution' is the 1960 view, reversed in 1973.
- 'Preamble is enforceable' fails because it is non-justiciable.
- 'Socialist' means nationalising everything: Laxmikanth says democratic socialism with a mixed economy.
- 'Secular' means separation: the positive concept applies.
- 'Republic' means the President is directly elected: India's President is indirectly elected.
- 'Justice from the French Revolution': Laxmikanth assigns justice to the Russian Revolution and liberty, equality, fraternity to the French.
- 'Amended many times': once.

## Elimination routine

1. Underline absolute words (only, never, always, all). 2. Mark each statement with one fact from this lesson. 3. If two statements are firmly true or false, remove options that conflict. 4. For definitional options, prefer the one that has both halves of a two-part definition (liberty: no restraints and opportunity to develop).

## Negative marking

A wrong Prelims answer costs one-third of 2 marks, 0.67. With four options and blind guessing the expected score is about zero. If you can eliminate two options with certainty, the chance of being right is 50 per cent and the expected gain per attempt is positive (0.5 x 2 - 0.5 x 0.67 = 0.67 marks).

### Notes: Key definitions

- Preamble: the introduction or preface to the Constitution, containing its summary or essence (Laxmikanth); called its identity card by N. A. Palkhivala.
- Objectives Resolution: the resolution drafted and moved by Nehru (13 December 1946) and adopted by the Constituent Assembly (22 January 1947) on which the Preamble is based.
- Sovereign: not a dependency or dominion of another State; no authority above it; free in internal and external affairs.
- Democratic socialism: the Indian brand of socialism, with a mixed economy in which public and private sectors co-exist; contrasted with state socialism that nationalises all means of production.
- Positive secularism: all religions have the same status and support from the State; NCERT calls the Indian approach principled distance.
- Republic: the head of State is elected, directly or indirectly, for a fixed period, with no hereditary office and no privileged class.
- Distributive justice: the combination of social and economic justice.
- Non-justiciable: not enforceable in courts of law.
- Constituent power: the power to make or change the Constitution itself (used in Article 368).

- Supreme Court (1974) on secularism: the framers wanted a secular State though the word was absent (Laxmikanth).
- Minerva Mills (1980): harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is a basic feature (Laxmikanth, Table 11.1).
- S. R. Bommai (1994): secularism, federalism, democracy and unity and integrity among basic features (Laxmikanth, Table 11.1).
- Balram Singh matter (November 2024): challenge to the words 'socialist' and 'secular' decided by the Supreme Court; details flagged to verify against the judgment.

### Notes: Preamble versus Fundamental Rights versus Directive Principles

- A three-way comparison for Prelims and Mains.

### Notes: Sources of the Preamble's ideas (Laxmikanth)

- Objectives Resolution drafted and moved by Nehru: the direct source of the Preamble's content.
- Russian Revolution (1917): the ideal of justice, social, economic and political.
- French Revolution (1789-1799) and French Constitution: the Republic and the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity.
- Soviet Constitution: Fundamental Duties and the ideal of justice (Laxmikanth's borrowed-features table).
- Do not confuse: the Directive Principles come from the Irish Constitution, and Fundamental Rights from the American.

### Notes: The 42nd Amendment's effect on the Preamble

- Words added: Socialist, Secular, Integrity.
- Before: sovereign democratic republic; unity of the Nation.
- After: sovereign socialist secular democratic republic; unity and integrity of the Nation.
- Held valid; the Preamble has been amended only once.
- Context: Emergency; NCERT calls the 42nd amendment wide-ranging and controversial; the 43rd and 44th amendments undid most disputed changes but not these words.
- Same amendment inserted Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A): see GS-166.

### Notes: Cause and effect: why the Preamble matters in law

- Enacted last, by vote, as part of the Constitution -> the Court's later view that it is a part matches the founders' intention.
- Part of the Constitution -> it can be amended under Article 368 -> but its basic features cannot be altered (Kesavananda).
- Non-justiciable and neither a source of power nor a limit -> laws cannot be struck down for conflicting with its wording alone.
- Ambiguity in an Article -> the Preamble helps interpret it (Berubari) -> interpretive importance despite non-enforceability.
- Basic elements in the Preamble -> many appear as basic features -> they limit the amending power.

### Notes: Concepts commonly confused

- Sovereign versus independent of the Commonwealth: Commonwealth membership does not reduce sovereignty.
- Democratic socialism versus communistic or state socialism.
- Secular (positive concept, principled distance) versus the Western strict-separation model.
- Republic versus democracy: republic is about the head of State; democracy is about rule by the people.
- Liberty versus licence; liberty of belief and worship versus economic liberty (not named).
- Social justice versus economic justice versus distributive justice.
- Preamble as part of the Constitution versus Preamble as enforceable.
- Preamble amended versus basic features amended: amendment is allowed, altering basic features is not.

### Notes: Articles most often tied to the Preamble's ideals

- Article 1: India is a Union of States (unity and integrity; States cannot secede).
- Articles 14 to 18: civic equality (law, non-discrimination, public employment, untouchability, titles).
- Articles 25 to 28: freedom of religion and the secular character.
- Articles 325 and 326: political equality and adult suffrage.
- Article 39: equal livelihood and equal pay (Directive).
- Article 51A: fraternity and dignity through duties.
- Article 368: amending power, relevant to the Preamble's amendability (see GS-167).

### Core concepts

- The Preamble is the summary of the Constitution, stating source of authority, nature of the State, objectives and date.
- It is based on the Objectives Resolution moved by Nehru and was enacted last.
- It is a part of the Constitution, amendable but not in its basic features, non-justiciable, and an aid to interpretation.
- Its ideals reach daily life through Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

**Q.** What is the Preamble?

**A.** The introduction or preface to the Constitution, containing its summary or essence.

**Q.** On what is the Preamble based?

**A.** The Objectives Resolution drafted and moved by Nehru and adopted by the Constituent Assembly.

**Q.** What do the words 'We, the people of India' show?

**A.** That the Constitution derives its authority from the people.

**Q.** Which three words did the 42nd Amendment add?

**A.** Socialist, Secular and Integrity.

**Q.** Why is the Preamble useful in interpretation even though it is non-justiciable?

**A.** It is a key to the minds of the makers and can help interpret ambiguous Articles; it guides readers but does not itself create enforceable rights.

**Q.** Explain why India's secularism is called 'principled distance'.

**A.** The State keeps a distance from all religions but may intervene or support depending on what promotes liberty, equality and social justice, for example abolishing untouchability or aiding minority institutions.

**Q.** Explain the difference between democratic and state socialism in Laxmikanth.

**A.** Democratic socialism keeps a mixed economy with public and private sectors; state socialism nationalises all means of production and abolishes private property.

**Q.** How does the Kesavananda holding connect the Preamble to the amending power?

**A.** The Preamble is a part of the Constitution and can be amended, but its basic features cannot be altered under Article 368.

**Q.** A speaker says: 'The Preamble was amended repeatedly to suit rulers.' Evaluate using two facts.

**A.** The Preamble has been amended only once (1976, three words); the Court held it can be amended but not in its basic features.

**Q.** A petition claims a law is void because it conflicts with the word 'socialist' in the Preamble. Assess the claim.

**A.** The Preamble is neither a source of power nor a prohibition on the legislature and is non-justiciable, so a law cannot be struck down merely for conflict with it; courts test laws against specific provisions.
