## GS-155: Secularism and constitutional practice

Mains GS1 + Prelims GS1 · Society · Indian society

*Goal:* Explain Secularism and constitutional practice and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### What makes a state secular

Separating religious and political institutions is necessary but not sufficient. A theocracy, run by religious authorities, typically denies freedom to other faiths, but even non-theocratic states may formally ally with one religion, as England did with the Anglican Church and as Pakistan does today. A truly secular state must reject both theocracy and an established religion, and must pursue goals drawn at least partly from non-religious sources: peace, religious freedom, freedom from religiously grounded oppression and exclusion, and equality both between and within religions. The form of separation can vary. In the mainstream Western model, state and religion stay out of each other's spheres; the state cannot fund religious schools or interfere if a religion bars women from priesthood. Turkey under Ataturk shows a different, interventionist and suppressive variant.

### The Indian model of principled distance

Indian secularism emerged in a society already deeply plural, where a culture of tolerance existed but could coexist with domination. Interaction with Western ideas of equality sharpened attention to injustice within communities. The result opposes oppression of Dalits and women within Hinduism, discrimination against women within Indian Islam or Christianity, and majority threats to minorities. It protects individual religious freedom and the right of religious minorities to maintain culture and educational institutions, which may receive state aid. It also allows state-backed reform: the Constitution bans untouchability and laws have tackled child marriage and the taboo on inter-caste marriage. Principled distance lets the state disengage from religion or engage with it, negatively against tyranny or positively through support, whichever best advances peace, freedom and equality.

### Criticisms and responses

Critics call secularism anti-religious; NCERT replies that it opposes institutionalised domination, not faith, and protects religious identity except dogmatic or violent forms. To the charge of being a Western import, it answers that India developed its own variant rooted in peaceful coexistence of communities. On 'minoritism', it uses analogies: a person with asthma should not be outvoted on smoking in a train compartment, and a ramp for wheelchair users is equal respect, not privilege, so minority rights protecting fundamental interests are justified. On interventionism, principled distance also allows non-interference; personal laws pose a real dilemma, and reform should come by supporting democratic voices within communities rather than coercion. The Class 12 text adds that the Indian everyday sense of secular is the opposite of communal, exemplified by public holidays for festivals of all religions.

### Worked example

Q: 'Indian secularism is distinct from the Western model.' Elaborate. Frame: define secular state (no theocracy, no established religion, non-religious ends); Western mutual exclusion; Indian differences (intra + inter-religious focus, community rights, state-supported reform); principled distance with examples (untouchability ban, aided minority institutions); criticisms (anti-religious, Western, minoritism, interventionist, vote banks) and responses; personal laws dilemma; conclude on secularism as a guarantee of equal citizenship.

### Recall

- - **q:** What is 'principled distance'?
  - **a:** A policy by which the state may disengage from or engage with religion, negatively or positively, depending on what best promotes peace, freedom and equality.
- - **q:** Give two ways Indian secularism differs from the Western model.
  - **a:** It addresses intra- as well as inter-religious domination and protects community (minority) rights, and it allows state-supported religious reform.
- - **q:** How did Nehru define secularism?
  - **a:** Equal protection by the State to all religions, without favouring any or adopting a state religion.

### Common trap

Reducing Indian secularism to 'equal respect for all religions'. NCERT argues principled distance permits 'equal disrespect' for some aspects of every religion, such as religiously sanctioned caste hierarchy, so it is more than tolerance or coexistence.
