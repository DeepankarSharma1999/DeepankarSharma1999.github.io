## GS-273: EIA stages and environmental clearance

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Environment · Ecology fundamentals

*Goal:* Explain EIA stages and environmental clearance and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### From 1978 practice to the 2006 notification

India first used impact assessment in 1978 for river valley schemes and later for large public sector proposals needing Public Investment Board approval. The practice became law with the 1994 notification, which initially listed 29 project categories, later 32, chosen mainly by investment size. The 2006 revision changed the logic: the trigger became the likely environmental impact rather than the money invested, coverage rose to 39 categories, and the system was decentralised so that states share the work. Its stated aims were transparency, efficiency, safeguards built in at the planning stage and stronger public participation.

### The four stages and the role of scoping

A February 2020 amendment restates that prior clearance moves through screening, scoping, public consultation and appraisal. Scoping is where detailed Terms of Reference are settled so that the EIA and Environment Management Report address every relevant concern. The Ministry has issued sector-specific standard ToR for all 39 classes; for highways in border states, projects in approved industrial estates and expansions of already-cleared projects, standard ToR are issued online within seven working days. Other new projects are referred to the EAC or SEAC within 30 days for any extra conditions. Category B2 activities skip scoping altogether, and ToR remain valid for four years, or five for hydro and river valley projects.

### Who decides, and what happens after clearance

Expert Appraisal Committees examine Category A proposals across sectors such as thermal power, mining, river valley, infrastructure, CRZ and nuclear projects; MoEFCC then grants or refuses clearance. Category B proposals are handled by State Level Impact Assessment Authorities. In 2025 the Centre cleared 502 projects. Conditions typically cover air and water quality, land degradation, biodiversity and wildlife habitat, plus rainwater harvesting and greenbelts. Compliance is checked by eleven Regional Offices, nine sub-regional offices and State Pollution Control Boards, and a separate Compliance and Monitoring Division was created in July 2023. The Environment Audit Rules, notified in August 2025, bring outside professionals into audits to widen the compliance-monitoring pool.
