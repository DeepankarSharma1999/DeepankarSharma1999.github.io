### Bridge: Signed velocities and relative velocity along a line

In a head-on collision everything happens on one line, so each velocity can be written as a single **signed number**: choose a positive direction (in this lesson, the initial direction of body 1, to the right) and give every leftward velocity a minus sign. A ball moving left at 3 m/s has velocity −3 m/s, not 3 m/s. Dropping this sign is the commonest error in one-dimensional collision problems.

The **velocity of body 1 relative to body 2** is u₁ − u₂. If body 1 is behind (to the left of) body 2, they get closer when u₁ − u₂ > 0, and this positive number is the **speed of approach**. After the collision the **speed of separation** is v₂ − v₁, which must be positive or zero; otherwise body 1 would pass through body 2.

Example: body 1 at +5 m/s and body 2 at −1 m/s (coming towards it) approach at 5 − (−1) = 6 m/s. If afterwards v₁ = −0.4 m/s and v₂ = +2.6 m/s, they separate at 2.6 − (−0.4) = 3.0 m/s. The ratio 3.0/6 = 0.5 is the coefficient of restitution of module 2. Relative velocities are the same for every observer moving at constant velocity (PH-011 bridge B1), which is why e is frame independent.

### Bridge: Components along and across a line

In an oblique impact the important directions are not the x and y axes but the **line of impact** n (for two spheres, the line joining their centres at contact) and the **tangent** t perpendicular to it. Any velocity u can be split into a part along n and a part along t. If the angle between u and n is φ,

$$ u_n = u cos φ,   u_t = u sin φ $$

and u² = u_n² + u_t² (Pythagoras), so the kinetic energy also splits into an n part and a t part. Because the two directions are perpendicular, a force along n changes only u_n and leaves u_t alone.

Example: u = 10 m/s at φ = 30° to n gives u_n = 10 × 0.866 = 8.66 m/s and u_t = 10 × 0.5 = 5.0 m/s; check 8.66² + 5.0² = 75 + 25 = 100 = 10². To return to x and y, write each final velocity as v_n n + v_t t and resolve n and t along x and y. Module 6 uses exactly these two steps.

### Bridge: Geometric series

A **geometric series** is a sum in which each term is a fixed multiple r of the previous one: a + ar + ar² + ar³ + .... If |r| < 1 the terms shrink fast enough for the infinite sum to be finite:

$$ S = a/(1 − r) $$

One-line proof: S − rS = a, because every other term cancels, so S(1 − r) = a. Example: 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + ... = 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2. Starting from the second term, ar + ar² + ... = ar/(1 − r); for a = 1 and r = 0.64 this is 0.64/0.36 = 1.778.

A bouncing ball produces two such series. Each rebound height is e² times the previous one (ratio e²) and each flight time is e times the previous one (ratio e). Module 5 adds them to find the total distance and the total time. If r = 1 (perfectly elastic bounces) the series does not converge: the ideal ball would bounce for ever.

## What the syllabus words mean

The Physics Paper I syllabus, section (b) Mechanics of Rigid Bodies, names **Elastic and inelastic collisions** between the conservation theorems and the rigid body. A **collision** is a short, strong interaction between two bodies: before and after it they move freely; during it a large mutual force changes their velocities abruptly and redistributes their momentum (Mathur, section 6.9). The bodies need not touch: an alpha particle turned back by a nucleus has also collided (a *scattering*). ### 2. Foundation: momentum through the impact, the two phases and the coefficient of restitution

*Objective:* Show that momentum is conserved through an impact, find the compression and restitution impulses, and define e in two equivalent ways.

## Conventions

One dimension: the x axis lies along the line of impact, positive in the initial direction of body 1, which is behind (to the left of) body 2. Velocities are signed numbers (bridge B1): u₁, u₂ before; v₁, v₂ after. 'Before' means before the mutual force starts and 'after' means once it has fallen to zero (Mathur 6.9). The bodies approach when u₁ > u₂.

## Momentum is conserved through the impact

Let F(t) be the force of body 1 on body 2; by Newton's third law body 2 pushes body 1 with −F(t) at every instant. Over the contact the impulses are J = ∫F dt on body 2 and −J on body 1:

$$ m₂(v₂ − u₂) = J,   m₁(v₁ − u₁) = −J $$

Adding gives m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁u₁ + m₂u₂, however complicated F(t) is (NCERT 5.11.1; Mathur 6.1: impulse equals the change in momentum). Momentum is equal at **every instant** of the contact; kinetic energy is not, because part of it is stored as deformation (NCERT, points to ponder 9). A finite external force such as gravity adds only force × (very short time), which is neglected (PH-013 bridge B2).

## The two phases

1. **Compression**: the bodies squeeze each other until their relative velocity is zero. They then share one velocity, which momentum conservation makes the CM velocity V = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/M, with M = m₁ + m₂.
2. **Restitution**: the stored energy pushes them apart, wholly or partly.

Compression impulse on body 2: J_C = m₂(V − u₂). Since V − u₂ = m₁(u₁ − u₂)/M,

$$ J_C = μ(u₁ − u₂) $$

Restitution: m₂(v₂ − V) = J_R and m₁(v₁ − V) = −J_R. Divide by m₂ and by m₁ and subtract: v₂ − v₁ = J_R(1/m₂ + 1/m₁) = J_R/μ, so J_R = μ(v₂ − v₁).

## The coefficient of restitution

Two definitions are in use. **Newton's experimental law** is the velocity form, e = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂) (PH-001 module 8); **Poisson's hypothesis** is the impulse form, e = J_R/J_C. Both say that for a given pair of surfaces the ratio

$$ e = J_R/J_C = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂) = speed of separation/speed of approach $$

is roughly constant. For smooth direct (central) impacts the two forms coincide, because both impulses carry the same factor μ; with friction or spin they can differ (see the completeness row on rough impacts). **e = 1**: elastic; **0 < e < 1**: inelastic; **e = 0**: perfectly inelastic (no restitution; both stay at V). The total impulse is J = (1 + e)μ(u₁ − u₂). Real e falls somewhat with impact speed; treat it as a model constant.

## A floor or a wall

For m₂ → ∞ at rest, μ → m₁ and V → 0, so the rebound speed is e times the arrival speed. Upadhyaya's example (extract p. 85): a 1 kg ball reaches the floor at 25 m/s and leaves at 10 m/s after 0.002 s of contact. With upward positive the impulse is 1 × [10 − (−25)] = 35 N s and the average force 35/0.002 = **17,500 N**; the extract prints 1750 N, an arithmetic slip. Here e = 10/25 = 0.40 (J_C = 25 N s, J_R = 10 N s). Gravity's impulse, 9.8 × 0.002 = 0.0196 N s, is negligible.

### 3. Core: head-on collisions for any e - the final velocities (Derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive v₁ and v₂ for a head-on collision with any e and any initial velocities, in the lab form and the CM form, and test the limits.

## Assumptions

Isolated pair (or the impulsive approximation); head-on impact of smooth bodies, so every velocity lies on the line of impact; constant e; speeds far below that of light; signs as in module 2.

## Derivation 1

Step 1 (momentum): m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂.   (1)

Step 2 (restitution, module 2): v₂ − v₁ = e(u₁ − u₂).   (2)

Step 3 (eliminate v₂). From (2), v₂ = v₁ + e(u₁ − u₂); put it into (1):

$$ m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v₁ + e m₂(u₁ − u₂) $$

$$ v₁ = [(m₁ − e m₂)u₁ + (1 + e)m₂u₂]/(m₁ + m₂) $$

Step 4 (eliminate v₁). From (2), v₁ = v₂ − e(u₁ − u₂); put it into (1):

$$ m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v₂ − e m₁(u₁ − u₂) $$

$$ v₂ = [(1 + e)m₁u₁ + (m₂ − e m₁)u₂]/(m₁ + m₂) $$

Step 5 (CM form). With u = u₁ − u₂ and V = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/M the same results read

$$ v₁ = V − e(m₂/M)u,   v₂ = V + e(m₁/M)u $$

Check: V − e m₂u/M = [m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ − e m₂u₁ + e m₂u₂]/M, which is Step 3.

## Meaning in the CM frame

Before impact the CM-frame velocities are (m₂/M)u and −(m₁/M)u (PH-011 module 2). After impact they are −e(m₂/M)u and +e(m₁/M)u: **the impact reverses each CM velocity and scales it by e.** Add V to return to the lab. This is the fastest examination route.

## Special cases (checks)

- e = 1, u₂ = 0: v₁ = (m₁ − m₂)u₁/M and v₂ = 2m₁u₁/M (PH-001 module 8; Mathur 6.10; NCERT eqs. 5.26-5.27).
- e = 1, m₁ = m₂: v₁ = u₂ and v₂ = u₁; the velocities are exchanged (Mathur case (a)).
- e = 0: v₁ = v₂ = V, perfectly inelastic (Mathur 6.10(I)).
- m₂ → ∞, u₂ = 0: v₁ → −e u₁ and v₂ → 0 (a wall).
- m₂ ≪ m₁, u₂ = 0: v₁ ≈ u₁ and v₂ ≈ (1 + e)u₁.

**Dimensions**: every term is (mass × velocity)/mass, a velocity. **Sign test**: a result with v₁ > v₂ would mean body 1 passing through body 2, so a sign or the value of e is wrong.

## Worked example

m₁ = 2 kg at u₁ = +5 m/s meets m₂ = 3 kg at u₂ = −1 m/s; e = 0.5. Then M = 5 kg, V = (10 − 3)/5 = 1.4 m/s, u = 6 m/s and μ = 6/5 = 1.2 kg.

v₁ = 1.4 − 0.5 × (3/5) × 6 = 1.4 − 1.8 = **−0.4 m/s** (body 1 rebounds); v₂ = 1.4 + 0.5 × (2/5) × 6 = 1.4 + 1.2 = **2.6 m/s**.

Checks: separation 2.6 − (−0.4) = 3.0 = 0.5 × 6; momentum 2(−0.4) + 3(2.6) = 7.0 = 10 − 3. Impulse on body 2: 3 × (2.6 + 1) = 10.8 N s = (1 + e)μu = 1.5 × 1.2 × 6, of which the compression part is μu = 7.2 N s.

### 4. Core: the kinetic energy lost, ΔK = ½μ(1 − e²)u² (Derivation 2), superelastic impacts and energy transfer

*Objective:* Derive the kinetic energy lost in a head-on collision in two ways, interpret it, extend it to superelastic impacts and find the energy given to a stationary target.

## Derivation 2

Assumptions as in module 3. Use the kinetic-energy split K = ½MV² + ½μ(relative speed)², proved in PH-009 module 4 and PH-011 module 3 (not repeated).

Step 1 (before): K_i = ½MV² + ½μu², with u = u₁ − u₂.

Step 2 (after): momentum conservation leaves V unchanged, and the relative velocity becomes v₂ − v₁ = eu (module 3), so K_f = ½MV² + ½μe²u².

Step 3 (subtract):

$$ ΔK = K_i − K_f = ½μ(1 − e²)u² $$

**Alternative (direct substitution).** With v₁ = V − e m₂u/M and v₂ = V + e m₁u/M,

$$ ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂² = ½MV² + (eVu/M)(m₂m₁ − m₁m₂) + ½e²u²(m₁m₂² + m₂m₁²)/M² $$

The middle term is zero and the last equals ½e²u² m₁m₂/M = ½μe²u², as before.

## Reading the formula

- Only the energy of relative motion, ½μu² (the CM-frame energy), can be lost; ½MV² survives every collision.
- e = 1: no loss. e = 0: loss ½μu², the largest possible (PH-009 module 9).
- Target at rest: fraction lost = (1 − e²)m₂/(m₁ + m₂); for a floor (m₂ → ∞) it is 1 − e².
- Dimensions: kg × (m/s)² = J; ΔK ≥ 0 requires e ≤ 1.
- Module 3 numbers: K_i = ½(2)(25) + ½(3)(1) = 26.5 J and K_f = ½(2)(0.16) + ½(3)(6.76) = 10.30 J, a loss of 16.2 J; the formula gives ½ × 1.2 × 0.75 × 36 = 16.2 J.

## Where the energy goes; superelastic impacts

In everyday impacts the lost kinetic energy becomes heat, sound and permanent deformation (Mathur 6.9). In an atomic collision it may raise an atom to an excited state of excitation energy x, so that final K + x = initial K; an atom that is already excited may give up x, so that the final kinetic energy exceeds the initial (Mathur). The Q value and excitation energy are defined in PH-011 module 8 with the same sign convention (Q = K_f − K_i; Q = −x for an excitation), so they are not re-derived here. For a head-on collision, ½μ(e² − 1)u² = Q, so e = √(1 + 2Q/(μu²)) and e > 1 (**superelastic**) when Q > 0. Total energy is conserved in every case; only kinetic energy changes. A negative e is impossible: the bodies would have to pass through each other.

## Energy given to a stationary target

Head-on with u₂ = 0, module 3 gives v₂ = (1 + e)m₁u₁/M, so

$$ K₂/K₁ = m₂v₂²/(m₁u₁²) = (1 + e)²m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)² $$

For e = 1 this is 4m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)², equal to 1 only for m₁ = m₂ (PH-011 module 7 adds the angle factor sin²(Θ/2)). NCERT Example 5.11 on neutron moderation: deuterium (m₂ = 2m₁) takes 8/9; carbon (m₂ = 12m₁) takes 48/169 = 0.284 and the neutron keeps 0.716. Mathur explains on the same basis why hydrogen, nearly equal in mass to the neutron, is so effective. For equal masses with e = 0.5 the target takes (1.5)²/4 = 0.5625 of the energy.

### 5. Core: measuring e with a bouncing ball - heights, distance and time

*Objective:* Relate e to rebound heights and sum the bounces to find the total distance and total time.

## Set-up

A small ball is released from rest at height h₀ above a hard floor. Assumptions: no air drag; the floor is fixed (m₂ → ∞); the same e at every bounce; upward positive; g = 9.8 m s⁻².

## Successive heights

The ball reaches the floor at v₀ = √(2gh₀). After impact n the rebound speed is vₙ = e vₙ₋₁ = eⁿv₀ (module 2, floor case), so it rises to hₙ = vₙ²/(2g) = e²ⁿh₀. Hence

$$ e = √(h₁/h₀) $$

a simple laboratory measurement: released from 1.00 m and rising to 0.64 m gives e = 0.80. Each impact keeps the fraction e² of the energy (module 4, floor limit).

## Total distance (bridge B3, ratio e²)

Down h₀, then up and down each hₙ:

$$ S = h₀ + 2h₀(e² + e⁴ + ...) = h₀ + 2h₀e²/(1 − e²) = h₀(1 + e²)/(1 − e²) $$

## Total time (ratio e)

The first fall takes t₀ = √(2h₀/g) = v₀/g. After impact n the ball goes up and comes back in 2vₙ/g = 2eⁿt₀. So

$$ T = t₀ + 2t₀(e + e² + ...) = t₀ + 2t₀e/(1 − e) = t₀(1 + e)/(1 − e) $$

## Numbers (h₀ = 2.0 m, e = 0.8)

h₁ = 0.64 × 2.0 = 1.28 m and h₂ = 0.4096 × 2.0 = 0.819 m. S = 2.0 × 1.64/0.36 = 9.11 m. t₀ = √(4.0/9.8) = 0.639 s and T = 0.639 × 1.8/0.2 = 5.75 s.

## Limits and model check

e = 1: S and T are infinite (no loss, endless bouncing). e = 0: S = h₀ and T = t₀ (the ball stops dead). The model predicts infinitely many bounces in a finite time; real balls have an e that changes at low speed and come to rest, so the series is an idealisation. The floor's impulse reverses the ball's momentum; the Earth's share of the kinetic energy is negligible because μ is practically the ball's mass.

### 6. Core: oblique impact of smooth spheres (Derivation 3) and the 2017 question

*Objective:* Solve an oblique impact by resolving along and across the line of centres, prove tan ψ = 2 tan φ/(1 − e) for equal masses, and analyse 2017 Q4(a).

## The smooth-sphere rule

When two smooth spheres collide, the contact force acts along the common normal, the **line of centres** n at the instant of contact (standard impact model, not in the supplied extracts; verify). Hence:

1. **Along n**: the head-on results of module 3 hold for the n-components, with the same e.
2. **Along the tangent t** (perpendicular to n): no impulse acts, so each sphere keeps its own t-component.

This supplies the extra information a general 2-D collision lacks (three equations for four unknowns: NCERT 5.11.3; Mathur 6.10(III)); the direction of n is fixed by how off-centre the impact is: for equal spheres of radius R whose centres move along parallel lines a distance b apart (the impact parameter), sin φ = b/(2R), so b = 0 is head-on and b → 2R is a graze.

## Derivation 3: equal masses, sphere 2 at rest

Coordinates: x along the incident velocity u of sphere 1; n at angle φ to x (0 < φ < 90°); t perpendicular to n.

Step 1 (resolve, bridge B2): u_n = u cos φ and u_t = u sin φ.

Step 2 (along n; module 3 with m₁ = m₂ and u₂ = 0): v₁n = ½(1 − e)u cos φ and v₂n = ½(1 + e)u cos φ.

Step 3 (along t): v₁t = u sin φ and v₂t = 0, so sphere 2 moves **along the line of centres**.

Step 4 (angle ψ between the final velocities). Since sphere 2 moves along n, ψ is the angle of v₁ from n:

$$ tan ψ = v₁t/v₁n = 2 tan φ/(1 − e) $$

Step 5 (checks). e = 1: tan ψ is infinite, so ψ = 90°, the right-angle rule (PH-011 module 7; NCERT Example 5.12). 0 ≤ e < 1: ψ < 90°, so an inelastic glancing collision of equal masses always opens **less** than a right angle. φ = 0: head-on, ψ = 0. Energy lost: ½(m/2)(1 − e²)(u cos φ)², which is module 4 with the normal relative speed only.

## 2017 Q4(a), 15 marks (analysis, not an official answer)

*A ball at 9 m/s strikes an identical stationary ball; afterwards each moves at 30° to the original line.*

**Speeds from momentum alone** (x along the original line; the initial y-momentum is zero, so the final y-momenta cancel and the balls must leave on opposite sides of the line):

- y: m v₁ sin 30° = m v₂ sin 30°, so v₁ = v₂ = v.
- x: 9 = 2v cos 30° = √3 v, so v = 9/√3 = 3√3 ≈ **5.20 m/s**.

**Kinetic energy**: before ½m(81) = 40.5m; after 2 × ½m(27) = 27m (joules, with m in kg). The ratio is 2/3, so kinetic energy is **not conserved**; one third is lost. Quick test: an elastic collision of equal masses with one at rest would open at 90°, not 60°.

**Smooth-sphere reading (beyond what is asked).** Sphere 2 leaves along n, so φ = 30° and ψ = 60°: √3 = (2/√3)/(1 − e), giving e = 1/3. Check: v₂ = ½(4/3)(9)(0.866) = 5.20 m/s; v₁ = √(2.598² + 4.5²) = √27 = 5.20 m/s; loss ½(m/2)(8/9)(60.75) = 13.5m = 40.5m − 27m.

### 7. Core: the ballistic pendulum, pendulum collisions and scattering as a collision without contact

*Objective:* Solve two-stage impact problems by switching laws between the impact and the motion that follows, and recognise scattering as an elastic collision.

## Ballistic pendulum (block on strings)

A bullet (mass m, speed v) embeds in a block (mass M) hanging at rest from light vertical strings; the block swings up through a height h. Two stages, two laws (PH-004 module 8 treats the rod version):

1. **Impact** (short, perfectly inelastic). The strings are vertical, so they give no horizontal impulse and horizontal momentum is conserved: mv = (m + M)V. Kinetic energy is **not** conserved.
2. **Swing**. The tension is perpendicular to the motion and does no work, so ½(m + M)V² = (m + M)gh and V = √(2gh).

$$ v = [(m + M)/m] √(2gh) $$

The fraction of kinetic energy surviving the impact is m/(m + M) (module 4 with e = 0 and the block at rest).

**Numbers**: m = 10 g, M = 2.0 kg, h = 5.0 cm. V = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.050) = √0.98 = 0.990 m/s and v = (2.010/0.010) × 0.990 = **199 m/s**. The bullet brings about 198 J; just after impact ½(2.01)(0.98) = 0.985 J remains, which is 0.50% (= 0.010/2.010). With strings 1.0 m long, cos α = 1 − h/L = 0.95, so the swing angle is α = 18.2°.

**Trap**: equating ½mv² to (m + M)gh skips the inelastic stage and gives v = √(2(m + M)gh/m) = 14.0 m/s, far too small.

## Two pendulum problems (Upadhyaya, extract p. 164)

- *Elastic*: a 1 kg bob released with its 1 m cord horizontal reaches the bottom at √(2gL) = √19.6 = 4.43 m/s and strikes a 5 kg block at rest elastically: v₁ = (1 − 5)/6 × 4.43 = −2.95 m/s (the bob rebounds) and v₂ = (2/6) × 4.43 = 1.48 m/s. Both printed values follow from rounding √(2gL) to 4.43 m/s: 4.43 × 2/3 = 2.953 and 4.43/3 = 1.477 (g = 9.81 gives 2.953 and 1.476).
- *Perfectly inelastic*: a bob m₁ released from height h sticks to a bob m₂ at rest, so V = m₁√(2gh)/(m₁ + m₂) and the pair rises h[m₁/(m₁ + m₂)]²; equal bobs rise h/4.

## Scattering: a collision without contact

Mathur (6.9) and NCERT (5.11.3) stress that colliding bodies need not touch. An alpha particle approaching a nucleus is repelled by the Coulomb force and leaves in a new direction; since the force is conservative and dies away at large distances, the kinetic energy long after equals that long before, so **Rutherford scattering is an elastic collision**. With the nucleus treated as fixed, angular momentum about it is conserved and the path is a hyperbola (Mathur 6.17(ii)); impact parameter and cross-section are taught in PH-010.

Head-on (impact parameter zero) the alpha particle stops where all its kinetic energy E has become potential energy, at the distance of closest approach d = 2Zq_e²/(4πε₀E) (derived in PH-010 module 6; the nucleus is treated as fixed, so recoil is ignored, and the centre-of-mass energy correction is about 2 percent for gold), with q_e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (written q_e because e is the coefficient of restitution here). Upadhyaya's example: 4 MeV on gold (Z = 79) gives d = 5.69 × 10⁻¹⁴ m (extract p. 150). A worked example on the previous page labels copper Z = 32; copper's atomic number is 29, so treat that label with care: with Z = 29 and a 5 MeV alpha particle, d = 1.67 × 10⁻¹⁴ m (the printed Z = 32 would give 1.84 × 10⁻¹⁴ m).

### 8. Master: two-dimensional elastic collisions - lab and CM frames, the 90° rule and the maximum deflection (recap map)

*Objective:* Quote the 2-D elastic results with their conditions, know where each is derived, and use the lab-frame discriminant check and the 2-D sticking result.

## Why a map, not a new derivation

In the lab frame two momentum equations and one energy equation govern the four unknowns v₁, v₂, θ, φ, so one quantity, usually an angle, must be given (NCERT 5.11.3; Mathur 6.10(III)); module 6 showed how smooth-sphere geometry supplies it. The general results are derived in PH-011.

## The CM picture (derived in PH-011 module 4)

The CM moves at V = m₁u/M. In the CM frame the incoming velocities are m₂u/M and −m₁u/M; an elastic collision keeps both speeds and turns both velocities through one angle Θ, which can be anything from 0 to 180° (Mathur 6.10-6.11). Adding V back gives the lab angles

$$ tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + m₁/m₂),   φ = (π − Θ)/2 $$

(component proof in PH-011 module 5).

## Results to quote

| Situation | Result | Derived in |
|---|---|---|
| m₁ = m₂, target at rest | θ + φ = 90° (or the projectile stops) | PH-001 m8, PH-011 m7 |
| m₁ > m₂ | sin θ_max = m₂/m₁, reached when Θ − θ = 90° | PH-011 m5-6 |
| m₁ = m₂ | θ = Θ/2, always below 90° | PH-011 m5 |
| m₁ < m₂ | any θ up to 180°: the projectile can rebound | Mathur 6.11 |
| energy to the target | K₂/K = 4m₁m₂ sin²(Θ/2)/M² | PH-011 m7 |

## Lab-frame route to θ_max

The 2022 question is posed in lab angles. Eliminating φ and v₂ from the three equations gives (PH-011 module 6)

$$ (m₁ + m₂)v₁² − 2m₁uv₁ cos θ + (m₁ − m₂)u² = 0 $$

A real v₁ needs a non-negative discriminant: m₁²cos²θ − (m₁² − m₂²) ≥ 0, that is m₂² ≥ m₁² sin²θ, or sin θ ≤ m₂/m₁. Example: m₁ = 2m₂ gives θ_max = 30°.

## Perfectly inelastic in two dimensions

If the bodies stick (all relative motion destroyed), the common velocity is the vector V = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/M and the loss is ½μ|u₁ − u₂|². Example: a 1000 kg car at 20 m/s east locks with a 1500 kg car at 10 m/s north. V = (20,000, 15,000)/2500 = (8, 6) m/s, that is 10 m/s at 36.9° north of east. K_i = 200,000 + 75,000 = 275,000 J and K_f = ½(2500)(100) = 125,000 J, so 150,000 J is lost; this equals ½μ|u₁ − u₂|² = ½(600)(500).

## Problem 1: head-on with e

*A 4 kg trolley at 6 m/s hits a 2 kg trolley at rest; e = 0.5. Find the velocities and the energy lost.*

Thinking: momentum always holds; e supplies the second equation; the CM form is fastest. V = 24/6 = 4 m/s and u = 6 m/s. v₁ = 4 − 0.5 × (2/6) × 6 = 3 m/s and v₂ = 4 + 0.5 × (4/6) × 6 = 6 m/s. Energy: 72 J before and ½(4)(9) + ½(2)(36) = 54 J after, so 18 J is lost, equal to ½ × (4/3) × 0.75 × 36. Separation check: 6 − 3 = 3 = 0.5 × 6.

## Problem 2: oblique impact

*Identical smooth spheres; sphere 1 at 6 m/s; the line of centres makes 45° with its path; sphere 2 at rest; e = 0.5.*

Thinking: resolve along n and t; 1-D along n; t unchanged. u_n = u_t = 4.243 m/s. Sphere 2: 0.75 × 4.243 = 3.18 m/s along n. Sphere 1: v₁n = 0.25 × 4.243 = 1.061 m/s and v₁t = 4.243 m/s, so |v₁| = √19.125 = 4.37 m/s and tan ψ = 4, ψ = 76.0°. In x-y components v₁ = (3.75, 2.25) m/s, at 31.0° to x, and v₂ = (2.25, −2.25) m/s: momentum per kilogram is 6.0 along x and 0 along y, as before. Energy per kilogram falls from 18 J to 14.625 J; the loss of 3.375 J equals ½ × 0.5 × 0.75 × 18.

## Problem 3: impact, then a spring

*A 0.2 kg clay ball at 10 m/s hits and sticks to a 0.8 kg block at rest on a smooth floor; the block is attached to a wall by a light spring (k = 500 N/m) at its natural length, and the clay moves towards the wall along the spring axis. Find the maximum compression.*

Thinking: two stages. Impact: the spring force is finite, so during the short impact momentum of ball and block is conserved: V = 2/1.0 = 2 m/s; the kinetic energy falls from 10 J to 2 J (80% lost, the fraction m₂/M). Compression: the floor is smooth, so the mechanical energy of block, ball and spring is conserved: ½(1.0)(2²) = ½(500)x², so x = √0.008 = 0.0894 m = 8.9 cm. During the compression momentum is **not** conserved, because the spring, fixed to the wall, exerts an external force.

### Notes: Definitions

- Collision: a short, strong interaction between two bodies that changes their velocities abruptly; before and after it they move freely; contact is not required (scattering).
- Elastic collision: total kinetic energy after equals that before (e = 1). Inelastic: kinetic energy changes (0 < e < 1). Perfectly inelastic: bodies move off together (e = 0).
- Superelastic collision: kinetic energy increases because internal energy is released (e > 1).
- Line of impact: the common normal at contact; for spheres the line of centres. Head-on (direct): velocities along it; oblique: not.
- Impulse J = ∫F dt (N s) equals the change in momentum.
- Compression impulse J_C = μ(u₁ − u₂); restitution impulse J_R = μ(v₂ − v₁); coefficient of restitution e = J_R/J_C = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂).
- Reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂); CM velocity V = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂).

### Notes: Governing equations

- Momentum: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ (every collision of an isolated pair).
- Restitution: v₂ − v₁ = e(u₁ − u₂).
- v₁ = [(m₁ − e m₂)u₁ + (1 + e)m₂u₂]/M = V − e(m₂/M)u;  v₂ = [(1 + e)m₁u₁ + (m₂ − e m₁)u₂]/M = V + e(m₁/M)u, with u = u₁ − u₂.
- Energy lost: ΔK = ½μ(1 − e²)u²; target at rest: fraction (1 − e²)m₂/M; floor: 1 − e².
- Energy transferred head-on to a stationary target: K₂/K₁ = (1 + e)²m₁m₂/M²; maximum 4m₁m₂/M² (e = 1).
- Bouncing ball: hₙ = e²ⁿh₀, e = √(h₁/h₀), S = h₀(1 + e²)/(1 − e²), T = t₀(1 + e)/(1 − e).
- Equal smooth spheres, one at rest: tan ψ = 2 tan φ/(1 − e).
- Ballistic pendulum: v = [(m + M)/m]√(2gh).
- 2-D elastic: tan θ = sin Θ/(cos Θ + m₁/m₂); φ = (π − Θ)/2; sin θ_max = m₂/m₁ (m₁ > m₂).

### Notes: Derivation chain to write closed-book

- 1. Impulses ±J on the two bodies give momentum conservation; at maximum compression both move at V; J_C = μ(u₁ − u₂), J_R = μ(v₂ − v₁); e = J_R/J_C.
- 2. Substitute v₂ = v₁ + e(u₁ − u₂) into momentum for v₁; substitute v₁ = v₂ − e(u₁ − u₂) for v₂; rewrite as V ∓ e(m/M)u.
- 3. K = ½MV² + ½μv_rel²; V unchanged and v_rel → e u; ΔK = ½μ(1 − e²)u².
- 4. Oblique: resolve along n and t; apply step 2 along n; keep t; equal masses give tan ψ = 2 tan φ/(1 − e).
- 5. Bouncing: vₙ = eⁿv₀, hₙ = e²ⁿh₀; sum two geometric series for S and T.

### Notes: Types of collision compared

- Momentum is conserved in every row; only the kinetic energy and the relative velocity after the impact differ.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Momentum is equal at every instant of the contact; kinetic energy is equal only before and after an elastic collision.
- Speed of approach uses signed velocities, u₁ − u₂; it is not the difference of the two speeds when the bodies move towards each other.
- The loss ½μ(1 − e²)u² uses the reduced mass and the relative speed, not the total mass and u₁.
- Fraction lost for a free target (1 − e²)m₂/M versus a fixed wall 1 − e².
- A bouncing ball's own momentum is not conserved at the floor; the ball-plus-Earth momentum is.
- The equal-mass right angle needs an elastic collision and a target at rest; an inelastic one opens at less than 90°.
- e is a property of the pair of surfaces in the impact, not of one body alone.
- Lab angle θ versus CM angle Θ (PH-011).

### Notes: Exceptions and limits of the model

- A constant e is a model: real e depends on the impact speed and the materials.
- The smooth-sphere model ignores friction at the contact; rough spheres also exchange tangential impulse and spin (not taught; impulsive rotation is in PH-013).
- The impulsive approximation needs a short contact; finite forces such as gravity are neglected only during the contact.
- If a support (hinge, wall-mounted spring, non-vertical string) acts during the impact, linear momentum is not conserved (PH-004 module 8, PH-013 module 3).
- All results are non-relativistic; at relativistic speeds relativistic momentum must be used (Mathur 6.1).

  - Treating every collision as elastic.
  - Unsigned velocities in one dimension.
  - Total mass instead of reduced mass in the energy loss.
  - Applying e to the full velocity in an oblique impact.
  - Energy conservation through a perfectly inelastic impact.
  - Quoting sin θ_max = m₂/m₁ for every mass ratio.

### Practice

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Define the coefficient of restitution in two equivalent ways.
    - **a:** e = (speed of separation)/(speed of approach) = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂) along the line of impact; equivalently e = J_R/J_C, the restitution impulse divided by the compression impulse.
  - - **q:** What is conserved in every collision of an isolated pair, and what only in an elastic one?
    - **a:** Linear momentum always; total kinetic energy only when e = 1. Total energy in all forms is always conserved.
  - - **q:** What is the line of impact for two spheres?
    - **a:** The line joining their centres at the instant of contact; for smooth spheres the contact force acts along it.
  - - **q:** Give the value of e for elastic, perfectly inelastic and ordinary inelastic collisions.
    - **a:** 1, 0, and between 0 and 1 respectively.
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why does the kinetic energy of the centre of mass, ½MV², survive every collision?
    - **a:** Momentum conservation fixes V, so ½MV² is the same before and after; only the relative-motion energy ½μu² can change.
  - - **q:** Why do both bodies have the velocity V at maximum compression?
    - **a:** At that instant the relative velocity is zero, so they share one velocity, and momentum conservation makes it (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/M = V.
  - - **q:** Why does the second sphere in a smooth oblique impact move along the line of centres if it starts at rest?
    - **a:** The only impulse on it is the contact impulse, which for smooth spheres acts along the line of centres; with no initial velocity, its final velocity lies along that line.
  - - **q:** Is e > 1 possible? Is e < 0?
    - **a:** e > 1 is possible when internal energy is released, so kinetic energy increases (superelastic). e < 0 is impossible, since the bodies cannot pass through each other.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A 0.15 kg ball moving at 12 m/s is hit straight back at 20 m/s by a bat in 0.001 s. Find the impulse and the average force.
    - **a:** Taking the final direction positive: impulse = 0.15 × [20 − (−12)] = 4.8 N s; average force 4.8/0.001 = 4800 N (Upadhyaya, extract p. 85).
  - - **q:** Two equal masses collide head-on with velocities +6 m/s and −2 m/s; e = 0.5. Find the final velocities.
    - **a:** V = (6 − 2)/2 = 2 m/s and u = 8 m/s, so v₁ = 2 − 0.5 × 0.5 × 8 = 0 and v₂ = 2 + 2 = 4 m/s; check: separation 4 = 0.5 × 8 and momentum 4 = 6 − 2 (per unit mass).
  - - **q:** A 1000 kg car at 20 m/s east locks with a 1500 kg car at 10 m/s north. Find the common velocity and the energy lost.
    - **a:** V = (8, 6) m/s, that is 10 m/s at 36.9° north of east; the loss is 150 kJ = ½ × 600 × 500.

    - **task:** Define the coefficient of restitution through the compression and restitution impulses, show that it equals (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂), and derive the final velocities of a head-on collision of masses m₁ and m₂ for any e. Timed written solution target: 12 minutes for a 10-mark answer (practice target, not an official allotment).

      - Figure and conventions: x along the line of impact, positive in the direction of u₁; body 1 behind body 2; velocities signed; approach requires u₁ > u₂.

      - Compression ends when the relative velocity is zero, with both bodies at V = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/M; J_C = m₂(V − u₂) = μ(u₁ − u₂), because V − u₂ = m₁(u₁ − u₂)/M.

      - Define e = J_R/J_C; then e = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂) (Newton's experimental law).

    - **checks:**
      - Dimensions: a velocity on both sides.
      - Limits: e = 1, u₂ = 0 gives (m₁ − m₂)u₁/M and 2m₁u₁/M; e = 0 gives V for both bodies; m₂ → ∞ gives v₁ = −e u₁.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-PH-MOMCONS (verified):** The total momentum of a closed system is conserved: the net external force on the system must be zero.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 9.3 'Conservation of Linear Momentum'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/9-3-conservation-of-linear-momentum)
- **F-PH-ELASTIC (verified):** In an elastic collision the objects bounce off each other and both momentum and kinetic energy of the system are the same before and after.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 9.4 'Types of Collisions'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/9-4-types-of-collisions)
- **F-PH-INELASTIC (verified):** In an inelastic collision the system loses kinetic energy; momentum is conserved but kinetic energy decreases.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 9.4 'Types of Collisions'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/9-4-types-of-collisions)
- **F-PH-PERFINEL (verified):** Any collision in which the objects stick together is perfectly inelastic and gives the maximum loss of kinetic energy; momentum is still conserved.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 9.4 'Types of Collisions'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/9-4-types-of-collisions)

### Memory skeleton

- Momentum conserved in every collision of an isolated pair, at every instant of contact
- Compression ends at the common velocity V; e = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂)
- Head-on, any e: v₁ = V − e(m₂/M)u, v₂ = V + e(m₁/M)u
- Energy lost ΔK = ½μ(1 − e²)u²; superelastic e > 1
- Energy transfer (1 + e)²m₁m₂/M²; maximum 4m₁m₂/M²
- Ballistic pendulum: momentum through impact, energy in the swing
- 2-D elastic map: tan θ, φ = (π − Θ)/2, θ_max (from PH-011)

### Last-week summary

- e = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂), signed velocities: 1 elastic, 0 perfectly inelastic, > 1 superelastic.
- v₁ = V − e(m₂/M)u, v₂ = V + e(m₁/M)u, u = u₁ − u₂; e = 1, equal masses: velocities exchange.
- ΔK = ½μ(1 − e²)u²: only relative-motion energy is lost; target at rest fraction (1 − e²)m₂/M, wall 1 − e².
- Ballistic pendulum v = [(m + M)/m]√(2gh); energy kept through impact m/(m + M).
