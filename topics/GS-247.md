## GS-247: Crop seasons and major crop zones

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS3 · Economy · Agriculture and food

*Goal:* Explain Crop seasons and major crop zones and apply the distinction in the worked example.

### Three seasons and their logic

India's farm year is organised around three seasons. Kharif begins with the arrival of the south-west monsoon and is harvested around September–October, so it suits tropical crops needing heat and water such as paddy, cotton, jute, jowar, bajra and tur. Rabi is sown as winter sets in, roughly October to December, and harvested in spring or early summer; the cooler weather favours temperate and subtropical crops like wheat, gram, barley and mustard. Between rabi harvest and the next monsoon lies zaid, a short summer window used on irrigated land for watermelon, cucumber, vegetables and fodder. Sugarcane does not fit any one season because it takes nearly a year to mature.

### Regional twists that examiners exploit

Season labels change with geography. In the north-west, winter showers brought by western temperate cyclones, along with Green Revolution irrigation across Punjab, Haryana, western UP and parts of Rajasthan, made the region the core of wheat and other rabi crops. In eastern India, Assam, West Bengal and Odisha grow three rice crops — Aus, Aman and Boro. In the south, temperatures stay high enough for tropical crops all year, so rice, maize, ragi, jowar and groundnut appear in both kharif and rabi columns, and a crop can be repeated three times if soil moisture lasts. Jowar is a two-season crop in the south but a kharif fodder crop in the north.

### Crop zones: matching crops to climate and soil

Major crop zones follow climatic requirements. Rice needs temperatures above 25°C, high humidity and over 100 cm of rain, yet canals and tubewells have pushed it into drier Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh. Wheat has two main belts: the Ganga–Satluj plains in the north-west and the black soil Deccan. Bajra tolerates sandy and shallow black soils of Rajasthan and Gujarat, while ragi grows in dry regions on red, black and sandy soils. Maize prefers 21–27°C and old alluvium. So a cropping pattern reflects both natural endowment and man-made irrigation, which is why the pattern shifts when irrigation expands.
