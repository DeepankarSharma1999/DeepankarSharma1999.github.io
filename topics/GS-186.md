## GS-186: Supreme Court: jurisdiction and review

Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2 · Polity · Union government

### Bridge: Court, case, judgment, jurisdiction, appeal, writ

A **court** is a public body that settles disputes by applying law, not by applying the wishes of whoever is more powerful. A **case** is one dispute that has been brought to a court; the person who brings it is the **petitioner** (or plaintiff), the other side is the **respondent** (or defendant). When the court decides, its reasoned decision is a **judgment**; the operative command inside it (pay this, release him, stop that) is the **order** or **decree**.

**Jurisdiction** is simply the legal power of a court to hear a particular kind of case. If a court has no jurisdiction over a matter, it cannot decide it, however just its decision might be. Jurisdiction is **original** when a case starts in that court, and **appellate** when the court re-examines a decision already made by a lower court. An **appeal**, then, is a request to a higher court to correct a lower court's decision.

A **writ** is a formal written command issued by a superior court ordering someone (usually a public authority or a lower court) to do or stop doing something. The five writ names you will meet - habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto - are Latin labels inherited from English law; each is explained in module 5.

Finally, **constitutional** means 'found in or derived from the Constitution', while **statutory** means 'created by an ordinary law (a statute) passed by Parliament or a State legislature'. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction is constitutional (Articles 124-147), but Parliament can add to it by statute, for example by widening its criminal appellate jurisdiction as it did in 1970.

### Bridge: Levels of courts in India

India has **one** ladder of courts for the whole country, not separate ladders for the Union and for each State. At the top sits the **Supreme Court of India** in Delhi. Below it, each State (or group of States) has a **High Court**. Below each High Court are the **district courts** (headed by a District Judge who hears serious civil and criminal matters) and, under them, the **subordinate courts** such as civil judges' courts and magistrates' courts.

The NCERT chapter calls this structure **pyramidal**: the Supreme Court's decisions bind every court below; it can move cases from any court to itself and from one High Court to another; High Courts supervise the courts below them and hear appeals from them; district courts hear appeals from the lowest courts. Because the same ladder enforces both Union laws and State laws, a single judgment of the Supreme Court settles the law for the whole country.

Contrast the **United States**, where federal courts enforce federal law and each State's courts enforce that State's law - a **double system**. Laxmikanth notes that India adopted its single system from the Government of India Act, 1935. Keep this image in mind: whenever a question says 'binding on all courts' (Article 141) or 'act in aid of the Supreme Court' (Article 144), it is describing the glue that holds the pyramid together.

### Bridge: Reading Article numbers: Part V, Articles 124-147, clause 124(4)

The Constitution of India is divided into **Parts** (large sections such as Part III on Fundamental Rights and Part V on the Union). Each Part contains numbered **Articles**, each dealing with one subject. Inside an Article there may be numbered **clauses**, written in brackets. So **Article 124(4)** means clause 4 of Article 124 - the clause that lays down how a Supreme Court judge may be removed.

When the Constitution was amended and a new Article had to be squeezed between two existing numbers, a letter was added: **Article 124A** (the National Judicial Appointments Commission, inserted by the 99th Amendment in 2014-15) sits between 124 and 125. When an Article is deleted, the number stays with the word **(Repealed)**, as with Article 131A.

The Supreme Court chapter of the Constitution runs from **Article 124 to Article 147** in Part V. A range such as 'Articles 132-136' means all the Articles from 132 to 136 inclusive - here the appellate jurisdiction Articles. You do not need to memorise all twenty-four Articles on Day 1; this lesson will keep returning to about twelve of them (124, 129, 131, 132, 133, 134, 136, 137, 141, 142, 143, 145), and Article 32 and Article 226 from elsewhere in the Constitution.

### 1. Why an independent judiciary, and why one integrated court system

*Objective:* Explain the purpose of an independent judiciary and the meaning of 'integrated judiciary', and place the Supreme Court in its constitutional and historical setting.

## Why do we need an independent judiciary?

The NCERT chapter begins with a simple observation: in any society disputes arise - between individuals, between groups, and between citizens and the government. Someone must settle them according to **rule of law**, the idea that the same law applies to the rich and the poor, to the minister and the clerk. The judiciary's principal job is to protect rule of law: it safeguards individual rights, settles disputes according to law and makes sure that democracy does not slide into the dictatorship of a person or a group.

It can do this only if it is **independent**: the legislature and executive must not restrain its functioning, must not interfere with its decisions, and judges must be able to decide 'without fear or favour'. NCERT adds an important caution you will use in Mains: independence does **not** mean arbitrariness or absence of accountability. The judiciary is accountable to the Constitution, to democratic traditions and to the people.

## What is the Supreme Court?

The Supreme Court of India was inaugurated on **28 January 1950**. It replaced the **Federal Court of India** (created under the Government of India Act, 1935) and also took over the role of the **British Privy Council**, which until 1950 heard final appeals from India. Its jurisdiction is therefore wider than its predecessor's. **Articles 124 to 147 in Part V** govern its organisation, independence, jurisdiction, powers and procedure, and Parliament is also authorised to regulate these matters.

## 'Integrated judiciary' explained

Laxmikanth opens the chapter with a contrast. The USA has a **double system**: federal courts enforce federal laws, State courts enforce State laws. India, although federal, has a **single integrated system**: one pyramid of courts (Supreme Court, High Courts, subordinate courts) that enforces both Central and State laws. This design came from the 1935 Act. Consequences you will meet later: the Supreme Court's law binds every court (Article 141), it can transfer cases between High Courts, and it exercises superintendence over all courts and tribunals.

## Four roles in one sentence

Laxmikanth summarises the Court as a **federal court** (umpire between Centre and States), the **highest court of appeal**, the **guarantor of Fundamental Rights** and the **guardian of the Constitution**. Every later module is one of these roles seen up close.

### 2. Composition and appointment: Article 124 and the collegium story

*Objective:* State the Court's strength and appointment rule, and narrate how 'consultation' became the collegium through the Judges cases and the NJAC episode.

## Strength (Article 124)

The Court today has **34 judges** - the Chief Justice of India (CJI) and 33 others. The original strength in 1950 was 8 (CJI plus 7). Parliament has raised the number of other judges step by step: 10 (1956), 13 (1960), 17 (1977), 25 (1986), 30 (2008) and 33 by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019. Note the mechanism: the Constitution fixes a base and lets **Parliament** change the number by ordinary law.

## Who appoints

Judges are appointed by the **President**. The CJI is appointed after consulting such Supreme Court and High Court judges as the President thinks necessary; other judges after consulting the CJI and such other judges as he thinks necessary. Consultation with the CJI is **obligatory** for appointing any judge other than the CJI. Because the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers, NCERT observes that originally the final say in practice rested with the executive.

## The meaning of 'consultation'

The whole collegium debate is about one word.

- **First Judges case (1982)**: consultation means only an exchange of views, not concurrence. Executive primacy.
- **Second Judges case (1993)**: reversed; consultation means **concurrence**. The CJI's advice binds the President, but the CJI must first consult two senior-most colleagues. The same judgment ruled that the senior-most judge alone should become CJI.
- **Third Judges case (1998)**: a presidential reference under Article 143. The CJI's sole opinion is not 'consultation'; he must consult a **collegium of the CJI plus the four senior-most judges (five members)**, and if two give an adverse opinion the recommendation should not go forward. A recommendation made without following these norms does not bind the government.

NCERT describes the result as the principle of **collegiality**: the group of senior judges now carries the greater weight in appointments.

## NJAC and the Fourth Judges case

The **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** and the **National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014** replaced the collegium with the NJAC (Articles 124A-124C). In **2015** the Supreme Court struck down both as unconstitutional and void (Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India, the Fourth Judges case), holding that the new system would affect the independence of the judiciary. The collegium therefore revived.

## The CJI seniority convention

From 1950 to 1973 the senior-most judge became CJI. The convention was broken in **1973** (A.N. Ray superseded three judges, after Kesavananda Bharati) and **1977** (M.H. Beg superseded H.R. Khanna, the ADM Jabalpur dissenter). The Second Judges case closed this door by requiring seniority.

### 3. Qualifications, oath, salary, tenure, removal and temporary judges

*Objective:* Reproduce the qualification list, the Article 124(4) removal rule and the Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968 steps, and distinguish acting, ad hoc and retired judges.

## Qualifications

A Supreme Court judge must be a **citizen of India** and either (a) a High Court judge for five years, or (b) a High Court advocate for ten years, or (c) a **distinguished jurist** in the opinion of the President. There is **no minimum age**. Trap: no distinguished jurist has so far been appointed, but the route exists.

## Oath and salary

The oath is taken before the President (or a person appointed by him): allegiance to the Constitution, upholding sovereignty and integrity, performing duties without fear or favour, and upholding the Constitution and laws. Salaries and allowances are fixed by **Parliament** and cannot be reduced after appointment **except during a Financial Emergency**. They are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (not votable). Retired judges draw 50 per cent of last salary as pension.

## Tenure

No fixed term. A judge holds office until **65 years**, may resign in writing to the President, or may be removed by the President on Parliament's recommendation.

## Removal: Article 124(4) plus the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

The Constitution gives only the skeleton: removal by presidential order after an **address by each House** passed by a **special majority** (majority of total membership AND two-thirds of members present and voting) in the same session, on the grounds of **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**. The Constitution does **not** define these terms. The procedure is in the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**:

1. Motion signed by **100 Lok Sabha** or **50 Rajya Sabha** members given to the Speaker/Chairman.
2. Speaker/Chairman **may admit or refuse** it.
3. If admitted, a **three-member committee** is set up: a Supreme Court judge (or the CJI), a High Court Chief Justice and a distinguished jurist.
4. If the committee finds the judge guilty, the House considers the motion.
5. Both Houses pass it by special majority; address goes to the President; President removes.

No Supreme Court judge has ever been removed. In the **V. Ramaswami case (1991-93)** the committee found misbehaviour, but the Lok Sabha motion failed because the Congress abstained: it had two-thirds of those voting but not a majority of total membership. A removal motion does not lapse on dissolution of the Lok Sabha.

## Acting, ad hoc and retired judges

- **Acting CJI** (Article 126): appointed by the President when the office is vacant or the CJI is absent or unable to act.
- **Ad hoc judge** (Article 127): when quorum is short, the CJI may appoint a qualified **High Court judge** for a temporary period, after consulting that High Court's Chief Justice and with the President's prior consent.
- **Retired judge** (Article 128): the CJI may request a retired Supreme Court judge, or a retired High Court judge qualified for the Supreme Court, to sit temporarily - with the President's prior consent **and the consent of the person**. He is not otherwise deemed a judge of the Court.

## Seat and benches

Delhi is the seat; the CJI may, with the President's approval, appoint other places (optional, cannot be compelled by any court). Constitutional cases and Article 143 references need a bench of **at least five judges**.

### 4. Independence safeguards and the accountability counterweight

*Objective:* List the constitutional provisions that protect the Court's independence and pair each with the accountability question it raises.

## Why independence matters here

Laxmikanth explains that because the Court is a federal court, highest appellate court, guarantor of rights and guardian of the Constitution, it must be free from executive and legislative pressure. The Constitution builds this freedom into ten provisions. Learn them as a list; Mains answers on 'constitutionally guaranteed judicial independence' (PYQ 2023) are built from exactly these.

## The ten safeguards

1. **Mode of appointment** - by the President in consultation with the judiciary itself, curbing executive discretion.
2. **Security of tenure** - removal only in the manner and on the grounds in the Constitution; judges do not hold office at the President's pleasure.
3. **Fixed service conditions** - salary cannot be cut after appointment except in a Financial Emergency.
4. **Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** - Parliament may discuss but cannot vote them down.
5. **Conduct cannot be discussed** in Parliament or a State legislature except on a removal motion.
6. **Ban on practice after retirement** - no pleading before any court or authority in India.
7. **Power to punish for contempt** - protects its authority and dignity.
8. **Freedom to appoint its own staff** - the CJI appoints officers and servants and fixes their service conditions.
9. **Jurisdiction cannot be curtailed** by Parliament, though Parliament may extend it.
10. **Separation from the executive** - Article 50 (a Directive Principle) directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services; the Criminal Procedure Code 1973 gave this effect (the CrPC has since been replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, in force from 1 July 2024; the Article 50 principle stands, but verify that the corresponding provisions carry over).

NCERT condenses the same idea: the legislature is kept out of appointments, judges have fixed tenure and a very difficult removal procedure, the judiciary is not financially dependent on the other organs, and judges are shielded from personal criticism through contempt power and the bar on discussing their conduct.

## The counterweight: accountability

Each shield raises a question an examiner may put to you. If judges appoint judges, who checks the appointers? If conduct cannot be debated and contempt silences critics, how is misconduct exposed? If removal has never succeeded, is the procedure a guarantee or a loophole? NCERT's answer is that independence 'does not imply arbitrariness or absence of accountability'; the judiciary answers to the Constitution, to democratic traditions and to the people. Keep this sentence; it is the hinge of every independence-versus-accountability answer in module 8.

### 5. Original jurisdiction (Article 131) and writ jurisdiction (Article 32)

*Objective:* Distinguish the exclusive federal original jurisdiction from the concurrent writ jurisdiction, list the exclusions, and explain each of the five writs.

## Original jurisdiction - the federal umpire (Article 131)

As a federal court the Supreme Court decides disputes (a) between the Centre and one or more States, (b) between the Centre and some States on one side and other States on the other, and (c) between two or more States. This jurisdiction is **exclusive** (no other court can hear such disputes) and **original** (heard in the first instance, not on appeal). Two conditions: the dispute must involve a **question of law or fact on which a legal right depends** - purely political questions are excluded - and a **private citizen** cannot sue the Centre or a State under this Article.

**Exclusions** (learn these; they are MCQ material): disputes from pre-Constitution treaties, covenants or sanads; disputes from agreements that themselves exclude this jurisdiction; **inter-State water disputes** (excluded by the Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956); matters referred to the **Finance Commission**; adjustment of certain expenses and pensions between Centre and States; ordinary commercial disputes between Centre and States; recovery of damages by a State against the Centre.

First suit: **West Bengal v Union of India (1961)**, challenging the Coal Bearing Areas (Acquisition and Development) Act, 1957; the Court upheld the Act.

*Open question (verify).* Decisions of the Court are reported to differ on how far Article 131 reaches when the real claimant or defendant is a State instrumentality or a private party rather than a government itself (compare State of Madhya Pradesh v Union of India, 2011, with State of Jharkhand v State of Bihar, 2015). Treat the scope of 'dispute between governments involving a legal right' as unsettled in detail, and check both judgments before relying on either.

## Writ jurisdiction - the guarantor of rights (Article 32)

An aggrieved person may move the Supreme Court **directly** for enforcement of Fundamental Rights, so this too is original jurisdiction. But it is **not exclusive**: High Courts also issue writs (**Article 226**). Two differences to memorise:

| Feature | Article 131 (federal) | Article 32 (writs) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Exclusive | Concurrent with High Courts |
| Parties | Units of the federation | Citizen versus government |
| Scope | Legal rights of federal units | Fundamental Rights only |

The High Court's writ power is **wider**: it issues writs for Fundamental Rights **and for any other purpose**; the Supreme Court issues them only for Fundamental Rights, though Parliament may confer the wider power (Article 139).

## The five writs (standard meanings; names from the sources)

- **Habeas corpus** ('have the body'): orders a detaining authority to produce the detained person and justify the detention; releases if unlawful.
- **Mandamus** ('we command'): orders a public authority to perform a public duty it has failed to perform. It does not lie against a private body unless that body is performing a public duty.
- **Prohibition** ('to forbid'): issued to a **lower court or tribunal** to stop proceedings that exceed its jurisdiction - preventive, before the decision.
- **Certiorari** ('to be certified'): issued to a lower court or tribunal to **quash** an order already made for excess of jurisdiction or error of law - curative, after the decision.
- **Quo warranto** ('by what authority'): questions a person's legal title to hold a **public office**; any interested person may seek it, not only the aggrieved.

### 6. Appellate, advisory, court of record, review, curative, transfer, Articles 141 and 142

*Objective:* Map Articles 129, 132-137, 139A, 141, 142 and 143 to their functions with the conditions attached to each.

## Appellate jurisdiction (Articles 132-136)

The Court is primarily a court of appeal, successor to the Federal Court and the Privy Council. Four heads:

- **Constitutional (Article 132)**: appeal from a High Court judgment if the High Court **certifies** that the case involves a substantial question of law requiring interpretation of the Constitution.
- **Civil (Article 133)**: appeal if the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law of **general importance** and that it needs to be decided by the Supreme Court. The old Rs 20,000 monetary limit was removed by the **30th Amendment, 1972**.
- **Criminal (Article 134)**: appeal lies **as of right** (no certificate) if the High Court on appeal reversed an acquittal and sentenced the accused to **death**, or withdrew a case from a subordinate court and sentenced him to death; otherwise if the High Court certifies the case fit. Parliament in **1970** extended the right to sentences of **life or ten years**. If the High Court reverses a conviction and acquits, there is no appeal as of right.
- **Special leave (Article 136)**: the Court may, **in its discretion**, grant leave to appeal from any judgment of **any court or tribunal** (except military courts), in any matter, final or interlocutory. It cannot be claimed as a right; the Court itself has said it must be used sparingly in extraordinary situations. This plenary power has no American parallel.

## Advisory jurisdiction (Article 143)

The **President** may refer (a) any question of law or fact of public importance - the Court **may** decline; (b) disputes from pre-Constitution treaties and sanads - the Court **must** opine. In both cases the opinion is **advisory, not binding** on the President. Fifteen references had been made up to 2019, from the Delhi Laws Act (1951) through Berubari (1960), Keshav Singh (1964), Cauvery (1992), Ram Janmabhoomi (1993), the 1998 Judges consultation reference, to the 2G natural-resources reference (2012). *Dated note (verify against the Court's opinion).* After the Court's April 2025 judgment in State of Tamil Nadu v Governor on the Governor's and President's handling of State Bills, the President was reported to have made a further Article 143 reference on whether timelines can be imposed for assent to State Bills; the Court's opinion was reported in November 2025. The count of 'fifteen references up to 2019' is therefore out of date as a total; confirm the outcome and the current count before using either in an answer. NCERT's point: the government gets an authoritative legal opinion before acting, avoiding later litigation.

## Court of record (Article 129)

Two powers: its records are of evidentiary value and serve as precedent; and it can **punish for contempt** - simple imprisonment up to six months or fine up to Rs 2,000 or both. Since 1991 it has held it can punish contempt of High Courts, subordinate courts and tribunals too. **Civil contempt** is wilful disobedience of a court order or breach of an undertaking; **criminal contempt** is publication or conduct that scandalises a court, prejudices a proceeding or obstructs justice. Fair reporting, fair criticism of judicial acts and comment on the administrative side are **not** contempt. These definitions are statutory (Contempt of Courts Act, 1971), not constitutional. Parliament's power to legislate on contempt comes from the Seventh Schedule: List I Entry 77 covers the constitution, organisation, jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court including contempt of that Court, and List III Entry 14 covers contempt of court other than contempt of the Supreme Court (standard knowledge; verify; this is the 2022 Prelims Q11 point). High Courts are likewise courts of record (Article 215) and are accepted to have power to review their own judgments for error apparent on the record, as the 2021 Prelims Q88 statement asks (standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify).

## Review (Article 137) and curative petitions

The Court may **review its own judgment** - it is 'a self-correcting agency' not bound by its earlier decisions (Kesavananda 1973 departed from Golak Nath 1967). After review fails, a **curative petition** (a judge-made remedy from Rupa Ashok Hurra, 2002) allows a final look to cure gross miscarriage of justice - standard content outside the extracts.

## Transfer, binding law, complete justice

- **Article 139A**: withdraw cases pending in High Courts and decide them itself; transfer cases between High Courts.
- **Article 141**: law declared by the Supreme Court binds **all courts** in India.
- **Article 142**: its decrees are enforceable throughout India and it may pass any order necessary for **complete justice** in a matter before it; the complete-justice power in Article 142(1) cannot be cut down by ordinary law (PYQ 2019), whereas the ancillary powers in Article 142(2) (securing attendance, discovery and production of documents, investigation or punishment of contempt) are expressly 'subject to the provisions of any law made in this behalf by Parliament'. The Court has itself said Article 142 cannot supplant substantive statutory law (Supreme Court Bar Association, 1998) and must be used with restraint (Shilpa Sailesh, 2023).
- **Article 144**: all civil and judicial authorities must act in aid of the Court.
- Other powers: decides presidential and vice-presidential election disputes (original, exclusive, final); inquires into UPSC members' conduct on presidential reference (advice binding); superintendence over all courts and tribunals. Parliament may enlarge its jurisdiction on Union List matters (Article 138).

### 7. Judicial review: meaning, constitutional basis, scope and limits

*Objective:* Define judicial review precisely, name the Articles usually cited as its basis, and state what it includes and excludes, including the basic structure link.

## Meaning

Laxmikanth: judicial review is the power of the Supreme Court (and High Courts) to **examine the constitutionality** of legislative enactments and executive orders of both the Central and State governments; if found **ultra vires** (beyond the powers granted by the Constitution), they are declared unconstitutional and void and cannot be enforced. NCERT adds that the **term 'judicial review' appears nowhere in the Constitution**; the power is implied by a written Constitution and Article 13, which makes laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights void.

## What it reviews

NCERT identifies two grounds: a law may be struck down because it **violates Fundamental Rights**, or because it **violates the federal distribution of powers** (for example, Parliament legislating on a State List subject). Executive orders are also reviewable. NCERT also records that areas once thought beyond review - the powers of the President and Governors - were later brought within it.

## Constitutional basis (standard list)

The Articles usually cited together are: **13** (laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights void), **32** and **226** (writs), **131-136** (original and appellate jurisdiction), **143** (advisory), **246** (distribution of legislative subjects), **251** and **254** (Central law prevails over inconsistent State law), and **372** (continuance of pre-Constitution laws subject to the Constitution). This list is standard textbook content beyond the extracted pages; verify it against the Constitution.

## What judicial review is NOT

- It is **not** a power to question the **wisdom or policy** of a law - only its constitutionality (compare PYQ 2017).
- It is **not** a pre-enactment veto; the Court reviews laws after they are made, when challenged in a case.
- It is distinct from **review under Article 137**, which is the Court re-examining its own judgment.
- Indian review is narrower than American review: India follows **'procedure established by law'** (Article 21) while the USA applies **'due process of law'**, so Indian courts traditionally test the legality of procedure rather than its reasonableness (Laxmikanth's comparison table). The gap has narrowed: in Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978) the Court read Article 21 to require that the procedure itself be fair, just and reasonable, importing an element of due process (standard knowledge, not in the extract; verify).

## Judicial review and the basic structure

In **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** the Court held that Parliament's amending power cannot destroy the Constitution's **basic structure**, and reserved to itself the power to decide what that structure contains - so the Constitution does not define it. Judicial review has itself been treated as part of the basic structure in later cases (Minerva Mills 1980; L. Chandra Kumar 1997), and I.R. Coelho (2007) extended review to Ninth Schedule laws inserted after 24 April 1973 - these three are standard content outside the extracts.

## Tools of interpretation

As final interpreter the Court uses doctrines such as severability, eclipse, pith and substance, colourable legislation, harmonious construction, prospective overruling and precedent. You will study them under Centre-State relations; here just know that **harmonious construction** and **federal supremacy** (PYQ 2019) are products of review in legislative-power disputes.

### 8. Judicial activism, PIL, restraint, overreach and the independence-accountability balance

*Objective:* Explain how PIL produced judicial activism, weigh its benefits against the overreach critique, and frame the independence-accountability balance as a Mains argument.

## From locus standi to PIL

Normally only a person who is personally aggrieved can approach a court (the rule of **locus standi**). Around **1979** the Supreme Court relaxed this: it heard cases filed by others on behalf of the affected, and even treated letters and newspaper reports as petitions. NCERT calls this **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** or Social Action Litigation. Early examples: **Hussainara Khatoon v Bihar (1979)** - undertrials jailed longer than any sentence they could have received; **Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration (1980)** - a prisoner's scribbled note on torture converted into a petition; **Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984)** - Justice Bhagwati explaining that the poor cannot enforce rights through the ordinary adversarial procedure.

## What activism achieved

Through PIL the Court **expanded rights**: clean air and water and a decent life were read into the right to life. It **democratised access** by letting public-spirited citizens and organisations petition for the needy. It **forced executive accountability** - directing the CBI in the hawala and other cases, requiring election candidates to file affidavits of assets and education. It made rights real for bonded labourers, quarry workers, blinded prisoners and exploited children.

## The critique: overreach

NCERT gives the negative side candidly. PILs have **overburdened** courts. Activism has **blurred the line** between judiciary and the executive-legislature: reducing pollution, investigating corruption or reforming elections are administrative tasks to be done under legislative supervision. Each organ respecting the others' jurisdiction is the basis of democratic government, and activism strains this. Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar warned in the Constituent Assembly that independence must not make the judiciary a 'super-legislature or super-executive'. When courts write detailed guidelines that look like statutes, critics call it **judicial legislation** (PYQs 2020 and 2026); defenders answer that courts fill a vacuum left by legislative inaction and that guidelines operate only until Parliament legislates.

## Judicial restraint

The opposite posture: courts defer to elected organs on policy, decide only what is necessary, and respect precedent. Restraint is not weakness; it protects the Court's legitimacy, which rests on reasoning rather than on votes.

## Independence versus accountability - the Mains hinge

The shields of module 4 protect judges; the accountability question asks who checks them. Three threads for an answer:

1. **Appointments**: the collegium protects independence but is criticised as opaque and self-selecting; NJAC tried to add executive and 'eminent persons' and was struck down as threatening independence (modules 2). A balanced answer accepts both the value and the defect.
2. **Conduct**: removal has never succeeded; the conduct of judges cannot be debated; contempt power deters criticism. Accountability therefore depends on internal mechanisms and transparency.
3. **Reach**: activism increases power without increasing electoral accountability; the Court must therefore be answerable to the Constitution, to democratic traditions and to the people (NCERT's formula), which the 2025 PYQ frames as **constitutional morality** - fidelity to constitutional values that binds judges as much as it binds other high functionaries.

Conclude Mains answers with a synthesis: an independent judiciary is a precondition of democracy, but independence is a means to impartial justice, not an end that exempts the Court from the Constitution it guards.

### 9. How UPSC tests this topic: Prelims patterns and Mains frameworks

*Objective:* Recognise the recurring question patterns in the 20 verified PYQs and apply a fixed elimination routine and answer frameworks.

## Prelims: what is actually asked

The eight verified Prelims questions (2017-2024) fall into four patterns.

1. **Definition precision** - 'Judicial review implies...' (2017) and 'A writ of prohibition is an order to...' (2024). The trap is a plausible but wrong recipient or scope: review is about constitutionality, not wisdom; prohibition goes to a lower court, not to an officer.
2. **Procedure details** - removal (2019). Statement sets mix the Constitution's skeleton with the Judges (Inquiry) Act's flesh. Ask of each statement: is this in the Constitution or in the Act? Does the Constitution define 'misbehaviour'? Can the Speaker refuse?
3. **Constitution versus statute** - contempt (2022), basic structure and judicial review (2020). The Constitution confers contempt power and implies review, but does **not** define civil and criminal contempt or basic structure. Statement words like 'defines', 'gives details', 'mentions' are the trigger.
4. **Power boundaries** - Article 142 (2019), retired judges and High Court review (2021), writs against companies and who may seek quo warranto (2022). Check each condition: prior consent of President **and** the person; public duty test for mandamus; 'any interested person' for quo warranto.

## A three-step elimination routine

- Step 1: underline the absolute words ('any', 'cannot', 'defines', 'all').
- Step 2: ask whether the fact belongs to the Constitution, a statute or a judgment.
- Step 3: eliminate options containing a statement you can disprove with one Article; pick among the rest by the most certain statement.

## Mains: what is actually asked

### Notes: Core definitions

- Integrated judiciary: a single hierarchy of courts (Supreme Court, High Courts, subordinate courts) enforcing both Central and State laws, adopted from the Government of India Act 1935; contrasts with the US double system.
- Jurisdiction: the legal power of a court to hear a type of case; original when the case starts there, appellate when it reviews a lower court's decision.
- Judicial review: the power to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders of the Centre and States and to declare those that are ultra vires void.
- Court of record: a court whose proceedings are preserved as evidence and precedent and which can punish for contempt of itself.
- Public Interest Litigation: litigation in which a person or body not personally aggrieved moves the court on behalf of others or in the public interest, relaxing the locus standi rule from about 1979.
- Judicial activism: the judiciary taking up issues on its own motion or on loose petitions and directing the executive and legislature; judicial restraint is the opposite posture of deference.

### Notes: Articles at a glance

- Article 124: establishment and constitution of the Supreme Court; 124(4) removal; 124A-124C NJAC (struck down 2015).
- Article 125 salaries; 126 acting CJI; 127 ad hoc judges; 128 retired judges at sittings; 129 court of record; 130 seat (Delhi).
- Article 131 original jurisdiction (federal disputes, exclusive); 131A repealed.
- Article 132 constitutional appeals; 133 civil appeals; 134 criminal appeals; 134A certificate for appeal; 135 Federal Court jurisdiction; 136 special leave.
- Article 137 review of own judgments; 138 enlargement of jurisdiction; 139 conferment of writ powers for other purposes; 139A transfer of cases; 140 ancillary powers.
- Article 141 law declared binding on all courts; 142 enforcement of decrees and complete justice; 143 presidential reference; 144 authorities to act in aid; 145 rules of court (constitution benches of at least five judges); 146 officers and expenses; 147 interpretation.
- Article 32 writs by Supreme Court for Fundamental Rights; Article 226 writs by High Courts for Fundamental Rights and other purposes; Article 13 laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights void; Article 50 separation of judiciary from executive.

### Notes: Chronology

- 1935: Government of India Act creates the Federal Court and the single court system India retained.
- 28 January 1950: Supreme Court inaugurated with 8 judges; replaces the Federal Court and the Privy Council as final appellate court.
- 1956, 1960, 1977, 1986, 2008, 2019: Parliament raises the number of other judges to 10, 13, 17, 25, 30 and 33 (total 34 in 2019).
- 1961: first Article 131 suit, West Bengal v Union of India (Coal Bearing Areas Act upheld).
- 1968: Judges (Inquiry) Act lays down the removal procedure.
- 1970: criminal appellate jurisdiction extended to life or ten-year sentences; 1972: 30th Amendment removes the civil monetary limit.
- 1973: Kesavananda Bharati (basic structure); A.N. Ray supersedes three judges. 1977: M.H. Beg supersedes H.R. Khanna.
- 1979-80: Hussainara Khatoon and Sunil Batra begin PIL. 1984: Bandhua Mukti Morcha.
- 1982 First Judges case; 1993 Second Judges case; 1998 Third Judges case (presidential reference).
- 1991-93: V. Ramaswami removal motion fails in Lok Sabha.
- 2014: 99th Amendment and NJAC Act; 2015: both struck down (Fourth Judges case); collegium revived.

### Notes: Jurisdiction types compared

- Original (Article 131) is exclusive and between federal units; writ (Article 32) is original but concurrent with High Courts and protects Fundamental Rights only.
- Appellate jurisdiction has four heads with different entry conditions: certificate (132, 133, 134 in most cases), as of right (134 death / life / ten years), discretion (136).
- Advisory opinions under Article 143 are not binding; review under Article 137 is the Court's own self-correction.

### Notes: Judgments to cite

- S.P. Gupta (First Judges case, 1982): consultation is not concurrence; executive primacy in appointments.
- Second Judges case (1993): consultation means concurrence; CJI advice binding after consulting two senior-most judges; senior-most judge to be CJI.
- Third Judges case (1998, presidential reference): collegium of the CJI plus the four senior-most judges (five members); two adverse opinions block a recommendation.
- Fourth Judges case (Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India, 2015): 99th Amendment and NJAC Act unconstitutional; collegium revived.
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973): basic structure limits amending power; Court decides what the basic structure is; departed from Golak Nath (1967).
- Hussainara Khatoon v Bihar (1979), Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration (1980), Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India (1984): foundations of PIL.
- ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976): H.R. Khanna's dissent upholding life and liberty during Emergency, which cost him the Chief Justiceship.
- West Bengal v Union of India (1961): first suit under Article 131.

### Notes: Removal process (Article 124(4) plus Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968)

- Motion signed by 100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya Sabha members is given to the Speaker or Chairman, who may admit or refuse it.
- If admitted, a three-member committee (Supreme Court judge or CJI, a High Court Chief Justice, a distinguished jurist) investigates the charges.
- If the committee finds misbehaviour or incapacity, each House must pass the motion by special majority (majority of total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting) in the same session.
- The address is presented to the President, who issues the removal order; the motion does not lapse on dissolution of the Lok Sabha.
- No Supreme Court judge has been removed; the only motion (V. Ramaswami, 1991-93) failed for want of a majority of the total membership.

### Notes: Independence safeguards

- Appointment by the President in consultation with the judiciary; security of tenure; service conditions not alterable to disadvantage except in Financial Emergency.
- Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India; conduct not discussable in legislatures except on a removal motion; ban on practice after retirement.
- Power to punish for contempt; CJI appoints Court staff; Parliament cannot curtail jurisdiction (may extend it); Article 50 separation of judiciary from executive, implemented through the CrPC 1973 (replaced by the BNSS 2023 from 1 July 2024).

### Notes: Cause and effect in the activism debate

- Relaxation of locus standi (1979) -> flood of PILs -> expansion of the right to life to environment, dignity and livelihood -> greater executive accountability.
- Letters and newspaper reports treated as petitions -> courts taking up issues suo motu -> the label 'judicial activism'.
- Detailed judicial guidelines to the executive -> charge of 'judicial legislation' -> strain on separation of powers and overburdened courts.
- Collegium opacity -> NJAC (99th Amendment) -> struck down 2015 for threatening independence -> continuing demand for transparency within the collegium.

### Notes: Commonly confused pairs

- Judicial review (constitutionality of laws and orders) versus review under Article 137 (Court re-examining its own judgment).
- Prohibition (stops a lower court before it decides) versus certiorari (quashes an order already made).
- Exclusive original jurisdiction (Article 131, federal disputes) versus concurrent original jurisdiction (Article 32 writs, shared with Article 226).
- Special majority for removal (total membership AND two-thirds present and voting) versus a simple two-thirds of those voting - the Ramaswami motion had the second but not the first.
- Advisory opinion (Article 143, not binding) versus the Court's advice on UPSC members' removal (binding on the President).
- Ad hoc judge (a sitting High Court judge, appointed for quorum) versus retired judge under Article 128 (needs the person's consent and the President's).

### Notes: Exceptions and limits

- Article 131 does not cover inter-State water disputes, Finance Commission matters, pre-Constitution treaties, commercial disputes between Centre and States, or suits by private citizens.
- Special leave under Article 136 does not extend to military courts and courts martial.
- Salaries can be reduced only during a Financial Emergency.
- Fair reporting, fair criticism of judicial acts and comment on the administrative side of the judiciary are not contempt.
- If a High Court reverses a conviction and acquits, there is no appeal to the Supreme Court as of right under Article 134.

### Core concepts

- Independent judiciary protects rule of law; independence is not absence of accountability.
- Integrated judiciary: one pyramid, binding Supreme Court law (Article 141).
- Consultation evolved into concurrence and then collegiality through the Judges cases; NJAC failed in 2015.
- Eight heads of jurisdiction and power: original, writ, appellate, advisory, court of record, judicial review, constitutional interpretation, other powers.
- Judicial review tests constitutionality, not wisdom; its term is not in the Constitution but its basis is.
- PIL made activism possible; overreach and judicial legislation are its critiques.

**Q.** What does 'integrated judiciary' mean and from which Act was it adopted?

**A.** A single hierarchy of courts enforcing both Central and State laws; adopted from the Government of India Act, 1935.

**Q.** Who appoints Supreme Court judges and whose consultation is obligatory?

**A.** The President; consultation with the Chief Justice of India is obligatory for appointing any judge other than the CJI.

**Q.** Until what age does a Supreme Court judge hold office, and to whom does he address a resignation?

**A.** 65 years; to the President.

**Q.** Name the five writs.

**A.** Habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.

**Q.** Why does Laxmikanth say the High Court's writ jurisdiction is wider than the Supreme Court's?

**A.** High Courts issue writs for Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose (Article 226); the Supreme Court issues them only for Fundamental Rights (Article 32) unless Parliament confers wider power.

**Q.** How can the Supreme Court be a 'self-correcting agency' and yet have binding law under Article 141?

**A.** Article 141 binds all other courts to its declared law, but Article 137 lets the Court itself review and depart from its earlier decisions, as in Kesavananda departing from Golak Nath.

**Q.** Why did the Court strike down the NJAC?

**A.** It held that the new appointment system would affect the independence of the judiciary, which the Court treats as part of the basic structure.

**Q.** Explain why advisory jurisdiction is still useful if nobody is bound by the opinion.

**A.** The government obtains an authoritative legal opinion before acting, which can prevent later litigation and lets it adjust its action or legislation.

**Q.** A citizen of Kerala says a Central law violates her freedom of speech. List two courts she may approach directly and the Articles involved.

**A.** The Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Court under Article 226; both can issue writs for Fundamental Rights.

**Q.** The President wants to know in advance whether a proposed Bill on citizenship for residents of a disputed territory is constitutional. Which jurisdiction does he use, and may the Court refuse?

**A.** Advisory jurisdiction under Article 143(1), a question of law of public importance; the Court may decline to opine.
