### Bridge: Words for the extension of British power

Seven words carry this whole topic.

A **princely state** (also called a native or Indian state) was a territory still ruled by an Indian prince while the British held the real power over it. A **ruler** here means a nawab, nizam, raja, maharaja or peshwa. **Sovereignty** is the final, independent right to make laws, wage war and conduct relations with other states. **Paramountcy** (NCERT, Class VIII) is the Company's claim that its authority was supreme and therefore greater than that of any Indian state; once it claimed this, it argued it could annex or threaten to annex any state to protect its interests.

A **Resident** was a Company officer posted at an Indian ruler's court. NCERT says Residents were political or commercial agents who served the Company's interests and began to interfere in internal affairs, even in who succeeded to the throne. A **subsidy** is a regular payment. In the Subsidiary Alliance the ruler paid it, and the British force he paid for was called the *subsidiary force*. To **annex** is to take a territory into one's own state. **Adoption** is the legal act by which a ruler without a natural son takes a boy as his heir. The **Doctrine of Lapse** asked what happens to a state when such a ruler dies.

Keep one picture in mind: a ruler still sits on the throne (the princely state), but a British officer sits beside him (the Resident), British soldiers sit in his fort at his cost (the subsidiary force), and the British decide who may succeed him (paramountcy).

### 1. Orientation: three tools for extending British power

*Objective:* Name the three devices of annexation by diplomacy, their governor-generals and their one-line ideas.

Spectrum says the Company extended its paramountcy between 1757 and 1857 by two methods: annexation by conquest or war, and annexation by diplomacy and administrative mechanisms. The wars against Bengal, Mysore, the Marathas and the Sikhs belong to other lessons (GS-025, GS-026 and GS-027 for the early ones). This lesson is about the second method, the quiet one. Spectrum names three examples.

| Tool | Governor-General | One-line idea |
|---|---|---|
| Ring-fence | Warren Hastings (1773 to 1785) | Defend a neighbour's frontier as a buffer for the Company, at the neighbour's cost |
| Subsidiary Alliance | Lord Wellesley (1798 to 1805) | A permanent British force, a subsidy to pay for it, a Resident, no other Europeans, no wars or treaties without British consent |
| Doctrine of Lapse | Lord Dalhousie (1848 to 1856) | An adopted son inherits private property, not the state; the paramount power decides whether he gets the state |

Notice the direction of travel. In the first, a state is protected. In the second, it is protected and controlled. In the third, a state whose ruler leaves no natural heir can disappear altogether. Spectrum calls Wellesley's system an extension of the ring-fence idea, and the whole sequence a ladder that ends in annexation.

## A sorting rule for state questions

When a question names a state, ask which route took it:

- **War**: Mysore (1799), the Marathas, the Sikhs.
- **Alliance**: a treaty that looked voluntary, such as Hyderabad (1798) or Awadh (1801).
- **Lapse**: a ruler died without a natural heir, such as Satara (1848).
- **Misgovernment**: Awadh (1856), where Dalhousie declared the state badly governed.

Awadh appears twice because it first accepted an alliance (1801) and was annexed later on the ground of misrule (1856). It was not a case of lapse. This is the single most useful distinction in the lesson, and Day 12's lesson on the Revolt (GS-032) uses it too.

### 2. Foundation: before Wellesley, from hired troops to the ring-fence

*Objective:* Trace how the idea of an Indian ruler paying for Company troops grew before 1798, and avoid mixing the two Hastingses.

Spectrum says it was *probably* Dupleix who first hired out European troops to Indian rulers to fight their wars. Spectrum's chapter on the Europeans calls him the originator of the practice of subsidiary alliance and gives the example: a French army under Bussy was placed at Hyderabad at the expense of the subahdar (GS-025 module 7 and GS-026 module 3 tell the story). Spectrum then says that almost all governor-generals from Clive onward (Spectrum's wording; Clive was Governor of Bengal, as the title governor-general began in 1773) applied the system to various states and brought it close to perfection. So the idea is French in origin and English in perfection.

## Awadh, 1765

After Buxar the Company signed a treaty with Awadh (GS-027 module 7). Spectrum says Awadh was the first Indian state to fall into the 'protection trap' that anticipated the alliance: the Company pledged to defend its frontiers on condition that the Nawab paid the cost of that defence. NCERT adds a second thing that began after Buxar: the Company appointed **Residents** in Indian states, and through them it interfered in internal affairs, tried to decide who was to succeed to a throne and who got administrative posts.

## The ring-fence of Warren Hastings

Warren Hastings became governor general at a time when the Company faced a possible combination of the Marathas, Mysore and Hyderabad. Spectrum describes his *ring-fence* as a policy of creating buffer zones to defend the Company's frontiers: defend the neighbour's frontier in order to safeguard your own. The chief danger was the Afghan invaders and the Marathas, so the Company undertook to organise the defence of Awadh's frontier, the Nawab defraying the expense. Spectrum adds that the defence of Awadh was in effect the defence of Bengal. States under the ring-fence were assured of help against outside attack but at their own expense: they had to maintain forces which Company officers organised, equipped and commanded, and which the rulers paid for.

## Cornwallis, 1787

Spectrum records one more step: in February 1787 the treaty Cornwallis signed with the Nawab of the Carnatic was the first in which the Company insisted that a subsidiary state should have no foreign relations. Wellesley's contribution, Spectrum says, was to make it a general rule to demand territory in full sovereignty to pay for the subsidiary force.

## Two Hastingses

NCERT Class VIII (page 10) says that under *Lord Hastings*, governor-general from 1813 to 1823, a new policy of **paramountcy** began: the Company claimed supreme authority and therefore the right to annex or threaten any state. That is a different man from **Warren Hastings** (1773 to 1785) of the ring-fence. Spectrum's account of the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817 to 19) says Lord Hastings had the design of imposing British paramountcy. The examiner's trap is to attribute the ring-fence to the later Hastings or paramountcy to the earlier.

## The ladder so far

1. Troops for hire (Dupleix, Bussy at Hyderabad).
2. A protected frontier at the ally's cost (Awadh, 1765).
3. Buffer states in the ring-fence (Warren Hastings).
4. No foreign relations for the ally (Carnatic, 1787).
5. A general rule: territory for the force (Wellesley).

### 3. Core: the Subsidiary Alliance, terms and logic

*Objective:* List the clauses of a Subsidiary Alliance, say why it was called subsidiary and state the British aims.

Lord Wellesley was governor general from 1798 to 1805. Spectrum says he used the system 'to build an empire in India'. Spectrum's own words are worth turning into six clauses you can recite.

1. **A permanent British force** is stationed inside the ruler's territory.
2. **A subsidy**: the ruler pays for that force.
3. **A Resident** is posted at the ruler's court.
4. **No other Europeans**: the ruler may not employ any European in his service without prior consultation with the Company. Spectrum links this to the French and says the treaties required rulers to dismiss Europeans other than the British.
5. **No independent wars or talks**: the ruler may not go to war or negotiate with any other Indian ruler without consulting the governor general.
6. **In return**, the British promise to defend the ruler against his enemies and to follow a policy of non-interference in his internal affairs.

## Why 'subsidiary'?

The word comes from *subsidy*. The force is auxiliary or subordinate to the ruler's own power in name, and the ruler pays a regular sum for it. The force is not a loan or a gift; it is a service sold to the ruler at a price he has no real power to refuse.

## The British aims

- **Keep out the French.** Spectrum says the fear of a Napoleonic expedition eastwards was real, with the French able to attack India's western coast from Mauritius. Clause 4 was meant to empty courts of French officers. It was the reason behind the 'Napoleonic danger' option you will meet in the 2018 question in module 10.
- **Station forces at strategic points.** Troops placed at key courts could be rushed anywhere. Spectrum's remark on Bassein (module 5) shows this.
- **Expand the Company's hold.** Spectrum says the system would draw more and more territory into the Company's fold.

## Reading a real treaty: Bassein

Spectrum prints the clauses of the Treaty of Bassein (31 December 1802) with the Peshwa. It shows all the types of clause in one place: a permanent force of not less than 6,000 native infantry with artillery (military); territory yielding a stated annual income, Rs 26 lakh, ceded to the Company and the city of Surat surrendered (financial); no Europeans of any nation at war with the English (personnel; note the wording is narrower than Spectrum's general summary); and the Peshwa's relations with other states placed under English control (diplomatic). Sort every clause of any treaty into military, financial, personnel and diplomatic types and you will not lose the thread.

### 4. Core: four stages and the cession of territory

*Objective:* Explain how an ally slid from helped friend to dependent payer, and how payment in territory worked.

Spectrum describes four stages in the evolution of the system. Use them as a slide.

| Stage | What the Company did | What the ally gave |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Offered troops to a friendly state for any war it was fighting | Gratitude, access |
| 2 | Made common cause and took the field with its own soldiers and the state's | Joint command |
| 3 | Asked not for men but for money; promised to raise, train and maintain a fixed number of soldiers under British officers for the ruler's protection | A regular payment |
| 4 | Fixed the protection fee, usually high; if the state could not pay on time, it had to cede territory in lieu | Land |

The step from stage 3 to stage 4 is the trap in the machine. A fixed fee is a debt. A debt that cannot be paid becomes land.

## An illustration with invented numbers

Imagine a state whose revenue is 100 units a year and whose fee for the force is fixed at 40. The ruler also needs his own court, officials and a small household force. In a famine year revenue falls to 60, but the fee is still 40. He cannot pay and cedes districts that yielded, say, 30 units. Next year revenue is only 70 and the fee is still 40. The cycle repeats. These numbers are made up to show the logic; the real cases are in the sources below.

## The real cases

NCERT Class VIII says that if Indian rulers failed to pay for the subsidiary forces, part of their territory was taken away as a penalty. Its example: when Wellesley was governor-general (1798 to 1805), the Nawab of Awadh was forced to give over more than half of his territory in 1801, because he failed to pay for the subsidiary forces; Hyderabad was forced to cede territory on similar grounds. The Bassein treaty (module 3) included a territorial clause from the start. Spectrum credits Wellesley with making this a general rule: territory in full sovereignty for the maintenance of the force.

## The Resident as engine

Spectrum says that once the Company had entered a state's affairs, it was for the Resident, installed in the state's capital under the treaty, to start, sustain and hasten the process of eventual annexation. NCERT's quotation from James Mill says that the Resident really was king of the country while the prince acted in perfect subservience, and that trouble began the moment the prince took a course the British thought wrong. So the Resident is the listening post, the adviser and the enforcer.

## The Maratha chain in short

Spectrum's account runs: Holkar defeated the Peshwa and Scindia at Hadapsar (25 October 1802), the Peshwa fled to Bassein and signed. Scindia and Bhonsle tried to save Maratha independence but were beaten by the English under Arthur Wellesley and made to conclude separate subsidiary treaties. Spectrum says the Peshwa's treaty was of great strategic value: Company troops were already in Mysore, Hyderabad and Lucknow, and adding Poona spread them more evenly so they could be rushed anywhere. The full Anglo-Maratha wars lie outside this topic.

## The 1818 entries

Wellesley left India in 1805. The 1818 entries in the list belong to a later governor-general and show that the system outlived its author. Spectrum's list calls them subsidiary alliances; keep its label for the exam but remember that Wellesley's own years are 1798 to 1805 (verify the exact 1818 treaty details).

## Beyond India's heartland

Spectrum records that in February 1839 the Amirs of Sindh accepted a treaty by which a British subsidiary force was stationed at Shikarpur and Bukkar and the Amirs paid Rs 3 lakh a year for it. It shows the same instrument in the 1830s, at the border of the Afghan war.

### 6. Core: gains, losses and the verdict

*Objective:* Weigh the effects of the alliance on the Company and the allied rulers with reasons from the sources.

## What the Company gained

- **Troops at the expense of others.** Spectrum says the Company could station forces at strategic locations at the ruler's expense. The cost of a standing army was shifted to Indian treasuries.
- **A shield against the French.** Clause 4 removed European officers from courts and cut off French influence.
- **A path to more land.** Spectrum says the system would expand the Company's hold and gradually bring more territory into the Company's fold. The territorial clauses of module 4 did that.
- **Supremacy.** Spectrum quotes the view that Wellesley turned the Company from one of the powers in India into the supreme power, claiming the whole country as its protectorate, and from his time the defence of India was the Company's responsibility.

## What the rulers lost

Spectrum puts the loss in one sentence: the rulers lost their independence by buying security. It lists the damage:

1. They were not free from the Resident's interference.
2. They lost much of their revenue in paying for the troops.
3. The alliance made them weak and irresponsible.
4. Their subjects were exploited, and it became practically impossible to depose oppressive rulers because the British protected them.

NCERT's one-sentence version is stronger: the Company put Residents in states and rulers lost their independent armed forces. Together the sources say that a state lost the three marks of sovereignty: control of its own army, of its relations with others, and of its own treasury.

## The honest ambiguity

A student should not accept either slogan. 'The British protected the ruler' is true only in the sense that the ruler became safe from his rivals and his own subjects. 'The British took everything' is not complete either: Spectrum records the promise of non-interference, while its own stage-4 and Resident passages (module 4) show how control grew in practice. The balanced reading is that the alliance bought the ruler peace and a throne, and gave the British a permanent army paid for by the ally and a ladder towards the whole country.

## The Awadh example as evidence

Spectrum's account of Awadh's annexation (module 8) says the cruel impact of the system produced continuous maladministration for 80 years under profligate nawabs. It says the Nawab's chronic bankruptcy was partly due to the heavy charges for the subsidiary troops, and that large sums were also demanded by Lord Hastings (1813 to 1823, not Warren Hastings), Lord Amherst and Lord William Bentinck for purposes unconnected with Awadh. In that account, the protector became the cause of the misrule it later used as an excuse for annexation.

### 7. Core: the Doctrine of Lapse, meaning and origin

*Objective:* State what the Doctrine said, who really began it and what Dalhousie changed.

## The rule in simple words

Spectrum states it simply: an adopted son could be heir to his foster father's *private property*, but not to the *state*. It was for the paramount power, the British, to decide whether to give the state to the adopted son or to annex it. NCERT Class VIII puts the consequence in one sentence: if an Indian ruler died without a male heir, his kingdom would lapse, that is, become part of Company territory.

Read the two together to avoid a common error. The doctrine did not stop adoption itself, nor did it take away the adopted son's private wealth. It took away the *succession to the state* unless the British consented.

## Hindu law and the excuse

Spectrum says the doctrine was claimed to rest on Hindu law and custom, but Hindu law seemed inconclusive on the point. It adds two precedents: Maharaja Ranjit Singh had himself annexed a few feudatory principalities for lack of heirs, and in 1820 the Company acquired a few petty Cis-Sutlej states for the same reason. Yet Spectrum adds that there was no clear case of an adopted son being deprived of a whole state on this ground. So the rule had a thin precedent, and its use against large states was new.

## Dalhousie did not invent it

Spectrum says the policy is *attributed* to Dalhousie (governor general from 1848 to 1856) but he was not its originator. The doctrine had been stated in theory on earlier occasions; it was a coincidence that during his years several important cases arose; and he showed too much zeal in enforcing it. NCERT, in contrast, writes that Dalhousie 'devised' the policy. The two books therefore differ in emphasis; for examinations, learn the safe line: the principle existed earlier, Dalhousie applied it widely and made it notorious.

## What Dalhousie changed

Spectrum draws the contrast neatly. His predecessors acted on the principle of avoiding annexation if it could be avoided; Dalhousie acted on the principle of annexing if it could be done legitimately. That is the difference of attitude, not of law.

## Why it looked legal and why it felt unjust

To the British it looked legal because it rested on the paramount power's right to approve a succession (module 2). To Indian rulers and subjects it felt like a breach of faith, because adoption by a childless ruler was an established custom for continuing a line. Spectrum's chapter on the Revolt places 'effective control', subsidiary alliance and the Doctrine of Lapse together as policies that earned the Company contempt, broken pledges and suspicion among almost all rulers, and says that the right of succession was denied to Hindu princes (GS-032 module 3 uses this).

Standard accounts add distinctions among independent, tributary and dependent states, and notable exceptions: Baghat was restored after annexation, and the Court of Directors overruled the lapse of Karauli (1852). These are outside the extracts (verify in a standard text).

## What to learn safely

- **Satara, 1848**: the first and best-known; all three sources agree on the year.
- **Jhansi and Nagpur**: the two most famous; learn them as annexed around 1853 and 1854 with the order and exact years flagged verify.
- **The small states**: Jaitpur, Sambalpur, Baghat and Udaipur; knowing the names matters more than the years (the lapsed Udaipur was a small state in present Chhattisgarh, not the Rajput state of Udaipur/Mewar; see GS-032 module 3 and GS-024).
- **The number**: Spectrum counts seven lapsed states plus Awadh as eight states in eight years and about a quarter million square miles (Spectrum's figures; verify). That count leaves out Dalhousie's annexations by war (Punjab 1849, Lower Burma 1852). It also leaves out Berar (1853), which the Nizam was made to hand over to meet arrears of payment for the subsidiary force (standard knowledge, verify). Berar is the clearest Dalhousie-era example of stage 4 in module 4.

## Awadh was not a lapse

Spectrum says plainly that Dalhousie annexed Awadh in 1856 after deposing Nawab Wajid Ali Shah on grounds of misgovernment, and the summary box writes 'Awadh (1856; on charge of mal-administration)'. NCERT says the same: the British claimed they were 'obliged by duty' to free the people from misgovernment. The ground was *misrule*, not a missing heir.

## The Awadh story in Spectrum

- Awadh was the oldest surviving state under the Subsidiary Alliance (the 1801 alliance of the table in module 5).
- Dalhousie sent the Resident, Sleeman, on a tour to see the situation; his report described anarchy. His successor, Outram (1854), supported it.
- Dalhousie hesitated to annex and preferred permanent British administration with the Nawab keeping his titles. But the Court of Directors ordered annexation and the end of the throne (1856).
- Wajid Ali Shah refused to sign a treaty giving up his rights and was exiled to Calcutta. Spectrum calls it a political blunder that cost the British dearly in 1857.

The 2026 Prelims question tests what the British did in Awadh *after* the annexation, namely the taluqdars and the revenue settlement. That belongs to GS-032, module 10 (the Awadh checklist for statement questions), which you should read next; this lesson only fixes the ground of annexation.

### 9. Master: the Revolt, the 1858 turn, the neighbours and the debate

*Objective:* Connect the three tools to 1857, to the 1858 reversal, to Britain's neighbours and to the question of intent.

## Link to 1857

Spectrum's account of the Revolt places the Doctrine of Lapse at the heart of the political causes and at the case of Jhansi. It says Dalhousie refused to let Rani Laxmibai's adopted son succeed after Raja Gangadhar Rao died and annexed the state by 'the infamous Doctrine of Lapse', and that the Rani then led the sepoys (GS-032 modules 3 and 6). GS-032 also records the Nana Saheb pension case. Awadh's 1856 annexation, not a lapse, added the taluqdar grievance. So both devices, alliance first and lapse later, turned allies into subjects, and subjects who had lost their patrons joined the rising.

## The 1858 reversal

Spectrum says the Queen's Proclamation ended the era of annexations and expansion and promised to respect the dignity and rights of the native princes, who now were to recognise the paramountcy of the Crown (GS-032 module 9). GS-033 module 9 shows what followed: annexation stopped, the right to adopt an heir was respected, and the states became a buffer and a subordinate ring of the empire. So Lapse is a pre-1858 device; the post-1858 policy used Crown paramountcy and Residents instead.

## The same toolkit abroad

Spectrum's neighbours section shows the Resident and the treaty used beyond the Company's borders:

| Country | Event | Resident or control element |
|---|---|---|
| Nepal | Treaty of Sagauli, 1816, after war under Lord Hastings | Nepal accepted a British Resident and ceded Garhwal and Kumaon |
| Burma | Treaty of Yandabo, 1826 (First Burma War) | A British Resident at Ava |
| Bhutan | 1865, after the 1863 to 64 envoy episode | Passes surrendered for an annual subsidy |
| Tibet | Treaty of Lhasa, 1904 | Some British control of Tibet's foreign affairs |
| Sindh | 1839 | A subsidiary force at Shikarpur and Bukkar |

The pattern is the same: force, a treaty, a Resident and a control clause. The Afghan and North-West Frontier relations in the same chapter belong to a later lesson and are not taught here.

## Was it planned? The debate

Spectrum's opening (PDF p113) presents John Seeley's view that the conquest was made blindly, in a 'fit of absent-mindedness', and the opposing view that it was planned. For this topic, the evidence leans toward design: Wellesley explicitly aimed to destroy Tipu's independence or force him into the alliance; Spectrum says Dalhousie annexed whenever he could legitimately do so; and the Resident was called the agent of eventual annexation. At the same time the Doctrine of Lapse's cases arose by chance (Spectrum), and the Awadh annexation was ordered by the Court of Directors against Dalhousie's own hesitation. So a balanced answer is: opportunism within a consistent aim, which is a middle position.

### 10. Exam: the two verified questions, frames and traps

Two official questions are verified for this topic: Prelims GS I 2018 Q75 and Prelims GS I 2026 Q17. Analysis only; no official key is asserted here.

## 2018 Q75: the statement that 'does not apply'

The question asks which statement does *not* apply to the Subsidiary Alliance introduced by Lord Wellesley. The four options offer: a large standing army at another's expense; keeping India safe from Napoleonic danger; securing a fixed income for the Company; and establishing British paramountcy over the Indian states.

Test each against Spectrum. A standing army at the ruler's expense is the heart of the system (module 3). The Napoleonic danger is Spectrum's stated aim (modules 3 and 6). Paramountcy is the stated result: the Company becoming the supreme power (module 6). The remaining option, a fixed income for the Company, is the one Spectrum never lists as an aim: the payment was a subsidy for the force, though stage 4 did fix the fee. By elimination, the fixed-income option is the best candidate for the statement that does not apply, but the stage-4 point means a careful candidate should read the wording again and check the official answer key; the elimination logic is analysis, not a key.

## 2026 Q17: Awadh after annexation

This question is about the taluqdars and the 1856 settlement, which GS-032 module 10 treats; its checklist says the extracts support dispossession and later restoration, and standard accounts (verify) support the assumption that taluqdars were outsiders and that revenue was to be taken directly from peasants. GS-032 reports that statement 1, which says taluqdars kept their arms and forts, conflicts with accounts of disarmament. From this lesson only one fact is added: the ground of annexation was misgovernment in 1856, and Awadh had been an alliance state since 1801. Use GS-032 for the rest.

## Answer frames

**Prelims statement sets**: always sort a state by route (war, alliance, lapse, misgovernment); watch years; and separate the two Hastingses.

**Mains 15 marks: 'Examine how the Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse extended British paramountcy.'** Introduce paramountcy with the three tools; give the six clauses; show the four-stage slide and territory payment; give Lapse as a principle with Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur; note Awadh as a separate misgovernment case; give effects on rulers and on the Company; link to 1857 and the 1858 policy; end with the planned or accidental debate.

## Traps to rehearse

- Awadh as a lapse. Wrong: misgovernment.
- Ring-fence credited to the wrong Hastings.
- Wellesley credited with the 1818 treaties.
- Dalhousie as the sole inventor of Lapse.
- 'Subsidiary' as a payment *by* the Company.
- Dupleix as the creator: Spectrum says he *probably* first hired out troops, and calls him the originator of the practice; the perfecter was Wellesley.

### Notes: Definitions

- Ring-fence: Warren Hastings's policy of creating buffer states and defending neighbours' frontiers at their cost (Spectrum).
- Subsidiary Alliance: Wellesley's system of a permanent British force in an ally's territory at the ally's cost, with a Resident, no other Europeans and no independent wars or treaties.
- Subsidiary force: the British-commanded troops stationed in an allied state and paid for by the ruler.
- Doctrine of Lapse: an adopted son inherits private property but not the state; the paramount power decides whether to give or annex the state.
- Paramountcy: the claim that British authority was supreme over Indian states (NCERT Class VIII, begun under Lord Hastings 1813 to 1823).
- Resident: a Company officer at an Indian ruler's court who served Company interests and interfered in internal affairs.

### Notes: Chronology

- 1751 (verify): Bussy posted at Hyderabad at the subahdar's expense (Dupleix's practice); Spectrum runs on from Ambur, 1749, without a separate date; see GS-025 module 7.
- 1765: Awadh treaty after Buxar, Company defends frontier at the Nawab's cost.
- 1773 to 1785: Warren Hastings governor general; ring-fence policy.
- February 1787: Cornwallis's treaty with the Nawab of Carnatic, no foreign relations.
- 1798 to 1805: Wellesley governor general; Hyderabad alliance September 1798 and 1800.
- 1799: Seringapatam; Mysore (Wodeyar boy) accepts the alliance; Tanjore October 1799.
- 1801: Awadh alliance, November; NCERT: over half of the territory ceded.
- 31 December 1802: Treaty of Bassein with the Peshwa.
- 1853 (verify): Berar taken from the Nizam to meet arrears of payment for the subsidiary force (standard knowledge).
- 1813 to 1823: Lord Hastings; paramountcy (NCERT).
- 1848 to 1856: Dalhousie; Satara 1848; Awadh annexed 1856 (misgovernment).

### Notes: Subsidiary Alliance clauses

- Permanent British force inside the ruler's territory.
- Subsidy paid by the ruler to maintain it.
- British Resident at the court.
- No European employed without consulting the Company; British-only rule against the French.
- No war or talks with other Indian rulers without consulting the governor general.
- British promise of defence and non-interference in internal matters.

### Notes: Four stages of the alliance

- Stage 1: Company troops offered to a friendly state for its wars.
- Stage 2: Company and the state fight together.
- Stage 3: Ally gives money; Company raises and maintains a fixed force under British officers.
- Stage 4: Fee fixed high; failure to pay means territory ceded.

### Notes: States and alliance dates (Spectrum's list, with slips flagged)

- Learn the order N-M-T-A-P-B-S, then 1818; Scindia's Burhanpur (February 1804) matches Spectrum; verify the Peshwa, Bhonsle, Holkar and 1818 dates.

### Notes: Lapsed states: three sources

- Learn Satara 1848 safely; flag all other years verify. Standard books: Satara 1848, Jaitpur and Sambalpur 1849, Baghat 1850, Udaipur 1852, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854 (verify). The Jhansi and Nagpur differences are mostly not misprints: Gangadhar Rao died November 1853 and Jhansi was annexed early 1854; Raghuji III died December 1853 and Nagpur was annexed 1854. A book may print the year of death or of annexation (verify). The lapsed Udaipur was in present Chhattisgarh, not Mewar.

### Notes: Effects of the Subsidiary Alliance

- Cause: permanent troops at the ally's cost leads to heavy payments and loss of revenue.
- Cause: Resident's influence leads to interference in internal affairs.
- Cause: protection from the British leads to weak, irresponsible rulers and exploited subjects (Spectrum).
- Effect for the Company: army paid for by the ally, French kept out, strategic stations, route to annexation.

### Notes: Awadh: alliance, annexation, aftermath

- 1765: first protection arrangement after Buxar.
- 1801: subsidiary alliance; NCERT says over half the territory ceded.
- Sleeman's tour and report; Outram's confirming report in 1854.
- Dalhousie preferred administration with the Nawab retaining titles; the Court of Directors ordered annexation and abolition of the throne in 1856.
- Wajid Ali Shah exiled to Calcutta; ground of annexation: misgovernment, not lapse.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Subsidiary Alliance (Wellesley, 1798 to 1805) is not the Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie, 1848 to 1856).
- Warren Hastings (ring-fence) is not Lord Hastings (paramountcy, 1813 to 1823).
- An adopted son keeps private property but not the state.
- Awadh: alliance (1801), annexation for misgovernment (1856); never a lapsed state.
- Subsidy is paid by the ruler to the Company, not by the Company to the ruler.

### Notes: The toolkit abroad

- Nepal: Treaty of Sagauli, 1816, a Resident accepted; Garhwal and Kumaon ceded.
- Burma: Treaty of Yandabo, 1826; a Resident at Ava; Arakan and Tenasserim ceded.
- Bhutan: 1865 passes surrendered for an annual subsidy.
- Tibet: Treaty of Lhasa, 1904; control over foreign affairs.
- Sindh: 1839 treaty with a subsidiary force at Shikarpur and Bukkar, Rs 3 lakh a year.

### Memory skeleton

- Annexation by diplomacy: ring-fence, Subsidiary Alliance, Lapse
- Ring-fence: Warren Hastings, buffer states defended at their cost
- Subsidiary Alliance: Wellesley 1798-1805, six clauses, four stages
- States that joined: Hyderabad first (1798), Mysore, Tanjore, Awadh, Peshwa (Bassein 1802)
- Effects: rulers weakened and indebted, Company army paid by allies, route to annexation
- Doctrine of Lapse: Dalhousie 1848-56, adopted son gets property not state; Satara 1848
- Awadh: alliance 1801, annexed 1856 for misgovernment, not lapse

### Last-week summary

- Ring-fence: Warren Hastings (1773-85), buffer states defended at their cost; paramountcy: Lord Hastings (1813-23).
- Subsidiary Alliance: Wellesley (1798-1805); force in the ally's territory paid by the ruler, Resident, no other Europeans, no wars or talks without the Company, defence promised.
- Hyderabad first (1798); Mysore after Seringapatam (1799); Awadh 1801 (over half the territory ceded); Peshwa by Bassein, 31 December 1802.
- Lapse: Dalhousie (1848-56); adopted son keeps private property, not the state; Satara 1848 first in every source. Awadh 1856 = misgovernment, never lapse.
