### Bridge: Words for Gandhi's early struggles

Seven words appear again and again in this topic.

**Satyagraha** is Gandhi's name for resisting a wrong by truth and non-violence, without hatred for the wrongdoer and with readiness to suffer. **Civil disobedience** means deliberately and peacefully breaking an order or law one considers unjust and accepting the penalty. **Non-cooperation** means withdrawing help from the authorities, for example by refusing to pay a tax or to work. A **strike** is a refusal by workers to work until demands are met. A **fast** is a self-imposed refusal of food as moral pressure; Spectrum counts the Ahmedabad fast as his first (read it as his first in a public struggle in India; module 6). A **tribunal** is a body of arbiters whose decision both sides promise to accept; this is **arbitration**. **Remission** means the government excuses all or part of a tax, here land revenue, in a bad year.

A **planter** is a European who grows a commercial crop such as indigo on estates worked by local peasants; a **tenant** or **ryot** cultivates land he does not own and pays rent. Keep these apart: Champaran was about tenants and planters, Kheda about owner-cultivators and the revenue demand, Ahmedabad about workers and mill owners.

### Bridge: What 'first' means in textbook labels

Spectrum gives each struggle a label: Champaran the first civil disobedience, Ahmedabad the first hunger strike, Kheda the first non-cooperation. The labels help memory, but a careful reader asks three questions of any 'first'.

1. **First by whom?** These are firsts in Gandhi's Indian career. South Africa came earlier (module 2; he made penitential fasts there in 1913-14, so the Ahmedabad fast is a first in India's public struggles; verify), and Indians had used boycott long before (GS-038 modules 4 and 5).
2. **First of what size?** NCERT calls these 'localised struggles' of a district or a city, in contrast with the Rowlatt satyagraha of 1919, which became national.
3. **First in what sense?** A label compresses a method. Kheda was a campaign of non-payment of taxes, which is one form of non-cooperation, and not the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920-22 (GS-043, to come on Day 14, not yet written).

Treat the labels as shorthand for the method that each case first tested in India, not as claims that nothing similar had happened before.

### 1. Orientation: three local struggles, 1917 to 1918

*Objective:* State in a few lines what happened at Champaran, Ahmedabad and Kheda and what each added to Gandhi's politics.

## The story in one page

Gandhi returned to India in January 1915 after two decades abroad, mostly in South Africa. Spectrum says he decided to tour the country for a year, take no position on political matters and join no organisation unless it accepted non-violent satyagraha. In 1917 and 1918 he was drawn into three struggles before the Rowlatt satyagraha of 1919: **Champaran** in Bihar (1917), where tenants were forced to grow indigo for European planters; the **Ahmedabad mill strike** (1918), a wage dispute between workers and mill owners; and **Kheda** in Gujarat (1918), where farmers sought remission of revenue after the crops failed. Spectrum's labels are first civil disobedience, first hunger strike and first non-cooperation. NCERT calls them localised struggles that marked Gandhi out as a nationalist with deep sympathy for the poor.

## The three at a glance

| Struggle | Where | Who | Issue | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Champaran, 1917 | Bihar | Indigo tenants against European planters | Tinkathia system, illegal dues | Inquiry; tinkathia abolished; part refund |
| Ahmedabad, March 1918 | Gujarat city | Mill workers against owners | Plague bonus and wage rise | Fast, tribunal, 35 per cent award |
| Kheda, 1918 | Gujarat district | Peasant farmers against the revenue demand | Remission after crop failure | Tax suspended, confiscations returned |

GS-031 module 7 only outlined Champaran. GS-053 module 5 places these struggles at the start of the Gandhian phase. The next step, the Rowlatt satyagraha, is taught in GS-042 (see GS-042).

### 2. Foundation: Gandhi before 1915 and the method of satyagraha

*Objective:* Describe Gandhi's South African years in two phases and state the main features of satyagraha as Spectrum lists them.

## Why South Africa matters

Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 at Porbandar, where his father was diwan. After studying law in England he went to South Africa in 1893 in connection with a case of his client Dada Abdullah. He saw white racism and the humiliation of Asians, and decided to stay and organise Indians; he remained until 1914. NCERT quotes the historian Chandran Devanesan: South Africa was 'the making of the Mahatma'. NCERT adds that there Gandhi first forged non-violent protest, first promoted harmony between religions, and first alerted upper-caste Indians to their treatment of low castes and women.

## Who the Indians were

Spectrum names three groups: indentured labourers (mainly from south India, on sugar plantations), merchants (mostly Meman Muslims), and ex-indentured labourers who had settled. They were largely illiterate, could not vote and had to live in prescribed locations.

## Two phases

**Moderate phase, 1894-1906.** Gandhi sent petitions and memorials, hoping that authorities would redress grievances of British subjects once informed. He founded the Natal Indian Congress in 1894 (standard: 22 August 1894; verify; NCERT Class VIII's photo caption says 1895) and a paper, *Indian Opinion*.

**Satyagraha phase, 1906-1914.** In 1906 a new law required Indians to carry registration certificates with fingerprints. Gandhi formed a Passive Resistance Association and the technique he named **satyagraha** was born. Later campaigns widened to restrictions on migration, the three-pound poll tax on ex-indentured Indians, and a court ruling that invalidated non-Christian marriages, which drew women into the struggle. The Transvaal Immigration Act protests brought a miners' strike. Settlements such as Phoenix Farm (1904) and Tolstoy Farm (1910) trained families to live simply. After talks involving Gandhi, Lord Hardinge, C.F. Andrews and General Smuts, the South African government conceded the major Indian demands (standard accounts name the Indian Relief Act of 1914 as the result; verify).

## What he learned (Spectrum)

1. The masses can sacrifice for a cause that moves them.
2. He could unite different religions, classes and women under his leadership.
3. Leaders must sometimes take decisions unpopular with enthusiastic supporters.
4. He evolved his own style of leadership and technique on a limited scale.

## Satyagraha in brief

Spectrum says satyagraha mixed Indian traditions, the Christian idea of turning the other cheek and Tolstoy's non-violent resistance. A satyagrahi does not submit to what he thinks wrong; stays truthful, non-violent and fearless; uses withdrawal of cooperation and boycott; may refuse taxes or honours; accepts suffering without hatred for the wrongdoer; and does not separate means from ends. Only the brave could practise it, and even violence was preferred to cowardice. Keep these points ready: modules 5 to 7 test them in India.

### 3. Core: the return, the year of silence and the Lucknow meeting

*Objective:* Describe what Gandhi did between January 1915 and December 1916 and why he began with local issues rather than the Home Rule agitation.

## The return

Gandhi returned to India in January 1915; the exact date, 9 January at Bombay, is standard knowledge not given in the extracts (verify). NCERT Class XII says the India he came back to was more politically active than the one he left in 1893: the Congress had branches in most cities, and the Swadeshi movement of 1905-07 had broadened its appeal among the middle classes, throwing up leaders like Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Lala Lajpat Rai (GS-038 and GS-040 cover this). Its Moderates included his political mentor **Gopal Krishna Gokhale**.

## The year of travel and silence

On Gokhale's advice Gandhi spent a year travelling in British India to know the land and its people (NCERT). Spectrum adds that he decided to take no position on any political matter for a year. He was convinced that moderate politics was limited and that only non-violent satyagraha could meet nationalist aims, and he said he would join no political organisation unless it accepted that creed. He did not favour the Home Rule agitation, since he thought it the wrong moment to press for it while Britain was at war. GS-040 module 3 makes the same point from the Home Rule side.

## The Banaras speech, February 1916

Gandhi's first major public appearance was at the opening of the Banaras Hindu University in February 1916. Annie Besant was among those present; Gandhi was relatively unknown in India and had been invited for his South African work. He charged the elite audience with indifference to the poor: he contrasted the 'richly bedecked noblemen' with the 'millions of the poor', and said that India's salvation would come through the farmer, not through lawyers, doctors or landlords. NCERT reads it at one level as a statement of fact, that nationalism was an elite phenomenon, and at another as a statement of intent to make it representative of the people.

## Lucknow, December 1916

At the annual Congress at Lucknow in December 1916 a peasant from Champaran in Bihar approached Gandhi about the harsh treatment of peasants by British indigo planters (NCERT). Spectrum names him **Rajkumar Shukla**, a local man who requested Gandhi to look into the problem. This is the same Lucknow session that saw the Moderate-Extremist reunion and the Lucknow Pact (GS-040 modules 6 and 7).

## Source caution

NCERT Class VIII says Gandhi, 'aged 46', arrived in 1915. Born on 2 October 1869 he was 45 in January 1915 and turned 46 in October 1915; the line is a small slip.

## Why local issues first?

His plan was practical: learn the people, test the method on concrete grievances, and avoid the larger political question on which the nation was divided. The three struggles of 1917-18 were the test.

### 4. Core: Champaran, the indigo system and Rajkumar Shukla

*Objective:* Explain the tinkathia system, why the planters pressed the peasants in the 1890s and after, and how Gandhi came to Champaran.

## Where and who

Champaran is a district of north Bihar. The planters were Europeans and the cultivators were peasants. GS-031 module 6 explained the indigo revolt of 1859-60 in Bengal and the systems of indigo, **nij** and **ryoti**, and records NCERT's statement that planters shifted to Bihar afterwards. That story is not repeated here; keep the link: the planters' system was older than Gandhi's visit.

## The tinkathia system

Spectrum describes the **tinkathia** system: European planters forced the peasants to grow indigo on **3/20** of their land. The fraction means three parts in twenty, so 3/20 = 0.15, or 15 per cent of the holding, a share that the peasant had no freedom to change. The term and the exact unit (the katha, a land measure of which twenty make a bigha) are standard knowledge (verify); the fraction itself is Spectrum's.

## Why pressure rose

Spectrum says that towards the end of the 19th century German synthetic dyes replaced indigo. The planters, facing lower profits, demanded high rents and illegal dues from the peasants in order to maximise their profits before the peasants could shift to other crops. In addition, peasants were forced to sell their produce at prices fixed by the Europeans. NCERT states Gandhi's aim as obtaining for the peasants security of tenure and freedom to cultivate crops of their choice. The detailed names of the illegal dues (sharahbeshi, tawan) are standard terms (verify).

## Rajkumar Shukla

Spectrum calls Shukla a local man who requested Gandhi to look into the farmers' problem; NCERT adds the Lucknow meeting of December 1916 (module 3). Shukla's persistence is the human bridge between a distant peasant grievance and a national leader who had no plan to go to Bihar.

## What to remember

1. The cause was economic (dues and forced cultivation of a commercial crop) as well as legal (lack of tenure and choice).
2. The crisis had two layers: the old compulsion, and the planters' shifting of the loss onto peasants after the dye market collapsed.
3. The planters were European and the authorities first ordered Gandhi out (module 5), which gave an agrarian issue a nationalist edge.

## A note on the sources

Spectrum, NCERT Class XII and NCERT Class VIII give the same outline but different emphases: Spectrum stresses dues and forced prices, NCERT stresses tenure and choice of crop. Combine them in answers: forced indigo at fixed prices, illegal dues, and no security of tenure.

### 5. Core: Champaran 1917, defiance, inquiry and outcome

*Objective:* Narrate Gandhi's visit, the defiance of the order, the inquiry, the outcome and the associates who joined.

## Arrival and defiance

In 1917 Gandhi went to Champaran, joined by **Rajendra Prasad, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parekh and J.B. Kripalani** (Spectrum). The authorities ordered him to leave the area at once. Gandhi defied the order and preferred to face the punishment. Spectrum calls this passive resistance, or civil disobedience of an unjust order, a novel method at that time. The authorities retreated and permitted him to inquire. Standard accounts date his arrival to April 1917 at Motihari and mention a court summons that was then withdrawn (not in the extracts; verify).

## The inquiry

The government appointed a committee to go into the matter and nominated Gandhi as a member (Spectrum). Gandhi convinced the authorities that the tinkathia system should be abolished and that the peasants should be compensated for the illegal dues. As a compromise with the planters, he agreed that only 25 per cent of the money taken should be compensated. Spectrum says that within a decade the planters left the area, and that Gandhi had won the first battle of civil disobedience in India.

## The Champaran Agrarian Act

Standard accounts say the committee's report led to the **Champaran Agrarian Act of 1918**, which ended the tinkathia system (verify; the extracts do not name the Act). Treat it as a likely follow-up, not as a Spectrum fact.

## Who joined

| Group | Names (Spectrum) |
|---|---|
| Came with Gandhi | Rajendra Prasad, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parekh, J.B. Kripalani |
| Other popular leaders | Brajkishore Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, Ramnavmi Prasad, Shambhusharan Varma |

NCERT Class VIII adds that in this period Gandhi came into contact with Rajendra Prasad and Vallabhbhai Patel. Note the pattern: lawyers and organisers from outside became Gandhi's lieutenants, while the peasants did the suffering; a mixed team of local and visiting workers is a recurring feature of his campaigns.

## What it showed

Spectrum's summary of the three struggles says Gandhi showed the people the efficacy of satyagraha. Champaran also taught a method: first inquire into facts, then negotiate within the law, and accept a compromise that secures the principle (abolition) while granting less than the whole claim (25 per cent refund). The compromise was a satyagraha habit, not a defeat.

## The question the examiner asks

The 2018 Prelims question asks what was very significant in Champaran. NCERT frames it as the first of the local initiatives that marked Gandhi out as a nationalist sympathetic to the poor, and Spectrum calls it the first civil disobedience. The best reading, taken up in module 9, is that it joined peasant discontent to the national movement.

### 6. Core: the Ahmedabad mill strike, March 1918

*Objective:* Explain the dispute, Gandhi's role and the fast, and state the settlement.

## The dispute

In March 1918 Gandhi intervened in a dispute between the cotton mill owners of Ahmedabad and their workers over the discontinuation of the **plague bonus** (Spectrum). The owners wanted to withdraw the bonus. The workers demanded a **50 per cent** rise in wages to cope with wartime inflation, which Spectrum says had doubled the prices of food grains, cloth and other necessities. The owners were ready to give only **20 per cent**. The workers went on strike. NCERT Class XII summarises the cause more loosely, as better working conditions for the mill workers; the bonus and the wage are Spectrum's more specific account.

## Escalation

The relations worsened: the striking workers were dismissed arbitrarily, and the owners decided to bring in weavers from Bombay. The workers turned to **Anusuya Sarabhai**, a social worker who was the sister of **Ambalal Sarabhai**, president of the Ahmedabad Mill Owners Association (founded 1891). She went to Gandhi, whom both sides respected. Spectrum notes that although Gandhi was a friend of Ambalal, he took up the workers' cause.

## Gandhi's method

Gandhi asked the workers to strike but demand **35 per cent** instead of 50, and advised them to remain non-violent during the strike. When negotiations stalled, he undertook a fast unto death to strengthen the workers' resolve (Spectrum). Spectrum calls it his first fast; read it as his first fast in a public struggle in India, since he made penitential fasts in South Africa in 1913-14 (verify). The fast also put pressure on the mill owners, who agreed to submit the issue to a tribunal. The strike was withdrawn. The tribunal awarded the workers a **35 per cent** wage hike.

Standard accounts give the strike's start in February 1918, the fast as three days in mid-March, and a pledge taken by the workers (not in the extracts; verify).

## One fast, two effects

Spectrum records both effects of the fast: it strengthened the workers' resolve and it put pressure on the mill owners. In an answer, state both effects and do not guess at motives.

## Afterwards

Anusuya Behn later formed the Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association in 1920 (Spectrum). This is the line of continuity: Gandhi's help in one strike led to a lasting union.

## Reading the numbers

| Party | Position |
|---|---|
| Workers' first demand | 50 per cent |
| Mill owners' offer | 20 per cent |
| Gandhi's advised demand | 35 per cent |
| Tribunal's award | 35 per cent |

The final award matched what Gandhi had advised the workers to ask; this is a mild lesson in choosing a demand that can be defended.

### 7. Core: the Kheda satyagraha, 1918

*Objective:* State why Kheda's farmers sought remission, how the campaign was organised and what the government conceded.

## The grievance

In 1918 the crops failed in Kheda district of Gujarat. Spectrum says the cause was drought; NCERT only says the harvest failed, and standard accounts tell of heavy rains in 1917 (verify). According to the **Revenue Code**, if the yield was less than one-fourth of the normal produce, the farmers were entitled to remission. The **Gujarat Sabha**, made up of the peasants (Spectrum's wording; standard accounts describe a political association with Gandhi as its president, and a launch around 22 March 1918; verify), petitioned the highest authorities in the province to suspend the revenue assessment. The government remained adamant, said the property of farmers would be seized if the tax was not paid, and refused remission. Spectrum prints the assessment as being 'for the year 1919', which looks like a slip; standard accounts place the dispute in the revenue year of the 1917-18 failure (verify).

## Gandhi's role and the organisers

Gandhi asked the farmers not to pay the tax. Spectrum stresses that Gandhi was mainly the spiritual head of the struggle. The villagers were organised by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** and a group of devoted Gandhians: **Narahari Parikh, Mohanlal Pandya and Ravi Shankar Vyas**. They went around the villages, told people what to do and gave the political leadership. Note the spelling: Spectrum writes Narhari Parekh at Champaran and Narahari Parikh at Kheda; they are the same associate, a spelling difference only (standard knowledge; verify).

## A disciplined revolt

Spectrum says the revolt was remarkable for discipline and unity. When the government seized the farmers' personal property, land and livelihood, most farmers stayed with Patel. Gujaratis elsewhere sheltered the relatives and property of the protesting farmers. Those who tried to buy confiscated lands were socially ostracised.

## The settlement

The government sought an agreement. It suspended the tax for the year and the next, reduced the increase in rate, and returned all the confiscated property (Spectrum). Standard accounts add that the order asked only the well-off to pay and that the campaign ended in the middle of 1918 (verify). Spectrum adds the long-term result: the Kheda struggle brought a new awakening among the peasantry, who became aware that they would not be free of injustice until the country was independent.

## Whose movement was it?

NCERT Class VIII mentions the **Patidar** peasants of Kheda organising non-violent campaigns against the high land revenue demand, but it lists the example among the people's initiatives of the 1919-22 period without giving the 1918 date. Class XII places Kheda clearly in 1918. Teach 1918 and remember that Class VIII's placement can mislead.

## Why 'non-cooperation'?

Kheda's method was not paying the revenue and accepting confiscation without violence: withdrawal of cooperation from the government in the matter of the tax. That is why Spectrum labels it the first non-cooperation (module 1 and bridge B2).

### 8. Master: methods, leaders, the 'first' labels and the limits

*Objective:* Compare the three struggles, state what each taught about satyagraha, list the leaders who joined, and judge the 'first' labels.

## The three side by side

| Feature | Champaran | Ahmedabad | Kheda |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fact-finding, petition or arbitration | Official committee with Gandhi as member | Tribunal on wages | Gujarat Sabha petitions |
| Defiance of an order | Yes: refused to leave | No | Refused to pay, accepted seizure |
| Strike or non-payment | No | Strike | Non-payment of revenue |
| Fast | No | Yes, first in India | No |
| Local organisers | Bihar lawyers (Rajendra Prasad, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Brajkishore Prasad) plus associates from outside (Desai, Parekh, Kripalani) | Anusuya Sarabhai | Patel, Parikh, Pandya, Vyas |
| Ending | Abolition, 25 per cent refund | Tribunal, 35 per cent | Suspension, return of property |

## What each taught about the method

1. **Champaran: facts first.** Inquire, then act; accept a compromise that wins the principle.
2. **Ahmedabad: a defensible demand and non-violence in a strike.** Gandhi lowered the demand from 50 to 35 per cent, kept the strike non-violent and accepted arbitration.
3. **Kheda: discipline, suffering and social pressure.** Farmers accepted confiscation; sympathisers sheltered families and ostracised buyers of confiscated land.

Together they illustrate Spectrum's list of tenets: remain truthful, non-violent and fearless; use withdrawal of cooperation; accept suffering; avoid hatred (Gandhi remained a friend of Ambalal Sarabhai while backing the workers).

## Leaders who joined

Rajendra Prasad, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Mahadev Desai, J.B. Kripalani and Narhari Parekh at Champaran; Anusuya Sarabhai at Ahmedabad; Vallabhbhai Patel, Narahari Parikh, Mohanlal Pandya and Ravi Shankar Vyas at Kheda. Spectrum's gains list says Gandhi found his feet among the masses, understood their strengths and weaknesses better and won respect and commitment, especially from the youth. These were also the lieutenants of later movements; Patel appears again in GS-031 module 7 (Bardoli).

## Reading the labels

Apply bridge B2. 'First civil disobedience': Gandhi defied an order in India; but the Swadeshi boycott of 1905-08 (GS-038) was already a passive-resistance tradition. 'First hunger strike' and 'first non-cooperation' are likewise firsts of Gandhi's Indian career. Spectrum itself calls the Rowlatt satyagraha the first mass strike, which signals that these three were smaller.

## Limits

- **Scale.** NCERT calls them localised struggles; they covered one district, one city and another district.
- **Class.** NCERT says Gandhi wished to build class unity, not class conflict, even though peasants hoped he would help them against landlords; in Ahmedabad he backed the workers without breaking with the owners.
- **Incomplete gains.** Champaran's refund was a quarter of what was taken, and Kheda's concession was a suspension, not the end of the assessment.

## What they set up

The three struggles tested the method locally, recruited lieutenants and showed Gandhi's concern for the poor (the Banaras speech, module 3). In 1919 the Rowlatt Act gave him a countrywide issue (see GS-042). GS-053 module 5 shows the whole Gandhian phase.

### 9. Exam: the three verified questions, frames and source slips

## 2018 Prelims Q69: the significance of Champaran

The options offer: (a) active all-India participation of lawyers, students and women; (b) involvement of Dalit and tribal communities; (c) joining of peasant unrest to the national movement; (d) a drastic fall in plantation and commercial crops. Test each against the extracts. Option (a) describes later countrywide movements, since NCERT calls the 1917-18 struggles localised. Option (b) is not in the Champaran account; NCERT mentions tribal action in the 1921-22 forest satyagrahas. Option (d) does not fit: indigo was already declining because of synthetic dyes, and Champaran did not cause a drastic fall in plantation and commercial crops; Spectrum says only that the planters left within a decade. Option (c) fits NCERT (the struggles marked Gandhi out as a nationalist sympathetic to the poor) and Spectrum (the first civil disobedience, a peasant cause under a nationalist leader). This is analysis, not an official key.

## 2026 Mains Q3 (10 marks): the masses

'The significance of the Gandhian movements lay in the masses they mobilized. Elucidate.' This question is shared with GS-048 (Quit India; see GS-048 modules 8 to 10, where module 10 analyses the Quit India angle) and must be answered across several movements; this lesson supplies only the early evidence. 'Elucidate' means explain with illustrations. Frame: (1) Introduction: Gandhi turned an elite politics into one that reached peasants and workers (the Banaras speech, February 1916). (2) Champaran tenants, Ahmedabad workers, Kheda farmers: three groups reached through concrete grievances. (3) Method: local organisers and satyagraha discipline. (4) Significance beyond numbers: lieutenants recruited, method tested, local struggles linked to the national movement. (5) Limits: localised, and class unity rather than class conflict. (6) Conclusion: their larger significance was seen in 1919-22 and later.

## 2018 Mains Q3 (10 marks): Gandhi's thought today

'Throw light on the significance of the thoughts of Mahatma Gandhi in the present times.' The early struggles give five usable points: non-violent dissent with discipline, fact-finding before agitation, arbitration, a concern for the poor and the farmer, and the unity of means and ends. This lesson cannot supply present-day examples; choose them from your own current-affairs notes and give a balanced view that notes critics' limits (verify any recent example).

### Notes: Definitions

- Satyagraha: resisting wrong by truth and non-violence, without hatred, and with readiness to suffer.
- Civil disobedience: peaceful, deliberate defiance of an order or law one holds unjust, accepting the penalty.
- Tinkathia system: forced cultivation of indigo on 3/20 of the peasant's land in Champaran.
- Remission: the state's excusing of all or part of land revenue in a bad year.
- Tribunal: a body of arbiters whose award both sides accept.
- Plague bonus: an allowance paid to Ahmedabad mill workers, whose withdrawal started the dispute.

### Notes: Chronology, 1869 to 1919

- 2 October 1869: Gandhi born at Porbandar.
- 1893: goes to South Africa; stays until 1914.
- 1906: satyagraha begins against the registration certificates.
- January 1915: returns to India (9 January is standard; verify).
- February 1916: Banaras Hindu University speech.
- December 1916: Lucknow Congress; Champaran peasant meets Gandhi.
- 1917: Champaran satyagraha (arrival April; verify).
- March 1918: Ahmedabad mill strike and fast.
- 1918: Kheda satyagraha.
- March 1919: Rowlatt Act passed (Spectrum p368); satyagraha call for 6 April 1919 (NCERT Class VIII).

### Notes: Tenets of satyagraha (Spectrum)

- Do not submit to what you consider wrong; stay truthful, non-violent and fearless.
- Use withdrawal of cooperation and boycott; refuse taxes and honours if needed.
- Accept suffering as part of love of truth.
- Bear no hatred for the wrongdoer.
- Never bow before evil whatever the consequence; only the brave can practise it.
- Do not separate thought from practice; ends cannot justify means.

### Notes: Cause and effect in the three struggles

- Synthetic dyes lowered indigo profits, so planters pressed tenants for dues, which led to Champaran.
- War inflation doubled prices, workers wanted more pay, owners withdrew the plague bonus, which led to the strike.
- Crop failure and a refusal of remission led to the Kheda no-tax campaign.
- Local successes recruited lieutenants and a method, which prepared Gandhi for the Rowlatt satyagraha.

### Notes: Leaders who joined

- Rajendra Prasad, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parekh, J.B. Kripalani (Champaran).
- Brajkishore Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, Ramnavmi Prasad, Shambhusharan Varma (Champaran popular leaders).
- Anusuya Sarabhai (Ahmedabad; later the Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association, 1920).
- Vallabhbhai Patel, Narahari Parikh, Mohanlal Pandya, Ravi Shankar Vyas (Kheda).

### Notes: Reading the 'first' labels

- Champaran: first civil disobedience in Gandhi's Indian career; the Swadeshi boycott of 1905-08 (GS-038) already used passive resistance.
- Ahmedabad: first hunger strike; Spectrum calls the fast his first, read as his first in a public struggle in India (he made penitential fasts in South Africa in 1913-14; verify).
- Kheda: first non-cooperation; a local no-tax campaign, not the movement of 1920-22.
- Rowlatt: first mass strike (Spectrum); NCERT says it was largely restricted to cities.

### Notes: Frequently confused

- Kheda non-payment campaign against the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920-22.

### Active recall

- **coreConcepts:**
  - Champaran, Ahmedabad and Kheda were local struggles through which Gandhi tested satyagraha in India.
  - Each combined a concrete grievance, non-violent discipline and a negotiated settlement.
  - Local organisers joined Gandhi and became his lieutenants.
  - The 'first' labels are shorthand for methods first tested in India.
- **essentialFacts:**
  - Gandhi returned to India in January 1915 and spent a year touring on Gokhale's advice.

### Memory skeleton

- South Africa 1893-1914: petitions, then satyagraha from 1906
- January 1915 return; a year of touring on Gokhale's advice
- Champaran 1917: indigo tenants vs planters, tinkathia, first civil disobedience
- Ahmedabad March 1918: mill workers vs owners, fast, 35 per cent award
- Kheda 1918: farmers vs government, revenue remission, first non-cooperation
- Gains: method tested, lieutenants recruited, road to the Rowlatt satyagraha of 1919

### Last-week summary

- Champaran 1917 (Bihar): tinkathia indigo on 3/20 of land; Rajkumar Shukla; Gandhi defied the order, inquiry, tinkathia abolished, 25 per cent refund; first civil disobedience.
- Ahmedabad March 1918: plague bonus withdrawn; workers asked 50, owners 20, tribunal awarded 35 per cent; Anusuya Sarabhai; Gandhi's first hunger strike.
- Kheda 1918: remission due if yield below one-fourth; non-payment of revenue; Patel, Parikh, Pandya, Vyas; suspension; first non-cooperation.
- All before the Rowlatt Act (March 1919), not 1918; 'first' means Gandhi's first use in India.
