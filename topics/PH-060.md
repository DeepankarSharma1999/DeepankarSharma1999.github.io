### Bridge: Reading the axes of a tilted ellipse from its equation

An ellipse centred on the origin and not lined up with the y and z axes has an equation of the form A y² + B y z + C z² = 1. You can read its longest and shortest radii without rotating anything. Write the coefficients as a square array with A and C on the diagonal and B/2 twice off the diagonal. Its two **eigenvalues** λ₁ and λ₂ are the two numbers λ that solve (A − λ)(C − λ) − (B/2)² = 0. The semi-axes are then 1/√λ₁ and 1/√λ₂, and the larger eigenvalue belongs to the shorter axis. Two facts save the work: the sum λ₁ + λ₂ equals the trace A + C, and the product λ₁ λ₂ equals the determinant A C − B²/4.

Worked example: 2 y² + 2 y z + 2 z² = 1. The trace is 4 and the determinant is 2 × 2 − 1 = 3, so λ₁ + λ₂ = 4 and λ₁ λ₂ = 3, giving λ = 1 and λ = 3. The semi-axes are 1/√1 = 1 and 1/√3 = 0.577. Direct check: along y = z = s the equation reads 6 s² = 1, so s = 0.408 and the radius is s √2 = 0.577 (the short axis lies along the diagonal y = z). Along y = s, z = −s the equation reads 2 s² − 2 s² + 2 s² = 2 s² = 1, so s = 0.707 and the radius is s √2 = 1 (the long axis).

If B = 0 the array is already diagonal and the eigenvalues are simply A and C, which is the untilted ellipse y²/a² + z²/b² = 1 with A = 1/a² and C = 1/b².

### 1. Orientation: what a state of polarisation is, and what this lesson adds to PH-207

*Objective:* Say in your own words what a state of polarisation is, name the five states, and list what this lesson adds to the Day 17 lesson PH-207.

## What a state is

Light is a wave whose electric field E points across the direction of travel. At one point in space the tip of E moves as time passes. The path it traces is the **state of polarisation** (Brijlal Section 20.5, PDF p. 501). Three paths are possible for a fully polarised beam: a straight line (**linear** or plane polarisation), a circle (**circular**) and an ellipse (**elliptical**). Light from ordinary sources has no steady path at all (**natural** or unpolarised light), and a mixture of a steady path with natural light is **partially polarised**. The eye cannot tell these apart (PDF p. 501), so every test turns a difference of polarisation into a difference of brightness.

## Where PH-207 stops and this lesson starts

The Day 17 lesson PH-207 (Production and detection of linearly/circularly polarised light) already contains, in its section 'Core derivation: two perpendicular coherent vibrations give a line, a circle or an ellipse', the elimination of time that gives the ellipse equation (Brijlal 20.43), its tilt formula and the five special cases. It also derives Malus's law, the quarter-wave and half-wave plates and the analyser-plus-plate scheme. Do not re-derive these; open PH-207 when a step is only quoted here.

### 2. Foundation: two numbers that fix the shape, and a table of values at sampled times

*Objective:* Set up the two perpendicular vibrations with a stated coordinate system, read the amplitude ratio and phase difference, and sample a state at the four quarter-periods.

## The set-up (assumptions first)

Light travels along +x. The electric field lies in the y–z plane, and (y, z, x) is a right-handed set, meaning y × z points along +x. At the point x = 0 the two components of E are

$$ E_y = E₁ cos ωt,   E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ) $$

This is the same pair as Brijlal equations 20.37 and 20.38 (PDF pp. 529 to 530) evaluated at x = 0, where the lesson PH-207 writes cos(kx − ωt + δ); at x = 0 that equals cos(ωt − δ). The vibrations are **coherent** (fixed δ) and have the same ω. E₁ and E₂ are the amplitudes and δ is the phase by which E_z **lags** E_y. (Brijlal may say E_z leads, because the phase argument kx − ωt + δ is larger by δ; at a fixed point E_z peaks later in time, which is the lag used here. The sin δ test of module 4 is unaffected.) A state is therefore fixed by three numbers, but the overall intensity E₁² + E₂² is just a scale, so the *shape* depends on the ratio E₂/E₁ and on δ. An equivalent pair is the angle ψ with tan ψ = E₂/E₁ (the angle of the box diagonal from the y-axis) and δ.

## Sampling at quarter-periods

Sampling is the quickest way to see what a state looks like. Take E₁ = 3, E₂ = 2 and δ = π/2 (E_z lags by a quarter of a period):

| ωt | E_y = 3 cos ωt | E_z = 2 cos(ωt − π/2) = 2 sin ωt | Where the tip is |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 3 | 0 | on the +y axis |
| π/2 | 0 | 2 | on the +z axis |
| π | −3 | 0 | on the −y axis |
| 3π/2 | 0 | −2 | on the −z axis |

The tip goes +y, +z, −y, −z: from y towards z. Because the points satisfy (E_y/3)² + (E_z/2)² = cos² + sin² = 1, the path is an ellipse with semi-axes 3 (along y) and 2 (along z). If E₁ = E₂ the same table gives a circle.

## The ladder of phase differences for equal amplitudes

For E₁ = E₂ = 1 the shape changes with δ as follows (all values recomputed in Python; b/a is the ratio of the minor to major semi-axis):

| δ | Shape | Major axis at | b/a |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | line | +45° from y towards z | 0 |
| π/4 | thin ellipse | +45° | 0.414 |
| π/2 | circle | none | 1 |
| 3π/4 | thin ellipse | −45° | 0.414 |
| π | line | −45° | 0 |
| 5π/4 | thin ellipse | −45° | 0.414 |
| 3π/2 | circle | none | 1 |
| 7π/4 | thin ellipse | +45° | 0.414 |

This is Brijlal Fig. 20.41 extended to a full cycle. Notice that δ and 2π − δ give the **same ellipse traced in opposite senses**: the sense is read from sin δ (module 4), not from the shape. For 0 ≤ δ ≤ π/2 and E₁ = E₂ the ratio is b/a = tan(δ/2): tan(π/8) = 0.414.

## Coherent against incoherent

The ellipse needs δ to stay fixed. In natural light δ wanders at random, so no single ellipse survives; module 7 treats that case. Brijlal states the same contrast: orthogonal coherent waves give a line, circle or ellipse, while waves with a common plane of polarisation simply add (PDF p. 529).

### 3. Core derivation: the size, shape and tilt of the polarisation ellipse (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive a² + b² = E₁² + E₂² and a b = E₁ E₂ |sin δ|, solve for the semi-axes, and compute the tilt with the correct quadrant for a worked example.

## Setting and assumptions

Light travels along +x, (y, z, x) is right-handed, and the components at x = 0 are E_y = E₁ cos ωt and E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ) with δ not equal to 0 or π (module 2). The ellipse equation, obtained in PH-207 (section 'Core derivation: two perpendicular coherent vibrations give a line, a circle or an ellipse') and printed as Brijlal equation 20.43 (PDF p. 530), is

$$ E_y²/E₁² + E_z²/E₂² − 2 (E_y E_z / E₁E₂) cos δ = sin²δ $$

## Derivation 1: the axes

1. Divide by sin²δ to put it in the form A E_y² + B E_y E_z + C E_z² = 1:

$$ A = 1/(E₁² sin²δ),   C = 1/(E₂² sin²δ),   B = −2 cos δ/(E₁E₂ sin²δ) $$

2. By bridge module B1 the numbers 1/a² and 1/b² are the eigenvalues of the array (A, B/2; B/2, C). Trace: A + C = (E₁² + E₂²)/(E₁² E₂² sin²δ).
3. Determinant: A C − B²/4 = (1 − cos²δ)/(E₁² E₂² sin⁴δ) = 1/(E₁² E₂² sin²δ).
4. Sum and product of 1/a² and 1/b²: (a² + b²)/(a² b²) = trace and 1/(a² b²) = determinant. From the second, **a b = E₁ E₂ |sin δ|**. Multiply the first by a² b² = E₁² E₂² sin²δ:

$$ a² + b² = E₁² + E₂² $$

This says the total intensity is the sum of the two component intensities, as it must be.
5. Write S = E₁² + E₂² and P = E₁ E₂ sin δ. Then a² and b² are the roots of u² − S u + P² = 0:

$$ a², b² = ½ [ S ± √(S² − 4P²) ] = ½ [ E₁² + E₂² ± √((E₁² − E₂²)² + 4 E₁² E₂² cos²δ) ] $$

The last form uses S² − 4P² = (E₁² + E₂²)² − 4E₁²E₂² + 4E₁²E₂² cos²δ.
6. **Tilt.** The long axis makes angle α with the y-axis (from y towards z), with tan 2α = 2 E₁ E₂ cos δ / (E₁² − E₂²) (PH-207; Brijlal p. 530). Two angles 90° apart satisfy this. To pick the long axis, take 2α in the quadrant where sin 2α has the sign of cos δ and cos 2α has the sign of E₁² − E₂² (in code: 2α = atan2(2E₁E₂ cos δ, E₁² − E₂²)). The reason is in module 6.

## Worked example (recomputed in Python)

E₁ = 3, E₂ = 2, δ = π/3. Then S = 13, P = 6 sin 60° = 5.196, E₁² − E₂² = 5, 2E₁E₂ cos δ = 6.

- √(5² + 6²) = √61 = 7.810, so a² = (13 + 7.810)/2 = 10.405 and b² = 2.595. Hence **a = 3.226, b = 1.611**, b/a = 0.499.
- Check: a b = 5.196 = P ✓ and a² + b² = 13 ✓.
- Tilt: 2α = atan2(6, 5) = 50.19°, so **α = 25.10°** from y towards z.
- Sampling both components over 200 000 times gives largest radius 3.2257 and smallest 1.6109 ✓.
- Sense: sin δ > 0, so the tip turns from y towards z (module 4).

## Limiting cases and checks

- **δ = π/2:** the root is |E₁² − E₂²|, so a = larger of E₁, E₂ and b = the smaller, with axes along y and z (Brijlal 20.46).
- **E₁ = E₂ = E:** the root is 2E² |cos δ|, so a² = E²(1 + |cos δ|), b² = E²(1 − |cos δ|), and for 0 < δ ≤ π/2 the ratio is b/a = tan(δ/2), matching the ladder table of module 2.
- **δ → 0:** b → 0 and a² → E₁² + E₂²: a line of length √(E₁² + E₂²) (its angle is tan⁻¹(E₂/E₁); the extract prints tan⁻¹(E₂ − E₁), see PH-207).


### 4. Core: handedness - the sign of sin δ, the viewpoint and the helix in space

*Objective:* Decide, from δ alone, whether light is right or left elliptical or circular under the Brijlal Chapter 20 rule and under the right-hand-rule naming, and state the viewpoint.

## Why this needs care

The same motion has two names in common use, and an answer that says only 'right circular' can be marked either way. The fix is to state the convention and the viewpoint every time. Brijlal Chapter 20 (PDF pp. 503 to 504) says: if the tip of E turns **clockwise as seen by an observer looking back towards the source**, the light is **right** circularly (or elliptically) polarised; anticlockwise is **left**.

## Step 1: the sense of rotation in time (derivation 2)

Use the pair of module 2 at x = 0. Define L = E_y (dE_z/dt) − E_z (dE_y/dt); L > 0 when the tip moves from +y towards +z.

1. dE_z/dt = −ω E₂ sin(ωt − δ) and dE_y/dt = −ω E₁ sin ωt.
2. L = ω E₁E₂ [ −cos ωt sin(ωt − δ) + cos(ωt − δ) sin ωt ].
3. Use sin A cos B − cos A sin B = sin(A − B) with A = ωt and B = ωt − δ: the bracket is sin(ωt − (ωt − δ)) = sin δ.

$$ L = ω E₁ E₂ sin δ $$

So **sin δ > 0: the tip turns y → z; sin δ < 0: it turns z → y.** A sweep over δ in Python confirms the sign (δ = π/2 gives L = +1, δ = −π/2 gives −1 for E₁ = E₂ = 1).

## Step 2: the viewpoint

The observer who looks back at the source faces the −x direction, and the +x axis points at the observer. With (y, z, x) right-handed, a rotation y → z is anticlockwise for that observer. Therefore, under Brijlal Chapter 20:

- **sin δ > 0: left.**
- **sin δ < 0: right.**

## Step 3: the helix in space (frozen time)

At a fixed instant the wave is E_y = E₁ cos(kx − ωt), E_z = E₂ cos(kx − ωt + δ). For circular light take E₁ = E₂ and δ = −π/2, so E_z = sin(kx − ωt). At t = 0 the direction of E makes angle kx with the y-axis, measured from y towards z, so it turns by 2π over one wavelength. Curling the fingers of the right hand from y towards z puts the thumb along +x (y × z = x), so an angle that grows with x is a **right-handed screw**. In time at a fixed x the angle kx − ωt decreases: z → y, clockwise to the observer. So Chapter 20's right-handed light is also a right-handed screw in space (checked by sampling). The statement 'right is clockwise to the observer' and 'right is a right-handed screw' agree.

## The two namings side by side

| | sin δ > 0 | sin δ < 0 |
|---|---|---|
| Tip turns | y → z | z → y |
| Seen by observer facing the source | anticlockwise | clockwise |
| Brijlal Chapter 20 name | left | right |
| Helix in space (frozen time) | left-handed screw | right-handed screw |
| Right-hand-rule name (thumb along propagation, fingers along the rotation) | right | left |

The last row is the naming used in some other texts and in antenna engineering; PH-207 notes that Brijlal Chapter 13 uses a thumb-along-propagation rule that can name the same motion the other way. The physics is the same. (The antenna-engineering attribution is standard knowledge: verify.)

## Reversal and the practical rule

A half-wave plate or a mirror at normal incidence reverses the name (PH-207, section 'Core: producing circular and elliptical light, handedness and left-right conversion'; Brijlal PDF pp. 534 and 545). **Rule for answers:** write E_y, E_z and δ, state the sign of sin δ, give the viewpoint and the convention, then name the hand.

### 5. Master: every fully polarised state as a sum of a right and a left circular part

*Objective:* Show that a left part of amplitude A_L and a right part of amplitude A_R add to an ellipse with a = A_L + A_R and b = |A_L − A_R|, and convert in both directions for a worked example.

## Why a second basis

Module 2 built every state from two linear components. A second description uses two **circular** components, one turning each way. The extract uses it for optical activity: linearly polarised light is a resultant of two circular vibrations rotating in opposite directions with the same angular velocity (Brijlal Section 20.30, PDF p. 548). Here it gives a quick way to see the ellipse and to see what changes the tilt.

## Derivation 3: adding a left and a right circle

**Set-up.** At x = 0 let the left part (turning y → z, Chapter 20 'left') and the right part (turning z → y) be

$$ L: (A_L cos a,  A_L sin a),   R: (A_R cos b′,  −A_R sin b′),   a = ωt + θ_L,  b′ = ωt + θ_R $$

(b′ is used to avoid confusion with the semi-minor axis b.)

1. Add: E_y = A_L cos a + A_R cos b′ and E_z = A_L sin a − A_R sin b′.
2. Square and add: |E|² = A_L² + A_R² + 2 A_L A_R (cos a cos b′ − sin a sin b′) = A_L² + A_R² + 2 A_L A_R cos(a + b′), with a + b′ = 2ωt + θ_L + θ_R.
3. The cosine runs between −1 and +1, so |E| runs between |A_L − A_R| and A_L + A_R:

$$ a = A_L + A_R (semi-major),   b = |A_L − A_R| (semi-minor) $$

4. **Direction of the long axis.** |E| is largest when a + b′ = 0, that is a = θ₀ and b′ = −θ₀ with θ₀ = (θ_L − θ_R)/2. There E_y = (A_L + A_R) cos θ₀ and E_z = (A_L + A_R) sin θ₀: the long axis makes angle **θ₀ = (θ_L − θ_R)/2** with the y-axis.
5. **Which hand.** The larger part wins: A_L > A_R gives left (y → z), A_R > A_L gives right. A_R = 0 is a pure circle; A_L = A_R gives |E| from 0 to 2A: a line through the origin along θ₀.

## Converting a state (recomputed in Python)

For E₁ = 3, E₂ = 2, δ = π/3 (module 3: a = 3.226, b = 1.611, α = 25.10°, sin δ > 0 so left):

- A_L = (a + b)/2 = 2.418 and A_R = (a − b)/2 = 0.807.
- Two invariants make this automatic: **A_L² + A_R² = (E₁² + E₂²)/2** (= 6.5; 5.848 + 0.652 = 6.500 ✓) and **A_L² − A_R² = E₁E₂ sin δ** (= 5.196; 5.848 − 0.652 = 5.196 ✓), the signed product a b. Their ratio, 2E₁E₂ sin δ/(E₁² + E₂²) = 0.80, is 0 for a line and ±1 for a circle.
- A separate sampled test with A_L = 1.5, A_R = 0.5 and θ_L − θ_R = −60° gave largest radius 2.000, smallest radius 1.000 and the long axis at −30° = (θ_L − θ_R)/2 ✓ (a = A_L + A_R = 2, b = A_L − A_R = 1).

## What changes the tilt

If the right part is delayed by an extra phase Δ relative to the left part, θ_R changes by −Δ and the long axis turns by **Δ/2**. A line of equal parts therefore stays a line and rotates by Δ/2, which is Fresnel's picture of optical rotation: in quartz the two circular parts travel with different speeds, so after thickness d the angle is π|μ_R − μ_L| d/λ, one half of the phase 2π|μ_R − μ_L| d/λ (Brijlal 20.58, PDF p. 549). The material side belongs to Day 22 lesson PH-062.

Both bases use three real numbers ((E₁, E₂, δ) or (A_L, A_R, θ_L − θ_R)), so both are complete for a fully polarised beam.

### 6. Core: what a turning analyser measures for any state (derivation 4)

*Objective:* Derive I(φ) for the general state, show that its maximum and minimum are a² and b², and read the signature of each state from the readings of a turned analyser.

## Setting and assumptions

An ideal analyser has its transmission axis at angle φ from the y-axis towards the z-axis. It passes only the component of E along that axis. The incident beam is the state of module 2, E_y = E₁ cos ωt and E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ). Intensity is the time average of the square of the field, and we use units in which the incident beam has intensity E₁² + E₂².

## Derivation 4: the analyser formula

1. The passed field is E_a = E_y cos φ + E_z sin φ = (E₁ cos φ) cos ωt + (E₂ sin φ) cos(ωt − δ).
2. This is the sum of two vibrations of the same frequency with amplitudes p = E₁ cos φ and q = E₂ sin φ and phase difference δ. Their resultant has amplitude squared p² + q² + 2 p q cos δ (the two-beam formula of PH-038).
3. The time average of E_a² is half of that amplitude squared, and the time average of the incident |E|² is half of E₁² + E₂², so in the chosen units

$$ I(φ) = E₁² cos²φ + E₂² sin²φ + E₁E₂ sin 2φ cos δ $$

4. Use cos²φ = (1 + cos 2φ)/2 and sin²φ = (1 − cos 2φ)/2:

$$ I(φ) = (E₁² + E₂²)/2 + [(E₁² − E₂²)/2] cos 2φ + E₁E₂ cos δ sin 2φ $$

5. A cos 2φ + B sin 2φ varies between −√(A² + B²) and +√(A² + B²), so I runs between (S ± R)/2 with S = E₁² + E₂² and R = √((E₁² − E₂²)² + 4E₁²E₂² cos²δ). Comparing with module 3 these are exactly a² and b²:

$$ I_max = a²,   I_min = b²,   I_max + I_min = E₁² + E₂² $$

6. The maximum occurs at 2φ = atan2(2E₁E₂ cos δ, E₁² − E₂²), that is, along the long axis; this is the quadrant rule of module 3. I(φ + 90°) + I(φ) = S for every φ, and I repeats every 180°: **two maxima and two minima per full turn**.

## Checks and special cases

- **Linear (δ = 0):** I = (E₁ cos φ + E₂ sin φ)² = (E₁² + E₂²) cos²(φ − ψ) with tan ψ = E₂/E₁: Malus's law, with I_min = 0.
- **Circular (E₁ = E₂, δ = ±π/2):** I = E² (cos²φ + sin²φ) = E², constant and equal to half of the incident E₁² + E₂² = 2E².
- **Elliptical with axes on y and z (δ = ±π/2):** I = E₁² cos²φ + E₂² sin²φ; the maximum E₁² is along y, as the extract states for an ellipse (the maximum lies along the semi-major axis, PDF p. 521).
- **Worked example** (E₁ = 3, E₂ = 2, δ = π/3, units E²): I(0°) = 9.00, I(25.1°) = 10.405 (maximum, a²), I(30°) = 10.35, I(45°) = 9.50, I(60°) = 7.85, I(90°) = 4.00, I(115.1°) = 2.595 (minimum, b²), I(135°) = 3.50. The ratio I_max/I_min = (a/b)² = 4.01.

## The signature table

| State | I_max / I_min | Zeros per turn | Long axis located by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | infinite | 2 | the maximum direction |
| Elliptical | (a/b)², finite and above 1 | none | the maximum direction |
| Circular | 1 | none | not defined |
| Natural | 1 | none | not defined |
| Partially polarised | finite and above 1 | none | the maximum direction |

What the analyser **cannot** tell: the hand (module 4), circular from natural, or elliptical from partial. Brijlal gives the same classification (PDF pp. 536 to 537), and PH-207 (section 'Master: detection and analysis of the state of polarisation with an analyser and a quarter-wave plate') adds the quarter-wave plate step that separates the pairs.

### 7. Master: natural and partially polarised light as incoherent sums, and the degree of polarisation

*Objective:* Average the analyser formula over a random phase difference to model natural and partially polarised light, compute P, and show a case where an elliptical beam and a partially polarised beam give the same analyser readings.

## Incoherent means the phase wanders

In module 2 the phase difference δ was fixed. In ordinary light each atom radiates a wave train that lasts about 10⁻⁸ s and each train has its own polarisation, with no correlation between trains (Brijlal Section 20.4, PDF p. 500). Brijlal's picture of natural light is therefore two equal perpendicular components with a **randomly changing** phase difference (Fig. 20.4(b)). A detector averages over very many trains, so every δ occurs equally often and the average of cos δ is zero.

## Derivation 5: natural and partial light from the analyser formula

Take module 6's I(φ) = E₁² cos²φ + E₂² sin²φ + E₁E₂ sin 2φ cos δ and replace cos δ by its average, 0:

$$ I(φ) = E₁² cos²φ + E₂² sin²φ $$

- **E₁ = E₂ = E (natural):** I = E² for every φ, half of the incident 2E². This is why one ideal polariser halves natural light (Brijlal 20.9, PDF p. 511). A Python test with 200 000 random δ gave 1.000, 0.998, 0.998, 1.000 at φ = 0°, 30°, 45°, 90° for E = 1 (the spread is sampling noise).
- **E₁ ≠ E₂ (partially polarised):** the two components are incoherent and unequal, which is Brijlal's description of partial polarisation (PDF p. 505). I runs from E₂² to E₁² with maximum along y and minimum along z, never zero.

## The degree of polarisation

Brijlal defines P = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) (20.1, PDF p. 505) and notes that P = 1 for linear light, P = 0 for natural light, and that if I_max = 2 I_min then P = 1/3. Example with E₁ = 2, E₂ = 1: I_max = 4, I_min = 1, P = (4 − 1)/(4 + 1) = **0.6**.

**Why P is the polarised fraction.** Split the beam into a linear part I_p (along the maximum direction) and natural light I_n. The natural part contributes I_n/2 at every angle, so I_max = I_p + I_n/2 and I_min = I_n/2. Then

$$ P = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = I_p/(I_p + I_n) $$

For the example, I_n = 2 I_min = 2 and I_p = 3, with total 5 = 4 + 1 ✓ and P = 3/5 ✓. The decomposition is a standard identity (the lesson's own derivation, verify), not a physical claim that the beam is made of two separate sources.

## An elliptical beam that looks partially polarised

Take the ellipse with E₁ = 2, E₂ = 1, δ = π/2 (axes 2 and 1 on y and z). Module 6 gives I(φ) = 4 cos²φ + sin²φ. The partially polarised beam above (unequal, incoherent amplitudes 2 and 1) gives the same function. Both give 4, 3.25, 2.5, 1.75, 1 at φ = 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°. **The analyser alone cannot tell them apart.** Brijlal says the same (PDF pp. 536 to 537): rotating the analyser gives a maximum and a non-zero minimum for either, and a quarter-wave plate is inserted to separate them. With the plate's axis along the maximum direction the ellipse becomes linear, so a second reading gives I_min = 0, while the partially polarised beam keeps I_min = 1 (the incoherent components stay incoherent).

## Why P is not defined for the ellipse

For a fully polarised ellipse the ratio I_max/I_min = (a/b)² is finite, so a formula like P would give a number below 1 although the light is completely polarised. For that reason Brijlal states that the degree of polarisation does not apply to circular and elliptical light (PDF p. 505).

### 8. Master: an ideal retarder shifts the phase difference, and what that does to a state

*Objective:* Use the rule 'a retarder changes δ to δ + Δ in its own axes' to predict the output of quarter-wave and half-wave plates on linear, circular and elliptical input.

## The plate as a black box

A **retarder** is a uniform plate of birefringent material with its optic axis in the plane of the plate. It splits the incident wave into two polarised waves that travel at different speeds and so adds a phase retardation of one relative to the other (Brijlal Section 20.19, PDF p. 532). Quarter-wave means a retardation Δ = π/2, half-wave Δ = π, full-wave Δ = 2π. The crystal physics, thickness formulas and the o-ray and e-ray are in the Day 17 lesson PH-207 and the Day 21 lesson PH-061. Here the plate is an ideal lossless element with a **fast axis u** and a **slow axis v** at right angles; the v component is delayed by Δ relative to the u component. Taking (u, v, propagation) as a right-handed set, as (y, z, x) was in module 2, the language of modules 2 to 4 applies unchanged.

## The one rule

Write the incoming state in the plate's axes: E_u = E_a cos ωt and E_v = E_b cos(ωt − δ_uv). The plate returns **the same amplitudes and the phase difference δ_uv + Δ**. Everything else is module 3 (shape) and module 4 (hand) applied to the new phase.

## Consequences (all checked numerically by Python)

1. **Linear in, quarter-wave.** Input at angle θ from u: δ_uv = 0, so δ′ = π/2. The output is an ellipse with axes along u and v and semi-axes E cos θ and E sin θ; θ = 45° gives a circle. Since sin δ′ > 0 the hand is left under Chapter 20 for this orientation (fast axis along y, slow along z, input between +y and +z). Interchanging fast and slow, or taking θ negative, gives right. PH-207 gets 'right' for calcite because its fast axis is z.
2. **Ellipse in, quarter-wave aligned with the ellipse.** Let u lie along the long axis. Then δ_uv = ±π/2 and δ′ = π or 0: **linear**, at angle ± tan⁻¹(b/a) from u. For the ellipse of module 3 (a = 3.226, b = 1.611, long axis at 25.10°, left) tan⁻¹(b/a) = 26.54°. With u fast the output line is at 25.10° − 26.54° = −1.44°; with the plate turned so that u is slow it is at 25.10° + 26.54° = 51.63°. Circular input (a = b) gives a line at ±45° from u. This is the reasoning behind the detection scheme in Brijlal Section 20.22 and the condition PH-207 states for it.
3. **Half-wave plate.** δ′ = δ_uv + π flips the sign of the v component, which is a **reflection of the state in the u axis**. If u makes angle β with y, the long axis moves from α to **2β − α** and the hand reverses. Check: β = 20° and α = 25.10° give 14.90° (sampled 14.9°); β = 0 gives −25.10°. A line at θ from u goes to −θ, a rotation by 2θ, which is Brijlal's statement (PDF p. 534). Circular light becomes the opposite circular light for any β.
4. **Combinations.** Two identical quarter-wave plates with parallel axes add to Δ = π (a half-wave plate); with crossed axes the retardations cancel.

## Wavelength matters

Δ = 2π (μ_o − μ_e) d/λ grows as 1/λ if the indices are treated as constant. A plate that is a quarter-wave at 589.3 nm gives 90° × 589.3/500 = 106° at 500 nm, so the output at the wrong wavelength is a different ellipse. Fresnel's rhomb, which PH-207 treats, avoids this.

### 9. Exam: the verified 2017 question (reflection from a calm lake) and likely variants

*Objective:* Solve the verified 2017 Q1(c) with stated assumptions, write it as a 10-mark answer, and apply the lesson's tools to two likely variants.

## The question as printed

**2017 Q1(c) (10 marks):** 'Sunlight is reflected from a calm lake. The reflected light is 100% polarized at a certain instant. What is the angle between the sun and horizon?' (Official-paper stem as verified in the project; no answer key is asserted here.)

## What the stem leaves out

It gives no refractive index of water. Use μ ≈ 4/3 = 1.33, a standard value that is not in the extract (verify). It also says 'horizon', which for a calm lake is the horizontal surface, so the angle asked for is the **elevation of the sun above the horizon**. A second reading, the angle from the vertical, is 90° minus that; state both.

## Solution (Brewster's law)

1. Complete polarisation of the reflected beam means incidence at the polarising angle θ_B, where the reflected and refracted rays are at right angles: θ_B + r = 90° (Brijlal PDF p. 506).
2. Snell's law: sin θ_B = μ sin r = μ cos θ_B, so **tan θ_B = μ** (Brijlal 20.3 and 20.6).
3. Numbers (recomputed): μ = 4/3 gives θ_B = tan⁻¹(1.3333) = **53.13°** from the normal. For a calm lake the normal is vertical, so the incident rays make 53.13° with the vertical and the sun's elevation is 90° − 53.13° = **36.87°, about 37°**. With μ = 1.33: θ_B = 53.06°, elevation 36.94°.
4. Check: r = 90° − 53.13° = 36.87°, sin r = 0.600, and sin θ_B/μ = 0.800/1.3333 = 0.600 ✓. The reflected ray also leaves 36.87° above the horizon.
5. Interpretation: the reflected light is linearly polarised with E horizontal (perpendicular to the plane of incidence), which is why polarising sunglasses with a vertical transmission axis cut the glare. The reflected fraction is small: R_s = ((μ² − 1)/(μ² + 1))² = 0.0784 for the perpendicular component, so only (0.0784 + 0)/2 = 3.9% of the incident natural light is reflected. 'At a certain instant' reminds you that the sun's elevation changes through the day, so the condition holds only at one time (twice a day, if the sun reaches that elevation). A real lake surface is never perfectly calm.

### Notes: Definitions

- State of polarisation: the locus of the tip of the electric vector E, in the plane across the beam, at one point as time passes (Brijlal PDF p. 501).
- Linear (plane) polarisation: the tip moves along a line; circular: along a circle; elliptical: along an ellipse; natural: no steady path; partially polarised: a steady linear part plus natural light.
- Coherent components keep a fixed phase difference; incoherent ones do not. Perpendicular coherent components give a line, a circle or an ellipse (orthogonal addition); collinear coherent components simply add (Brijlal PDF p. 529).
- Phase difference δ in this lesson: E_y = E₁ cos ωt, E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ); E_z lags E_y by δ; light travels along +x and (y, z, x) is right-handed.
- Semi-major axis a and semi-minor axis b of the polarisation ellipse: a² + b² = E₁² + E₂² and a b = E₁ E₂ |sin δ|.
- Right (Brijlal Chapter 20): the tip turns clockwise as seen by an observer looking back towards the source; left: anticlockwise. In this lesson's axes, sin δ < 0 is right and sin δ > 0 is left.
- Analyser: a polariser used to examine light; its reading I(φ) is the time-averaged intensity of the component of E along its axis at angle φ.
- Degree of polarisation P = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min); defined for linear, partial and natural light; not applied to circular or elliptical light (Brijlal PDF p. 505).
- Retarder: a plate that delays one of two perpendicular components relative to the other by Δ (quarter-wave π/2, half-wave π, full-wave 2π); an ideal one changes δ to δ + Δ in its own axes.

### Notes: Equations to know

- E_y = E₁ cos ωt, E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ)
- Ellipse: E_y²/E₁² + E_z²/E₂² − 2 (E_y E_z/E₁E₂) cos δ = sin²δ (Brijlal 20.43)
- Axes: a² + b² = E₁² + E₂²; a b = E₁E₂ |sin δ|; a², b² = ½[E₁² + E₂² ± √((E₁² − E₂²)² + 4E₁²E₂² cos²δ)]
- Tilt of the long axis from y towards z: 2α = atan2(2E₁E₂ cos δ, E₁² − E₂²)
- Sense of rotation: L = E_y dE_z/dt − E_z dE_y/dt = ω E₁E₂ sin δ; L > 0 means y → z
- Equal amplitudes, 0 < δ ≤ π/2: b/a = tan(δ/2), long axis at +45°

### Notes: Confused concepts

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-PH-MALUS (verified):** Malus's law: when linearly polarised light of intensity I0 passes through a polarising filter whose axis is at angle theta to its polarisation direction, the transmitted intensity is I = I0 cos^2(theta).
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 1.7 'Polarization'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization)
- **F-PH-POLHALF (verified):** Unpolarised light passing through a first ideal polarising filter is polarised along the filter axis and its intensity is decreased by a factor of 2.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 1.7 'Polarization'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization)
- **F-PH-BREWSTER (verified):** Brewster's angle: light reflected at angle theta_b with tan(theta_b) = n2/n1 is completely polarised (n1 the medium of incident and reflected light, n2 the reflecting medium); at this angle the reflected and refracted rays are 90 degrees apart.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 1.7 'Polarization'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization)
- **F-PH-POLDIR (verified):** The direction of polarisation of an electromagnetic wave is the direction of its electric field, which is perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Unpolarised light, such as sunlight, is composed of many waves whose electric fields point in all possible directions.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 1.7 'Polarization'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization)
- **F-PH-SKYPOL (verified):** Light scattered by air (blue sky) is partially polarised: looking at the sky through rotating polarising sunglasses, it brightens and dims.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 1.7 'Polarization'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization)
- **F-PH-REFLPOL (verified):** Light reflected from water or glass is partially polarised: when unpolarised light reflects from a surface, vertically polarised light is preferentially refracted, so the reflected light is left more horizontally polarised, and a polarising filter cannot block it completely.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 1.7 'Polarization'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization)

### Last-week summary

- E_y = E₁ cos ωt, E_z = E₂ cos(ωt − δ): the shape needs only cos δ (δ and 2π − δ trace one ellipse); the sense is the sign of sin δ.
- L = ωE₁E₂ sin δ; looking back at the source y → z is anticlockwise: sin δ > 0 left, sin δ < 0 right (Chapter 20), also a right-handed screw; always name convention and viewpoint.
- E₁ = E₂: δ = 0 line at +45°, π/2 circle, π line at −45°; b/a = tan(δ/2) up to π/2. tan 2α = 2E₁E₂ cos δ/(E₁² − E₂²) by atan2; 45° only for equal amplitudes.

- P = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min): 1 linear, 0 natural, not used for circular or elliptical. Lake: tan θ_B = μ from the vertical, elevation 90° − θ_B; μ of water is not in the stem.
