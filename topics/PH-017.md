### Bridge: Density: how a body's mass is spread (linear, surface, volume)

**Density** tells you how much mass sits in a given amount of space. For a uniform body the density is the same everywhere and equals total mass divided by the total size.

- **Linear density** λ (lambda), for a thin rod or wire: λ = M/ℓ, unit kg/m. A piece of length dx has mass **dm = λ dx**.
- **Surface density** σ (sigma), for a thin sheet (a lamina): σ = M/Area, unit kg/m². A piece of area dA has mass **dm = σ dA**.
- **Volume density** ρ (rho), for a solid: ρ = M/Volume, unit kg/m³. A piece of volume dV has mass **dm = ρ dV**.

Worked example. A uniform rod has M = 3 kg and ℓ = 1.2 m, so λ = 3/1.2 = 2.5 kg/m. A piece 0.02 m long has mass 2.5 × 0.02 = 0.05 kg. A uniform disc of radius R has σ = M/(πR²); a uniform solid sphere has ρ = M/(4πR³/3) = 3M/(4πR³); a uniform solid cone has ρ = M/(πR²h/3) = 3M/(πR²h).

Why this matters. Every moment-of-inertia integral in this lesson starts by writing dm for a small piece, and the density is the bridge between the *mass M you are given* and the *size of the piece you draw*. Always eliminate the density at the end by substituting back ρ (or σ, λ) in terms of M, as the problems give M, not ρ.

A small word of warning. The densities above are *uniform* (constant). If a question says the density varies, for example σ proportional to the distance from the centre, then σ is a function of position and must stay inside the integral.

### Bridge: Integration with strips, rings, discs and shells (area and volume elements)

An **integral** ∫ f(x) dx between limits a and b is the sum of f(x) × (a tiny width dx) over all slices from x = a to x = b. The only rule you need is the power rule:

$$ ∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)    so    ∫_0^R x³ dx = [x⁴/4]_0^R = R⁴/4 $$

The notation [F(x)]_a^b means F(b) − F(a). When the interval is symmetric, such as −ℓ/2 to ℓ/2, an even function such as x² gives twice the integral from 0 to ℓ/2.

**Choosing the piece (the element).** For moments of inertia, pick a piece whose every point has the same perpendicular distance from the axis:

- **Strip** of a rectangle, parallel to the axis: area = (length) × dx.
- **Thin ring** of a disc, radius x and width dx. Cut the ring and unroll it: a strip of length 2πx and width dx, so **area = 2πx dx**.
- **Thin disc** of a solid, perpendicular to an axis, thickness dz: volume = (cross-section area) × dz. A sphere of radius R cut at height z (measured from the centre) gives a disc of radius √(R² − z²).
- **Thin cylindrical shell** of radius x, wall thickness dx and length ℓ: volume = 2πx dx × ℓ (unrolled: a sheet of length 2πx, width ℓ, thickness dx).
- **Thin ring on a spherical shell**: at polar angle φ from the axis the ring has radius R sin φ and width R dφ, so area = 2π (R sin φ)(R dφ) = 2πR² sin φ dφ.

Similar triangles are the tool for cones and pyramids: a slice at distance x from the apex of a cone of height h and base radius R has radius xR/h.

Two integrals used repeatedly: ∫_0^π sin³φ dφ = [−cos φ + cos³φ/3]_0^π = 4/3, and ∫_{−R}^{R} (R² − z²)² dz = 16R⁵/15 (checked in Module 5).

### Bridge: Vector cross product recap (for torque and angular momentum)

A **vector** has size and direction; bold letters (**r**, **F**, **ω**) are vectors, plain letters their sizes. The **cross product** of two vectors **a** and **b** is a new vector **a × b**:

- magnitude |**a × b**| = a b sin θ, where θ is the angle between them (0° to 180°);
- direction perpendicular to the plane of **a** and **b**, given by the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers of the right hand from **a** toward **b**; the thumb points along **a × b**;
- **b × a = −(a × b)**: reversing the order flips the sign.

Where it appears in this lesson. **Torque** about O is **τ = r × F**, size r F sin θ = (perpendicular distance from O to the line of the force) × F. **Angular momentum** of a particle is **l = r × p**. The velocity of a particle of a body turning with angular velocity **ω** is **v = ω × r** (PH-015, Module 5); the speed is ω r sin φ = ω r⊥, where r⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the axis. That is exactly why the perpendicular distance r⊥ enters the moment of inertia: it is the 'sin φ × r' of the cross product.

Unit-vector example: **x̂ × ŷ = ẑ**, **ŷ × ẑ = x̂**, **ẑ × x̂ = ŷ**; any vector crossed with itself is **0**.

Distance from a line. If n̂ is a unit vector along an axis through O and **r** is a particle's position, the perpendicular distance from the axis is |n̂ × r| = r sin φ, and r⊥² = r² − (r · n̂)² (Pythagoras); this is used in Module 9.

## The idea before any formula

Push a heavy playground roundabout: the same push spins it faster when the children sit near the centre than when they sit at the rim. The *mass is the same*; what changed is **where the mass is relative to the axis it spins about**. The number that records this is the **moment of inertia**, written I. It plays for rotation the role that mass plays for straight-line motion.

### 2. Definition of moment of inertia and radius of gyration

*Objective:* Compute I for a few particles and for a continuous body by the five-step recipe, and state K, units, scalar character and additivity.

## One sentence

Mass measures how hard it is to change a body's straight-line motion; the **moment of inertia I** measures how hard it is to change its spinning about a chosen **axis**. Unlike mass, I depends on the axis and on how the mass is spread around it.

## Definition for particles

Choose an axis (a straight line fixed in the body). For a particle of mass mᵢ let r⊥ᵢ be its **perpendicular distance** from that axis: the shortest distance from the particle to the line. Then

$$ I = Σ mᵢ r⊥ᵢ² = m₁ r⊥₁² + m₂ r⊥₂² + m₃ r⊥₃² + ... $$

**Example.** A 2 kg particle at 0.5 m and a 3 kg particle at 1.0 m (perpendicular distances): I = 2(0.5)² + 3(1.0)² = 0.5 + 3.0 = 3.5 kg·m². The 3 kg particle is only 1.5 times heavier but contributes 6 times more, because distance is squared. (The squared distance is explained in Module 7: a particle's kinetic energy is ½ m (r⊥ω)² = ½ (m r⊥²) ω².)

## Continuous bodies and the five-step recipe

Split the body into pieces of mass dm, each at perpendicular distance r⊥, and let the sum become an integral: **I = ∫ r⊥² dm**. With dm = λ dx, σ dA or ρ dV (Bridge PH-017-B1):

1. Draw the body and the axis.
2. Choose an **element all of whose points have the same r⊥** (strip, ring, thin disc, shell).
3. Write dm = (density) × (size of the element), with density = M/(total size).
4. Write dI = r⊥² dm.
5. Integrate with limits that cover the body exactly once; simplify; then check units and a limiting case.

## Radius of gyration

Define K by **I = M K²**, so K = √(I/M). K is the distance at which the whole mass M, gathered into a thin ring about the axis, would have the same I. It is a length and can never exceed the largest r⊥ in the body. Examples (derived later): ring K = R; disc K = R/√2 ≈ 0.707 R; solid sphere K = √(2/5) R ≈ 0.632 R.

## Units, scalar, additivity

- **Dimension** M L²; **SI unit** kg·m² (1 kg·m² = 10⁷ g·cm²).
- For one fixed axis I is a single positive number (a scalar); reversing the sense of spin does not change it. The full three-dimensional story (the inertia tensor) is in PH-015 and Module 9.
- I is a sum, so for the **same axis**: I_total = I₁ + I₂, and if a part is removed, **I_rest = I_whole − I_part** (Module 6, the disc with a hole).

## Translation and rotation side by side

| Straight-line motion | Rotation about a fixed axis |
|---|---|
| mass m | moment of inertia I |
| velocity v | angular velocity ω |
| momentum p = m v | angular momentum L = I ω |
| force F | torque τ |
| F = m a | τ = I α |
| kinetic energy ½ m v² | kinetic energy ½ I ω² |
| power F v | power τ ω |

### 3. The parallel-axis and perpendicular-axis theorems, with proofs and conditions

*Objective:* Prove both theorems from the definition, state the exact condition for each, and refuse to use them where they do not apply.

## Parallel-axis theorem (Steiner)

**Statement.** Let C be the centre of mass (CM) of a body of mass M and I_cm its moment of inertia about an axis through C. For any axis parallel to it at perpendicular distance h:

$$ I = I_cm + M h² $$

**Setup.** Origin at C; the axis through C is the z-axis. The parallel axis cuts the xy-plane at the point (a, b), so h² = a² + b². A particle mᵢ sits at (xᵢ, yᵢ, zᵢ) measured from C.

1. Squared distance from the C-axis: xᵢ² + yᵢ², so I_cm = Σ mᵢ (xᵢ² + yᵢ²).
2. Squared distance from the parallel axis (z does not matter, the axis is parallel to z): (xᵢ − a)² + (yᵢ − b)².
3. Expand: xᵢ² + yᵢ² − 2a xᵢ − 2b yᵢ + a² + b².
4. Multiply by mᵢ and add:

$$ I = Σ m (x² + y²) − 2a Σ m x − 2b Σ m y + (a² + b²) Σ m $$

5. C is the origin, so the CM coordinates vanish: Σ m x = M x_cm = 0 and Σ m y = M y_cm = 0 (for a continuous body ∫ x dm = 0). Also Σ m = M.
6. Hence I = I_cm + M h². ∎

**Conditions.** (i) The two axes are parallel. (ii) **One of them passes through the CM**; the cross terms vanish only because the origin was the CM. (iii) h is the perpendicular separation. (iv) It holds for any rigid body, flat or solid. **Consequences:** I_cm is the *smallest* I among all parallel axes; to go between two axes that both miss the CM, step through the CM: I₂ = I₁ + M(h₂² − h₁²). Check: rod, I_end = Mℓ²/12 + M(ℓ/2)² = Mℓ²/3.

## Perpendicular-axis theorem (flat bodies only)

**Statement.** For a plane lamina in the xy-plane, with O a point in that plane: **I_z = I_x + I_y**, where the x- and y-axes lie in the plane at right angles through O and the z-axis is perpendicular to the plane through O.

**Proof.** Every element has z = 0, so its distance from the x-axis is |y|, from the y-axis |x|, and from the z-axis √(x² + y²). Then I_x = Σ m y², I_y = Σ m x², and I_z = Σ m (x² + y²) = I_x + I_y. ∎

**Exact conditions.** (i) All the mass lies in **one plane** (a lamina; negligible thickness). (ii) The three axes are mutually perpendicular and **meet at one point O**: two in the plane, one perpendicular to it. (iii) O may be any point of the plane (it need not be the CM, and for a ring, O is the centre, where there is no material). (iv) The two in-plane axes need not be symmetry axes, so I_x + I_y is the same for **every** perpendicular pair through O.

**Why it fails for solids.** With z ≠ 0: I_x + I_y = Σ m (x² + y² + 2z²) = I_z + 2 Σ m z². For a three-dimensional body the correct identity is I_x + I_y + I_z = 2 Σ m r². **Uses:** ring about a diameter: 2 I_d = MR², so I_d = MR²/2; a square lamina of side a: I_z (centre) = Ma²/6, so every in-plane axis through the centre has Ma²/12.

### 4. Integrations I: rod, rectangular plate, ring, disc and annulus

*Objective:* Derive I for a rod, rectangular plate, ring, disc and annulus with every step and limit, and use the theorems for the other axes.

## Thin uniform rod (length ℓ, mass M, λ = M/ℓ)

*Axis through the centre, perpendicular to the rod.* Put x along the rod with x = 0 at the centre. Element dx at x: dm = (M/ℓ) dx, r⊥ = |x|, dI = x² (M/ℓ) dx. Limits −ℓ/2 to ℓ/2:

$$ I_c = (M/ℓ) ∫_{−ℓ/2}^{ℓ/2} x² dx = (M/ℓ) [x³/3]_{−ℓ/2}^{ℓ/2} = (M/ℓ)(ℓ³/24 + ℓ³/24) = Mℓ²/12 $$

*Axis through one end.* Same element, limits 0 to ℓ: I = (M/ℓ)[x³/3]_0^ℓ = (M/ℓ)(ℓ³/3) = **Mℓ²/3**. Check by the parallel-axis theorem: Mℓ²/12 + M(ℓ/2)² = Mℓ²/12 + 3Mℓ²/12 = Mℓ²/3. ✓

## Rectangular plate (sides a along x, b along y, σ = M/(ab))

*Axis through the centre parallel to side b.* Strip of width dx at x, length b: every point is at distance |x|. dm = σ b dx = (M/a) dx; I_y = (M/a) ∫_{−a/2}^{a/2} x² dx = **Ma²/12**. Likewise I_x = **Mb²/12**. By the perpendicular-axis theorem the axis through the centre perpendicular to the plane has I_z = **M(a² + b²)/12**. Through a corner (perpendicular to the plane): h² = (a/2)² + (b/2)², so I = M(a² + b²)/12 + M(a² + b²)/4 = **M(a² + b²)/3**. Limit b → 0: Ma²/12, the rod. ✓

## Thin ring (radius R)

Every element is at r⊥ = R: I = ∫ R² dm = R² M = **MR²**. About a diameter: the two perpendicular diameters are equivalent by symmetry, so I_d + I_d = MR² (perpendicular-axis theorem), giving **I_d = MR²/2**.

## Uniform disc (radius R, σ = M/(πR²))

Element: a ring of radius x and width dx, area 2πx dx. dm = σ 2πx dx = 2M x dx/R², dI = x² dm = 2M x³ dx/R². Limits 0 to R:

$$ I = (2M/R²) ∫_0^R x³ dx = (2M/R²)(R⁴/4) = MR²/2 $$

About a diameter: 2 I_d = MR²/2, so **I_d = MR²/4**. About a tangent in the plane: MR²/4 + MR² = **5MR²/4**; about a tangent perpendicular to the plane: MR²/2 + MR² = **3MR²/2** (parallel-axis, h = R).

## Annulus (outer radius R, inner radius r, σ = M/[π(R² − r²)])

Ring element as for the disc, but the limits are r to R and the density uses the area of the annulus: dm = 2M x dx/(R² − r²).

$$ I = [2M/(R² − r²)] ∫_r^R x³ dx = [2M/(R² − r²)] (R⁴ − r⁴)/4 = (M/2)(R² + r²) $$

using R⁴ − r⁴ = (R² − r²)(R² + r²). About a diameter: **M(R² + r²)/4**. **Limits:** r → 0 gives MR²/2 (disc); r → R gives MR² (ring). ✓ **Trap:** do not use the full-disc σ = M/πR² for an annulus.

### 5. Integrations II: solid and hollow cylinders, solid sphere, spherical shell and hollow sphere

*Objective:* Derive I for cylinders and spheres by slicing into discs, rings or shells, and check each result by limiting cases.

## Solid cylinder (radius R, length ℓ, mass M)

*Own axis.* The cylinder is a stack of discs: each thin disc (mass dm) has dI = ½ dm R², so I = ½ R² ∫ dm = **½MR²**, independent of the length. (ℓ → 0 gives the disc.)

*Transverse axis through the centre.* Put z along the axis, z = 0 at the centre. A disc at z has dm = (M/ℓ) dz. About its own diameter it has ¼ dm R²; moving to the transverse axis through the centre (distance z) adds dm z² (parallel-axis theorem):

$$ I = (M/ℓ) ∫_{−ℓ/2}^{ℓ/2} (R²/4 + z²) dz = (M/ℓ) [R² z/4 + z³/3]_{−ℓ/2}^{ℓ/2} = (M/ℓ)(R²ℓ/4 + ℓ³/12) = MR²/4 + Mℓ²/12 $$

Limits: R → 0 gives the rod Mℓ²/12; ℓ → 0 gives the disc diameter MR²/4. ✓

## Hollow cylinder (inner r, outer R)

The same stacking with annular discs (Module 4): own axis **M(R² + r²)/2** (thin wall, r → R: MR²); transverse axis through the centre **M(R² + r²)/4 + Mℓ²/12**.

## Solid sphere (radius R, ρ = 3M/(4πR³)), about a diameter

Take z along the axis from the centre. The slice at z is a disc of radius √(R² − z²), thickness dz: dm = ρπ(R² − z²) dz, and dI = ½ dm (R² − z²) = (ρπ/2)(R² − z²)² dz. Limits −R to R (use symmetry: 2 × the integral from 0 to R):

$$ I = ρπ ∫_0^R (R⁴ − 2R²z² + z⁴) dz = ρπ [R⁴z − 2R²z³/3 + z⁵/5]_0^R = ρπ R⁵ (1 − 2/3 + 1/5) = (8/15) ρπ R⁵ $$

Substituting ρπ = 3M/(4R³): **I = (8/15)(3M/4R³) R⁵ = 2MR²/5**. About a tangent: 2MR²/5 + MR² = **7MR²/5**.

## Thin spherical shell (σ = M/(4πR²))

Ring at polar angle φ from the axis: radius R sin φ, width R dφ, area 2πR² sin φ dφ, so dm = (M/2) sin φ dφ and r⊥ = R sin φ. Limits φ = 0 to π:

$$ I = (MR²/2) ∫_0^π sin³φ dφ = (MR²/2)(4/3) = 2MR²/3 $$

Check by the three-dimensional identity: I_x + I_y + I_z = 2 Σ m r² = 2MR², and by symmetry the three are equal, so I = 2MR²/3. ✓

## Thick hollow sphere (inner r, outer R)

Subtract two solid spheres of the same density (additivity, Module 2): I = (2/5)(4π/3)ρ(R⁵ − r⁵) with M = (4π/3)ρ(R³ − r³), so

$$ I = (2/5) M (R⁵ − r⁵)/(R³ − r³) $$

Limit r → R: (R⁵ − r⁵)/(R³ − r³) → 5R²/3, so I → 2MR²/3, the shell. ✓ Limit r → 0: 2MR²/5. ✓

### 6. Integrations III: the cone, composite bodies and the diatomic molecule

*Objective:* Derive I of a solid cone about its axis and about a perpendicular axis through the vertex, and use the add/subtract method and the reduced mass for composite bodies and molecules.

## Solid cone (height h, base radius R, mass M, ρ = 3M/(πR²h))

Put the origin at the **vertex** and measure x along the axis towards the base. By similar triangles a slice at distance x has radius s = xR/h and thickness dx, so

$$ dm = ρ π s² dx = ρ π (R²/h²) x² dx $$

**(i) Axis of the cone.** The slice is a disc about its own axis: dI = ½ dm s² = ½ ρπ (R⁴/h⁴) x⁴ dx. Limits 0 to h:

$$ I = ½ ρπ (R⁴/h⁴) [x⁵/5]_0^h = ρπ R⁴ h/10 = (3M/πR²h)(πR⁴h/10) = 3MR²/10 $$

**(ii) Axis through the vertex, perpendicular to the cone's axis (parallel to the base).** The slice about its own diameter has ¼ dm s²; the axis through the vertex lies at distance x from the slice, so the parallel-axis theorem adds dm x²:

$$ dI = dm (s²/4 + x²) = ρπ (R²/h²) x² (R²x²/(4h²) + x²) dx $$

$$ I = ρπ (R²/h²) (R²/(4h²) + 1) [x⁵/5]_0^h = ρπ (R⁴h/20 + R²h³/5) = 3MR²/20 + 3Mh²/5 $$

using ρπ = 3M/(R²h). **Other axes by the theorem.** The CM is at x_cm = (1/M) ∫ x dm = (ρπR²/(Mh²)) (h⁴/4) = 3h/4 from the vertex, so about the parallel axis through the CM: I_cm = 3MR²/20 + 3Mh²/5 − M(3h/4)² = **3MR²/20 + 3Mh²/80**; about a diameter of the base (h/4 from the CM): 3MR²/20 + 3Mh²/80 + Mh²/16 = **3MR²/20 + Mh²/10**. **Sense checks:** K² = 3R²/10 < R²/2 of a disc, because most of a cone's mass is near its axis; and the vertex result exceeds a rod's Mh²/3 because the mass is concentrated at the far (base) end.

## Composite bodies: add and subtract (same axis only)

**Example.** A uniform disc of radius R has a circular hole of radius R/2 whose centre is R/2 from the disc's centre. Let M₀ be the mass of the complete disc. The hole has area ¼ of the disc, so its mass is M₀/4 and the remaining mass is M = 3M₀/4. About the axis through the original centre, perpendicular to the plane:

$$ I_hole = ½ (M₀/4)(R/2)² + (M₀/4)(R/2)² = 3M₀R²/32,    I = M₀R²/2 − 3M₀R²/32 = 13M₀R²/32 = 13MR²/24 $$

(the CM of the remainder is at R/6 on the side opposite the hole, so about its own CM axis I_cm = 13MR²/24 − M(R/6)² = 37MR²/72).

## Rigid diatomic molecule (point masses m₁, m₂ at separation r₀)

Let C be the CM, distances r₁ and r₂ from the atoms: r₁ + r₂ = r₀ and m₁r₁ = m₂r₂. Hence r₁ = m₂r₀/(m₁ + m₂) and r₂ = m₁r₀/(m₁ + m₂). About an axis through C perpendicular to the bond (two such axes):

$$ I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² = m₁m₂² r₀²/(m₁+m₂)² + m₂m₁² r₀²/(m₁+m₂)² = m₁m₂ r₀²/(m₁ + m₂) = μ r₀² $$

with **reduced mass** μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). About the bond axis I = 0 (point masses). For hydrogen with m_p = 1837 mₑ, the CM lies at distance mₑ r₀/(m_p + mₑ) = r₀/1838 from the proton.

### 7. Equation of motion for rotation, rotational energy and rolling without slipping

*Objective:* Derive τ = Iα and K = ½Iω² for a fixed axis, and derive a = g sinθ/(1 + I/mR²) for a rolling body with the friction condition.

## τ = Iα from angular momentum

Rotation about a fixed axis, taken as the z-axis, with ω along +z (right-hand rule). A particle mᵢ at perpendicular distance r⊥ᵢ moves on a circle with speed vᵢ = ω r⊥ᵢ (PH-015, Module 5), tangent to the circle. Its angular momentum about the axis is mᵢ r⊥ᵢ vᵢ = mᵢ r⊥ᵢ² ω. Add over the body (all particles share the same ω):

$$ L_z = (Σ mᵢ r⊥ᵢ²) ω = I ω $$

Differentiate. Internal forces come in equal and opposite pairs along the line joining the particles, so their torques cancel (PH-015, Module 6) and only the external torque survives: dL_z/dt = τ_ext. For a rigid body I is constant, so

$$ τ = I α,    α = dω/dt $$

(For a non-rigid system use τ = d(Iω)/dt.) **Sign:** τ and α are positive when along +z.

## Rotational kinetic energy, work and power

$$ K = Σ ½ mᵢ vᵢ² = ½ (Σ mᵢ r⊥ᵢ²) ω² = ½ I ω² $$

Work by a torque: dW = τ dθ = I (dω/dt) dθ = I ω dω, so W = ½Iω₂² − ½Iω₁²; power P = τ ω.

## Rolling without slipping

The contact point is momentarily at rest, so v_cm = ωR and the motion is a pure rotation about the contact axis: K = ½ I_P ω² with I_P = I_cm + MR² (parallel axis). Writing I_cm = Mk²:

$$ K = ½ I_cm ω² + ½ M v² = ½ M v² (1 + k²/R²) $$

(rotation about the CM plus motion of the CM; Mathur's equation (i)-(ii)).

## Rolling down an incline (angle θ, mass m, radius R)

Take down-slope as positive. Static friction f acts **up the slope**; it does no work (the contact point does not move), so mechanical energy is conserved.

$$ m a = m g sinθ − f    and    I_cm α = f R,    a = α R $$

so f = I_cm a/R², and m a = m g sinθ − I_cm a/R², giving

$$ a = g sinθ / (1 + I_cm/(mR²)) = g sinθ / (1 + k²/R²) $$

Energy check: m g s sinθ = ½ m v² (1 + k²/R²) with v² = 2as gives the same a. The result does not depend on m or R, only on the shape. Friction needed: f = m g sinθ (k²/R²)/(1 + k²/R²) ≤ μ_s m g cosθ, so **μ_s ≥ tanθ (k²/R²)/(1 + k²/R²)**.

| Body | I_cm | a/(g sinθ) | least μ_s / tanθ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thin ring or hollow cylinder | MR² | 1/2 | 1/2 |
| Solid cylinder or disc | MR²/2 | 2/3 | 1/3 |
| Solid sphere | 2MR²/5 | 5/7 | 2/7 |
| Thin spherical shell | 2MR²/3 | 3/5 | 2/5 |

**Limit check:** k → 0 gives a = g sinθ, a frictionless slider. Race order: sphere, then disc and solid cylinder together, then shell, then ring. **Example:** a solid sphere on 30° from rest: a = 9.8 × 0.5/1.4 = 3.5 m/s²; to cover 14 m, t = √(2 × 14/3.5) = √8 = 2.83 s.

### 8. Pendulums and the Atwood machine: where I enters the motion

*Objective:* Derive the period of a compound pendulum, its equivalent length and centre of oscillation, the torsion-pendulum period, and the acceleration in an Atwood machine with a massive pulley.

## Compound (physical) pendulum

A rigid body of mass M swings about a fixed horizontal axis through O, its CM a distance h below O; θ is the angle from the vertical. The weight acts at the CM, so the torque about O is τ = −M g h sinθ (restoring: negative for positive θ). With I_O = I_cm + M h² = M (k² + h²) (parallel axis, k = radius of gyration about the CM):

$$ I_O θ̈ = − M g h sinθ ≈ − M g h θ    (small θ) $$

$$ ω₀² = M g h/I_O = g h/(k² + h²),    T = 2π √((k² + h²)/(g h)) = 2π √(L/g),    L = h + k²/h $$

L is the **equivalent simple-pendulum length**. The point on the line OC at distance L from O (a distance k²/h beyond the CM) is the **centre of oscillation**. **Interchangeability:** with the pivot at that point, h′ = k²/h and L′ = h′ + k²/h′ = k²/h + h = L, so the period is unchanged. Two parallel axes on opposite sides of the CM with h h′ = k² have the same T; T is least when h = k, T_min = 2π√(2k/g). **Limit:** k → 0 gives the simple pendulum 2π√(h/g). **Numbers:** a uniform rod ℓ = 1.0 m pivoted at an end: k² = 1/12, h = 0.5, L = (1/12 + 1/4)/0.5 = 2/3 m, T = 2π√(2/(3 × 9.8)) = 1.64 s; the centre of oscillation is 2/3 m from the pivot. A ring hung on a nail at its rim: h = R, k = R, L = 2R.

## Torsion pendulum

A body hung from a wire; a twist θ produces a restoring torque −κθ (κ the torsion constant, N·m/rad). Then I θ̈ = −κθ and

$$ T = 2π √(I/κ) $$

For a wire of radius a, length ℓ, shear modulus η: a ring of radius ρ and width dρ at twist θ has shear strain ρθ/ℓ, so shear stress η ρθ/ℓ acting on area 2πρ dρ at arm ρ gives dτ = (η ρθ/ℓ)(2πρ dρ) ρ; integrating 0 to a: κθ = (2πηθ/ℓ)(a⁴/4), **κ = πηa⁴/(2ℓ)**. Comparing periods with a known disc gives an unknown I: I_x/I_ref = (T_x/T_ref)².

## Atwood machine with a massive pulley

Masses m₁ > m₂ on a light inextensible string over a pulley (moment of inertia I, radius R); the string does not slip on the pulley. Take m₁ downward positive, so a = αR:

$$ m₁g − T₁ = m₁a,    T₂ − m₂g = m₂a,    (T₁ − T₂) R = I α = I a/R $$

Adding the first two and substituting T₁ − T₂ = I a/R²:

$$ (m₁ − m₂) g = (m₁ + m₂ + I/R²) a,    a = (m₁ − m₂) g/(m₁ + m₂ + I/R²) $$

For a disc pulley I/R² = M/2. The tensions differ (T₁ > T₂) because the pulley needs a net torque to accelerate. **Limit:** I → 0 gives (m₁ − m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂), T₁ = T₂. **Numbers:** m₁ = 3, m₂ = 1, M = 2 kg: a = 2 × 9.8/(4 + 1) = 3.92 m/s²; T₁ = 3(9.8 − 3.92) = 17.64 N; T₂ = 1(9.8 + 3.92) = 13.72 N; T₁ − T₂ = 3.92 N = (M/2) a. ✓

### 9. Moment of inertia about an arbitrary axis: direction cosines, the inertia tensor and principal axes

*Objective:* Derive I about a line with direction cosines (α, β, γ), link it to the inertia tensor, and use it for the cube, the rod and the 2025 question.

## Derivation of I about any axis through O

Let the axis through O have unit vector n̂ with **direction cosines** (α, β, γ), the cosines of the angles it makes with x, y, z (many books write l, m, n), so α² + β² + γ² = 1. For a particle at **r** = (x, y, z) the component along the axis is **r** · n̂, and by Pythagoras its squared perpendicular distance is

$$ r⊥² = r² − (r · n̂)² $$

1. r · n̂ = αx + βy + γz, so (r · n̂)² = α²x² + β²y² + γ²z² + 2αβ xy + 2βγ yz + 2γα zx.
2. Use α² + β² + γ² = 1 to write r² = (α² + β² + γ²)(x² + y² + z²).
3. Subtract and group: r⊥² = α²(y² + z²) + β²(z² + x²) + γ²(x² + y²) − 2αβ xy − 2βγ yz − 2γα zx.
4. Multiply by m and sum, with I_xx = Σ m (y² + z²), etc. and **products of inertia** P_xy = Σ m x y:

$$ I = I_xx α² + I_yy β² + I_zz γ² − 2 P_xy αβ − 2 P_yz βγ − 2 P_zx γα $$

**Link to the tensor and a sign trap.** In PH-015 the tensor elements are I_xx = Σ m (y² + z²), **I_xy = −P_xy** (off-diagonal elements carry the minus sign). Then I = n̂ᵀ **I** n̂ = Σ_jk n_j I_jk n_k, which has **+2 I_xy αβ** in tensor notation but **−2 P_xy αβ** with products of inertia; books that write I_xy for Σ m x y have the minus sign shown in the display. Check: n̂ = (1, 0, 0) gives I_xx. ✓

## Principal axes (intuition)

Every body has three mutually perpendicular axes through O along which all products of inertia vanish: the **principal axes**, with principal moments I₁, I₂, I₃ (PH-015, Module 6; Mathur PDF p. 604 gives the determinant). An axis of symmetry is principal. Along principal axes I = I₁α² + I₂β² + I₃γ², a weighted average lying between the smallest and largest principal moments. Spin about a principal axis keeps **L ∥ ω**; spin about any other axis gives L not parallel to ω, and the bearings feel a sideways torque.

## Worked cases

**Rod** (x along the rod, through its centre): I_xx = 0, I_yy = I_zz = Mℓ²/12. For an axis at angle φ in the xy-plane, n̂ = (cosφ, sinφ, 0): I = (Mℓ²/12) sin²φ (φ = 90° gives Mℓ²/12; φ = 30° gives Mℓ²/48).

**Cube**, side a, origin at a corner, edges along the axes: ⟨y²⟩ = (1/a)∫_0^a y² dy = a²/3, so I_xx = M (a²/3 + a²/3) = **2Ma²/3**; P_xy = M ⟨x⟩⟨y⟩ = M (a/2)(a/2) = **Ma²/4** (the coordinates are independent). Body diagonal (α = β = γ = 1/√3): I = (2/3)(1/3)(3) − 2(1/4)(1/3)(3) = 2/3 − 1/2 = **Ma²/6**; face diagonal (1/√2, 1/√2, 0): 2/3 − 2(1/4)(1/2) = **5Ma²/12**.

**Cube spinning about the x-edge (2025 Q3(c)):** with ω = (ω, 0, 0), L = **I**ω has L_x = (2/3)Ma²ω, L_y = I_yx ω = −Ma²ω/4, L_z = I_zx ω = −Ma²ω/4, and K = ½ ω · L = ½ (2/3) Ma²ω² = Ma²ω²/3. L is not parallel to ω. **Pyramid (2022 Q3(a))**: the method is the same, using cross-sections that shrink linearly with height, but the figure fixing the apex and axes is not reproduced in our text, so we do not compute it here.

### 10. Examination application: tipping rod, rolling inside a cylinder, turntable and timed answers

*Objective:* Work the 2026 tipping-rod and 2025 rolling-cylinder questions and a turntable problem from first principles, and plan timed written answers.

## The tipping rod (2026 Q1(a) analysis)

A uniform rod (length L, mass m) stands vertically on the floor, tips over from rest and the lower end O does not slip, so the rod pivots about O. The centre of mass drops from L/2 to 0, so the potential energy lost is m g L/2. The kinetic energy is ½ I_O ω² with I_O = mL²/3:

$$ ½ (mL²/3) ω² = m g L/2    ⟹    ω² = 3g/L,    ω = √(3g/L) $$

At angle θ from the vertical, ω² = (3g/L)(1 − cosθ). The top end moves at Lω = √(3gL). **Checks:** dimension [g/L] = T⁻², so ω is T⁻¹. The CM speed (L/2)ω = 0.866 √(gL) is less than free fall √(gL), as it should be because the floor pushes up. Assumption to state: the floor reaction N = (mg/4)(1 − 3cosθ)² stays non-negative, but the horizontal force needed is (3mg/4)(3cosθ − 2) sinθ, so a real floor of finite friction would let the end slip earlier; 'rough floor' is read as 'the end pivots'. For L = 2 m, ω = 3.83 rad/s.

## A cylinder rolling inside a cylinder (2025 Q1(a) analysis)

Large cylinder (fixed, radius R); small solid cylinder (radius r, mass m) rolls without slipping. Let θ be the angle of the line of centres from the downward vertical. The small cylinder's centre moves on a circle of radius R − r with speed (R − r)θ̇. No slipping at the contact: its spin rate is ω = (R − r)θ̇/r (opposite sense). Energy: K = ½ m (R − r)²θ̇² + ½ (½ m r²) ω² = ¾ m (R − r)²θ̇²; U = −m g (R − r) cosθ. Differentiating E = K + U = constant:

$$ (3/2) m (R − r)² θ̇ θ̈ + m g (R − r) sinθ θ̇ = 0    ⟹    θ̈ = −(2g/(3(R − r))) sinθ $$

**Force method (check):** along the path m(R − r)θ̈ = −m g sinθ + f; torque about the centre: ½ m r² φ̈ = f r with φ̇ = −(R − r)θ̇/r gives f = −½ m (R − r)θ̈; substituting, (3/2)(R − r)θ̈ = −g sinθ. ✓ For small θ, SHM with period T = 2π√(3(R − r)/(2g)), as if a simple pendulum of length 3(R − r)/2. Friction needed f = (mg/3) sinθ.

## Turntable: conservation of angular momentum

A student on a turntable with a frictionless vertical axis experiences no external torque about it, so L = Iω is constant. Arms out: I_total = 4.0 + 1.5 = 5.5 kg·m² at 30 rev/min (3.14 rad/s). Arms in: I_total = 2.0 + 1.5 = 3.5 kg·m². Then ω_f = ω_i (5.5/3.5), i.e. 47.1 rev/min. Kinetic energy rises from 27.1 J to 42.7 J, an increase of 15.5 J supplied by the student's muscles.

### Notes: Definitions

- Moment of inertia about an axis: I = Σ mᵢ r⊥ᵢ² (discrete) or ∫ r⊥² dm (continuous), where r⊥ is the perpendicular distance of the mass element from the axis; dimension M L², SI unit kg·m².
- Radius of gyration K about an axis: I = M K², so K = √(I/M); the distance at which the whole mass, gathered into a thin ring, would give the same I.
- Product of inertia: P_xy = Σ m x y (and similarly P_yz, P_zx); the tensor off-diagonal element in PH-015 is I_xy = −P_xy.
- Principal axes: the three mutually perpendicular axes through a point about which all products of inertia vanish; principal moments I₁, I₂, I₃.
- Centre of oscillation of a compound pendulum: the point on the line through the pivot and the CM at distance L = h + k²/h from the pivot; pivot and centre of oscillation are interchangeable.
- Reduced mass of two particles: μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂); a rigid diatomic molecule has I = μ r₀² about an axis through its CM perpendicular to the bond.

### Notes: Theorems stated exactly, with conditions

- Parallel axes (Steiner): I = I_cm + M h². Condition: the axes are parallel and one passes through the CM; h is the perpendicular distance between them; valid for any rigid body.
- Perpendicular axes: I_z = I_x + I_y. Condition: the body is a flat lamina (all mass in one plane); the x and y axes lie in the plane, perpendicular to each other and meeting at O; the z-axis is perpendicular to the plane at O.
- Three-dimensional identity (any body): I_x + I_y + I_z = 2 Σ m r² for three mutually perpendicular axes through O.
- Additivity: for one common axis, I_total = ΣI; a removed part is subtracted: I_rest = I_whole − I_part.
- Arbitrary axis: I = I_xx α² + I_yy β² + I_zz γ² − 2P_xy αβ − 2P_yz βγ − 2P_zx γα with α² + β² + γ² = 1.

### Notes: Standard moments of inertia (uniform bodies)

- Each entry is (number) × M × (length)²; the third column gives K².

### Notes: Key equations

- I = Σ m r⊥² = ∫ r⊥² dm = M K².
- τ = I α about a fixed axis (from L = I ω and dL/dt = τ); τ = d(Iω)/dt if I varies.
- K = ½ I ω²; work W = ∫ τ dθ = ½ I ω₂² − ½ I ω₁²; power P = τ ω.
- Rolling without slipping: v_cm = ω R; K = ½ M v² (1 + k²/R²); a = g sinθ/(1 + k²/R²); least μ_s = tanθ (k²/R²)/(1 + k²/R²).
- Compound pendulum: T = 2π√((k² + h²)/(g h)); L = h + k²/h; T_min = 2π√(2k/g) at h = k.
- Torsion pendulum: T = 2π√(I/κ); wire κ = π η a⁴/(2ℓ).
- Atwood machine, massive pulley: a = (m₁ − m₂) g/(m₁ + m₂ + I/R²); T₁ − T₂ = I a/R².
- Conservation of angular momentum about a fixed axis with no external torque: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂.

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: parallel-axis theorem

- Origin at the CM; axis through the CM is z; parallel axis through (a, b), h² = a² + b².
- Squared distances: x² + y² and (x − a)² + (y − b)².
- Expand and sum: I = Σm(x² + y²) − 2aΣmx − 2bΣmy + (a² + b²)Σm.
- CM at the origin: Σmx = Σmy = 0; Σm = M.
- Conclusion: I = I_cm + M h².

### Notes: Procedure for any moment-of-inertia integral

- Draw the body and the axis; choose coordinates (origin at an axis point or at the centre).
- Choose an element all of whose points are at one perpendicular distance from the axis (strip, ring, thin disc, shell).
- Write dm = density × size of the element, with density = M/(total size).
- Write dI = (distance)² dm; if the element is a disc or ring about its own axis, use its known I.
- Integrate with limits that cover the body once; evaluate; substitute the density back in terms of M.
- Check dimension M L²; check a limiting case (r → 0, r → R, ℓ → 0, b → 0).
- Use the theorems for the other axes instead of a new integral.

### Notes: Rolling without slipping on an incline (same mass and radius)

- a = g sinθ/(1 + I_cm/(mR²)); a and the race result do not depend on m or R.

### Notes: Easily confused

- Perpendicular distance from the axis (r⊥) versus distance from the origin (r): they differ for any particle off the plane through the origin perpendicular to the axis.
- Parallel-axis theorem (needs the CM axis) versus perpendicular-axis theorem (needs a flat lamina); using the latter on a solid sphere or cylinder is wrong.
- Axis perpendicular to the plane of a disc (MR²/2) versus a diameter (MR²/4) versus a tangent in the plane (5MR²/4) versus a tangent perpendicular to the plane (3MR²/2).
- Solid versus hollow: at equal M and R, the shell has the larger I (2/3 versus 2/5) so it rolls more slowly.
- Tensor element I_xy = −Σmxy versus product of inertia P_xy = +Σmxy: the sign in the arbitrary-axis formula depends on which is meant.
- Equivalent length L = h + k²/h of a compound pendulum versus the distance h of the CM from the pivot.

### Notes: Exceptions and conditions

- The parallel-axis theorem fails if neither axis passes through the CM; go through the CM in two steps.
- The perpendicular-axis theorem fails for any body with thickness along the third axis (cylinder, sphere, cube); the three-dimensional identity is I_x + I_y + I_z = 2Σmr².
- τ = Iα with a constant I needs a rigid body about a fixed axis; for a changing I use τ = d(Iω)/dt.
- L = I ω (with scalar I) holds only along a principal axis; otherwise L is not parallel to ω.
- Rolling formulas assume no slipping, and the incline friction must be at least the value in the table.
- The tipping rod is analysed as a pivot about the lower end; with finite friction the end slips before the rod reaches the floor.

### Notes: Dimensions and units

- [I] = M L², SI unit kg·m²; 1 kg·m² = 10⁷ g·cm².
- [τ] = M L² T⁻² = N·m (not joule, even though the dimensions agree); [L] = M L² T⁻¹; [K] = M L² T⁻² = J; [κ] = M L² T⁻² per radian = N·m/rad.
- Angles must be in radians in α = a/R, v = ωR and K = ½Iω²; 1 rev/min = 2π/60 rad/s; 1 rev/s = 2π rad/s.
- Compound pendulum: [(k² + h²)/(g h)] = T², so T is a time.

### Notes: Worked values to remember

- Ring and disc of equal M, R: I = MR² and MR²/2, K² in the ratio 2 : 1.
- Solid sphere K = √(2/5) R = 0.632 R; disc K = 0.707 R.
- Rod 1.0 m pivoted at an end: equivalent length 2/3 m, T = 1.64 s (g = 9.8); minimum period at h = k = 0.289 m is 1.53 s.
- Cube, side a: edge 2Ma²/3, any axis through the centre Ma²/6, face diagonal through a corner 5Ma²/12.
- Solid sphere rolling at 3.0 m/s with M = 0.5 kg: K = 3.15 J; disc 0.5 kg, 0.2 m at 100 rev/s: K = 1974 J (to four figures).

    - **a:** I = I_cm + M h²; the two axes must be parallel and one must pass through the CM.
  - - **q:** Why does the perpendicular-axis theorem fail for a cylinder?
    - **a:** Elements have z ≠ 0, so I_x + I_y = I_z + 2Σmz².
  - - **q:** Why does a ring roll down an incline more slowly than a disc?
    - **a:** Its I = MR² is larger, so more of the energy goes into rotation: a = g sinθ/2 versus 2g sinθ/3.
  - - **q:** What is the physical meaning of the radius of gyration?
    - **a:** The distance at which the whole mass concentrated in a thin ring about the axis gives the same I.

    - **a:** The pulley needs a net torque (T₁ − T₂)R = Iα to accelerate.
  - - **q:** When is L parallel to ω?
    - **a:** When ω lies along a principal axis of the body about the chosen point.

- **foundationChecks:**
  - - **q:** Define the moment of inertia of a system of particles about an axis and give its SI unit and dimensions.
    - **a:** I = Σ m r⊥², where r⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the axis; SI unit kg·m²; dimensions M L².
  - - **q:** A 4 kg particle is 0.5 m from an axis and a 1 kg particle is 2 m from it. Find I and K.
    - **a:** I = 4(0.25) + 1(4) = 1 + 4 = 5 kg·m². M = 5 kg, so K = √(5/5) = 1 m.
  - - **q:** State what the radius of gyration K means and show that K = R for a thin ring.
    - **a:** K = √(I/M), the distance at which the whole mass, concentrated in a thin ring, gives the same I. For a thin ring I = MR², so K = √(MR²/M) = R.
  - - **q:** What is dm for a thin ring of radius x and width dx cut from a uniform disc of radius R and mass M?
    - **a:** dm = σ × 2πx dx = [M/(πR²)] 2πx dx = 2Mx dx/R².
- **conceptual:**
  - - **q:** Why is the perpendicular-axis theorem restricted to laminae?
    - **a:** Its proof uses z = 0 for every element, so r² = x² + y². With z ≠ 0, I_x + I_y = I_z + 2Σmz².
  - - **q:** Why is I_cm the smallest moment of inertia among all parallel axes?
    - **a:** Because I = I_cm + Mh² with Mh² ≥ 0, equal to zero only for h = 0.
  - - **q:** A thin ring and a solid disc have the same mass and radius. Which has the larger I about its central perpendicular axis, and why?
    - **a:** The ring: I = MR² versus MR²/2, since all its mass is at the largest distance R, while the disc has mass closer to the axis.
  - - **q:** Why does the tension on the two sides of a massive pulley differ in an Atwood machine?
    - **a:** A net torque (T₁ − T₂)R = Iα is needed to give the pulley an angular acceleration; if I → 0 the tensions become equal.
- **application:**
  - - **q:** A uniform rod of mass 0.5 kg and length 0.8 m is pivoted at one end. Find I about the pivot and the torque needed to give it an angular acceleration of 6 rad/s².
    - **a:** I = Mℓ²/3 = 0.5 × 0.64/3 = 0.10667 kg·m²; τ = Iα = 0.10667 × 6 = 0.64 N·m.
  - - **q:** A thin ring of radius 0.3 m and mass 2 kg is pivoted on a nail at a point of its rim. Find I about the nail and the equivalent simple-pendulum length.
    - **a:** I = MR² + MR² = 2MR² = 2 × 2 × 0.09 = 0.36 kg·m². L = h + k²/h = R + R²/R = 2R = 0.6 m.
  - - **q:** A solid disc and a thin ring of the same mass and radius are released from rest at the top of the same incline and roll without slipping. Which reaches the bottom first, and what is the ratio of their accelerations?
    - **a:** The disc. a_disc/a_ring = (2/3)/(1/2) = 4/3.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-PH-PARALLEL (verified):** Parallel-axis theorem: for an object of mass m, if d is the distance from an axis through the centre of mass to a new parallel axis, then I(parallel axis) = I(centre of mass) + m d^2.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 10.5 'Calculating Moments of Inertia'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-5-calculating-moments-of-inertia)
- **F-PH-ROD-C (verified):** A thin uniform rod of mass M and length L has I = (1/12) M L^2 about an axis perpendicular to the rod through its midpoint (derived in the cited section).
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 10.5 'Calculating Moments of Inertia'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-5-calculating-moments-of-inertia)
- **F-PH-ROD-E (verified):** The same rod has I = (1/3) M L^2 about a perpendicular axis through one end (the cited section derives it by integration and confirms it with the parallel-axis theorem).
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 10.5 'Calculating Moments of Inertia'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-5-calculating-moments-of-inertia)
- **F-PH-DISK (verified):** A uniform thin disk of mass m and radius R has I = (1/2) m R^2 about the axis through its centre perpendicular to the disk (derived in the cited section).
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 10.5 'Calculating Moments of Inertia'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-5-calculating-moments-of-inertia)
- **F-PH-PERP (verified):** Perpendicular-axis theorem (planar object only): the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane equals the sum of the moments of inertia about two perpendicular axes lying in the plane and meeting at the same point.
  Source: [HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), 'Perpendicular Axis Theorem'](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/perpx.html)
