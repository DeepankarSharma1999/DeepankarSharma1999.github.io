### Bridge: Small angles, radians and arcseconds

Diffraction angles in telescope problems are tiny, so you need three habits.

**Habit 1: radians.** An angle in radians is arc length divided by radius. A full circle is 2 pi radians = 360 degrees, so 1 rad = 57.2958 degrees. All the formulas of this lesson, such as theta = 1.22 lambda/D, give theta **in radians**. Wavelength and diameter must be in the same unit (both in metres) so that the ratio is a pure number.

**Habit 2: small-angle replacement.** For theta much smaller than 1 rad, sin(theta) and tan(theta) both equal theta to a very good approximation. For example theta = 0.01 rad gives sin = 0.0099998 and tan = 0.0100003. So sin(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D can be read as theta = 1.22 lambda/D. A screen at distance f then shows a linear distance x = f theta.

**Habit 3: arcminutes and arcseconds.** 1 degree = 60 arcminutes (60') and 1 arcminute = 60 arcseconds (60"). So 1 arcsecond = (pi/180)/3600 rad = 4.848e-6 rad, and 1 arcminute = 2.909e-4 rad. To convert a result in radians to arcseconds divide by 4.848e-6. *Check:* 6.71e-6 rad / 4.848e-6 = 1.384 arcsec.

**Trap.** A common error is to leave the answer in radians and compare it with a separation given in arcseconds. Always convert one of them before comparing.

### Bridge: A function with named zeros: what the Bessel function J1 is for

The single slit gave the function sin(alpha)/alpha, whose zeros are at alpha = pi, 2 pi, 3 pi. A **circular** opening gives a different named function. Do not be afraid of its name: it is a curve that mathematicians have tabulated, just like sine, and your calculator or table gives its values.

**Definition used here.** The Bessel function of the first kind and order 0, J0(z), is the average of cos(z cos(phi)) over a full turn: J0(z) = (1/2 pi) times the integral from 0 to 2 pi of e^{i z cos(phi)} d(phi). This is the 'integral representation'. Order 1, J1, is related by the identity **d/ds [ s J1(s) ] = s J0(s)**, which will be used once, in module 3.

**Small-argument series.** J0(z) = 1 - z^2/4 + z^4/64 - ... and J1(u) = u/2 - u^3/16 + u^5/384 - ... So for small u, 2 J1(u)/u = 1 - u^2/8 + ... tends to 1. At u = 1 the series gives 0.875 and the exact value is 0.8801.

**Zeros.** J1(u) = 0 at u = 0 (trivially), 3.8317, 7.0156, 10.1735, ... (computed with scipy). These numbers play the role that pi, 2 pi, 3 pi play for the slit. Notice they are **not** spaced exactly pi apart (3.8317 to 7.0156 is 3.1839, close to but not equal to pi 3.1416), and they approach equal spacing for large u.

**Picture.** Like a decaying sine wave: J1 rises from 0, peaks near u = 1.84 (value 0.582), crosses zero at 3.8317, dips negative, and so on with falling amplitude.

### 1. Orientation: why a round hole makes a bull's-eye and what the examiner asks

*Objective:* Name the Airy pattern, the Airy disc and the Rayleigh criterion, and list the three results this lesson must deliver for the examination.

## The picture first
Shine a laser pointer through a tiny round pinhole onto a wall. You do not see a sharp dot. You see a bright round **disc** surrounded by faint **rings**, like a bull's-eye. The disc and rings are the **Airy pattern**, the diffraction pattern of a circular opening. The bright central region is the **Airy disc**. The reason is that light is a wave: it spreads after passing through any opening (diffraction), and a round opening spreads it into this circular pattern.

## Why a telescope or an eye cares
A lens is a round opening. When a telescope looks at a distant star (a point), the lens does not form a point image; it forms a tiny Airy pattern. Two stars that are close together produce two overlapping Airy patterns. If they overlap too much you see one blob. The **resolving power** of an instrument is its ability to show two close objects as separate (Brij Lal, PDF p. 479). The **Rayleigh criterion** is the agreed rule for 'just separate': the centre of one pattern falls on the first dark ring of the other (PDF p. 480).

## Three results to carry out of this lesson
1. The first dark ring of a circular aperture of diameter D lies at sin(theta) = **1.22 lambda/D** (not lambda/D, which is the single-slit value).
2. The smallest resolvable angle is d(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D, so resolving power = 1/d(theta) = D/(1.22 lambda): larger D and shorter lambda resolve more.
3. For a microscope the limit is a distance, d = 0.61 lambda/(numerical aperture) (Rayleigh) or lambda/(2 N.A.) (Abbe), so ultraviolet light and oil immersion help.

## Notation used throughout
D = 2a is the aperture diameter, a its radius; lambda is the wavelength in vacuum or air; theta is the angle from the axis; f is the focal length of the lens that focuses the pattern; u = pi D sin(theta)/lambda is a dimensionless variable introduced in module 3; I0 is the central intensity. Angles are in radians unless stated (bridge B1).

### 2. Foundation: Fraunhofer versus Fresnel and the single slit as the stepping stone

*Objective:* State the condition for Fraunhofer diffraction, recall the single-slit pattern I = I0 (sin alpha/alpha)^2 with its minima, and see why a circular aperture needs a new function.

## Two kinds of diffraction
In **Fresnel** diffraction the source or the screen (or both) is at a finite distance, so the wave fronts reaching the opening or the screen are curved. In **Fraunhofer** diffraction the incident wave front is plane and the diffracted light is gathered on the screen by a lens, which is the same as putting the source and the screen at infinity (Brij Lal, PDF p. 442). Telescopes and eyes work in the Fraunhofer regime because the star is effectively at infinity and the image forms in the focal plane.

**Test (standard; verify).** With aperture radius a, wavelength lambda and distance L to the screen, the dimensionless **Fresnel number** is N_F = a^2/(lambda L). Fraunhofer needs N_F much less than 1. For a 1 mm hole (a = 0.5 mm) and lambda = 600 nm: at L = 1 m, N_F = 0.42 (not small, so Fresnel); at L = 10 m, N_F = 0.042 (close to Fraunhofer). A lens in front of the screen brings the far field to its focal plane, so Fraunhofer patterns are seen at modest distances.

## Single slit recap (derived in the extract, PDF pp. 443 to 445)
A slit of width a, light at angle theta. Path difference between the two edges = a sin(theta). Dividing the slit into thin strips, the amplitudes add as the sides of a circular-arc vibration polygon, giving amplitude A = A0 sin(alpha)/alpha with **alpha = pi a sin(theta)/lambda**, so

$$ I = I0 (sin alpha / alpha)^2 $$

*Minima:* sin(alpha) = 0 with alpha not 0, that is a sin(theta) = n lambda, n = 1, 2, 3 (extract eq. 18.2). The first minimum is at sin(theta) = lambda/a. *Central maximum width* on a screen at focal distance f: W = 2 f lambda/a (eq. 18.6). The secondary maxima are weak: the first has intensity 0.047 I0 (sinc squared at alpha = 1.43 pi, recomputed).

## The extract's quick argument for the circular opening
For a circular hole of diameter d the extract argues like the slit (PDF p. 450): take the path difference d sin(theta) between the ends of a diameter, set it equal to n lambda, and so the first dark ring is at sin(theta) = lambda/d. It then says the true first dark ring is a little wider, 'according to Airy' sin(theta) = 1.22 lambda/d (eq. 18.27). That is a **quote, not a derivation**.

## Why the slit argument is not enough
A slit has the same width everywhere along its length. A circle does not: the strip of the opening at height y above the centre has width 2 sqrt(a^2 - y^2), narrow near the top and bottom edges. Light from a circle must be added with these varying weights in two dimensions, and that integral is not sin(alpha)/alpha but a **Bessel function** (module 3). The first zero of that function is 3.8317, not pi = 3.1416, and the ratio 3.8317/pi = 1.2197 is the famous 1.22.

## Traps
- Do not use lambda/D for a circular aperture and call it exact.
- Do not forget that Fraunhofer here means plane wave in and lens (or infinity) out.

### 3. Core: the circular-aperture intensity I = I0 [2 J1(u)/u]^2 (derivation 1)

*Objective:* Derive the Fraunhofer amplitude of a circular aperture as an integral over the disc and reduce it to 2 J1(u)/u using the Bessel identities.

## Setting and assumptions
A circular opening of radius a (diameter D = 2a) lies in a plane, lit by a monochromatic plane wave at normal incidence. Every point of the opening acts as a source of secondary waves of equal amplitude and phase (Huygens). We want the far-field amplitude in a direction making angle theta with the axis. Assumptions: Fraunhofer conditions (module 2), small obliquity so all secondary waves have the same amplitude factor, and a scalar wave. Coordinates: polar (rho, phi) on the opening, centre at rho = 0. By the circular symmetry we may take the observation direction to lie in the x-z plane. k = 2 pi/lambda.

## Step 1: the phase of each element
A point at (rho, phi) has x = rho cos(phi). Its wavelet travels a path shorter or longer than the one from the centre by x sin(theta), so its phase relative to the centre is k x sin(theta) = k rho sin(theta) cos(phi). Therefore the amplitude at the screen is

$$ E(theta) = C times the integral over the disc of e^{i k rho sin(theta) cos(phi)} rho d(rho) d(phi) $$

with C a constant. (Taking the real part gives the physical wave; the intensity is |E|^2.)

## Step 2: the phi integral
Using the integral form of J0 (bridge B2), the integral over phi from 0 to 2 pi of e^{i z cos(phi)} d(phi) = 2 pi J0(z). With z = k rho sin(theta):

$$ E(theta) = 2 pi C times the integral from 0 to a of J0(k rho sin(theta)) rho d(rho) $$

## Step 3: the rho integral
Substitute s = k rho sin(theta), so rho = s/(k sin(theta)) and rho d(rho) = s ds/(k sin(theta))^2. The upper limit becomes u = k a sin(theta). Then E = [2 pi C/(k sin(theta))^2] times the integral from 0 to u of s J0(s) ds. Use the identity d/ds [s J1(s)] = s J0(s): the integral equals u J1(u). Hence

$$ E(theta) = 2 pi C u J1(u) / (k sin(theta))^2 $$

Since k sin(theta) = u/a, (k sin(theta))^2 = u^2/a^2 and

$$ E(theta) = pi a^2 C times [2 J1(u)/u] $$

## Step 4: the intensity
At theta = 0 (u tends to 0, and 2 J1(u)/u tends to 1) E = pi a^2 C, so with I0 = |pi a^2 C|^2:

$$ I(theta) = I0 [ 2 J1(u) / u ]^2,   u = k a sin(theta) = pi D sin(theta) / lambda $$

## Checks
- *Dimensions:* u is dimensionless (k in 1/m times a in m) ✓.
- *Limit u tends to 0:* 2 J1(u)/u = 1 - u^2/8 + ..., so I = I0 (1 - u^2/4 + ...) falls smoothly from the centre. The slit analogue is 1 - alpha^2/3.
- *Same structure as the slit:* amplitude is 'area of the opening' times a shape function that is 1 at the centre. The slit gives sin(alpha)/alpha; the circle gives 2 J1(u)/u.
- *Size scaling:* a bigger opening (larger D) makes u larger at the same angle, so the pattern shrinks, as the extract says (PDF p. 451).
- I0 grows as the area squared (pi a^2)^2, so doubling D multiplies the central intensity by 16 for the same illumination.

The extract does not contain this derivation (it quotes Airy); the steps above are standard (verify).

### 4. Core: zeros, rings and energy of the Airy pattern

*Objective:* Obtain the first dark ring at sin(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D, the ring maxima and the 83.8 per cent power in the Airy disc, with the numbers recomputed.

## First dark ring
I vanishes where J1(u) = 0 with u not 0. The first non-zero zero is u1 = 3.8317 (scipy). Since u = pi D sin(theta)/lambda,

$$ sin(theta1) = 3.8317 lambda / (pi D) = 1.2197 lambda / D = 1.22 lambda / D $$

This agrees with Airy's 1.22 quoted in the extract (PDF p. 451, eq. 18.27). *Why not 1?* The slit's first zero is at alpha = pi (3.1416); the circle's is at u = 3.8317, 22 per cent larger, because the circular opening has less 'width' at its edges to cancel the light from the middle.

## All the dark rings
J1 zeros: 3.8317, 7.0156, 10.1735, 13.3237. So sin(theta_n) = 1.2197, 2.2331, 3.2383, 4.2411 times lambda/D. The dark rings are **not** equally spaced in the strict sense (1.2197, then 1.0134 more, then 1.0052 more), tending to spacing lambda/D far out.

## Bright rings
Between dark rings the intensity has maxima where d/du (J1(u)/u) = 0, which is the condition J2(u) = 0 (standard identity; verify), at u = 5.1356, 8.4172, 11.6198, that is sin(theta) = 1.635, 2.679, 3.699 times lambda/D. Their intensities (recomputed) relative to the centre:

| Ring | u at maximum | sin(theta) in units of lambda/D | I / I0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central disc | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1st bright ring | 5.136 | 1.635 | 0.0175 (1.75 per cent) |
| 2nd bright ring | 8.417 | 2.679 | 0.00416 |
| 3rd bright ring | 11.620 | 3.699 | 0.00160 |

The rings are very faint, which is why a star image looks like a disc with a hint of a halo.

## Radius on the screen
For a lens of focal length f and small angles, x = f theta, so the radius of the first dark ring (the Airy disc) is

$$ r1 = 1.22 lambda f / D $$

(extract eq. 19.9 gives 1.22 lambda f/D for the radius and says the diameter of the first dark ring equals the diameter of the central image, PDF p. 486). *Number (recomputed):* f = 1.0 m, D = 5.0 cm, lambda = 600 nm gives r1 = 1.464e-5 m = 14.6 micrometres. The *angular radius* is the same 1.22 lambda/D in radians.

## Energy in the Airy disc
The power inside the first dark ring, as a fraction of the total, is 1 - J0^2(u1) - J1^2(u1) (standard result; verify). With J0(3.8317) = -0.4028 and J1 = 0 this gives

$$ fraction = 1 - J0(3.8317)^2 = 0.8378 = 83.8 per cent $$

(also checked by numerical integration of [2 J1(u)/u]^2 u du from 0 to 3.8317, giving 1.6756 out of the total 2). Cumulative power within the 2nd, 3rd and 4th dark rings: 91.0, 93.8 and 95.2 per cent. So 16 per cent of the starlight lies outside the Airy disc, spread over rings and a faint halo.

## Trap
Do not say '84 per cent of the light is in the first bright ring'. It is inside the first dark ring, which is the whole central disc.

### 5. Core: Rayleigh criterion, telescope and eye (derivation 2)

*Objective:* Derive d(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D and the resolving power D/(1.22 lambda) from the Rayleigh criterion, and apply it to a telescope and to the human eye.

## Setting and assumptions
Two distant point sources of equal brightness, mutually **incoherent** (two different stars), seen through a circular objective of diameter D at wavelength lambda. Each source makes its own Airy pattern in the focal plane; the intensities (not amplitudes) add because the stars are incoherent. Angles are small (bridge B1). Let the angular separation of the stars be d(theta), measured at the objective.

## The Rayleigh criterion (extract, PDF pp. 480 and 483)
Rayleigh's rule is a convention: the two images are **just resolved** when the central maximum of one pattern falls on the first dark ring of the other, equivalently when the distance between the centres equals the radius of the Airy disc.

## Derivation 2: the limit of resolution
1. Star 1 sends parallel light along the axis; its pattern is centred on the axis at the focal point P1 and its first dark ring is at angle 1.22 lambda/D from the axis (module 4).
2. Star 2 is at angle d(theta) from star 1. By the properties of a lens its pattern is centred at angle d(theta) from P1, at P2.
3. The Rayleigh condition says P2 lies on the first dark ring of pattern 1, so

$$ d(theta) = 1.22 lambda / D $$

4. **Resolving power** is defined as the reciprocal of the smallest resolvable angle (extract PDF p. 479, eq. 19.8):

$$ RP = 1/d(theta) = D / (1.22 lambda) $$

5. On the screen the centres are separated by f d(theta) = 1.22 lambda f/D = r1, the Airy radius, which is the second form of the criterion.

*Dimension check:* lambda/D is dimensionless (radians) ✓. *Limits:* D tends to infinity gives zero angle (geometrical optics: perfect resolution); lambda tends to zero similarly. *Why lambda enters:* diffraction spreading is proportional to lambda/D.

## What the Rayleigh dip actually is
Adding two Airy patterns at the Rayleigh separation, the intensity midway between the centres is 0.735 of each peak: a dip of **26.5 per cent** (recomputed with scipy). For a slit pattern (sinc squared) the same construction gives 0.811, a dip of 18.9 per cent. The extract (PDF p. 484, Fig. 19.7) says 'approximately 20 per cent' for two spectral lines in a spectrograph. Spectral lines are slit patterns, so that matches the slit value 18.9 per cent; for a circular aperture use 26.5 per cent. The Rayleigh limit is a convention, not a sharp edge: with good detectors one can sometimes resolve a little below it, and a noisy image cannot reach it.

## The eye
Pupil diameter 2 mm, most sensitive wavelength 550 nm (extract PDF p. 481): d(theta) = 1.22 x 550e-9 / 2.0e-3 = 3.36e-4 rad = 1.15 arcminute, matching the extract's 'about one minute of arc'. At the least distance of distinct vision 25 cm this is a separation of 0.084 mm, the extract's 'order of 0.1 mm'. A smaller pupil makes the eye resolve less.

## Telescope numbers
- 10 cm objective, 550 nm: 6.71e-6 rad = 1.384 arcsec (module 8 uses this for PYQ 2024).
- The objective needed to resolve 1 arcsec at 550 nm: D = 1.22 lambda/(4.848e-6) = 13.8 cm (the extract prints 13.9 cm).
- Extract Example 19.1: a 500 cm telescope at 5500 Angstrom resolves 1.342e-7 rad, which on the Moon (3.8e8 m) is 51 m (recomputed 50.996 m).
- 254 cm objective: 0.0545 arcsec (the extract says about 1/20 arcsec).

## Magnification versus resolution (extract PDF pp. 486 to 487)
The normal magnification of a telescope is M = D/d_exit, with d_exit equal to the eye's pupil diameter. Since the eye limit is 1.22 lambda/d_eye and the telescope limit is 1.22 lambda/D, their ratio is exactly D/d_eye = M: **normal magnification times the telescope limit equals the eye limit**. More magnification than this gives no new detail ('empty magnification', PDF p. 489).

### 6. Core: the microscope, numerical aperture, UV light and why magnification is not resolution

*Objective:* Obtain the microscope limit d = 0.61 lambda0/(mu sin alpha), define numerical aperture, and explain how ultraviolet light, oil immersion and electron beams improve resolution.

## What is resolved
A telescope separates two **angles**; a microscope separates two **points a short distance d apart** on a small object (extract PDF p. 483: the limit of resolution of a microscope is x_m, the smallest distance resolved).

## The extract's path-difference route (PDF pp. 487 to 488)
For self-luminous points A and B at distance d apart, the extreme rays from B reaching the image of A differ in path by 2 d sin(alpha), where alpha is the half-angle of the cone of light accepted by the objective. Setting this equal to 1.22 lambda (the circular-aperture factor) gives d = 1.22 lambda/(2 sin alpha) = 0.61 lambda/sin(alpha). With an immersion medium of refractive index mu, lambda is lambda0/mu:

$$ d = 0.61 lambda0 / (mu sin alpha) = 0.61 lambda0 / N.A.,   N.A. = mu sin alpha $$

## A cleaner derivation (sine condition; standard, verify)
1. On the image side the objective is a circular aperture; the Airy radius is h' = 1.22 lambda' v/D, and with sin(alpha') = D/(2v) this is h' = 0.61 lambda'/sin(alpha'), where lambda' = lambda0/mu'.
2. For a well-corrected objective the **Abbe sine condition** holds: mu h sin(alpha) = mu' h' sin(alpha').
3. Hence the object-side distance corresponding to the Rayleigh image separation is h = mu' h' sin(alpha')/(mu sin(alpha)) = 0.61 lambda0/(mu sin(alpha)). The same result.

## Abbe's value
Real specimens are lit by a condenser, not self-luminous, and the resolution depends on the illumination. Abbe's result (extract eq. 19.15 is lost in the text layer; its example matches this form) is

$$ d = lambda0 / (2 mu sin alpha) = lambda0 / (2 N.A.) $$

Check with the extract's example: lambda0 = 5500 Angstrom, N.A. = 1.6 gives d = 5.5e-5 cm / 3.2 = 1.72e-5 cm = 0.172 micrometre ✓ (the highest practical N.A. is about 1.6, PDF p. 488).

## Numbers (recomputed)
| Case | lambda | N.A. | Rayleigh 0.61 lambda/N.A. | Abbe lambda/(2 N.A.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Visible, oil objective | 550 nm | 1.4 | 240 nm | 196 nm |
| Ultraviolet, same objective | 250 nm | 1.4 | 109 nm | 89 nm |
| Visible, dry low-power | 550 nm | 0.25 | 1342 nm | 1100 nm |

## Ways to increase resolution (extract PDF p. 489)
1. **Increase N.A.** Fill the gap between object and objective with oil (refractive index near 1.5): mu goes up and loss by reflection goes down.
2. **Decrease lambda.** Ultraviolet light with quartz lenses (glass absorbs UV) gives the 'ultra-violet microscope'. The image must be recorded on film or a detector because the eye cannot see UV. The ratio of improvement is 550/250 = 2.2 here.
3. **Electron beams.** The de Broglie wavelength of the electrons is about 0.12 Angstrom in the extract's example, over a thousand times shorter than visible light, but the useful N.A. is much smaller (the extract says so). A 10 kV non-relativistic electron has lambda = 12.27/sqrt(V) Angstrom = 0.12 Angstrom (standard formula; verify).

## Magnification is not resolution
The first magnification is done by the objective. If two details are blurred together there, no later lens can separate them (PDF p. 489). For a standard observer the eye limit is about 1 arcminute, about 0.01 cm at 25 cm. The necessary magnification is the ratio of the eye limit to the instrument limit; extra magnification is **empty magnification**. Example 19.3 of the extract: 10 cm objective, 600 nm, eye limit 2 arcmin gives 7.32e-6 rad versus 5.82e-4 rad, a useful magnifying power of about 79.5 (recomputed).

## Trap
Writing 'resolving power increases with magnification'. It increases with N.A. and with shorter lambda; magnification only enlarges what is already resolved.

### 7. Master: limits, caveats and what the Rayleigh limit does not say

*Objective:* List the assumptions behind 1.22 lambda/D and the real-world effects (atmosphere, obstruction, other criteria, radio telescopes) that modify it.

## The assumptions
The Rayleigh limit assumes: (1) a clear circular aperture of diameter D; (2) incoherent point sources of equal brightness; (3) monochromatic light; (4) an aberration-free lens (a 'diffraction-limited' instrument); (5) a good detector that can see a 26 per cent dip. Drop any one and the number changes.

## Ground telescopes and the atmosphere (standard; verify)
Air turbulence blurs star images to about 0.5 to 1 arcsec (the 'seeing'). The diffraction limit of a 10 cm telescope is 1.4 arcsec, so a small telescope is diffraction-limited. A 2.4 m telescope would be 0.058 arcsec if there were no atmosphere, which is why large ground telescopes use adaptive optics or fly in space. The extract's remark that a larger objective has larger spherical aberration (PDF p. 486) is another reason a bigger lens does not automatically give the ideal limit.

## Central obstruction
Reflecting telescopes have a secondary mirror in the beam. The extract says covering the centre of an objective with a stop (to reduce spherical aberration) 'does not affect the resolving power'. More carefully, the first dark ring shrinks slightly but the rings brighten and contrast falls (standard; verify). The criterion to remember for the examination is 1.22 lambda/D for the full aperture.

## Radio telescopes
Radio waves are about 10^5 times longer than light, so the same formula demands huge dishes. For lambda = 0.21 m and D = 100 m, d(theta) = 1.22 x 0.21/100 = 2.56e-3 rad = 8.8 arcmin (recomputed). Radio astronomers therefore combine dishes as interferometers: the extract describes the Michelson stellar interferometer in its Chapter 19 (outside this lesson).

## Other criteria
Rayleigh's criterion is one convention. The Sparrow criterion (the pair is resolved when the dip first appears) gives about 0.95 lambda/D (standard; verify). Both give an angle of the same order; examination answers must use Rayleigh's 1.22 lambda/D unless the question says otherwise.

## Coherent versus incoherent
If the two sources are coherent (for example two slits lit by the same laser) the amplitudes, not the intensities, add, and the dip depends on the phase between them. The Rayleigh numbers apply to independent, incoherent sources.

## Wavelength matters
Red light (700 nm) gives 1.75 times worse resolution than blue (400 nm) for the same D.

## Spectral versus geometrical resolution
The word 'resolving power' also means lambda/d(lambda) for a prism or a grating (extract PDF p. 483). That is a different quantity and belongs to the grating lessons; do not mix the two. In this lesson 'resolving power' always means the geometrical one: 1/d(theta) for telescopes and 1/d for microscopes.

### Notes: Definitions

- Fraunhofer diffraction: plane wave in, far field out (or the far field brought to a lens focus); Fresnel number N_F = a^2/(lambda L) much less than 1 (standard; verify).
- Airy pattern: the Fraunhofer diffraction pattern of a circular aperture, a bright central disc (Airy disc) surrounded by faint rings.
- Airy disc: the region inside the first dark ring, containing 83.8 per cent of the power.
- Rayleigh criterion: two equal incoherent point images are just resolved when the centre of one falls on the first dark ring of the other.
- Resolving power: the reciprocal of the smallest resolvable angle (telescope, eye) or distance (microscope).
- Numerical aperture N.A. = mu sin(alpha), with alpha the half-angle of the cone of light accepted by the objective and mu the refractive index of the medium in front of it.

### Notes: Equations to know

- Slit: I = I0 (sin alpha/alpha)^2, alpha = pi a sin(theta)/lambda; minima a sin(theta) = n lambda.
- Circle: I = I0 [2 J1(u)/u]^2, u = pi D sin(theta)/lambda.
- First dark ring: J1(u) = 0, u = 3.8317, sin(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D; screen radius r1 = 1.22 lambda f/D.
- Rayleigh limit: d(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D; resolving power D/(1.22 lambda).
- Eye: 1.22 lambda/d_pupil, about 1 arcmin for a 2 mm pupil at 550 nm.
- Microscope: d = 0.61 lambda/N.A. (Rayleigh) and d = lambda/(2 N.A.) (Abbe); N.A. = mu sin(alpha).
- Normal magnification M = D/d_exit; M x (telescope limit) = eye limit.
- Power within the first dark ring: 1 - J0(u1)^2 = 0.838.

### Notes: Single slit versus circular aperture

- Both patterns come from the same Fraunhofer principle; the circle needs the Bessel function because its width varies across the opening.

### Notes: Numbers recomputed with scipy

### Notes: Derivation skeleton: circular aperture

- Phase of element at (rho, phi): k rho sin(theta) cos(phi).
- phi integral: 2 pi J0(k rho sin(theta)).
- rho integral with s = k rho sin(theta): [2 pi/(k sin(theta))^2] u J1(u), using d/ds[s J1(s)] = s J0(s).
- Amplitude pi a^2 [2 J1(u)/u]; intensity I0 [2 J1(u)/u]^2 with u = pi D sin(theta)/lambda.
- First minimum from J1(u) = 0: 3.8317, so sin(theta) = 1.22 lambda/D.

### Notes: Confused concepts

- Magnification versus resolution: magnification enlarges; resolution is how much detail exists to enlarge.
- Resolving power versus limit of resolution: one is the reciprocal of the other; a good instrument has a small limit and a large resolving power.
- Geometrical resolving power (telescope, microscope) versus spectral resolving power lambda/d(lambda) (prism, grating).
- Airy disc radius (a length on the screen, 1.22 lambda f/D) versus angular radius (1.22 lambda/D in radians).
- D (diameter) versus a (radius): u uses D with pi, or a with 2 pi.

  - - **id:** PH-057-D1
    - **task:** Using Fraunhofer diffraction theory derive the intensity distribution of a circular aperture and obtain the condition for the first minimum (15 marks; practice target about 18 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - Set-up: plane wave, normal incidence, opening of radius a (D = 2a), polar coordinates (rho, phi) on the opening, k = 2 pi/lambda, observation direction in the x-z plane at angle theta, Huygens secondary waves of equal amplitude and phase.
      - Phase of an element at (rho, phi): path difference to the centre is x sin(theta) = rho cos(phi) sin(theta), so phase = k rho sin(theta) cos(phi).
      - Amplitude: E(theta) = C times the integral over the disc of e^{i k rho sin(theta) cos(phi)} rho d(rho) d(phi).
      - phi integral: the integral from 0 to 2 pi of e^{i z cos(phi)} d(phi) = 2 pi J0(z), with z = k rho sin(theta). So E = 2 pi C times the integral from 0 to a of J0(k rho sin(theta)) rho d(rho).
      - Substitute s = k rho sin(theta): rho d(rho) = s ds/(k sin(theta))^2 and the upper limit is u = k a sin(theta).
      - Use d/ds [s J1(s)] = s J0(s): the integral of s J0(s) ds from 0 to u is u J1(u).
      - Therefore E = 2 pi C u J1(u)/(k sin(theta))^2 = pi a^2 C [2 J1(u)/u], because k sin(theta) = u/a.
      - At theta = 0, 2 J1(u)/u tends to 1, so E(0) = pi a^2 C and I0 = |pi a^2 C|^2. Hence I(theta) = I0 [2 J1(u)/u]^2 with u = pi D sin(theta)/lambda.
      - First minimum: J1(u) = 0, first non-zero root u1 = 3.8317, so sin(theta1) = 3.8317 lambda/(pi D) = 1.22 lambda/D.
      - Interpretation: a bright disc inside the angle 1.22 lambda/D, surrounded by rings of intensity 0.0175, 0.0042, ... times I0.
    - **checks:**
      - Dimension: u is dimensionless.
      - Limit: 2 J1(u)/u = 1 - u^2/8 + ... tends to 1, so I tends to I0.
      - Number: 3.8317/pi = 1.2197, scipy-verified; the single slit has 1.0.
      - Size: D larger means a smaller pattern.
    - **traps:**
      - Using the radius a where the formula has the diameter D, or omitting pi.
      - Writing I = I0 (sin u/u)^2, the slit formula.

  - - **id:** PH-057-D3
    - **task:** Show that the fraction of power inside the first dark ring of the Airy pattern is 1 - J0(u1)^2 and evaluate it (10 marks; practice target about 12 minutes, not an official allotment).
    - **steps:**
      - The power within angle theta (u = pi D sin(theta)/lambda) is proportional to the integral of I times the area element, that is of [2 J1(s)/s]^2 s ds from 0 to u (small angles; ring area proportional to s ds).
      - That integrand is 4 J1(s)^2/s. So P(u) is proportional to 4 times the integral from 0 to u of J1(s)^2/s ds.
      - Use J0'(s) = -J1(s) and J1'(s) = J0(s) - J1(s)/s. Then d/ds [J0^2 + J1^2] = -2 J0 J1 + 2 J1 (J0 - J1/s) = -2 J1^2/s.
      - Integrate from 0 to u, using J0(0) = 1 and J1(0) = 0: J0(u)^2 + J1(u)^2 - 1 = -2 times the integral of J1^2/s ds, so the integral equals (1/2)[1 - J0(u)^2 - J1(u)^2].
      - Therefore P(u) is proportional to 2 [1 - J0(u)^2 - J1(u)^2]. As u tends to infinity both J0 and J1 tend to 0, so the total is proportional to 2.
      - Fraction within u: F(u) = 1 - J0(u)^2 - J1(u)^2.
      - At the first dark ring u1 = 3.8317: J1 = 0 and J0 = -0.4028, so F = 1 - 0.1622 = 0.838.
    - **checks:**
      - Numerical integration of 4 J1(s)^2/s from 0 to 3.8317 with scipy.integrate.quad gives 1.6756; the total to infinity is 2, so the ratio is 0.8378, matching 1 - J0^2.
      - F(u) tends to 0 as u tends to 0 and to 1 as u tends to infinity.
      - Rings 2, 3, 4 give 91.0, 93.8 and 95.2 per cent.
    - **traps:**
      - Saying the central 84 per cent is the first bright ring.
      - Forgetting the area element s ds.

### Answer Lab facts

- **F-OSX3-CIRCAP (verified):** For a circular aperture of diameter D (large compared with the wavelength), the first minimum of the diffraction pattern is at theta = 1.22 lambda/D (theta in radians). By the Rayleigh criterion two point objects are just resolvable when the centre of one diffraction pattern lies over the first minimum of the other, i.e. when their angular separation is theta = 1.22 lambda/D.
  Source: [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 4.5 'Circular Apertures and Resolution'](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/4-5-circular-apertures-and-resolution)
- **F-HYP-AIRY (verified):** A point source seen through a small circular aperture gives not a bright dot but a diffuse disc (Airy's disc) surrounded by much fainter concentric rings; when this smearing exceeds that from aberrations the imaging is diffraction-limited. HyperPhysics' aperture calculator puts the first three dark rings at sin(theta) = 1.22, 2.233 and 3.238 times lambda/d.
  Source: [HyperPhysics (R. Nave), 'Circular Aperture Diffraction'](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/phyopt/cirapp2.html)

### Memory skeleton

- Fraunhofer: plane wave in, far field (lens focus) out; the single slit is the stepping stone
- Circular aperture: amplitude 2J₁(u)/u, u = πD sin θ/λ; Airy disc plus faint rings
- First dark ring: J₁ zero 3.8317 = 1.22π, sin θ = 1.22λ/D, screen radius 1.22λf/D
- Rayleigh: centre of one pattern on the first dark ring of the other; dθ = 1.22λ/D, RP = D/(1.22λ)
- Eye about 1 arcmin; normal magnification × telescope limit = eye limit
- Microscope: d = 0.61λ₀/N.A., N.A. = μ sin α; raise N.A., lower λ; magnification is not resolution

### Last-week summary

- Circle: I = I₀[2J₁(u)/u]², u = πD sin θ/λ; first zero 3.8317 = 1.22π, so sin θ = 1.22λ/D (slit: λ/a).
- Rayleigh: just resolved when one centre sits on the other's first dark ring; incoherent, intensities add; dθ = 1.22λ/D, RP = D/(1.22λ).
- Eye: 1.22 × 550 nm / 2 mm = 1.15 arcmin, about 1 arcmin; 1 arcsec = 4.848e-6 rad.
- Microscope: 2d sin α = 1.22λ → d = 0.61λ₀/(μ sin α); oil raises N.A., UV (quartz lenses) and electrons lower λ; extra magnification is empty.
