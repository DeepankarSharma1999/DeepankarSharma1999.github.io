### Module 0: UPSC orientation

*40 minutes · lesson*

Understand what the Civil Services Examination is, how its stages, papers and marks fit together, what the Physics optional demands, what the 680-topic registry does and does not promise, and what Days 91-120 will accomplish.

#### What the Civil Services Examination is

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) runs the Civil Services Examination (CSE) to select officers for the central services. The official 2026 notification says the examination "comprises two successive stages": the Civil Services (Preliminary) Examination, which is objective type and exists only to select candidates for the Main Examination, and the Civil Services (Main) Examination, which has a written part and an Interview (Personality Test) and selects candidates for the various services and posts.

You are preparing for CSE 2027. One honest caveat before anything else: every fact in this module comes from the CSE 2026 notification, because the 2027 notification has not been published yet. When it appears, this site will re-check every clause word by word. Until then, treat the structure below as "almost certainly the same, to be confirmed", not as a promise.

## Words you will see constantly

- **Candidate**: you, once you have filled the application form.
- **Notification**: the official document that announces the exam, its rules and its syllabus.
- **Syllabus**: the official list of what can be asked. This site's 680 topics are a map of it, not a replacement for it.
- **Stage**: Prelims or Mains. You cannot sit Mains without clearing Prelims in the same year.
- **Paper**: one examination sitting with its own marks and duration.

## How to use this module

You do not need to memorise anything here today. Read each section once, answer the checks at the end, and move on. Every number you meet below will come back on the exact day it matters (for example, negative marking returns the first time you sit a timed MCQ set). The purpose is orientation: knowing what the whole mountain looks like before you take the first step.

#### Prelims: two objective papers, 400 marks

The Preliminary Examination "will consist of two papers of Objective type (multiple choice questions) and carry a maximum of 400 marks". Each paper carries 200 marks and lasts two hours, and the question papers are set in both Hindi and English.

- **General Studies Paper I**: current events, history, geography, polity and governance, economic and social development, environment and ecology, and general science. This is the paper whose marks decide who is on the Prelims pass list.
- **General Studies Paper II (CSAT)**: comprehension, reasoning, basic numeracy and data interpretation. This paper is qualifying only (next section).

## Prelims is a screening test

The notification is explicit: Prelims "is meant to serve as a screening test only". The marks you get in Prelims are not counted when the final merit list is prepared. Roughly twelve to thirteen times the number of vacancies are admitted to Mains.

Two consequences follow:

1. There is no prize for a very high Prelims mark. You need to be safely above the cut-off, not at the top.
2. Everything you learn for Prelims GS I is also raw material for Mains, so Prelims study is never wasted even though its marks vanish.

## You must appear in both papers

Note 3 under the Prelims syllabus says it is "mandatory for the candidate to appear in both the Papers". A candidate who skips either paper is disqualified from that year's examination, even if the other paper went well. On this site, Day 1 already includes one CSAT topic (CT-001, main idea and theme) so that the second paper is never an afterthought.

#### CSAT's qualifying role and negative marking

Two official rules shape how you should think about Prelims.

## Rule 1: CSAT is pass or fail at 33 percent

The qualifying list for Mains is drawn "based on the criterion of minimum qualifying marks of 33% in General Studies Paper-II" and the total qualifying marks in General Studies Paper I that the Commission fixes. In plain words: CSAT must be cleared at 33 percent of 200 marks, and after that it plays no further role. GS Paper I then decides whether you are on the list.

For this learner the practical meaning is: CSAT needs steady, low-drama maintenance, not heroics. A short timed set most days (the site's recurring 25-40 minute CSAT block) keeps it safe. The one thing you may never do is ignore it: a brilliant GS Paper I with a CSAT below 33 percent is a failed Prelims.

## Rule 2: negative marking

Every Prelims question has four alternatives. For each wrong answer, "one-third (0.33) of the marks assigned to that question will be deducted". If you mark more than one option, it is treated as wrong even if one of your marks was right. If you leave a question blank, "there will be no penalty".

The marks used below are those of GS Paper I, where each question carries 2 marks, so a wrong answer costs 2 / 3 = 0.67 marks. (CSAT questions are generally 2.5 marks each, so a wrong CSAT answer costs 2.5 / 3, about 0.83; check the marks printed on the question paper.)

A worked example with 2-mark questions: four correct and two wrong gives 8 minus 2 x 0.67 = 8 minus 1.33, about 6.7 marks. Four correct and two blank gives 8.

What guessing does on average: with four equally likely options a blind guess earns 1/4 x 2 = 0.5 and loses 3/4 x 0.67 = 0.5 on average, so it gains nothing on average and only adds risk. After you eliminate one option the expected value turns positive (about +0.22 marks per question); after you eliminate two it is clearly positive (about +0.67). A guess has a positive expected value whenever your chance of being right is better than one in four. This is why the site teaches elimination as a skill and records "poor question selection" as its own error category in the Exam Lab.

## What this means on Day 1

Nothing yet. You will meet negative marking in practice the first time you sit a timed set. Today you only need to know that a blind guess among four options gains nothing on average and adds risk, that elimination is what turns a guess into a good bet, and that CSAT is a threshold, not a race.

#### Mains: nine written papers and an Interview

The Main Examination "will consist of a Written Examination and an Interview/Personality Test". The written part has "9 papers of conventional essay type", meaning you write answers by hand in answer booklets, not multiple choice.

Of the nine papers, two are qualifying (the language papers) and seven are counted for merit. The notification says marks "obtained for all the compulsory papers (Paper-I to Paper-VII) and Marks obtained in Interview/Personality Test will be counted for ranking".

| Paper | Name | Marks | Counted for merit? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Indian language (Hindi for you) | 300 | No, qualifying |
| B | English | 300 | No, qualifying |
| I | Essay | 250 | Yes |
| II | General Studies I | 250 | Yes |
| III | General Studies II | 250 | Yes |
| IV | General Studies III | 250 | Yes |
| V | General Studies IV | 250 | Yes |
| VI | Optional Paper 1 (Physics) | 250 | Yes |
| VII | Optional Paper 2 (Physics) | 250 | Yes |

The written sub-total is 1750 marks; the Personality Test adds 275; the grand total is 2025.

## The standard the examiners state

The notification says Mains "is intended to assess the overall intellectual traits and depth of understanding of candidates rather than merely the range of their information and memory". It adds that candidates "must give relevant, meaningful and succinct answers". Keep those three adjectives; the site's Mains rubric is built from them.

## What this means for Day 1

Today's polity topics (for example GS-179, legislative procedure) sit in both Prelims GS I and Mains GS II. When you see "Prelims GS1 + Mains GS2" on a topic card, it means the same facts are tested twice in two different ways: once by recognition, once by written explanation.

#### Essay and General Studies I to IV

Five of the seven merit papers are common to every candidate, whatever their optional.

## Essay (Paper I, 250 marks)

A paper of conventional essay writing. It is where reading, structure and clear English or Hindi expression meet. The registry carries 12 Essay topics under the Writing/Language family.

## The four General Studies papers (250 marks each)

The official headings are:

- **GS I**: Indian Heritage and Culture, History and Geography of the World and Society.
- **GS II**: Governance, Constitution, Polity, Social Justice and International Relations.
- **GS III**: Technology, Economic Development, Bio-diversity, Environment, Security and Disaster Management.
- **GS IV**: Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude.

For GS IV the notification explains the method: the paper tests "attitude and approach to issues relating to integrity, probity in public life" and problem solving, and "questions may utilise the case study approach".

## The level

The notification says the nature and standard of the GS papers "will be such that a well-educated person will be able to answer them without any specialized study", testing "general awareness of a variety of subjects" and the "ability to analyze, and take a view on conflicting socio-economic goals, objectives and demands".

Read that generously and strictly at once. Generously: you do not need a degree in each subject. Strictly: "take a view" means the examiner expects an argument, not a list. The difference between a Mains answer and a Prelims option is exactly this: in Mains you must show the reasoning.

## Why this matters today

Six of today's seven polity topics are Mains GS II material as well as Prelims material. When the site asks you to write a short answer on Directive Principles (GS-165), it is practising a GS II skill, not giving you extra homework.

#### The Physics Optional: Papers I and II

You have chosen Physics as your optional subject. The official rule on level is blunt: the scope of optional papers "is broadly of the honours degree level i.e. a level higher than the bachelors' degree and lower than the masters' degree". Two papers, 250 marks each, 500 marks in total, all counted for merit.

## What Paper I covers (official section headings)

- Mechanics of particles; mechanics of rigid bodies; mechanics of continuous media; special relativity.
- Waves; geometrical optics; interference; diffraction; polarisation and modern optics.
- Electrostatics and magnetostatics; current electricity.
- Electromagnetic waves and blackbody radiation.
- Thermodynamics; statistical physics.

## What Paper II covers

- Quantum mechanics.
- Atomic and molecular physics.
- Nuclear and particle physics.
- Solid state physics, devices and electronics.

## What "honours level" demands in practice

Physics questions in Mains ask you to derive, not to recall. A typical item begins "Derive..." or "Show that..." and expects a labelled diagram, every assumption stated, correct algebra and calculus, units carried through and a limiting-case check. The site's Physics rubric lists exactly these criteria, and the registry's 208 Physics topics are organised so that each one has a derivation family and a numerical family.

Starting from zero, this is the part of the examination with the longest runway. That is why three Physics topics appear on Day 1 (PH-001 Newton's laws and conservation laws, PH-008 gravitational self-energy, PH-015 rigid-body degrees of freedom and Euler's theorem) and why Module 4 contains the mathematics bridges you need before you open them. Nobody expects you to derive the self-energy of a sphere today. The goal today is to be able to read the derivation when you meet it.

#### Qualifying language papers: Hindi Paper A and English Paper B

Two of the nine Mains papers add no marks to your rank but can silently remove you from the competition.

## The rule

Paper A is "one of the Indian Language to be selected by the candidate from the Languages included in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution", 300 marks. Paper B is English, 300 marks. Both "will be of Matriculation or equivalent standard and will be of qualifying nature", and their marks "will not be counted for ranking".

The sting is in the second note: the Essay, General Studies and Optional papers of "only such candidates will be taken cognizance who attain 25% marks in 'Indian Language' and 25% in English". If you fall below 25 percent in either language paper, your other seven papers are not evaluated at all. Months of work can disappear on a paper you considered easy.

## For this learner

You have Hindi as your Paper A language. The registry carries 14 Hindi topics and 11 English topics (together with 12 Essay topics they make the 37 Writing/Language topics). The stated aim of these papers is to test the ability "to read and understand serious discursive prose, and to express ideas clearly and correctly" in the language concerned.

The strategy the site follows is low frequency, non-zero attention: a Hindi or English check appears periodically in the schedule, and the Days 91-120 calendar reserves a diagnostic for each. You will not spend hours on them, but you will never go months without confirming they are safe.

## A quick self-check

Can you read a serious Hindi editorial and summarise it in Hindi? Can you write a 150-word formal paragraph in Devanagari without help? Module 2 asks you exactly these questions, and the answer decides how often the language blocks appear for you.

#### The Personality Test (Interview)

Candidates who reach the written cut-off "shall be summoned by them for an Interview/Personality Test". The number summoned is "about twice of the number of vacancies". The Interview "will carry 275 marks (with no minimum qualifying marks)".

## What is assessed

The notification describes the object as assessing "the personal suitability of the candidate for a career in public service by a Board of competent and unbiased observers". The Board has your career record in front of it and asks "questions on matters of general interest". Qualities named include "mental alertness, critical powers of assimilation, clear and logical exposition, balance of judgement, variety and depth of interest, ability for social cohesion and leadership, intellectual and moral integrity".

## What it is not

The technique "is not that of a strict cross-examination but of a natural, though directed and purposive conversation". It "is not intended to be a test either of the specialized or general knowledge of the candidates which has been already tested through their written papers". Candidates are expected to have "taken an intelligent interest" in events around them and "in modern currents of thought and in new discoveries".

## Why it is mentioned on Day 1

Because 275 marks out of 2025 is a large share, and because the qualities it names are built by the way you study, not by a crash course at the end. The habit of reading a news item and connecting it to a syllabus area, the habit of saying an idea aloud in your own words (teach-back in Module 3), the habit of taking a balanced view in a written answer: these are interview preparation disguised as daily work. You do not need to think about the Interview again for a long time, but you should know the whole path.

#### The marks structure: merit papers versus qualifying papers

Here is the whole examination in one table, using only the official figures.

| Component | Marks | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Prelims GS Paper I | 200 | Screening; decides the Prelims list; not counted for final merit |
| Prelims GS Paper II (CSAT) | 200 | Screening; qualifying at 33%; not counted for final merit |
| Mains Paper A: Hindi | 300 | Qualifying at 25%; not counted for ranking |
| Mains Paper B: English | 300 | Qualifying at 25%; not counted for ranking |
| Mains Paper I: Essay | 250 | Merit |
| Mains Papers II-V: GS I-IV | 1000 | Merit (250 each) |
| Mains Papers VI-VII: Physics 1 and 2 | 500 | Merit (250 each) |
| Written sub-total | 1750 | Merit |
| Personality Test | 275 | Merit; no minimum |
| Grand total | 2025 | Final ranking |

## Three kinds of marks

1. **Screening marks** (Prelims, 400): they open the door and are then discarded.
2. **Threshold marks** (Hindi, English, 600 together; CSAT inside Prelims): you must cross a line, after which the surplus is irrelevant.
3. **Merit marks** (1750 written plus 275 Interview): every single mark here moves your rank.

## How the final rank is produced

Marks "obtained by the candidates in the Civil Services (Main) Examination (Written part as well as Interview/Personality Test) would determine their final ranking". Service allotment then follows rank and the preferences you submitted.

## The planning consequence

Time should flow toward merit marks, with threshold papers kept safe at minimum cost. Physics (500) and the four GS papers (1000) together are 1500 of the 2025 marks. That is why this site's daily plan gives Physics a daily block from Day 1 and treats CSAT and the language papers as maintenance.

#### Prelims versus Mains, and why preparation must be integrated

## The two stages ask different things of the same knowledge

| | Prelims | Mains |
|---|---|---|
| Format | Multiple choice, four options | Hand-written answers, conventional essay type |
| Marks | 400, screening only | 1750 written + 275 Interview, all merit |
| Penalty | One-third per wrong answer | None, but irrelevant content earns nothing |
| Skill tested | Recognition, elimination, precision about facts | Understanding, analysis, "take a view", succinct expression |
| Papers | GS I, CSAT | Essay, GS I-IV, Optional I-II, Hindi, English |

The stages are "successive": you cannot attempt Mains in a year unless you have qualified Prelims that year. They are not, however, separate subjects. Today's topic GS-186 (Supreme Court: jurisdiction and review) can appear in Prelims as a statement to judge true or false and in Mains GS II as a 15-mark question asking you to evaluate judicial review.

## Why integrated preparation

Preparing Prelims first and Mains later fails for three reasons:

1. **Time**: the same months must carry nine Mains papers; there is no separate season long enough for Physics at honours level.
2. **Depth**: a fact learned only for recognition is forgotten fast; a fact learned with its reasoning is both easier to recall in an MCQ and usable in an answer.
3. **Physics**: the optional appears only in Mains, but 500 merit marks at honours level cannot be built in the weeks between Prelims and Mains. It must be built from Day 1.

## How the site integrates

Each topic card tells you which papers it serves. Polity topics carry objective checks and answer-writing prompts side by side. Physics topics carry derivations and numericals. CSAT appears as a daily timed set. The revision queue mixes all of them. You are never "in Prelims mode" or "in Mains mode"; you are building one body of knowledge tested two ways.

#### The 680-topic registry: what it is and what it does not guarantee

This site is organised around a registry that maps the official syllabus to a fixed set of micro-topics.

## What is in it

- **102 official syllabus clauses**, each recorded with its exact official wording, a plain-language explanation and the kind of question examiners ask on it.
- **16 official plan provisions** (the rules you have just read: stages, marks, qualifying thresholds, negative marking, the Interview).
- **2 project-defined requirements** (current affairs as a continuous stream, and full-paper examination skills) that the official text implies but does not list as clauses.
- **680 canonical micro-topics**: 398 General Studies, 208 Physics, 37 CSAT and 37 Writing/Language (14 Hindi, 12 Essay, 11 English). Every topic is attached to at least one primary official clause, and every clause has topics attached.

A "micro-topic" is a unit small enough to study in one sitting and large enough to be examined. GS-193, "Finance Commission and grants", is one such unit.

## What it does not guarantee

The registry states its own limits, and you should take them seriously:

- "A mapping does not prove depth; depth does not prove review; review does not prove learner mastery." A topic existing in the list means the syllabus clause has a home here, nothing more.
- **Coverage is not mastery.** Opening all 680 topics would make you someone who has seen the syllabus, not someone who can pass. Mastery is tracked per topic in stages 0-6 (Module 1) and only rises on evidence.
- **Current affairs are dynamic.** The clause on current events is marked "dynamic coverage required" because no fixed list can hold it. The daily current-affairs block exists for this reason.
- **The syllabus is the 2026 syllabus.** Every mapping will be re-checked when the 2027 notification appears.

## How to hold this in mind

Treat the 680 topics as the site's map of the territory. The map is checked against the official document, but you still have to walk every path, and the site only marks a path walked when you leave evidence on it.

## The principles that make it work

- Every cycle runs attempt, analyse, classify, repair, retest, record; nothing counts as done without the record.
- Analysis time is at least equal to attempt time while errors remain on merit papers.
- Official UPSC papers are the primary material, and each official paper is used for a full simulation at most once.
- The 12-13 hour daily window protects 7-8 hours of sleep, three meals and breaks.
- The calendar is a plan: "nothing in it is attempted, completed or scored until the learner does the work and records it".

#### Checks

**Q.** How many stages does the Civil Services Examination have, and what does each one decide?

**A.** Two successive stages. Prelims (objective) only selects candidates for Mains; its marks are not counted for final merit. Mains (nine written papers plus the Interview/Personality Test) decides selection and rank.

**Q.** What is the minimum you need in CSAT, and what happens to a wrong answer in Prelims?

**A.** CSAT (GS Paper II) is qualifying at 33 percent. For every wrong answer one-third of that question's marks is deducted; marking more than one option counts as wrong; a blank answer carries no penalty.

**Q.** Which Mains papers are counted for merit, and what is the written total?

**A.** Essay (250), GS I-IV (250 each), Optional Paper 1 and Paper 2 (250 each): seven papers, 1750 marks. The Personality Test adds 275 for a grand total of 2025. Paper A (Hindi) and Paper B (English), 300 each, are qualifying at 25 percent and not counted.

**Q.** What happens if you get less than 25 percent in the English paper but write a superb Essay?

**A.** The Essay is not evaluated. Essay, GS and Optional papers are taken cognizance of only for candidates who reach 25 percent in both the Indian language paper and English.

**Q.** At what level are the Physics optional papers set, and how many marks do they carry?

**A.** Honours-degree level: above a bachelor's degree and below a master's. Two papers of 250 marks each, 500 in total, all counted for merit.

**Q.** What does the 680-topic registry guarantee, and what does it not?

**A.** It guarantees that every official syllabus clause has at least one mapped topic (398 GS, 208 Physics, 37 CSAT, 37 Writing/Language) with the exact official wording recorded. It does not guarantee depth, review or your mastery; coverage is not mastery, current affairs are dynamic, and everything will be re-checked against the 2027 notification.

### Module 1: How to use this site

*20 minutes · lesson*

Learn the parts of the site in plain words, how progress is recorded, what the mastery stages mean, and why 'read' is not 'retained'.

#### The shape of a study day

Every day on this site has the same skeleton, so you never have to decide what to do, only when to start.

1. **Day modules** (today only): the onboarding you are doing now.
2. **Topics of the day**: a short list of canonical micro-topics (today eleven: seven Polity, three Physics, one CSAT). Each opens as a focused lesson.
3. **Recurring blocks**: current affairs with issue-to-syllabus links, a timed CSAT set, and ten to fifteen minutes of active recall plus error logging.
4. **Revision queue**: items from earlier days that are due today.
5. **Done for now**: a button that saves your place and closes the screen without judgement.

Nothing is locked. You may open any topic at any time. But the day view puts the recommended order in front of you so that a tired brain can simply press "Open" on the first card.

## One module at a time

Lessons are shown one section per screen with a "Next" button. There is no long scrolling page. If a section feels slow, you still finish that section, press Next, and the previous one disappears. This is deliberate: the screen holds one idea, and the "All sections" list is folded away until you want it.

## What counts as finishing

Opening a topic does nothing to its mastery stage. Reading all its sections marks it "read", which is stage 0 with a note. Mastery moves only when you pass checks, write answers, or recall it later. Section 7 explains the stages.

#### Lesson modules and master notes

## Focused lesson modules

Each canonical topic opens as a lesson built from short sections, exactly like this module. A section carries one idea, a worked example where useful, and sometimes a small table. The headings are stable, so when you return for revision you can jump to "Exceptions" or "Common traps" without re-reading the whole thing.

Where a topic has a detailed, reviewed lesson, its card says so. Where it has not yet reached that state, the card shows what exists (sources, verified questions, practice) and what is pending. The registry's coverage status is shown rather than hidden; "topic exists but content is insufficient" is a label you will see, and it is honest.

## Master notes

The master note is the single condensed page for a topic: definitions, the article or equation numbers that matter, the two or three exceptions examiners love, and the links to sources. It is written to be read in five minutes on a revision day.

Master notes are not meant to be copied into your own notebook. Module 3 explains why copying is the lowest-value activity in preparation. Instead, read the master note, close it, and write from memory what you can; then compare. The gap you find is your study list.

## Your own notes

Every topic has a notes box that is yours. Use it for the mnemonic you invented, the mistake you made, the question you want to ask. Keep it short; the master note already holds the content.

#### Active-recall notes and the revision queue

## Active-recall notes

Under every lesson sits a set of recall cards: a question on the front, the answer folded away. Reading the answer without first trying to produce it is the one way to use them wrongly. The site cannot check this; it depends on your honesty.

## The revision queue

The queue is driven by one button. When you press **Mark mastered (your own label)** on a topic, the topic is scheduled to return **+1, +3, +7 and +21 study days** after the day on which you pressed it. The day numbers are the site's own day counter, not calendar dates, so a day off does not make the queue pile up.

When a topic is due it appears in the queue on the day view, sorted by its due day (the earliest due first). Try to recall the topic from memory, then press **Revised** to record that recall slot as done; the topic then waits for its next interval. The queue is the first thing to do after the warm-up, because retrieval is most valuable when it is slightly difficult.

## Mark mastered is not a mastery stage

"Mark mastered (your own label)" is a scheduling label that you set yourself. It is separate from the six evidence stages in section 7, which rise only on evidence the site can see. Pressing the button does not raise a stage, and a stage never makes a topic appear in the queue.

## Why four intervals

The spacing is chosen so that each retrieval happens just as the memory is starting to fade.

## What the queue does not do

It does not add new material. It only asks you to retrieve what you have already met, in small doses, at the right moments.

#### Source pages and verified PYQs

## Source pages

Behind each topic are the original documents: NCERT chapters, standard references, official texts. The source page shows the excerpt the lesson is built from, with the book, chapter and page range stated. Where the exact page has not yet been verified against the physical PDF, the page carries a notice: "page-level mapping pending". That notice means "we know the chapter, we have not yet confirmed the page numbers", not "this is unreliable". Treat an unverified page as something you check, not something you trust blindly.

## Verified PYQs

PYQ means "previous year question". The site stores official UPSC questions only after they have been verified against an image of the actual question paper, and it says so on each one. No answer key is stored. UPSC releases official Prelims answer keys only after the final result; this project has not verified any key, so none is asserted. This is a deliberate decision for two reasons:

1. UPSC publishes questions, not explanations, and the site will not present a third-party key as official.
2. Working out the answer, with the source open, is the learning. A stored key would turn the exercise into reading.

When you open a PYQ you write your analysis: what the question is really testing, which option you choose and why, which syllabus clause it belongs to. Saving that analysis is what earns stage 3 ("UPSC application") for the topic. You will do this for the first time today with the 2026 polity questions in the exam drill.

## Original practice

Alongside PYQs each topic carries original practice items written for this site: MCQs in UPSC style, short answer prompts, derivations and numericals for Physics, timed CSAT passages. These are clearly labelled as original, never dressed up as official.

#### Error log and the Exam Lab

## The error log

Every wrong answer, every blank you should have attempted, every derivation you could not finish goes into the error log with a category. The categories are the same sixteen used in the Days 91-120 laboratory, among them conceptual misunderstanding, factual recall, question interpretation, elimination error, calculation error, unit error, sign error, careless reading, time pressure, poor question selection, incomplete answer and weak structure.

Logging takes thirty seconds and is the highest-value thirty seconds of the day, because the pattern of your errors is more useful than the count. Five "careless reading" entries in a week are a specific, fixable problem. Five scattered errors are noise.

## Exam Lab

The Exam Lab is where timed work happens: a CSAT set, an MCQ set on a topic, a Physics numerical with a clock, a 150-word answer with a seven-minute limit. It enforces exam rules: the timer runs, there are no hints, and solutions stay hidden until you close the attempt. After an attempt it asks you to classify each error and sets a retest date.

The Lab also keeps the mock-test records (section 9) and is the home of the full-paper simulations from Day 91.

## The honest rule

Nothing you do in the Exam Lab is counted until you record it: the attempt, the analysis and the repair list. A test stays marked "analysis incomplete" until every error in it has a category. The site does not stop you starting another test, so the discipline is yours, and it protects you from the most common failure: taking test after test without learning from any of them.

#### Timers, 'Done for now' and the controls built for attention

This site assumes that attention is a resource to be managed, not a virtue to be demanded.

## Timers

The site has a focus timer. You choose a block of 5, 10, 15, 20 or 25 minutes, then start it, pause it or reset it. The length is a default, not a target: 25 minutes is the longest block the timer offers, and a shorter block on a bad day is still a block. The timer does not prompt you to take a break; take the break yourself (Module 3 gives the pattern).

## Done for now

The "Done for now" button appears on the lesson and day screens. It saves your place and returns you to the day view. It does not mark anything complete and it does not mark anything failed. It exists so that stopping is always safe and never costs you progress.

## One screen, one task

Lessons show one section at a time. The diagnostic shows one area at a time. The practice set shows one question at a time. Navigation lists are folded away until you ask for them. The aim is that at any moment the screen contains the thing you are doing and nothing else.

## Focus controls

The site has a dark mode, switched from the top bar, for evening sessions. Beyond that the controls are deliberately few.

## Minimum viable session

If the day is going badly, the site's definition of a completed study day is deliberately small: one recall block or one section, logged. Module 3 explains why this rule exists and why it works.

#### Mastery stages 0 to 6 and their evidence rules

Each of the 680 topics carries a mastery stage. Stages rise only on evidence the site can see; they never rise because you opened something.

| Stage | Name | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Not started | Nothing yet (reading the lesson keeps you here, with a "read" marker) |
| 1 | Foundation | A module check or foundation check passed (self-marked after revealing the answer) |
| 2 | Conceptual understanding | At least 60 percent of the topic's module checks passed (minimum 2), or two conceptual checks passed |
| 3 | UPSC application | A saved analysis of a verified official question, at least 40 words |
| 4 | Objective or applied proficiency | 70 percent or better on an unseen objective or numerical set of at least 4 items |
| 5 | Written or analytical | An independent written task (answer, derivation or solution) rated 60 percent or better on the rubric |
| 6 | Retained mastery | A delayed check at 80 percent or better, at least 7 study days after the stage 4 evidence |

## How to read the table

- The stages are strictly cumulative: stage n needs the evidence for stage n minus 1 first. A PYQ analysis saved before the module checks are passed is kept, but it counts toward stage 3 only once stages 1 and 2 are in place. The topic shows its current stage and lists the missing evidence for the next one.
- "Unseen" means items you have not attempted before. The site has a Reset set button and cannot check whether a set is truly new to you, so this rule rests on your honesty: repeating a set you have already seen measures memory of the answers, not the skill.
- The rubric for stage 5 is the site's Mains rubric (demand identification, structure, evidence, balance, word limit) or the Physics rubric (method, assumptions, algebra, units, limiting cases). You rate your own answer against it, criterion by criterion.
- Stage 6 is the only stage with a waiting period. It cannot be rushed, which is the point.

## What a stage is for

A stage is a planning signal. It is not a grade and it is not shown to anyone but you. The revision queue (section 3) is separate: it is sorted by due day and is started by the Mark mastered button, not by a stage.

#### 'Read' is not 'retained': the vocabulary of progress

Preparation fails most often through a vocabulary error: calling something "done" when it has only been seen. The site uses ten distinct words, and it is worth knowing them.

| Word | What it means | How the site records it |
|---|---|---|
| Read | You went through the lesson sections | A read marker; stage unchanged |
| Encountered | The idea appeared in a PYQ, a news item or another topic | A cross-link on the topic card |
| Understood | You can explain it in your own words | Module checks passed (stage 1-2) |
| Recalled | You produced it closed-book after a gap | Recall cards used closed-book; "Revised" pressed in the queue |
| Practised | You applied it to questions with the material available | Original practice attempts logged |
| Proficient | You applied it to unseen questions, timed, without material | 70 percent on 4+ unseen items (stage 4) |
| Exam ready | You wrote or derived it independently to the rubric standard | Rubric 60 percent or better (stage 5) |
| Retained | It survived a delay of at least seven study days | Delayed check 80 percent or better (stage 6) |
| Tested under examination conditions | It appeared in a full timed paper you sat in the Exam Lab | Mock-test record linked to the topic |
| Retested after error correction | You got it wrong, repaired it, and were retested on fresh items | Error-log entry closed with a retest record |

## The trap this table prevents

"I have read Polity" describes the first row. "I am ready for Polity in Mains" requires the seventh and eighth rows for the relevant topics. Most of the distance between the two is covered by rows four to six, which feel slower than reading and are where the marks are.

## How to use it today

When you finish a Day 1 topic, look at which row you actually reached. For most topics today the honest answer will be "read" or "understood". That is exactly right for Day 1.

#### Mock-test records, backup and export

## Mock-test records

Every timed attempt in the Exam Lab produces a record: the paper or set, the date, the duration actually used, the raw result, the negative-marking loss where relevant, accuracy and attempt rate, the time per question where you recorded it, the per-topic breakdown, and the error classification. For written work the record holds your rubric ratings criterion by criterion.

Records are kept for the whole preparation. From Day 91 the laboratory reads them to pick the next paper and to check that repairs actually held on retest. A record without an analysis is flagged as "analysis incomplete" until every error has a category.

## Where your data lives

Everything you enter (notes, recall ratings, diagnostic answers, error log, mock records, mastery stages) is stored in your browser on this device. Nothing is sent to a server. This gives you privacy and speed, and it carries one responsibility: if the browser data is cleared, the record is gone.

## Backup and export

Settings has an **Export progress (JSON)** button, which downloads a single file containing all your progress, and an **Import** button, which restores from such a file on the same or another device. Do this once a week, and always before clearing browser data or switching machines. The export is a JSON data file for restoring; the site does not produce printable summaries.

## A habit to start today

At the end of Day 1, open Settings and export your first file. It will be small. The habit is what matters, and the day you need it, you will be glad it is boring and automatic.

#### Checks

**Q.** What does opening and reading a lesson do to the topic's mastery stage?

**A.** Nothing. Reading sets a 'read' marker and leaves the stage at 0. Stages rise only on evidence: checks passed, a saved PYQ analysis, unseen-set results, rubric-rated answers and delayed recall.

**Q.** What starts the revision queue for a topic, and on which study days does the topic return?

**A.** Pressing "Mark mastered (your own label)" on the topic. It then returns +1, +3, +7 and +21 study days after that day, shown in the queue sorted by due day; you press "Revised" after each recall. The label is separate from the six evidence stages.

**Q.** Why does the site store verified PYQs without an answer key?

**A.** UPSC publishes questions, not official explanations, and releases official Prelims answer keys only after the final result; this project has not verified any key, so none is asserted. Working out the answer with the source open, and saving your analysis, is the learning; that saved analysis is the evidence for stage 3.

**Q.** What is the difference between 'proficient' and 'retained' on this site?

**A.** Proficient (stage 4) is 70 percent or better on an unseen set of at least four items, usually soon after study. Retained (stage 6) is a delayed check at 80 percent or better taken at least seven study days later, showing the knowledge survived a gap.

**Q.** What does 'Done for now' do, and what does it not do?

**A.** It saves exactly where you were and returns you to the day view. It does not mark anything complete and does not mark anything failed; stopping is always safe.

### Module 3: Learning foundations

*25 minutes · lesson*

Twelve short, practical techniques the daily plan depends on: how to recall, space, correct, mix, explain, focus, rest, survive a bad day, and return after an interruption.

#### Active recall: produce it before you read it

Active recall means asking yourself a question and producing the answer from memory before you look. It is the single most effective study technique known, and it is uncomfortable, which is why people avoid it.

## How to do it here

After reading a lesson section, close it and say or write what it said. Then reopen and compare. Use the recall cards under each lesson the same way: read the question, answer aloud or on paper, then reveal.

## Why it works

Retrieving a memory strengthens it far more than re-exposing yourself to it. The effort of pulling the answer out is the learning. A smooth reading session feels productive and leaves little behind; a halting recall session feels like failure and leaves a durable trace.

## Rule for today

For every Day 1 topic, before you open the master note, write three things you already know or guess about the title. Wrong guesses are fine; they make the correct version stick.

#### Spaced repetition: return just as it starts to fade

Memory decays on a curve. A fact recalled a day later, then three days later, then a week, then three weeks, is retained far longer than the same fact repeated five times in one evening.

## How the site does it

The revision queue schedules each finished topic for study day +1, +3, +7 and +21. You do not have to plan this; you only have to do the "due today" list when it appears, before new material.

## Two habits that make it work

1. Do the due items first. They take ten to fifteen minutes and are the highest-value part of the day because the memory is at the point of fading.
2. Rate yourself honestly after each reveal. An honest "struggled" brings the item back sooner, which is exactly what you want.

## What spacing does not do

It does not teach. It maintains. If an item was never understood, spacing it will only remind you that it was never understood, which is itself useful: send it back to the lesson.

#### Closed-book retrieval: the book is not in the exam hall

Closed-book retrieval is active recall with a stricter rule: no notes, no master note, no phone, for the whole block. It is how every revision-queue item is presented and how every Exam Lab attempt runs.

## Why the strictness matters

With the book open you are testing recognition ("yes, I have seen this"). With the book closed you are testing production ("I can state this"). UPSC tests production in Mains and, through four confusable options, something close to it in Prelims. Only closed-book practice measures the thing the exam measures.

## How to run a block

1. Write the topic title at the top of a blank page.
2. For ten minutes write everything you can: definitions, numbers, exceptions, a diagram.
3. Open the master note and mark, in a different colour, what you missed or got wrong.
4. The marked items become today's recall cards.

## Today

Do one closed-book block at the end of the day on whichever Polity topic you spent longest on. Expect to produce little. The gap is the point.

#### Error correction: the log is the curriculum

A wrong answer is the most informative event in a study day, and the one most people discard fastest.

## The routine

Every error goes into the error log with one of the sixteen categories (conceptual misunderstanding, factual recall, question interpretation, elimination error, calculation error, unit error, sign error, careless reading, time pressure, poor question selection, and so on). Each entry gets a one-line repair ("re-read Article 368 majorities; make a card") and a retest date.

## Why the category matters more than the count

Ten errors spread across ten categories are noise. Ten errors with six in "careless reading" are a single fixable habit: slow down on the stem, underline "not" and "only". The log makes the pattern visible within a week.

## Retest, do not reread

A corrected error is not fixed until you have answered a fresh item on the same point, closed-book, after a gap. The log holds the retest date and the Exam Lab serves the item. Rereading the explanation feels like repair but is not.

#### Interleaving: mix the kinds of problem

Interleaving means practising different topics or problem types in a mixed order rather than in blocks of one kind. Studying ten amendment questions in a row teaches you the pattern of amendment questions; mixing amendments with Finance Commission and Supreme Court items teaches you to recognise which topic a question belongs to, which is what the exam demands.

## How the site interleaves

Daily CSAT sets mix passage types. Topic practice sets draw from the day's topics together. The revision queue by its nature mixes days. Day 1 itself interleaves Polity, Physics and CSAT.

## Why it feels worse and works better

Blocked practice feels fluent because the previous question primes the next. Interleaved practice feels harder and slower, and the slower, effortful identification of "what kind of problem is this?" is the skill that transfers to the exam hall.

## A rule of thumb

Learn in blocks; practise in mixtures. First exposure to a topic can be focused. Every practice set after that should mix.

#### Teach-back: explain it to an empty chair

Teach-back means explaining a concept aloud, in full sentences, as if to someone who has never heard of it. No notes. If you cannot explain it simply, you have found the exact spot you do not yet understand.

## How to do it

1. Pick one idea from a section, for example "why Directive Principles are not enforceable in court".
2. Set a two-minute timer and explain it aloud to the chair opposite you.
3. Notice where you hesitate, wave your hands or say "basically". That is the gap.
4. Reopen the section, read only the part you fumbled, and try again.

## Why it works for Mains and the Interview

Mains asks for "clear and logical exposition" in writing; the Personality Test asks for it in speech. Teach-back practises both, and it exposes the difference between recognising an explanation and producing one.

## Today

After the Polity bridges in Module 4, teach back the difference between the government and the Constitution. Two minutes.

#### Focused study blocks: one task, one timer

A focused block is a fixed period, typically 25 minutes, on one task with everything else closed. It ends when the timer ends, not when the task ends.

## Why a fixed length

Open-ended sessions invite drift. A block with a known end makes starting easier ("it is only 25 minutes") and makes stopping clean. Over a 12-13 hour window you will run perhaps fourteen to eighteen blocks with breaks and meals between them; the number of blocks, not the number of hours, is the honest measure of a day.

## Setting the length

If the diagnostic placed attention at "build alongside", start with 15-minute blocks. Increase by five minutes only after a week of blocks that ended with you wanting more. If 25 minutes already feels short, 40 is the ceiling before a break.

## During the block

One tab, one topic, one page. Write intrusive thoughts on a parking sheet beside you and return to them at the break. If you finish the task early, start the recall for it; do not open a new topic mid-block.

#### Strategic breaks: part of the work, not a reward

A break is what lets the next block be a real block. Skipping breaks does not add study time; it converts later blocks into tired rereading.

## The pattern

- Five minutes after a 25-minute block (the longest block the site's timer offers); ten after a longer block that you time yourself.
- A longer break (30-45 minutes) after every three or four blocks.
- Three meals and 7-8 hours of sleep are fixed points in the 12-13 hour window, as the Days 91-120 calendar also assumes.

## What a good break contains

Stand up, water, a window, stairs. Nothing with a feed. A phone break is not a break for attention; it is a switch to a different demanding task.

## What to do at the end of the break

Return to the exact screen you left. The site keeps it waiting. The first minute back is for re-reading the last paragraph, not for deciding what to do; the deciding was done when you planned the block.

#### Minimum viable study session: the day is never lost

Some days collapse: health, family, a low attention day. The minimum viable session is a definition of "I studied today" that is small enough to be done on the worst day.

## The definition used here

One revision-queue block (ten to fifteen minutes) or one lesson section with its check, logged. That is a study day. The queue advances and tomorrow starts from a saved place.

## Why this matters more than it sounds

The biggest losses in long preparation are not weak days; they are the chains of zero days that follow a weak day, when "I missed yesterday" becomes "I am behind" becomes "I will restart Monday". A minimum session breaks the chain.

## Two rules

1. Never do less than the minimum, even at midnight.
2. Never scold yourself for doing only the minimum. Record it, back up, sleep.

#### Avoid passive rereading: fluency is not knowledge

Rereading a chapter feels productive because the second time through it reads smoothly. That smoothness is familiarity, not memory, and it disappears the moment the text is not in front of you.

## The test

After reading a section, close it and write the three main points. If you can, rereading was unnecessary. If you cannot, rereading would not have helped; recall would.

## What to do instead

- First read: read to understand, slowly, once.
- Second contact: recall cards, not the text.
- Third contact: a question or a closed-book block.
- Return to the text only to check a specific gap the recall exposed.

## Highlighting

Highlighting is rereading with a pen. Use it only to mark what you got wrong in a closed-book check. A page that is half yellow has told you nothing.

#### Avoid unnecessary note copying: the master note already exists

Copying a chapter into a notebook feels like work and produces a worse copy of the book. It consumes the hours that recall and practice need.

## When notes are worth making

- The one-line mnemonic you invented.
- The exception you got wrong twice.
- A diagram you drew from memory and corrected.
- A table that reorganises something the book presents as prose.

## When they are not

- Rewriting definitions that are already in the master note.
- Summarising a chapter you have read once and not yet recalled.
- Making the notebook neat.

## The site's design

Every topic has a master note, so there is nothing to copy. Your notes box is for the four kinds of note above. If you catch yourself writing a paragraph that exists in the master note, stop, close the note, and write it from memory instead; that is the version worth keeping.

#### Recovering from interruptions: return, do not restart

Interruptions will happen across a 12-13 hour day. The skill is not avoiding them but returning cheaply.

## The routine

1. When interrupted, press "Done for now" or pause the timer. One line on the parking sheet: where you were and what you were about to do.
2. Deal with the interruption.
3. Return to the saved screen. Read the parking line, re-read the last paragraph, resume the timer.
4. Do not reconsider the plan. Deciding again is the expensive part.

## After a long gap

If the gap was hours, start the next block with five minutes of recall on the interrupted topic rather than new reading. This restores context faster than rereading and counts as study.

## The habit

A visible pause and a visible resume are the same skill whether the interruption is a phone call or a lost hour. Practise it from today.

**A.** One revision-queue block or one lesson section with its check, logged. It exists to prevent one bad day from becoming a chain of zero days.

**Q.** What should you do in the first minute after returning from an interruption?

**A.** Return to the saved screen, read the parking-sheet line, re-read the last paragraph and resume the timer. Do not re-decide the plan; if the gap was long, start with five minutes of recall rather than new reading.
